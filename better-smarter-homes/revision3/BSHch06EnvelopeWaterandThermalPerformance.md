# Chapter 6 — Envelope, Water, and Thermal Performance

*How the building stays dry, warm, and cool*

The envelope is the shell that separates inside from outside: walls, roof, floor over ground, windows, and doors. Everything in this chapter works together to keep water out, keep conditioned air in, and keep the temperature inside comfortable with minimum energy input.

This chapter also covers how to measure thermal performance yourself — both for testing sample wall assemblies in the garage before construction and for verifying the finished home meets or exceeds the design targets.

---

## 6.1 Insulation Strategy Overview

**Plain language:** Insulation goes on the outside of the concrete structure wherever possible. This keeps the thermal mass (the heavy concrete) inside the warm zone, where it stabilizes temperatures. Insulation acts like a thermos — the concrete is the liquid, the foam is the wall of the thermos, and the outer block wythe is the outside of the thermos that you can touch.

**Technical detail:** Insulation placement by building element:

- **Above-grade walls:** 4 inches of polyiso rigid foam inside the double-wythe cavity (R-24 to R-26 continuous)
- **Below-grade walls:** Same cavity wall assembly plus exterior waterproofing stack with an additional insulation layer behind it (see the exterior waterproofing stack)
- **Under slab:** 4 inches of Type XIV EPS (R-16 continuous)
- **Slab edge:** 2-4 inches of rigid foam at the perimeter (code minimum R-10)
- **Roof option A (concrete slab):** Rigid foam above the slab under the waterproof membrane, or inside the service layer on the underside
- **Roof option B (steel deck shed roof):** 4-6 inches of closed-cell spray polyurethane foam on the underside of the steel deck
- **Service layer:** Conditioned or unconditioned depending on design — if unconditioned, insulation at the ceiling of the living space below; if conditioned, insulation at the roof/exterior envelope only

**Why insulation outside, mass inside:**
- Concrete has high thermal mass (stores heat/cool for hours or days)
- When mass is inside the insulation, indoor temperature changes slowly — stable comfort
- When mass is outside the insulation (like a stucco house), it doesn't help indoor comfort and can actually hurt energy bills in climates with cold winters

**Contradiction resolved:** The design philosophy wants both "concrete on the outside for the castle look" and "insulation on the outside for thermal performance." The double-wythe cavity wall solves both: the outer decorative block wythe is visually solid stone, but structurally it's just a protective skin over the insulation, which sits between it and the actual load-bearing inner wythe.

**Key code references:**
- ORSC N1101 (energy efficiency — thermal envelope requirements)
- ORSC N1102 (thermal envelope — insulation minimum R-values)
- ORSC Table N1101.1(1) (prescriptive envelope requirements)

## 6.2 Insulation R-Value Requirements by Climate Zone

**Plain language:** How much insulation the code requires depends on how cold the winters get. Colder climates need more. This design exceeds the minimum significantly in all climate zones, so the same wall assembly works everywhere in Oregon.

**Technical detail:** Oregon ORSC 2023 minimum R-values:

| Element | Code Minimum | This Design |
|---|---|---|
| Above-grade walls | R-15 continuous | R-24 to R-26 continuous |
| Below-grade walls | R-15 continuous | R-24+ continuous |
| Slab under-floor | R-10 continuous | R-16 continuous |
| Slab edge | R-10 | R-10 to R-15 |
| Floors over unheated space | R-21 | (n/a — no unheated spaces below floors) |
| Flat ceilings / roof | R-38 | R-38 to R-60 depending on roof option |
| Vaulted ceilings | R-21 to R-38 | (n/a — this design uses flat or shed roof) |

**Climate-zone-dependent scaling:**

- **Mild climates (Willamette Valley):** Code minimums are adequate; this design exceeds them comfortably
- **Cold climates (mountain zones, East of Cascades):** Consider increasing roof insulation to R-49 or R-60 and adding an additional 1-2 inches of rigid foam to the exterior walls
- **Very cold climates (above 4,000 ft, -10°F design temp):** Double-wall construction may warrant 6 inches of cavity foam instead of 4 inches

**Key code references:**
- ORSC N1102 (thermal envelope — climate-specific R-value requirements)

## 6.3 Cavity Insulation Choice — Polyiso vs. XPS vs. EPS vs. Mineral Wool

**Plain language:** Four main materials could fill the 4-inch cavity between the two block wythes. Each has trade-offs. The recommendation for this design is polyiso (the foil-faced yellow foam board), but any of the four would meet code and work in the assembly.

**Technical detail:** Comparison table:

| Material | R-value per inch | Cost per board-foot | Fire behavior | Water resistance | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Polyiso** (polyisocyanurate) | R-6 to R-6.5 | $0.80-$1.20 | Chars, doesn't burn rapidly | Good when protected | **Recommended** — highest R per inch, foil facing reflects radiant heat |
| **XPS** (extruded polystyrene) | R-5 | $0.60-$0.80 | Melts, can burn | Excellent | Below-grade or water-exposed |
| **EPS** (expanded polystyrene) | R-4 | $0.40-$0.50 | Melts, can burn | Good (retains R over time better than XPS) | Budget-conscious, long life |
| **Mineral wool** | R-4 | $1.20-$1.60 | Non-combustible | Good | Fire-critical areas, pest resistance |

**Recommendation: Polyiso in two staggered 2-inch layers.**

- Highest R-value per inch minimizes cavity thickness
- Foil facing adds radiant reflection bonus
- Stacks cleanly between block courses during construction
- 2 inches is a stocked thickness at any big-box store (cheaper and easier to source than 4-inch)
- Staggered seams eliminate thermal bridges at any seam

**Temperature derating caution:** Polyiso loses about 10-15% of its rated R-value in very cold conditions (below 30°F). For cold-climate builds, slightly oversizing (adding an extra 0.5-1 inch) compensates.

**Alternative: Mineral wool in fire-critical climates.** Slightly lower R-value but non-combustible. Worth considering if the build is in a wildfire-prone area.

**Key code references:**
- ASTM C578 (standard specification for rigid cellular polystyrene)
- ASTM C1289 (standard specification for polyiso foam)

## 6.4 Exterior Waterproofing Stack (Below-Grade Walls)

**Plain language:** The basement and lower-floor walls that are in contact with dirt need serious waterproofing. Water sits against the wall 24/7, and if it gets in, the home has mold and damage forever. The design uses multiple layers so if one fails, the next one still works. Expensive up front. Saves the building for its whole life.

**Technical detail:** Below-grade waterproofing stack, applied to the exterior of the inner structural wythe (the outer decorative wythe is above grade or mostly above grade on most walls):

**Layer 1 — Penetrating sealer:**
- Chemical sealer that soaks into the concrete, reacting internally to form a waterproof barrier
- Products: silicate sealers, silane/siloxane sealers
- Applied with a garden sprayer as a liquid; absorbs into the concrete over hours
- Effect: the concrete itself becomes water-repellent from the inside out
- Lifespan: essentially permanent once cured into the concrete

**Layer 2 — Rubberized asphalt or liquid membrane:**
- A thick, rubberized coating painted or rolled onto the concrete surface
- Hot-applied rubberized asphalt (commercial standard) OR cold-applied liquid rubber for DIY
- Thickness: 60-80 mils (about the thickness of 2-3 playing cards stacked)
- Forms a monolithic waterproof skin over the concrete
- Lifespan: 30-50+ years

**Layer 3 — Dimpled drainage membrane (bubble plastic wicking board):**
- Plastic sheet with dimples or channels molded into it, against the waterproofing layer
- Creates an air gap between the waterproofing and the soil
- Any water that reaches this layer drains downward by gravity to the foundation drain
- Products: Delta-MS, Platon, similar brands (HDPE plastic dimple boards)
- Lifespan: 50+ years (buried plastic is very stable)

**Layer 4 — Drainage rock and French drain at footing:**
- Clean crushed rock (3/4 inch to 1.5 inch, no fines) between the dimple membrane and the soil
- Perforated pipe at the base of the foundation, wrapped in filter fabric, carrying any water away from the building to daylight or a dry well
- Slope the drain pipe at least 1% (1/8 inch per foot) for gravity flow
- Discharge minimum 10 ft from the building

**Backfill:**
- Gravel backfill for the bottom few feet against the foundation for drainage
- Native soil above that with slope away from the building (minimum 6 inches drop over 10 feet)
- Surface water management directs gutters and runoff away from the foundation

**Sacrificial corrosion protection:**
- Cathodic protection tabs or strips can be attached to the rebar during construction, designed to corrode first and protect the rebar
- Zinc or magnesium anodes, wired to the rebar
- Optional upgrade for extreme longevity goals
- Adds 25-75 years of extra rebar life

**Key code references:**
- ORSC R406 (foundation waterproofing and dampproofing)
- ORSC R405 (foundation drainage)

## 6.5 Below-Grade Drainage Design

**Plain language:** Water always finds a way. The design assumes water WILL eventually reach the foundation wall, and the waterproofing stack handles that. But it's much better if most of the water never gets that close in the first place. This part of the chapter is about routing water away before it even reaches the building.

**Technical detail:** Site-level drainage strategy:

**Surface grading:**
- Slope the ground away from the building at minimum 1:20 (6 inches over 10 feet) for the first 10 feet
- Maintain positive slope in all directions
- No depressions within 10 feet of the foundation

**Gutters and downspouts:**
- Gutters on every eave catching roof runoff
- Downspouts extending minimum 6 feet from the foundation
- Or connected to a rainwater cistern for non-potable use (matches the design philosophy)
- Or connected to underground drain lines carrying water to daylight

**Foundation drains:**
- Perforated pipe at the base of the foundation wall (footing level)
- Wrapped in filter fabric to prevent soil infiltration
- Slope to daylight or dry well
- Minimum 4-inch pipe, 1% slope

**Swales and French drains:**
- Shallow landscaped channels routing surface water around the building
- French drains (buried perforated pipes in gravel) where needed to intercept subsurface water

**Critical rule:** Roof drain water, foundation drain water, and surface drain water should all exit the property separately from the septic drainfield area. Mixing them can overload the septic system and cause sewage backup.

**Key code references:**
- ORSC R401.3 (drainage — requirements for site drainage)
- ORSC R405 (foundation drainage)

## 6.6 Air Sealing Strategy

**Plain language:** Even a well-insulated wall loses heat if air can leak through it. Air sealing is about making the building envelope airtight — every gap, seam, and penetration sealed. The design goal is a fortress of a building with controlled, designed openings only (vents for fresh air in, vents for stale air out, on purpose, through known paths).

**Technical detail:** Air sealing approach:

**Concrete masonry is naturally airtight when properly built:**
- Solid-grouted reinforced cells are airtight
- Ungrouted cells can leak air through the mortar joints if not parged
- Parge coat (a thin cement plaster skim) on the interior of the inner wythe seals the block surface

**Cavity insulation gaps:**
- Seams in the polyiso boards sealed with foil tape on both sides
- Gaps at edges (around openings, at the top and bottom of the cavity) sealed with spray foam
- Cavity closed at top and bottom with flashing or metal closure strips

**Penetrations:**
- Every electrical box, plumbing penetration, and mechanical run through the envelope sealed with fire-rated caulk or spray foam
- Service layer penetrations sealed similarly
- Window and door perimeters sealed with backer rod and sealant on both interior and exterior sides

**Target airtightness:**
- Oregon code threshold: 3 ACH50 (air changes per hour at 50 Pascals of pressure)
- This design target: 1.0 ACH50 or lower (passive house levels)
- Verified by blower door test (a large fan temporarily mounted in a doorway that pressurizes the building to measure leakage)

**Blower door test procedure:**
- Temporary fan and frame installed in an exterior door
- Fan runs, pressurizing the interior to 50 Pa above outside pressure
- Meter measures the airflow required to maintain that pressure
- Divided by the building volume = ACH50 value

**Controlled ventilation:**
- Since the envelope is very tight, designed ventilation is required (see the HVAC, Ventilation, and Air Quality chapter)
- HRV or ERV (heat/energy recovery ventilator) provides controlled fresh air exchange without losing conditioned air

**Key code references:**
- ORSC N1102.4 (air leakage — air barrier requirements)
- ORSC N1102.4.1.2 (testing — blower door test requirements)

## 6.7 Thermal Bridging Avoidance

**Plain language:** A thermal bridge is any spot where heat can skip around the insulation via a more conductive path. Metal studs, steel wall ties, uninsulated concrete edges — these all create cold spots in winter and condensation risks. The design specifically addresses every known thermal bridge path.

**Technical detail:** Known thermal bridges and mitigations:

**Wall ties (inner wythe to outer wythe):**
- FRP ties instead of steel (already specified in the wall-tie discussion)
- Thermal conductivity ~200x lower than steel

**Slab edge at grade:**
- Exposed concrete slab edge is a major thermal bridge
- Rigid foam at slab edge (R-10 to R-15) interrupts the bridge
- Extends from top of slab down to top of footing or for at least 24 inches below grade

**Roof-to-wall transition:**
- The top of the exterior wall is a classic thermal bridge location
- Continuous rigid foam strip between the roof insulation and the wall insulation
- Any structural connections through the thermal envelope get thermal-break details

**Service layer penetrations:**
- Pipes and wires passing through the thermal envelope at the service layer sealed with spray foam
- Any metal structural connections to the exterior wall use thermal break materials

**Windows and doors:**
- Window frame material matters: vinyl, fiberglass, or thermally broken aluminum (aluminum with a plastic thermal break in the middle of the frame)
- Never use solid metal frames without thermal breaks
- Install windows with proper insulation wrap on all sides

**Why it matters:**
- A "R-20" wall with 10% steel stud area actually performs closer to R-15
- This design's FRP ties and continuous insulation result in whole-wall R-values very close to the nominal insulation R-value

**Key code references:**
- ORSC N1102.2.7 (thermal bridging — addressing thermal bridges in construction)

## 6.8 Windows and Doors at Masonry Openings

**Plain language:** Every window and door has to have a structural support (called a lintel) spanning across the top of the opening to carry the weight of the wall above. In a concrete block wall, these lintels are either precast concrete beams or specially poured and reinforced sections. Windows also have to meet emergency escape requirements for bedrooms.

**Technical detail:** Opening design:

**Lintels (structural beam over each opening):**
- Options: precast concrete lintel, U-shaped bond beam block filled with rebar and grout, or steel angle lintel
- Sized based on opening width and the weight of wall above
- Must extend minimum 4-8 inches past the opening on each side (bearing length)
- Rebar and grout for any custom-poured lintel

**Sills (bottom of the opening):**
- Often a simple precast sill block or a steel angle
- Sloped to drain water away from the interior

**Window types and U-factors:**
- **Vinyl frame:** cheapest, U-0.28 to U-0.35, 15-25 year life
- **Fiberglass frame:** mid-price, U-0.20 to U-0.28, 40+ year life
- **Aluminum-clad wood:** traditional look, U-0.25 to U-0.30, 30-50 year life
- **Thermally broken aluminum:** commercial look, U-0.30 to U-0.40, 40+ year life
- **Triple-pane glass** (three panes with two gas-filled gaps): best performance, U-0.15 to U-0.20
- **Double-pane glass:** standard, U-0.25 to U-0.32

**Oregon code minimum:**
- U-0.30 for windows in the 2023 ORSC

**This design recommendation:**
- Triple-pane glass in fiberglass or thermally broken aluminum frames
- U-0.18 to U-0.22 target
- Larger upfront cost, much lower heating load over the building's life

**Egress windows (required in bedrooms):**
- Minimum net clear opening: 5.7 sq ft (or 5.0 sq ft at grade level)
- Minimum clear opening height: 24 inches
- Minimum clear opening width: 20 inches
- Maximum sill height above floor: 44 inches
- Must be openable from the inside without tools or special knowledge
- Purpose: allow escape in a fire and access by emergency responders

**Exterior doors:**
- Minimum main entry door: 36 inches wide, 6 ft 8 in tall
- Insulated metal or fiberglass skin with foam core (U-0.20 to U-0.35)
- Weatherstripping on all four sides
- Door sweep at the bottom
- Storm door as an optional secondary layer

**Key code references:**
- ORSC R310 (emergency escape and rescue openings — bedroom egress)
- ORSC R606.9 (masonry openings — lintels and support)
- ORSC N1102.3 (fenestration — window and door U-factors)

## 6.9 Radon Mitigation Options

**Plain language:** Radon is an invisible radioactive gas that can seep up out of the ground in some parts of Oregon. It's the second leading cause of lung cancer in non-smokers. A radon mitigation system is a simple set of pipes under the slab that vents any radon safely to the outside above the roof.

**Technical detail:** Radon mitigation approaches:

**Passive radon system (installed during construction, low cost):**
- 4-inch perforated pipe in a loop under the slab, surrounded by clean gravel (the capillary break layer already planned in this design)
- One riser pipe (4-inch PVC) from the sub-slab loop, running up through the interior of the building, exiting above the roof
- No fan — relies on natural stack effect (warm air rising carries radon up and out)
- Cost: $200-$500 in materials, installed during normal construction
- Effect: reduces radon levels 50-90%, often sufficient on its own

**Active radon system (add a fan if post-construction testing shows high radon):**
- Same as passive plus an in-line fan in the riser pipe, running continuously
- Fan located in the attic, service layer, or outside the home
- Creates suction that pulls air and radon from under the slab and vents it above the roof
- Cost: $300-$1,500 additional for the fan and wiring
- Effect: reduces radon levels 90-99%

**When to install:**
- Oregon has radon zones: some counties are Zone 1 (high), others Zone 2 or Zone 3 (lower)
- Douglas County is Zone 2 (moderate risk)
- Installing a passive system during construction is cheap insurance — adding it later requires breaking up floors
- Recommendation: install passive system always; test after occupancy; add fan only if needed

**Testing:**
- Short-term test kits (2-7 days): $15-$30, mail to lab, results in a week
- Long-term test kits (3-12 months): $30-$60, most accurate
- Continuous radon monitors: $150-$300, give real-time data forever

**Key code references:**
- ORSC Appendix F (radon control — passive system design)
- EPA radon zone maps (state of Oregon, by county)

## 6.10 DIY Thermal Testing — Measuring R-Value at Home

**Plain language:** This part of the chapter explains how to actually measure how well a wall or insulation material blocks heat, using tools and methods that fit in a garage with hardware store materials. This matters for two reasons: first, to test different wall assembly samples before committing to a design choice, and second, to verify the finished home actually performs the way it was designed.

Three methods are covered here, from least equipment required to most accurate. All three are based on first principles — the physics of heat flow — rather than requiring expensive laboratory equipment.

### 6.10a Method 1 — Infrared Thermometer (Existing Wall Verification)

**Plain language:** Point a handheld infrared thermometer at a wall on a cold day. Compare the wall's surface temperature to the room's air temperature and the outdoor temperature. Use a simple formula to calculate the effective R-value. Cheap, fast, and surprisingly useful. Works even better with a real thermal imaging camera.

**Technical detail:**

**First principles:** Heat flowing through a wall creates a temperature gradient. The interior surface of an exterior wall is cooler than the room air because heat is conducting through the wall toward the cold outside. The amount the interior surface is cooler than the room air tells you how insulative the wall is. A well-insulated wall's interior surface is nearly the same temperature as the room air. A poorly-insulated wall's interior surface is noticeably cooler.

**Equipment:**
- Handheld infrared thermometer (cheap: $20; nice: $100) OR infrared thermal camera (already owned)
- Outdoor thermometer (for ambient outdoor temperature)
- Indoor thermometer (for room air temperature)

**Procedure:**

1. Pick a cold day with at least a 25°F difference between indoor and outdoor temperature
2. The sun must not be shining on either side of the test wall (test at night or on a cloudy day)
3. Wait until indoor and outdoor temperatures have been stable for several hours
4. Measure:
   - **Ta** = indoor air temperature (point the IR gun at an interior wall or piece of furniture far from the exterior wall)
   - **Th** = interior surface temperature of the exterior wall (point the IR gun at the wall from inside)
   - **Tc** = outdoor air temperature (point the IR gun at an object outside that's in thermal equilibrium with the air, like a trash can)
5. Apply the formula:

**R = ((Th - Tc) / (Ta - Th)) × 0.68 + 0.68**

where 0.68 is the interior air-film R-value (the thin layer of still air on the interior wall surface acts as a small insulator itself).

**Example:**
- Ta = 70°F (indoor air)
- Th = 65°F (interior wall surface)
- Tc = 30°F (outdoor air)
- R = ((65 - 30) / (70 - 65)) × 0.68 + 0.68
- R = (35 / 5) × 0.68 + 0.68
- R = 7 × 0.68 + 0.68
- R = 4.76 + 0.68 = **R-5.44** (effective whole-wall R-value)

**Limitations:**
- Assumes steady-state (temperatures have been stable for many hours)
- Works best with large temperature differences
- Loses resolution as insulation gets very good (above R-20, the interior wall surface is only 1-2°F cooler than room air, hard to measure accurately)
- Ignores radiation from the wall interior, which matters at high R-values
- Not accurate for shiny surfaces (adjust emissivity on the IR gun for different materials, or put a piece of matte black tape on the wall and measure the tape)

**With a thermal imaging camera:** Same method, but the camera shows the whole wall at once. This is great for identifying cold spots (thermal bridges, gaps in insulation, air leaks) that a single-point thermometer would miss. The camera also reveals where cold air is leaking in (streaks of cold along the wall indicate air infiltration, not just conduction).

**References:**
- Matthias Wandel's blog (woodgears.ca) documents this method in detail and provides a spreadsheet with the full calculation including radiation effects
- Bo's Boring Blog (czbo.blogspot.com) provides the simple formula approach

### 6.10b Method 2 — DIY Hot Box (Testing Sample Wall Materials)

**Plain language:** Build an insulated box, put a known heater inside, measure how much power it takes to maintain a steady temperature inside while the outside is cold. If you know the power input and the temperature difference, you can calculate the R-value of the walls of the box. Replace one wall with a sample of the material being tested, and you can isolate that material's performance.

This is the right approach for testing sample wall assemblies before committing to the design choice for the real build.

**Technical detail:**

**First principles:** Heat flows from hot to cold at a rate proportional to the temperature difference, divided by the R-value of the barrier, multiplied by the area. In steady state, all heat produced inside the box must be flowing out through the walls. If five of the six walls are made of known high-R material (so negligible heat flows through them) and only one wall is the test specimen, then essentially all the heat is flowing through the test wall. Measuring the power input to the heater gives the heat flow. Measuring the temperature difference gives the other variable needed for the R-value calculation.

**Formula (steady-state heat transfer):**

**Q = A × ΔT / R**

Rearranged for R-value of the test specimen:

**R = A × ΔT / Q**

where:
- Q = heat flow in BTU/hr (or watts)
- A = area of the test specimen in square feet (or square meters)
- ΔT = temperature difference across the specimen
- R = R-value in °F·ft²·hr/BTU (imperial) or equivalent metric units

**Equipment to build the hot box:**

- Plywood shell (4x4x4 feet outer dimensions, or smaller to scale)
- All six walls made of 4-inch XPS foam (R-20, very insulating) EXCEPT the test wall
- Test wall: removable panel, same size as the other walls (e.g., 2x2 feet)
- Electric heater inside (incandescent bulbs or a small ceramic heater) — total 100-500 watts
- Kill-A-Watt meter ($25) measuring the actual wattage being drawn
- 4 thermocouples or digital temperature sensors: one in the box air, one on the inside face of the test wall, one on the outside face of the test wall, one in the room air outside the box
- Data logger or Arduino reading the temperatures continuously ($30-$100)

**Calibration step (critical for accuracy):**

Before testing unknown materials, test the rig with a known material. Buy a 2-inch slab of R-10 XPS from Home Depot. Install it as the test wall. Run the experiment. Measure the R-value the rig reports. If the rig says R-8.5 instead of R-10, apply a calibration factor (1.18x) to all future measurements. If the rig reports accurately, no calibration needed. This step reveals errors in the rig setup (edge losses, thermocouple placement, heater efficiency assumptions) and corrects for them.

**Procedure:**

1. Place the hot box in a cold garage (consistent cold temperature, ideally below 50°F)
2. Install the test wall (first a known calibration sample, later actual test samples)
3. Seal all seams with tape and caulk
4. Turn on the heater, set to maintain roughly 100°F inside the box
5. Wait for steady state (4-12 hours of stable temperatures)
6. Record:
   - Heater wattage (Q in watts — 1 watt = 3.412 BTU/hr)
   - Inside box air temp
   - Inside test wall surface temp
   - Outside test wall surface temp
   - Outside (garage) air temp
7. Calculate R-value of the test specimen

**Sample wall assemblies to test for this project:**
- Different insulation materials (polyiso vs. XPS vs. EPS vs. mineral wool at same thickness)
- Different cavity configurations (empty air gap vs. filled with foam vs. filled with grout)
- Various concrete block assemblies (ungrouted, grouted, with exterior foam, etc.)
- Complete wall mockup (inner wythe + cavity + foam + outer wythe)

**Reference:** University of Oregon students documented building a similar calibrated hot box with step-by-step photos and methodology.

### 6.10c Method 3 — Heat Flux Sensor (Highest Accuracy)

**Plain language:** A heat flux sensor is a thin sensor that measures heat flow directly. Stick it on a wall, connect it to a data logger, wait a day, and read off the R-value. More expensive and more accurate than the other two methods. Useful for verifying the finished home after construction.

**Technical detail:**

**First principles:** A heat flux sensor is a thin sandwich of materials with thermocouples on both sides. The temperature difference across the sensor's known thickness, combined with the sensor's known thermal properties, gives the heat flux (watts per square meter) flowing through whatever it's attached to. If you also measure the temperature difference across the wall, you have both variables needed for R-value directly.

**R = ΔT / q** (where q is heat flux in watts per square meter)

**Equipment:**

- Heat flux sensor (commercial — FluxTeq PHFS series $300-$500, Omega HFS series $400-$800)
- Temperature sensors (thermocouples or thermistors)
- Data logger (reads sensors continuously over 24-48 hours)

**Procedure:**

1. Attach the heat flux sensor to the interior surface of an exterior wall
2. Attach temperature sensors to the interior and exterior surfaces
3. Log data for at least 24 hours (longer in mild weather)
4. Calculate average R-value over the measurement period

**Advantages:**
- Works in any weather (just needs some temperature difference)
- Accurate even for high R-values
- Non-destructive — no need to open the wall
- Can verify the finished home actually achieves its design R-value

**Limitations:**
- Hardware cost is higher than the other methods
- Requires careful sensor placement
- Must account for thermal storage in high-mass walls (wait for steady-state over several cycles)

### 6.10d Which Method to Use When

- **Before construction (testing sample assemblies):** Method 2 (DIY hot box). Let you test different material combinations side-by-side with known inputs.
- **During construction (field verification of installed insulation):** Method 1 with thermal imaging camera. Fast, shows whole walls at once, identifies thermal bridges and air leaks.
- **After construction (verifying performance of finished home):** Method 3 (heat flux sensor) for hard numbers, or Method 1 with thermal camera for quick qualitative verification.
