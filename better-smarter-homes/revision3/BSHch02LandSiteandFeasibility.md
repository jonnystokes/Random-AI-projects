# Chapter 2 — Land, Site, and Feasibility

*How to evaluate land before committing the house to it*

The right house on the wrong land is still the wrong project. This chapter is the checklist for evaluating any piece of property before buying it. Most of these items are questions, not answers, because the answers depend on the specific piece of land. The goal is to know what to ask, what to verify, and what to walk away from.

The design of the home itself works across a wide range of climates and conditions, but the exact insulation thickness, snow load on the roof, window sizes, and a few other details scale up or down based on where the land is. Ranges are given throughout the document. This chapter helps determine which end of each range applies.

---

## 2.1 Climate and Environment

**Plain language:** How cold, how hot, how much snow, how much rain, how wet the soil, how high the elevation. These numbers change what the house needs to handle.

**Technical detail:** The building envelope (the walls, roof, windows, and doors that separate inside from outside) is sized for the worst-case load the climate puts on it. Design conditions include winter low temperature, summer high temperature, ground snow load (the weight of accumulated snow on the roof), basic wind speed, seismic zone (risk of earthquake shaking), and soil frost depth (how deep the ground freezes in winter, which sets minimum foundation depth).

**Range planning values for Oregon:**

- **Winter design temperature:** -10°F (high mountain zones) to 25°F (Willamette Valley). Affects insulation thickness and heating system sizing.
- **Summer design temperature:** 85°F (coast) to 105°F (high desert east of the Cascades). Affects cooling strategy and shading.
- **Ground snow load:** 15 psf (pounds per square foot — a measure of distributed weight) in the Willamette Valley lowlands, 25-50 psf in typical rural hills and low mountains, 100-300+ psf in true mountain zones above 3,000-4,000 ft elevation. This is the single biggest variable for roof structural design.
- **Basic wind speed:** 95-115 mph in most of Oregon, higher near the coast. Concrete masonry walls handle this trivially.
- **Seismic zone:** Most of western Oregon is in a high-seismic zone (near the Cascadia Subduction Zone). Concrete masonry requires specific reinforcement patterns to perform well in earthquakes.
- **Frost depth:** 12-18 inches in most valleys, 24-36 inches in cold mountain zones. Foundation footings must extend below this depth.
- **Annual rainfall:** 15-20 inches (east of Cascades) to 60-100+ inches (coast and west slopes). Affects drainage and waterproofing requirements, not the building envelope much.

**Key code references:**
- ORSC Table R301.2(1) (design criteria — climate design values by region)
- ORSC R301.2.1 (wind design — wind speed and exposure)
- ORSC R301.2.2 (seismic provisions — earthquake design)
- ORSC R301.2.3 (snow loads — roof snow weight)

## 2.2 Zoning Types

**Plain language:** Every piece of land is labeled with rules about what can be built on it. Some land lets you build a house easily. Some land makes it nearly impossible. Some land is in between. Before buying, verify the zoning allows a home, ideally with few restrictions.

**Technical detail:** Oregon zoning varies by county. The most common rural zoning categories and their suitability for this project, ranked from most favorable to least favorable:

**Rural Residential (RR-5, RR-10, RR-20, etc.)** — The sweet spot. Named after minimum lot size in acres (RR-5 requires at least 5 acres per lot). Allows residential use as of right (meaning no special permission needed, just a standard permit). Usually allows accessory dwelling units (a second smaller home for a family member), home occupations (running a business from home), hobby farming, outbuildings, and flexible use of the land. This is what to look for first.

**Mixed Use Agricultural (MUA-10, MUA-20, etc.)** — Depends heavily on the specific county. Often allows a primary home as of right along with farming, timber, and other uses. Can be a good option for cheaper rural land that still allows a home. Douglas County specifically uses MUA zones in many areas.

**Farm-Forest (F1, F2)** — Mixed agricultural and forestry zoning. Can allow homes under certain conditions, often with more paperwork than RR zones. Varies by county.

**Non-Resource Rural Lands** — Land specifically designated as NOT for farm or forest use. Rare but very flexible when it exists.

**Exclusive Farm Use (EFU)** — The most restrictive common rural zone. Generally requires qualifying as a farm income-producing parcel or meeting "template dwelling" criteria before building a home. Second dwellings (a separate home for a family member) are typically prohibited. Non-farm uses are very restricted. Avoid unless the property is exceptional and the paperwork burden is acceptable.

**Urban zoning (R1, R2, etc.)** — In-town residential zones. Not the target for this project (too close to neighbors, more restrictions, no space for testing concrete mixes or operating a block press), but worth knowing about for comparison.

**Restrictions to watch for in any zone:**
- Minimum lot size (can you actually build on this parcel?)
- Setbacks (how far the building must sit from property lines and roads)
- Maximum building height
- Maximum lot coverage (percentage of the lot that can be covered by buildings)
- Outbuilding limits (number and size of additional structures)
- Home occupation limits (what businesses can and can't run from the property)
- Structure use limits (can you have a 300-foot statue, a grain silo, a studio?)
- Dwelling count (one home per parcel, or multiple allowed?)
- Agricultural use limits (can you farm the land?)

**How to verify:**
- County planning department's zoning map (most are online and public)
- Direct call to county planning staff to confirm allowed uses
- Pre-purchase consultation with a local land use planner if the zoning is ambiguous

## 2.3 Property Rights Checklist

**Plain language:** When buying land, what you're actually buying may not include everything on or under the property. The timber growing on the land, the minerals beneath it, the water flowing through it, and the right to drive across a neighbor's road to reach it are all separate rights that might or might not be included in the sale.

**Technical detail:** These rights are verified through a title report (a legal document from a title company listing what the property includes) and in some cases through separate state or federal agency records.

**Rights to verify before purchase:**

- **Mineral rights** — ownership of oil, gas, and hard rock minerals beneath the surface. In some older properties these were separated from surface ownership decades ago and may be owned by someone else.
- **Timber/lumber rights** — ownership of standing trees. Some properties have had timber rights sold separately, meaning another party can come and log the trees even though the buyer owns the land.
- **Water rights** — Oregon operates under prior appropriation doctrine (first to use, first in right). Water rights (for surface water, springs, and in some cases groundwater) are separate legal rights that may or may not transfer with the land. A well permit for domestic use is usually available, but irrigation water rights require a specific allocation.
- **Access easements** — legal rights to cross other property to reach the land (if the parcel is not directly on a public road) and the rights of utility companies or neighbors to cross this property.
- **Riparian rights** — rights related to creeks, rivers, ponds, or lakes that touch or pass through the property.
- **Hunting and fishing rights** — sometimes deeded separately, especially in timber-company-adjacent areas.
- **Air rights** — restrictions on building height, wind turbine installation, etc. Less commonly separated but worth verifying.
- **Grazing rights** — if the property is adjacent to federal grazing land, these may be attached to the parcel.
- **Conservation easements** — legal restrictions that permanently limit certain uses of the land (often signed by a previous owner for tax benefits). These run with the land and bind future owners.
- **Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions (CC&Rs)** — private rules from a subdivision or homeowners association that restrict what can be built.

**How to find out:**
- **Preliminary title report** from a title company (standard part of any purchase)
- **County recorder's office** for recorded deeds and easements
- **Oregon Water Resources Department** for water rights (oregon.gov/owrd)
- **Oregon Department of Forestry** for timber-related encumbrances
- **BLM (Bureau of Land Management) records** for federal mineral rights in some cases
- **County assessor** for tax lot records

## 2.4 Utilities Check

**Plain language:** Does the property already have power, water, internet, and road access, or will bringing them in cost a fortune?

**Technical detail:** Questions to answer for each utility:

**Electrical power:**
- Is there a utility pole at the property line or nearby?
- How far is the nearest transformer?
- What is the cost estimate from the utility for extending service to the building site?
- What is the utility (PGE, Pacific Power, local cooperative) and what are their requirements?

**Water:**
- Is there a municipal water line at the property?
- If not, is there an existing well? If so, what is the yield (gallons per minute) and water quality test result?
- If no well exists, is the property in an area where drilling a well is likely to find water, and at what approximate depth?
- What are the water rights attached to the property?

**Sewer / wastewater:**
- Is there a municipal sewer line? (Rare for the target property range.)
- If not, can the property support an on-site septic system? (Covered in the septic site evaluation discussion.)

**Natural gas:**
- Is there a gas line at the property? Generally not a concern for this design (the home uses electric and water-based systems), but worth knowing.

**Internet:**
- What providers serve the area? Fiber, cable, DSL, fixed wireless, satellite?
- What speeds are actually available at this specific address (not just "in the region")?

**Road access:**
- Is the property on a public road or private easement?
- Is the road paved, gravel, or dirt?
- Who maintains the road?
- Is the road passable year-round in all weather?

**How to verify:**
- Utility companies (call directly and provide the address)
- Seller disclosures (Oregon requires sellers to disclose what they know)
- County road department
- Direct site visit

## 2.5 Septic Site Evaluation

**Plain language:** Before buying rural land, it is critical to verify the property can support a septic system (the underground system that treats wastewater when there's no municipal sewer). Some properties cannot, which makes them unbuildable.

**Technical detail:** A **Site Evaluation** is a formal process in Oregon where a licensed soil scientist or septic installer digs test pits on the property to classify the soil and determine what type of septic system (if any) the property can support.

**Why this matters:**
- Some soils don't drain well enough for a standard septic system, requiring a more expensive alternative system ($20,000-$50,000 instead of $8,000-$15,000)
- Some soils drain too fast and threaten groundwater, requiring even more expensive engineered solutions
- Some properties can't support any septic system at all, making them impossible to build on (at least in a way that satisfies code)

**When to do it:** Before closing on the land purchase. Make the purchase offer contingent on a satisfactory site evaluation.

**What it costs:** Roughly $500-$1,200, depending on county and complexity. Small price for avoiding a catastrophically wrong purchase.

**How to verify:**
- Oregon DEQ (Department of Environmental Quality) Onsite Wastewater Program
- County Environmental Health department (often contracts with DEQ)
- Licensed septic site evaluators in the area

## 2.6 Topography

**Plain language:** The shape of the land. How flat, how sloped, which direction it faces, where water drains. A flat property is easier to build on. A sloped property costs more to excavate but can offer views, drainage, and walk-out basement options.

**Technical detail:** Topography affects:

- **Excavation cost** — flatter is cheaper, more slope means more dirt moving
- **Foundation complexity** — flat sites allow a simple mat foundation, sloped sites may need stepped foundations or retaining walls
- **Drainage** — water wants to go downhill, and a site that drains naturally is much easier to waterproof than a site in a low spot
- **Solar orientation** — a south-facing slope is ideal for solar gain in winter. A north-facing slope is cold and dark.
- **Views** — elevated sites with long sight lines increase resale value
- **Wind exposure** — ridgeline sites get more wind, valley sites get more still air but may pool cold air in winter
- **Flood risk** — low-lying sites near creeks or rivers may be in designated flood zones
- **Access** — steep driveways are difficult in winter, expensive to maintain

**How to verify:**
- USGS topographic maps (free online)
- Google Earth elevation profiles
- Site visit in wet weather to see where water actually flows
- FEMA flood maps for flood zone designation

## 2.7 Distance to Town

**Plain language:** How far from the nearest town. Closer is more convenient. Farther is more private and often cheaper per acre. A range of 15 minutes to 1 hour is the target for this project.

**Trade-offs:**

**Closer to town (15-30 minutes):**
- Easier access to building materials and hardware stores (saves hundreds of round trips)
- Faster emergency services response
- Better internet and utility options
- Higher land cost
- Less privacy
- More restrictive zoning more likely
- Faster resale when listing

**Farther from town (30-60 minutes):**
- Cheaper land per acre
- More privacy and flexibility
- Potentially more relaxed zoning
- Better for testing noisy equipment (concrete block press, excavator) without complaints
- Slower access to materials (every hardware run is a half-day)
- Slower emergency services
- May limit utility options (no fiber internet, longer utility extensions)
- Smaller buyer pool at resale

**How to evaluate for a specific property:**
- Drive the route to the nearest town for hardware, groceries, medical care at different times of day
- Check winter road conditions (can trucks reach the site in January?)
- Consider where materials deliveries will originate from

## 2.8 Location Appraisal Checklist

**Plain language:** Before making an offer on any piece of land, walk through this checklist. If any item is a dealbreaker, walk away. If everything checks out, buy with confidence.

**The checklist:**

- [ ] Zoning allows a primary residence as of right (not EFU unless exceptional)
- [ ] Parcel size meets zoning minimum
- [ ] Septic site evaluation complete and favorable
- [ ] Water source identified (municipal, well, or well potential confirmed)
- [ ] Electrical service available or extension cost acceptable
- [ ] Road access confirmed and year-round passable
- [ ] Internet service available at acceptable speed
- [ ] Title report clean (no hidden easements, liens, or encumbrances)
- [ ] Mineral rights included or not separately owned by a threatening party
- [ ] Timber rights included
- [ ] Water rights documented
- [ ] No conservation easements restricting building
- [ ] Not in a flood zone (or acceptable flood insurance cost)
- [ ] Climate design values known (snow load, wind speed, seismic zone)
- [ ] Topography suitable for the planned design
- [ ] Solar orientation acceptable for future solar array
- [ ] Distance to town, hardware stores, and emergency services acceptable
- [ ] Setback requirements allow the planned building footprint
- [ ] Building height limit allows the planned three-story structure
- [ ] Outbuilding and accessory dwelling unit rules acceptable

If all items check, this is a buildable property for this design.
