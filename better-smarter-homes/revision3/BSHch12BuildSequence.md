# Chapter 12 — Build Sequence

*From empty lot to certificate of occupancy, one weekend at a time*

This chapter covers how the house actually gets built: the phases in order, what the owner-builder can realistically do alone versus what needs help, how to pace the work across years rather than months, how to coordinate with inspectors and suppliers, and how to get to a livable house as efficiently as possible before continuing to refine the rest.

The guiding philosophy: **build in stages that each reach a useful state**; **weekend pace plus strategic hired help**; **get to a Temporary Certificate of Occupancy (TCO) as fast as practical**, then continue finishing while living in the house; **document everything as it's built**; **embrace the long timeline — this is a craft, not a race**.

---

## 12.1 Timeline Reality

**Plain language:** A professional crew of 10–15 people can build a 3,000 square foot custom home in 6–12 months. An owner-builder working weekends while holding a day job builds the same house over 3–7 years. This is not a failure — it's a different kind of project, with different advantages (total control, zero mortgage interest if paying as you go, deep knowledge of every system, lower cost). The timeline is long but the result is better.

**Technical detail:** Realistic timeline for an owner-builder working weekends (with strategic contractor help):

**Phase timeline estimate for a 3,000 sqft home on a 1-acre rural lot:**

| Phase | Weekend Time (Owner) | Contractor Days | Calendar Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Site selection and purchase | 3–12 months of searching | 0 | 3–12 months |
| Site prep (surveying, clearing, driveway) | 4–8 weekends | 5–15 days (excavator) | 1–4 months |
| Well and septic | 2–4 weekends (oversight) | 3–10 days (driller, installer) | 1–3 months |
| Foundation (excavation, footing, mat slab) | 6–12 weekends | 5–15 days (excavator, concrete crew, crane) | 2–6 months |
| Basement walls (CMU) | 20–40 weekends | 3–5 days (concrete pump for grouting) | 6–14 months |
| Floor 1 structural slab | 4–8 weekends | 3–5 days (concrete crew) | 2–4 months |
| Floor 1 walls (CMU) | 20–40 weekends | 3–5 days (concrete pump) | 6–14 months |
| Service layer framing (steel + grating) | 10–20 weekends | — | 3–6 months |
| Floor 2 structural slab | 4–8 weekends | 3–5 days | 2–4 months |
| Floor 2 walls (CMU) | 20–40 weekends | 3–5 days | 6–14 months |
| Roof (structural + waterproofing) | 10–20 weekends | 5–10 days (crane, steel, roofing) | 3–6 months |
| Windows and exterior doors | 4–8 weekends | — | 1–3 months |
| **MILESTONE: Dried-in** (building watertight, can work inside regardless of weather) | | | Year 2–4 |
| Rough-in plumbing | 15–30 weekends | 0–5 days (inspection-driven helper) | 6–12 months |
| Rough-in electrical | 15–30 weekends | 0–5 days (inspection-driven helper) | 6–12 months |
| Rough-in HVAC (ventilation ducts, hydronic loops) | 10–20 weekends | 5–15 days (heat pump install) | 4–8 months |
| Insulation (exterior assembly and interior) | 8–15 weekends | 0–3 days | 3–6 months |
| Radiant floor topping slabs | 4–8 weekends | 3–5 days per pour (concrete crew) | 2–4 months |
| Drywall and plaster | 10–20 weekends | 5–15 days (hanging and taping are rate-limiting, often contracted) | 3–6 months |
| Interior finishes (flooring, tile, cabinets) | 30–60 weekends | 5–20 days (specific trades like tile, cabinet install) | 12–24 months |
| **MILESTONE: TCO** (Temporary Certificate of Occupancy — can move in) | | | Year 3–5 |
| Final finishes, trim, paint | 20–40 weekends | 0–10 days | 12–24 months |
| Site finishes (landscaping, outbuildings) | indefinite | varies | ongoing |
| **MILESTONE: Certificate of Occupancy (CO)** | | | Year 4–6 |

**Total calendar time estimate: 4 to 7 years** from site purchase to CO.

**Total weekend-hours estimate: 1,500–3,000 weekend-hours** of owner labor.

**Total contractor days estimate: 60–150 days** of paid professional help at strategic points.

**This is deliberately a conservative estimate.** Aggressive owner-builders with partners or helpers can compress the timeline to 2–4 years. Owner-builders with minimal help, complex sites, or life-disruptions (job changes, new children, illness) can stretch to 8–10 years. The timeline is elastic — what matters is that work is steady and quality is high.

**Critical insight: the TCO is the mid-game.** Once the house is habitable enough to move into (kitchen, bathroom, HVAC, water heater working — see the Legal, Permits, and Inspections chapter), the daily commute disappears and the weekend construction gets twice as productive. Aim for TCO as the intermediate goal, not CO.

## 12.2 Work Rhythm and Pace

**Plain language:** Building a house over years while holding a day job requires sustainable pacing. Burning out in year 2 of a 5-year project is worse than progressing slowly for all 5 years. This design assumes 1–2 weekend days per week of work on the house, with occasional week-long "build vacations" for big pours or rapid-progress phases. The rhythm matters more than the raw hours.

**Technical detail:** Sustainable work rhythm:

**Typical weekly schedule during the build:**
- **Monday–Friday:** day job, minimal house work (maybe an hour of ordering, planning, paperwork)
- **Saturday:** 6–10 hours of active construction
- **Sunday:** 4–8 hours of construction OR a deliberate rest day for longevity

**Rest days matter:**
- **One full rest day per week minimum** — body and mind need recovery
- **Off-weekends during peak life seasons** (family events, holidays, birthdays) — the build will be here next weekend
- **Vacation weeks from the day job used strategically** — week-long "build camps" for foundation pours, wall lifts, or crew-dependent phases

**Peak work periods:**
- **Spring (April–June):** dry weather, mild temps — best for concrete work, outdoor tasks
- **Fall (September–October):** dry weather, cooling — good for exterior finish work
- **Avoid:** July–August for intense outdoor work (heat fatigue); November–March for exterior work (cold, wet)
- **Year-round:** interior work once the envelope is dried-in

**Hour budgets:**
- **Low-productivity weekend (planning, shopping, cleanup):** 4 hours
- **Normal construction weekend:** 10–16 hours across two days
- **Peak weekend (big pour, crew coordination):** 20+ hours across two days — use sparingly

**Energy management:**
- **Hardest tasks early in the day** — when fresh, do concrete work, heavy lifting, precision tasks
- **Easier tasks later** — cleanup, organizing, planning, ordering
- **Eat and hydrate consciously** — construction labor is athletic work
- **Good PPE and ergonomics** from day one — back injuries end projects

**Mental rhythm:**
- **Rotate task types** — if frustrated with masonry, switch to plumbing layout for a day
- **Set micro-goals per weekend** — "finish this wall section," "rough-in this bathroom's drains" — completion feels good
- **Celebrate milestones** — foundation complete, first floor slab, dried-in, rough-in passing inspection, TCO

**Social rhythm:**
- **Involve the family** — non-technical help (supply runs, cleanup, meal prep) keeps the partnership strong
- **Invite friends for specific big-day events** — wall lifts, pour days, insulation parties
- **Share progress** — blog, Instagram, family updates — external accountability and support
- **Accept help when offered** — even small contributions accumulate

**Signs of unsustainable pace:**
- **Weekend dread** — looking at Saturday with exhaustion instead of anticipation
- **Work-quality decline** — cutting corners, mistakes, rework
- **Family stress** — partner or children consistently frustrated with the time commitment
- **Physical injury** — back pain, repetitive strain, sleep deprivation
- **Response:** reduce pace, reset expectations, add paid help, take a month off — the project waits

## 12.3 DIY vs. Hire Out — Decision Framework

**Plain language:** Some tasks are ideal for an owner-builder working weekends. Others are specialty jobs where a professional does in a day what would take the owner a month. The art of the build is knowing the difference. This discussion lays out which tasks to DIY, which to hire, and which to work alongside a pro (best of both worlds).

**Technical detail:** Task categorization:

**DIY — do it yourself:**

These tasks are ideal weekend work: not time-critical, not requiring specialty equipment, repeatable, forgiving of pace.

- **CMU block laying** — learnable skill, repetitive, weather-flexible, huge share of the build
- **Plumbing rough-in and finish** (owner-builder exemption permits) — see the Legal, Permits, and Inspections chapter
- **Electrical rough-in and finish** (owner-builder exemption permits) — see the Legal, Permits, and Inspections chapter
- **Ventilation duct installation** (rigid metal duct, straightforward)
- **PEX radiant floor loop installation** — methodical, forgiving
- **Insulation installation** — foam boards, mineral wool, air sealing
- **Drywall installation** (hanging — finishing is harder, see "consider hiring")
- **Interior painting** — patience and technique, not exotic skill
- **Cabinet installation** (pre-built cabinets, not fabrication)
- **Interior trim and casing**
- **Floor finishing** (polishing concrete, laying tile for the patient, engineered wood click-lock)
- **Landscaping and site finishing**
- **Final detail work** — punch list items, caulking, touch-up paint

**Hire a pro — absolutely:**

These tasks need specialty equipment, specialty skill, or time-compression that's impossible for a solo weekender.

- **Site surveying** — licensed surveyor required for legal accuracy
- **Excavation and grading** (mat foundation dig, driveway cut) — excavator and operator
- **Well drilling** — drill rig is millions of dollars, specialists only
- **Septic system installation** — licensed installer required in Oregon
- **Large concrete pours** — footing, mat slab, structural floor slabs need a ready-mix truck and a crew; a pumper truck may be needed for upper floors
- **Core-fill CMU grouting** — needs a concrete pumper and a small crew, hard to DIY solo
- **Heat pump installation** (refrigerant work) — EPA 608 certification required for refrigerant handling
- **Roof installation** (large metal panels, standing seam) — specialty equipment, high work, crews are faster
- **Structural steel erection** — crane and crew
- **Drywall taping and finishing** (especially Level 5 smooth finish) — a journeyman taper does in 2 days what a DIYer does in 2 weeks, and it's invisible quality the trained eye recognizes
- **Spray foam insulation** (if used) — specialty equipment and safety gear
- **Custom millwork, custom cabinetry** (if chosen) — cabinetmaker's shop, not a weekend-garage job
- **Tile (large tile, natural stone, complex patterns)** — excellent tile setters are a joy to hire, and the result shows

**Work alongside a pro — best of both worlds:**

Hire a pro for the skill transfer and critical sections, do the rest yourself.

- **First course of CMU** — hire a mason for the first 4 feet of wall, absorb technique, continue solo
- **First concrete pour** — hire a finisher, watch and learn, then DIY subsequent small pours
- **First PEX manifold assembly** — plumber shows once, DIY the rest
- **First window install** — trim carpenter does the first window, DIY the rest using the same technique
- **First cabinet install** — cabinetmaker or installer sets the kitchen, DIY the bathroom vanity
- **First tile job** — tile setter does the primary bathroom, DIY the secondary

**Decision framework for any specific task:**
1. **Is specialty equipment needed?** (crane, pumper, drilling rig) → hire
2. **Is specialty licensing required?** (surveying, septic) → hire
3. **Is the skill gap steep and infrequent?** (Level 5 drywall, refrigerant work) → hire
4. **Is the time-compression critical?** (foundation must pour in one day before rain) → hire or crew-up
5. **Is the task repetitive and learnable?** (block, plumbing, electrical) → DIY
6. **Does the first instance benefit from coaching?** (first of each type of task) → hire-alongside

**Budget allocation for hired work:**
- **15–25% of total build cost** for hired labor — realistic for weekend-DIY with strategic help
- **40–60% for materials**
- **10–20% for permits, inspections, engineering, site work**
- **5–10% contingency** for "I didn't know this would come up"

## 12.4 Phase 1 — Site Selection and Purchase

**Plain language:** Before any construction, the right site has to be found and purchased. This phase can take years, includes significant due diligence, and sets the stage for everything that follows. Many owner-builders underestimate how critical this phase is.

**Technical detail:** Site selection and purchase sequence (expanding on the Land, Site, and Feasibility chapter):

**Phase 1A — Search and shortlist (3–12 months):**
- **Define target region** — Douglas County, Willamette Valley outskirts, Eastern Oregon — criteria from the Land, Site, and Feasibility chapter
- **Establish budget** — land cost as a percentage of total home cost (target: 15–30% for rural)
- **Work with realtor** specializing in rural or buildable land — different skill set than residential realtor
- **Online searches** — LandWatch, Zillow, county assessor websites
- **Drive target areas** — see properties in person; Google Earth deceives

**Phase 1B — Due diligence per candidate site (1–4 weeks per site):**
- **Zoning check** with the county planning department — confirm residential use allowed
- **Setback verification** — confirm buildable footprint fits the design
- **Well water testing if well exists** — quality, flow rate, depth
- **Percolation test for septic siting** — confirms septic system viability
- **Utility availability check** — electric, internet, gas, cellular
- **Road access** — year-round, public-maintained vs. private road; easements
- **Flood zone check** (FEMA maps)
- **Environmental constraints** — wetlands, endangered species habitat, archaeological sensitivity
- **Title search and preliminary title report** — liens, easements, covenants
- **Geotechnical assessment** (if hillside or complex soil) — soils engineer report

**Phase 1C — Offer and close (30–90 days):**
- **Make offer with contingencies** — soil test, well test, zoning verification, financing
- **Due diligence period** — typically 30–60 days to complete final investigations
- **Closing** — title transfer, recording, payment

**Common site mistakes to avoid:**
- **Land that looks great but can't get a septic permit** — expensive lesson; always percolation test first
- **Land with unrecorded easements** — title search reveals; don't skip
- **Land at the bottom of a hill with drainage issues** — high water table, flooding, septic failures
- **Land with a problematic driveway approach** — steep, long, or across someone else's property
- **Land without reliable water** — dry well, low-flow well, or contaminated well can be dealbreakers

**Recommended minimum criteria (reiteration from the Land, Site, and Feasibility chapter):**
- **1+ acre**, suitable topography (not extreme slopes)
- **Percolation test passing** for conventional septic
- **Well with 10+ GPM sustained flow** (or municipal water in rare cases)
- **Legal buildable status** confirmed by county
- **Access to electricity** (overhead or underground service available)
- **Reasonable distance to town** — 15 min to 1 hour per owner preference

## 12.5 Phase 2 — Design, Permits, and Pre-Construction

**Plain language:** Once the land is owned, several months of design work and permit applications happen before a shovel hits the ground. Drawings get made, engineering gets done, permits get submitted and approved, and all the contractor relationships get established. This phase often takes longer than expected.

**Technical detail:** Pre-construction sequence:

**Phase 2A — Final design drawings (2–6 months):**
- **Architectural plans** — floor plans, elevations, sections, details
- **Structural engineering** — masonry design per ORSC R606/R608, foundation design, lateral load analysis (engineer stamp required for masonry)
- **MEP design** (mechanical, electrical, plumbing) — schematic level minimum for permits
- **Site plan** — building location, septic, well, driveway, setbacks
- **Energy compliance documentation** — meets Oregon Energy Efficiency Specialty Code (OEESC)

**Self-design vs. hire an architect:**
- **Owner-builder can design their own home** in Oregon for their own occupancy (no architect's stamp required in most jurisdictions for single-family residential)
- **Structural engineer's stamp IS required** for engineered elements (masonry walls, spans over certain distances)
- **Hiring a residential designer or architect** is optional but often valuable for:
  - Complex aesthetic decisions
  - Code-compliance experience
  - Coordination with engineers
  - Professional drawings that contractors understand quickly
- **Budget:** architect ~5–15% of construction cost; residential designer ~2–5%; structural engineer ~$2,000–$10,000 for this design type

**Phase 2B — Permit applications (1–4 months):**

The inspection sequence in the legal, permits, and inspections chapter gives the full order. Permits apply in this order:

- **Land use / zoning approval** — confirms the proposed building meets zoning
- **Septic permit** (from Oregon DEQ or county health dept) — site evaluation then design approval
- **Well permit** (from Oregon Water Resources Department) — if new well
- **Driveway and access permit** (from county roads or ODOT)
- **Building permit** (from county building department) — the main permit, covers structural, mechanical, electrical, plumbing
- **Owner-builder registration** (see the Legal, Permits, and Inspections chapter) — simple form, filed with building permit
- **Electrical permit** (from state Electrical Board or local jurisdiction)
- **Plumbing permit** (from local jurisdiction)
- **Mechanical permit** (HVAC)

**Permit wait times:**
- **Septic: 4–12 weeks** (site eval + review)
- **Well: 2–6 weeks**
- **Driveway: 2–4 weeks**
- **Building: 4–12 weeks**
- **Others: 1–4 weeks each**
- **Plan for 3–6 months total** before any ground-breaking

**Phase 2C — Utility coordination (1–3 months):**
- **Electric service application** with the utility (Pacific Power, PGE, or REA) — request service size (see the Electrical Power and Lighting chapter), location of interconnect, overhead vs. underground drop
- **Internet service application** — identify available providers (fiber, cable, wireless ISP, satellite as backup); order installation if available before building
- **No gas service planned** for all-electric home

**Phase 2D — Contractor and supplier relationships (ongoing):**
- **Excavator** — interview 2–3, get quotes, establish relationship before you need them
- **Concrete supplier** — identify the ready-mix plant serving the area, know their delivery radius and minimum orders
- **Concrete finishing crew** — for large pours
- **Well driller** (if well not in place)
- **Septic installer** (if septic not in place)
- **HVAC contractor** — for heat pump installation and refrigerant work
- **Roofing crew** — for major roof installation
- **Drywall crew** — for taping and finishing
- **Lumber and building supply yard** — account, delivery terms, contractor pricing
- **Masonry supply** — CMU, mortar, rebar, cement — establish account

**Phase 2E — Financing and budget finalization:**
- **Construction loan vs. pay-as-you-go** decision (see the Land, Site, and Feasibility chapter3 for owner's financial philosophy)
- **Detailed cost estimate** by phase — materials, labor, permits, contingency
- **Cash flow projection** — when will money be needed
- **Savings and income plan** — how will each phase be funded

## 12.6 Phase 3 — Site Preparation

**Plain language:** Before anything gets built, the site has to be prepared. This means surveying to mark exact boundaries and building location, clearing vegetation where needed, building the driveway, installing the well and septic system, and running utility service. Much of this is contractor work. The owner-builder's role is coordination, quality control, and decision-making.

**Technical detail:** Site preparation sequence:

**Surveying:**
- **Licensed land surveyor** marks property corners, building location, setback lines
- **Stake the building footprint** at the precise location specified in the permit
- **Stake the septic field and well location**
- **Cost:** $1,500–$4,000 depending on site complexity

**Clearing:**
- **Only clear what's needed** — preserve mature trees where possible for shade, wind break, aesthetic
- **Remove only trees in the building footprint, driveway path, septic field, and solar-access zone**
- **Stump removal** for the building footprint and driveway
- **Topsoil stockpiled** separately for future reuse in landscaping

**Driveway:**
- **Base preparation** — excavate to appropriate depth, install geotextile fabric, compact base rock
- **Gravel or paved finish** — gravel is simpler and often sufficient for a rural driveway; pavement can come later
- **Drainage considerations** — crown the driveway for water runoff, install culverts at low points
- **Width:** 12 feet minimum, 14–16 feet preferred for truck access
- **Fire department access requirement** — Oregon typically requires all-weather driveway capable of supporting a fire truck, max grade 10%, turnaround if over a certain length

**Well installation:**
- **Permit already in hand** (see the design, permits, and pre-construction phase)
- **Drilling:** 1–5 days depending on depth and soil; driller will provide a well log (depth, static water level, yield, water quality)
- **Pump installation:** submersible pump at appropriate depth, pressure tank at the house (see the Plumbing Systems chapter), wellhead protected
- **Casing and cap** — meets Oregon Water Resources Department standards
- **Documentation:** well log filed with the state; copies in the owner's project binder

**Septic installation:**
- **Permit already in hand** (see the design, permits, and pre-construction phase)
- **Installation by licensed septic installer** — Oregon requires licensed installer for septic work
- **Tank set, drain field constructed** per approved design
- **Inspection by county or DEQ** before backfill (see the Legal, Permits, and Inspections chapter)
- **Documentation:** as-built drawing with tank and field locations; installer certification

**Utility service:**
- **Electric service trench and conduit** — from utility transformer or pole to the planned interconnect box location
- **Depth per utility spec** (typically 24–36 inches)
- **Conduit:** PVC Schedule 80 or RMC, sized per service capacity (see the Electrical Power and Lighting chapter)
- **Marker tape** at 12 inches above conduit for later excavation safety
- **Internet / communications conduit** in the same trench where possible

**Erosion control:**
- **Silt fence** at downhill edges of disturbed area
- **Check dams** in drainage paths
- **Straw wattles or erosion mat** on exposed slopes
- **Maintain during construction** — inspect after every rain

**Staging area:**
- **Designate an equipment and material staging area** — compacted gravel, away from the building footprint
- **Storage for long-lead materials** (CMU blocks arrive on pallets; rebar; lumber; windows when they arrive)
- **Temporary power** from the utility or a generator (for concrete mixers, power tools, site lighting)
- **Portable toilet** (construction requirement — OSHA applies even to owner-builders with workers on site)
- **Water source** for concrete mixing and cleanup (well pump or delivered water)

## 12.7 Phase 4 — Foundation

**Plain language:** The foundation is the one phase where professional help is essentially required. Excavation, forming, pouring, and finishing a mat foundation is a multi-day operation with a ready-mix truck, pump truck, concrete finishers, and unforgiving time pressure. The owner-builder's role during this phase is coordination, quality verification, and managing the steps before and after the actual pour day.

**Technical detail:** Foundation sequence:

**Sub-phase 4A — Excavation (2–5 days):**
- **Mark the dig extent** based on survey stakes and engineer's details
- **Excavator operator** digs to design depth (below frost line, below undisturbed soil, typically 4–6 feet below grade for a mat foundation)
- **Over-excavate slightly** — foundation needs working room around it for waterproofing and drainage
- **Footing key** cut into undisturbed soil at the perimeter if called for by structural design
- **Inspection:** pre-footing / pre-pour inspection required before any concrete placed (see the Legal, Permits, and Inspections chapter)

**Sub-phase 4B — Sub-slab preparation (3–7 days):**
- **Compacted crushed rock** (4-6 inches of 3/4 inch minus) over undisturbed soil — provides drainage and a stable base
- **Radon vent piping** (see the Envelope, Water, and Thermal Performance chapter) — embedded in the sub-slab drainage layer before insulation
- **Under-slab insulation** — Type XIV EPS 40 psi, 4 inches total (see the Envelope, Water, and Thermal Performance chapter), continuous under the entire slab and extending under the edge
- **Vapor barrier** — 10-15 mil polyethylene sheet, overlapped and sealed at seams, continuous under the slab
- **Rebar mat** — per structural engineer's design, typically #5 or #6 bars at 12-18 inch spacing both ways, tied at every intersection
- **Rebar for the walls** — dowels bent up from the slab into the wall locations, length per engineer's spec
- **Utility sleeves and pipe stubs** — any pipes passing through the slab (sewer out to septic, water in from well, electrical service conduit) are placed before the pour

**Sub-phase 4C — The big pour (1 day):**
- **Ready-mix concrete truck(s)** deliver the concrete mix (see the Structural Design chapter — specific pozzolanic mix with corrosion inhibitor)
- **Concrete pumper truck** places concrete at the desired location
- **Concrete finishing crew** (3–5 people) screeds, bull-floats, edges, floats, and trowels the surface
- **Critical timing** — concrete has limited working time; a crew is required for a pour this size
- **Owner's role:** verify mix delivery (slump test), verify proper placement, take photos for documentation, prepare water and cleanup area, pay the crew
- **Cure time:** 7 days minimum before wall construction; 28 days for full design strength

**Sub-phase 4D — Waterproofing the foundation (3–7 days, after cure):**
- **Exterior foundation waterproofing** per the Envelope, Water, and Thermal Performance chapter — liquid-applied membrane or sheet membrane
- **Protection board** over the membrane
- **Drainage mat** (dimpled plastic) over the protection board
- **Perforated drainpipe** at the base of the footing, wrapped in filter fabric, extending to daylight or a sump
- **Gravel backfill** immediately around the foundation (12-24 inches) for drainage
- **Inspection:** foundation / damp-proofing inspection before backfill (see the Legal, Permits, and Inspections chapter)

**Owner-builder DIY portions:**
- **Rebar tying** — tedious but learnable; major cost savings
- **Sub-slab preparation** — rock, insulation, vapor barrier, sleeves — learnable, pace-flexible
- **Waterproofing application** — learnable with practice
- **Cleanup and support during the pour** — always needed

**Contractor portions:**
- **Excavation** — excavator and operator
- **Concrete supply and pumping** — ready-mix truck, pumper
- **Finishing crew** — 3–5 trained finishers for a one-day pour

**Cost estimate for foundation phase:**
- **Excavation:** $5,000–$15,000
- **Rebar:** $3,000–$8,000
- **Insulation:** $5,000–$12,000
- **Concrete:** $8,000–$20,000 for a 40x50 mat slab
- **Finishing crew:** $3,000–$6,000
- **Waterproofing materials:** $2,000–$5,000
- **Pumper truck:** $1,500–$3,000
- **Total typical:** $30,000–$70,000

## 12.8 Phase 5 — Masonry Walls

**Plain language:** This is the longest single phase of the build, and the most DIY-friendly. Laying CMU block by hand, course by course, weekend by weekend, is the owner-builder's main physical activity for 12 to 24 months. It's meditative, skill-building work. The occasional help is needed for grouting with a pumper and for the heaviest lintels.

**Technical detail:** Masonry wall sequence:

**Sub-phase 5A — Material staging (ongoing):**
- **CMU block deliveries** — a typical delivery is 1,000–2,000 blocks on pallets
- **Storage** on compacted gravel, covered to keep dry until use
- **Rebar** cut to length and bundled by type
- **Mortar bags or silo** — plan for pallets of bagged mortar mix
- **Sand and cement supply** for on-site mortar mixing (alternative to pre-bag)
- **Grout** as needed for core-fill operations

**Sub-phase 5B — First course — the foundation of the walls:**
- **Mason's line set** to exact wall alignment from survey points
- **First course laid in a full bed of mortar** on the foundation
- **Alignment verified** with level, plumb, and diagonal checks
- **Rebar dowels from foundation engage the first course cores**
- **Anchored to foundation** — the first course is the most critical — get it right
- **Recommendation: hire a mason for the first day** of each wall — have them lay the first corner and the first course perfectly; owner-builder continues from there

**Sub-phase 5C — Ongoing courses (weekend rhythm):**
- **Typical weekend output: 100–300 blocks laid** (varies widely with skill, help, complexity)
- **Vertical rebar continuous** from foundation through the entire wall height, spliced at specified intervals
- **Horizontal rebar (bond beam) in selected courses** — typically every 4th course, or at top of wall, per engineer's design
- **Mortar joints struck smooth** — consistent joint width and appearance matters both structurally and aesthetically
- **Weep holes** at the bottom of veneer walls to drain any moisture from the cavity (see the Envelope, Water, and Thermal Performance chapter)
- **Flashing** at tops of openings and at the base of the wall to direct water outward
- **Block saw** on site for cutting to length and creating special block (half, corner, lintel shapes)

**Sub-phase 5D — Wall cavity components (as walls rise):**
- **Inner wythe** (structural 8 inch CMU) laid first
- **Insulation and FRP ties installed** in the cavity as the inner wythe progresses (see the Envelope, Water, and Thermal Performance chapter)
- **Outer wythe** (decorative 4 inch CMU or stone) laid to the same height as the cavity is completed

**Sub-phase 5E — Grout lifts:**
- **Vertical cores grouted** in sections at designed lift heights (typically every 4-8 feet of wall)
- **Small grout pour** — can be done by hand (hose from a grout bucket) for small sections
- **Large grout pour** — concrete pumper truck for a whole wall's worth at once
- **Timing:** grouting typically happens after a defined wall section is complete, before stacking further weight above
- **Inspection:** grouting inspection often required before filling cores (see the Legal, Permits, and Inspections chapter)

**Sub-phase 5F — Openings and lintels:**
- **Window and door openings** framed by special lintel blocks or pre-cast concrete lintels
- **Lintel size** per structural design — typically bears 8 inches onto the wall on each side
- **Heavy lintels over wide openings** (like a great-room opening) may require a crane or a crew to set
- **Temporary shoring** in place during wall completion above the lintel

**Sub-phase 5G — Wall tops and bond beams:**
- **Top course often a bond beam** — horizontal rebar continuous around the entire wall perimeter
- **Anchor bolts or straps cast in** for attaching the floor diaphragm above or the roof structure
- **Alignment verified one final time** — walls must be plumb and level for the floor slab above to work

**DIY vs. contractor on masonry:**
- **DIY:** block laying, mortar mixing and placement, rebar installation, cavity components, small grout pours
- **Contractor:** large grout pours (concrete pumper and crew), heavy lintel setting (if needed), first course setup if desired

**Productivity estimates:**
- **First weekend:** 20-50 blocks (learning)
- **Experienced solo owner:** 100-250 blocks per weekend
- **Owner with helper:** 200-400 blocks per weekend
- **Total blocks for a 3,000 sqft two-story home:** 6,000–12,000 blocks
- **Total weekends of masonry work:** 40–80 weekends across all floors

## 12.9 Phase 6 — Floor Slabs and Service Layer

**Plain language:** Between each floor of walls, a concrete floor slab gets poured, and a service layer (the walkable space with systems running through it) gets set up below the next floor's walls. This is where all the careful coordination between plumbing, electrical, HVAC, and structure happens.

**Technical detail:** Floor slab and service layer sequence:

**Sub-phase 6A — Service layer structure:**
- **Structural system** — either:
  - **Steel open-web joists** spanning wall to wall, with a steel deck above (composite slab option)
  - **OR** **Precast concrete joists** or **poured-in-place concrete beams** with a slab above
- **Owner-builder-friendly option:** steel open-web joists with corrugated steel deck above — lighter, faster to set, standard sizes available from steel supplier
- **Joist hangers bolted into the CMU wall** at the designed elevation (the sideways block course — see the Structural Design chapter)
- **Joists span wall-to-wall** with steel deck fastened on top

**Sub-phase 6B — Service layer infrastructure (while accessible):**
- **Plumbing supply and drain lines** installed above the grating level, accessible from below
- **Electrical conduit runs** — trunk routes, subpanel feeders, branch circuits
- **HVAC ventilation ducts** — rigid metal, supported from the structure above
- **Low-voltage cabling** — Cat6A, coax, speaker wire
- **Radiant floor hydronic primary piping** (copper or PEX-A) from manifolds on one floor to manifolds on another
- **Labeling** — every run labeled during installation (see the Plumbing Systems and Electrical Power and Lighting chapters)

**Sub-phase 6C — Grating installation:**
- **Perforated metal grating** bolted to the structural system
- **Rubber isolation gaskets** between grating and steel to reduce noise transmission
- **Access panels** at strategic locations — small panels that can be removed to reach specific components
- **Inspection:** the service layer is visible from above and below during this phase — document extensively with photos

**Sub-phase 6D — Concrete floor slab above:**
- **Composite slab** — concrete poured over steel deck, steel deck acts as permanent formwork and tension reinforcement
- **Slab thickness:** per structural design, typically 4-6 inches
- **Rebar or welded wire reinforcement** in the concrete
- **Fiber reinforcement** (steel + poly fibers — see the Structural Design chapter)
- **Embedded radiant loops** — PEX tubing laid on top of an insulation layer on the slab, then the topping slab poured separately (see the roof phase)
- **Inspection:** pre-pour inspection required (see the Legal, Permits, and Inspections chapter)
- **Pour** — ready-mix truck, pumper, crew — similar to foundation pour in logistics

**Sub-phase 6E — Post-pour:**
- **Cure** the slab for 7 days before loading
- **Next floor's walls begin** after slab cure
- **Service layer below is now accessible** from above (through hatches cut in the slab) and below (from the floor below) — this is the beauty of the design

**Timing — how service layer enables parallel work:**
- **Once one floor's walls are complete and the floor slab above is poured**, the owner can work on the service layer of that floor while continuing wall work above — parallel paths
- **This reduces overall timeline** versus strictly sequential work

## 12.10 Phase 7 — Roof

**Plain language:** The roof is a defining moment in the build. Before the roof is on, every rain shuts down interior work. After the roof is on, work continues year-round. This phase typically involves hired help — either for the concrete slab roof option or the metal deck option — because of the crane, the height, and the time pressure to get the envelope closed before rain season.

**Technical detail:** Roof sequence (two options from the Structural Design chapter):

**Option A — Concrete slab roof with parapet:**

**Sub-phase 7A-A (Option A) — Formwork:**
- **Heavy formwork** over the top-floor walls, supported by temporary posts or extensive shoring
- **Form deck material** — plywood or steel deck as sacrificial forming
- **Perimeter forms for the parapet** — the raised edge that defines the roof
- **Internal drain provisions** — scupper or drain stubs placed before pour
- **Skylights or other penetrations** framed into the formwork

**Sub-phase 7A-B (Option A) — Reinforcement:**
- **Rebar mat** — typically two layers, top and bottom, per structural design
- **Additional steel at openings** — trimmer bars around any penetration
- **Embedded weldments or fasteners** for future solar rack attachment (see the solar provisions discussion in the Electrical Power and Lighting chapter)

**Sub-phase 7A-C (Option A) — Concrete pour:**
- **Concrete pumper** required — no other way to get concrete 20+ feet up
- **Same pozzolanic mix** as other structural concrete (see the Structural Design chapter)
- **Finishing** to a flat surface with positive slope (1/4 inch per foot) to drains

**Sub-phase 7A-D (Option A) — Waterproofing:**
- **After cure** (14–28 days), apply high-performance roof membrane — TPO, EPDM, or liquid-applied modified bitumen
- **Mechanically fastened or adhered** per manufacturer
- **Parapet coping** — stone, metal, or precast concrete — covers the top of the parapet with a drip edge
- **Drain and scupper details** — professional waterproofing critical around every penetration
- **Walkway pads** on roof for maintenance access
- **Inspection:** weatherproofing inspection

**Option B — Steel deck with open-web joists, metal roofing:**

**Sub-phase 7B-A (Option B) — Structural steel:**
- **Open-web joists** set on the top of the top-floor walls, spanning the full width
- **Crane required** for setting joists (they're long and awkward)
- **Joist spacing** per structural design (typically 4–6 feet on center)
- **Steel deck (corrugated)** fastened to the tops of the joists — this becomes the roof deck
- **Standing-seam roof panels or architectural metal panels** on top of the deck

**Sub-phase 7B-B (Option B) — Insulation and vapor management:**
- **Rigid foam insulation above the deck** (R-40+ for Oregon) with layers staggered to reduce thermal bridges
- **Or closed-cell spray foam on the underside** for a "warm roof" assembly
- **Vapor barrier** positioning depends on assembly type
- **Continuous edge detailing** to prevent moisture migration

**Sub-phase 7B-C (Option B) — Metal roof:**
- **Standing-seam metal panels** fastened with concealed clips — watertight, long-lived, supports mechanical snow-shedding
- **Slope: 3:12** (as noted in the Structural Design chapter for this design)
- **Ridge, eaves, rakes flashed** per manufacturer details
- **Gutters and downspouts** routed to the rainwater cistern (see the Plumbing Systems chapter)
- **Butyl tape at every panel lap** for extra weather protection
- **Inspection:** roofing inspection (see the Legal, Permits, and Inspections chapter)

**Why hire out the roof:**
- **Crane** — renting and operating a crane safely is a pro skill
- **Height work** — fall-protection rules, gear, and experience matter
- **Speed** — getting the envelope dried-in quickly protects everything below
- **Weather windows** — a crew can finish a roof in a week that would take an owner-builder months

**Timing of the roof in Oregon:**
- **Goal: dried-in before the first serious rain of the fall (mid-October)**
- **If not possible**, full temporary weather cover (tarps, plastic) must maintain the interior through the wet months — expensive and slow
- **Plan the build schedule so the roof is done in late summer** if at all possible

## 12.11 Phase 8 — Windows and Exterior Doors

**Plain language:** With walls up and roof on, windows and exterior doors get installed. The openings were framed during wall construction; now the actual windows arrive and get set into the openings, sealed, and flashed against water intrusion. This is careful work — windows that leak are a lifetime headache.

**Technical detail:** Windows and exterior doors sequence:

**Sub-phase 8A — Pre-delivery verification:**
- **Openings dimensionally verified** against window manufacturer's rough-opening specs
- **Window order** placed with lead time (custom windows often 8–16 weeks lead time — order before walls complete)
- **Delivery scheduling** when site can accept and store

**Sub-phase 8B — Window installation:**
- **Flashing sequence** at each opening — sill flashing first, side flashing next, head flashing last; always bottom-up for water shedding
- **Window set into the opening** — typically sitting on shims to level, secured per manufacturer's fastening pattern
- **Air sealing** around the perimeter — backer rod + sealant, or low-expansion spray foam
- **Interior trim deferred** until after drywall

**Window types and installation nuances:**
- **Triple-pane, high-performance frames** (fiberglass, solid wood, or composite) — per the Envelope, Water, and Thermal Performance chapter
- **Fixed windows** — easier to install, simpler, used where ventilation not needed
- **Casement or awning windows** — provide ventilation, more complex hardware, tight-sealing when closed
- **Sliding windows** — economical, compromise on air sealing, use selectively

**Exterior doors:**
- **Pre-hung door units** — door + frame + threshold + weatherstripping as one unit — best performance
- **Set into the rough opening** with shims, level, and fastened
- **Flashing at the sill, sides, and head** per manufacturer

**Post-install inspection:**
- **Water test** — spray the window/door with a hose from outside, inspect for leaks inside
- **Document** — photo every flashing detail before covering with siding/veneer/trim

## 12.12 Phase 9 — Rough-In (Parallel Plumbing, Electrical, HVAC)

**Plain language:** With the building dried-in, the systems go in. Plumbing pipes, electrical conduit, HVAC ducts, and radiant heat loops all get installed in parallel. This is where all the plumbing, electrical, and HVAC decisions become physical. It's a long phase — 6 to 12 months of weekend work — and it benefits from a careful sequence.

**Technical detail:** Rough-in sequence:

**Sub-phase 9A — Plumbing rough-in:**

See the Plumbing Systems chapter for detailed design.
- **Drain, waste, vent (DWV) piping first** — larger, harder to relocate, follows gravity rules
- **Water supply piping next** — trunk lines, then branches
- **Radiant floor loops on the floor slabs** (before topping slab — see the insulation and topping-slab phase)
- **All pipe stub-outs** for future fixtures (toilet rough-ins, shower valves, sink supplies) roughed-in and capped
- **Pressure test** on the supply system before covering
- **Inspection:** plumbing rough-in inspection (see the Legal, Permits, and Inspections chapter)

**Sub-phase 9B — Electrical rough-in:**

See the Electrical Power and Lighting chapter for detailed design.
- **Service entrance** from the interconnect box — conduit and conductors ready for utility hookup
- **Main panel and subpanels** mounted and wired (but not energized)
- **Branch circuits** — home runs from panels to each outlet/switch/fixture box
- **Low-voltage** — Cat6A, coax, security, audio in parallel conduits
- **Grounding electrode conductor** from the foundation rebar stub to the main panel
- **Inspection:** electrical rough-in inspection (see the Legal, Permits, and Inspections chapter)

**Sub-phase 9C — HVAC rough-in:**

See the HVAC, Ventilation, and Air Quality chapter for detailed design.
- **Ventilation ducts** — rigid metal, sealed, supported
- **Radiant hydronic primary piping** — from heat pump location to each floor's manifold
- **Manifolds installed** with zone valves
- **Radiant floor PEX loops on each floor** (before topping slab)
- **ERV installation** — unit mounted, ducts connected
- **Inspection:** mechanical rough-in inspection (see the Legal, Permits, and Inspections chapter)

**Sub-phase 9D — Coordination between systems:**
- **Sequence matters when multiple systems cross in the service layer** — generally, plumbing drains (larger, gravity-dependent) first, then HVAC ducts, then electrical conduit (most flexible)
- **Vertical runs:** stacks and main trunks go in before horizontal branches
- **Regular coordination check-ins** (daily if possible) to catch conflicts before they become rework

**Sub-phase 9E — Documentation:**
- **Photograph every wall and ceiling area before covering** — multiple angles, ruler in frame for scale
- **Mark the photos with labels** — "north wall, first floor, between Grid A and Grid B"
- **Store in the project binder and digital backup**
- **Future self will thank present self** when trying to hang a picture without hitting a pipe

## 12.13 Phase 10 — Insulation and Topping Slabs

**Plain language:** With rough-in complete and inspected, the envelope gets insulated and the radiant floor topping slabs get poured. This locks in the building's thermal performance and sets the stage for finishes.

**Technical detail:** Insulation and topping sequence:

**Sub-phase 10A — Wall cavity insulation:**

See the Envelope, Water, and Thermal Performance chapter for detailed design.
- **Cavity insulation already installed during CMU construction** (see the masonry wall phase) for the main envelope
- **Interior partition walls:** mineral wool batts for sound control
- **Rim joist and band areas:** closed-cell spray foam or rigid foam for air sealing

**Sub-phase 10B — Above-floor insulation (for radiant):**
- **Type XIV EPS or XPS** on top of the structural floor slab
- **Vapor barrier** over the insulation
- **Radiant floor PEX already stapled to the insulation** in the rough-in phase

**Sub-phase 10C — Topping slab pour:**
- **Concrete pumper** required for most floors
- **Thin (1.5-2 inch) fiber-reinforced concrete**
- **Poured over the radiant loops** (which are filled with water and pressurized to prevent collapse during pour)
- **Screeded flat**, troweled smooth
- **Cure time:** 7-14 days before finish flooring installation

**Sub-phase 10D — Air sealing details:**
- **Every penetration sealed** — pipes, conduits, wires through any wall or floor
- **Top plate sealing** where walls meet ceilings
- **Window and door perimeter sealing** verified
- **Blower door test** — diagnostic test after air sealing to quantify the envelope tightness (target ≤ 0.6 ACH50 for passive-house-level; ≤ 3 ACH50 for code)

## 12.14 Phase 11 — Drywall and Wall Finishes

**Plain language:** Drywall goes up on every interior wall, gets taped and finished, and then gets painted. This phase is often partially contracted — good drywall taping is a specific skill that takes years to master, and the visible quality matters.

**Technical detail:** Drywall sequence:

**Sub-phase 11A — Drywall hanging (DIY-able):**
- **5/8 inch Type X drywall** throughout for fire rating
- **Moisture-resistant drywall** in wet areas
- **Cement board** behind tile in showers
- **Hung tight to ceilings and floors**, screw pattern per manufacturer
- **Typical owner-builder time:** a weekend per average room

**Sub-phase 11B — Drywall finishing (consider hiring):**
- **Tape and mud seams** — three coats typical, each sanded before next
- **Corner bead** installed at every outside corner
- **Level of finish:**
  - **Level 4** — standard residential, acceptable for flat paint
  - **Level 5** — skim-coated for gloss paint or raking-light locations
- **Hire a professional drywall finisher** for at least the primary living areas — the visible quality difference is substantial and they work 3-5x faster than a DIYer

**Sub-phase 11C — Paint prep and primer:**
- **Caulk every trim-to-wall joint**
- **Spackle every screw and nail head**
- **Sand smooth**
- **Primer coat** — tinted if going to a strongly-colored finish

**Sub-phase 11D — Paint finish:**
- **Two coats of finish paint minimum**
- **Ceiling first, then walls, then trim** — don't go backward
- **Cut-in with brush at edges, roll the main field**

## 12.15 Phase 12 — Finishes (Flooring, Tile, Cabinets, Trim)

**Plain language:** With walls painted, the finish work proceeds: tile in bathrooms and entry, cabinets in kitchen and bathrooms, trim everywhere, wood or engineered flooring in specific rooms, and final sealing of the polished concrete floors. This phase often stretches beyond TCO — some finishes can wait until after move-in.

**Technical detail:** Finish sequence:

**Sub-phase 12A — Tile (hire a pro for primary bathroom):**
- **Bathrooms, entry, mudroom** — tile over thin-set on waterproof membrane
- **Kitchen backsplash**
- **Grout (epoxy in wet areas, cementitious elsewhere)**
- **Silicone at change-of-plane joints**

**Sub-phase 12B — Cabinets and millwork:**
- **Kitchen cabinets** installed on level floor, plumb to wall
- **Bathroom vanities** installed
- **Built-in shelving, bookcases, entry bench**
- **Countertops templated and installed** after cabinets are set

**Sub-phase 12C — Flooring:**
- **Polished concrete floors finished** — grinding, staining, sealing
- **Engineered wood installed** with click-lock or glue-down on radiant
- **Transitions between flooring types** — thresholds, T-strips

**Sub-phase 12D — Trim:**
- **Baseboard** after flooring
- **Door and window casings**
- **Crown molding in selected rooms**
- **Caulk all joints, paint touch-ups**

**Sub-phase 12E — Final plumbing and electrical:**
- **Toilets, sinks, faucets installed**
- **Shower fixtures and glass enclosures**
- **Light fixtures hung**
- **Outlet and switch plates installed**

**Sub-phase 12F — Appliances:**
- **Kitchen appliances installed and tested**
- **Laundry connections made**
- **HVAC equipment commissioning completed** (see the HVAC commissioning discussion)

## 12.16 The TCO Push and Life After Move-In

**Plain language:** Temporary Certificate of Occupancy — TCO — is the key milestone where the owner can legally move into the house even though not every detail is finished. Getting to TCO quickly accelerates everything: no more commute to the build site, daily presence for small tasks, lower rent costs, and the psychological boost of living in the house. Continued work happens from the inside.

**Technical detail:** TCO strategy:

**What's required for TCO (see the Legal, Permits, and Inspections chapter):**
- One working bathroom (toilet, sink, shower or tub)
- Working kitchen (sink, hot and cold water, minimum one appliance)
- Heat source functional
- Water heater installed and working
- All final inspections passed for the portions being occupied
- Egress meeting code (working doors and emergency-egress windows)
- Smoke and CO detectors installed and functional
- Stairs and handrails installed and safe

**What's NOT required for TCO:**
- Every room finished
- Every floor complete
- Landscaping
- Garage
- Every detail of finish work
- Outdoor utility service bay (if basic utilities work)

**Post-move-in priorities:**
- **First 30 days:** live with the house, identify immediate issues, develop the maintenance rhythm
- **First 6 months:** finish the remaining major finish work (last floor of drywall, the last bathroom, the last room's paint)
- **First year:** complete landscape basics (driveway, immediate yard), outdoor lighting, exterior details
- **Year 2:** optional additions — solar, EV charger, workshop, detached structures
- **CO achievement:** everything complete, final final inspection passed

**Reasons NOT to rush TCO:**
- If a major inspection will fail for minor issues, fix them first
- If moving in will compromise ongoing work quality (dust, family disruption, safety)
- If the secondary work plan is vague (living in an unfinished house without a plan is exhausting)

**Reasons TO push for TCO:**
- Interest and rent savings
- Commute elimination (daily presence on-site)
- Psychological milestone
- Insurance clarity (homeowner's vs. builder's risk)
- Schedule for the remaining work
