# Chapter 18: Inspection And Approval Sequence

This chapter explains how inspections fit into the build. It covers what inspections are for, how to prepare, what usually happens at each stage, what records to keep, how to handle corrections, and how the project moves toward final approval.

Inspection preparation needs practical field guidance, but this chapter does not display forms. Use the guidance here to prepare for inspections, then use the jurisdiction's actual permit system, inspection cards, correction notices, and approved plans as the official record.

Exact inspection names, required inspections, scheduling rules, owner-builder responsibilities, trade inspection requirements, correction rules, reinspection fees, Temporary Certificate of Occupancy requirements, and Certificate of Occupancy requirements must be verified with the building department for the actual project.

## Inspection Scope

**Simple**

An inspection is a pause point.

The inspector checks work before it gets covered, buried, energized, occupied, or treated as finished. The point is to catch problems while they are still reachable.

**Applied**

Plan the project around inspection gates:

| Gate Type | What It Protects |
|---|---|
| Site approval | The house is in the right place and the site can legally be built. |
| Foundation approval | Soil, forms, reinforcement, sleeves, and concrete prep are visible. |
| Structural approval | Walls, floors, roof, reinforcement, anchors, and bracing are visible. |
| Rough trade approval | Plumbing, electrical, mechanical, and low-voltage work are visible. |
| Energy and insulation approval | Air sealing and insulation are visible before cover. |
| Final approval | Systems, safety items, fixtures, equipment, and occupancy items are complete. |

Do not schedule the next phase so tightly that a correction becomes a crisis.

**Professional**

Inspections are not quality control by themselves. They are third-party code checks at defined moments. The owner-builder still owns daily quality control, documentation, sequencing, and correction.

The inspection strategy should match the build sequence. Every time work is about to disappear under concrete, backfill, drywall, insulation, roofing, tile, or finish, ask whether an approval or photo record is needed first.

**Code**

Required inspections are controlled by the adopted code, permits, approved plans, local jurisdiction, utility requirements, septic or well authority, and trade-specific permit path.

The building official controls what must be inspected and when work may continue.

## Inspection Mindset

**Simple**

The inspector is not the enemy.

A failed inspection is annoying, but it is better than hiding a serious mistake inside the house.

**Applied**

Show up prepared:

- Approved plans are on site.
- Permit information is available.
- The work is complete enough to inspect.
- The work is clean and visible.
- Ladders, lights, and access are ready.
- Test gauges are connected where needed.
- Product instructions are available for unusual items.
- Photos are taken before the inspector arrives.
- Questions are written down.

Be direct. Ask what needs correction. Fix it. Document it.

**Professional**

A prepared owner-builder gets better information from inspections. The inspector can focus on code and installation quality instead of searching through clutter, missing documents, covered work, or incomplete assemblies.

The best inspection relationship is professional, boring, and consistent: complete work, clear access, clean site, approved plans, honest questions, prompt corrections, and no arguing from memory.

**Code**

Inspectors enforce the adopted code and approved permit documents. They do not rewrite the design in the field, and they are not a substitute for the engineer, designer, manufacturer, or code book.

If an inspector requires a correction, record the correction clearly and resolve it through the proper channel.

## Master Readiness Pattern

**Simple**

Before every inspection, ask the same basic question:

Can the inspector see what they need to see?

**Applied**

Inspection readiness means:

- The relevant work is complete.
- The work is not covered.
- The site is safe to walk.
- The correct documents are present.
- Required tests are set up.
- Specialty products are identifiable.
- Prior corrections are fixed.
- The owner or representative can answer basic questions.
- The next work step is ready, but not started too early.

If any answer is weak, wait before calling.

**Professional**

A repeatable readiness pattern prevents failed inspections. For each inspection, define the scope, visible items, required tests, required documents, expected access, common failure points, and the next allowed phase after approval.

Do not rely on memory. Keep inspection readiness notes in the project record, but keep the official approval record in the jurisdiction's system and project binder.

**Code**

The jurisdiction decides which inspections require the work to remain exposed and which documents must be available. Covering work too early can require removal, destructive inspection, engineer letters, or other corrective action.

When in doubt, ask before covering.

## Site And Preconstruction Approvals

**Simple**

Some inspections happen before the house exists.

The site, septic, well, driveway, erosion control, access, address, and building location may all need review before construction really starts.

**Applied**

Prepare site approvals around:

- Property boundaries.
- Building location.
- Setbacks.
- Septic area.
- Well or water source.
- Driveway and road access.
- Fire and emergency access where required.
- Erosion and sediment control.
- Tree or environmental protection where required.
- Temporary construction entrance.
- Staging and material storage.

Do not start major earthwork without knowing which site controls apply.

**Professional**

Site approvals are a risk filter. They prevent the house from being placed in the wrong location, built without legal access, routed over a septic area, placed too close to a boundary, or started without erosion control.

For rural land, septic and water approvals can control the whole project. Treat them as core build approvals, not paperwork on the side.

**Code**

Site approvals may involve planning, building, environmental health, Oregon DEQ or county septic authority, Oregon Water Resources Department, county roads, ODOT, fire authority, erosion control, utility providers, and the local building department.

Verify the required site approvals before starting work that cannot be easily moved.

## Foundation And Underground Approvals

**Simple**

Foundation inspections happen before concrete and before backfill.

Once concrete is poured or dirt is pushed back, the important parts are hidden.

**Applied**

Foundation readiness usually centers on:

- Excavation location and bearing surface.
- Forms.
- Reinforcement.
- Chairs and supports.
- Sleeves and penetrations.
- Grounding electrode work where applicable.
- Under-slab layers.
- Vapor barrier.
- Insulation where applicable.
- Drainage.
- Waterproofing.
- Open trenches before backfill.

Photograph each layer before it disappears.

**Professional**

Foundation approval should be coordinated with geotechnical assumptions, structural drawings, drainage, waterproofing, radon or soil-gas strategy, utility penetrations, grounding, concrete delivery, and weather.

The worst foundation inspection problem is not a correction. It is discovering the correction after the concrete truck is already scheduled with no buffer.

**Code**

Footing, foundation, under-slab, underground utilities, grounding, waterproofing, drainage, and backfill inspections are controlled by approved plans and the adopted code.

Do not pour concrete or backfill underground work until the required approval is complete.

## Concrete Placement Quality Control

**Simple**

Not every concrete check is a formal building inspection.

Some checks are owner quality control. They still matter.

**Applied**

Before and during concrete placement, verify:

- The pour is approved to proceed.
- Forms are braced.
- Reinforcement is still in position.
- Sleeves and embeds are in place.
- Access and pump location are ready.
- Crew roles are clear.
- Concrete tickets are saved.
- Placement, vibration, screeding, finishing, and curing are watched.
- Weather protection is ready.
- Photos are taken.

Concrete does not wait while the crew argues about missing tools.

**Professional**

Concrete inspection and concrete quality control overlap but are not the same. The inspector may approve visible pre-pour conditions. The owner-builder still needs delivery verification, placement observation, consolidation control, finishing timing, curing protection, and test documentation where specified.

If the approved design or testing program requires slump, cylinders, temperature, air content, or other checks, those should be arranged before the truck arrives.

**Code**

Concrete placement must follow approved plans, adopted code, mix design, inspection requirements, and testing requirements where applicable.

Do not treat owner observation as a substitute for a required inspection or required test.

## Masonry And Structural Approvals

**Simple**

Masonry inspections catch what will be hidden inside the wall.

Rebar, grout cells, lintels, ties, anchors, bond beams, and openings need to be checked before they are buried.

**Applied**

Masonry readiness focuses on:

- Wall layout.
- Reinforcement.
- Open cells that need grout.
- Clean cells.
- Bond beams.
- Lintels.
- Wall ties.
- Insulation continuity.
- Flashing and weeps where applicable.
- Anchors and embeds.
- Temporary bracing.
- Photos before grout or cover.

Keep the approved structural drawings on site.

**Professional**

Concrete and masonry structures often need inspection at several moments, not just when the wall is finished. Reinforcement, grout lifts, lintels, floor supports, roof supports, service-layer structure, and wall-to-floor connections each may create inspection gates.

The owner-builder should keep structural questions separated from finish preferences. A clean-looking wall is not enough if the reinforcement, grout, anchorage, or load path is wrong.

**Code**

Masonry, reinforced cells, grout, lintels, bond beams, structural steel, floor slabs, roof structure, anchors, bracing, and special engineered items must follow approved structural documents and adopted code.

Do not grout or cover required structural work before inspection.

## Plumbing Rough And Final Approvals

**Simple**

Plumbing inspections check water in, waste out, vents, pressure, leaks, and fixture safety.

A plumbing mistake hidden behind a wall can become a leak, smell, clog, or flood.

**Applied**

Rough plumbing readiness includes:

- Pipe material and fittings visible.
- Drain slope visible.
- Vents visible.
- Cleanouts visible.
- Supports visible.
- Shutoff locations visible.
- Test pressure or test setup ready.
- Fixture rough-in locations checked.
- Water and drain paths coordinated with structure.
- Manufacturer instructions ready for unusual products.

Final plumbing readiness means fixtures are installed, tested, accessible, and not leaking.

**Professional**

Plumbing inspections should be staged around underground, rough-in, pressure or leak tests, water heater, fixtures, pumps, backflow, non-potable systems if used, and final operation.

The owner-builder should test before the inspector arrives. The inspection should confirm a system that has already been checked, not discover the first obvious leak.

**Code**

Plumbing inspections, testing, pipe materials, fixture clearances, water heaters, backflow, vents, drains, pumps, and fixture installation must follow the adopted plumbing code, approved plans, and product instructions.

Keep test gauges and access visible where required.

## Electrical, Low-Voltage, And Alarm Approvals

**Simple**

Electrical inspections check whether power can move safely.

The inspector looks at panels, grounding, boxes, wires, breakers, devices, lighting, alarms, and protection.

**Applied**

Rough electrical readiness includes:

- Panels mounted where allowed.
- Boxes installed.
- Conductors or raceways routed.
- Grounding and bonding visible.
- Nail protection installed where needed.
- Box fill and device space considered.
- Low-voltage separation handled.
- Smoke and CO alarm wiring coordinated.
- Panel schedule drafted.
- Work is not covered.

Final readiness means devices, breakers, labels, lights, alarms, and protection devices are installed and tested.

**Professional**

Electrical inspections are easier when the design is organized. Dedicated circuits, subpanels, low-voltage pathways, smart-switch boxes, exterior equipment, surge protection, backup provisions, and future solar or EV pathways should be visible and labeled.

Low-voltage work should not be treated as an afterthought. Cameras, network, alarms, controls, speakers, and sensors need correct separation, support, listing, penetration sealing, and documentation where required.

**Code**

Electrical, low-voltage, fire alarm, smoke alarm, CO alarm, communications, grounding, bonding, panel, breaker, box, device, lighting, and equipment inspections must follow the adopted electrical code, product listings, and approved plans.

Do not energize or cover work before required approval.

## Mechanical, Ventilation, And HVAC Approvals

**Simple**

Mechanical inspections check heat, ventilation, exhaust, ducts, equipment, drains, and controls.

The house needs air on purpose. The inspector checks that the installed system matches the approved path.

**Applied**

Mechanical rough-in readiness includes:

- Equipment locations set.
- Ducts routed and supported.
- Exhaust routes visible.
- Outdoor intakes and terminations planned.
- Hydronic piping visible where applicable.
- Manifolds accessible.
- Pressure tests ready where required.
- Condensate paths planned.
- Combustion details visible if any fuel-burning equipment is used.
- Manufacturer instructions on site.

Final readiness means equipment starts, controls work, exhaust runs, drains work, and access remains clear.

**Professional**

Mechanical approval should follow ventilation design, radiant or hydronic layout, equipment installation, exhaust design, kitchen hood strategy, dryer exhaust, condensate, makeup air, controls, service access, and commissioning.

The inspection path should not turn into a last-minute attempt to explain a system the plans did not show. Nonstandard systems need clear approved documents.

**Code**

Mechanical inspections, ventilation, exhaust, ducts, equipment, hydronic components, combustion appliances, condensate, controls, terminations, and final operation must follow adopted mechanical code, energy provisions, manufacturer instructions, and approved plans.

Smart-home control should not replace required equipment controls or safety devices.

## Insulation, Energy, And Pre-Cover Approvals

**Simple**

Insulation inspections happen before walls and ceilings are closed.

The inspector checks that the house is actually insulated and sealed the way the approved energy path says it should be.

**Applied**

Pre-cover readiness includes:

- Rough trade approvals complete.
- Insulation installed in the right locations.
- Air sealing visible.
- Vapor control visible where required.
- Firestopping complete.
- Exterior penetrations sealed.
- Window and door sealing complete.
- Energy documentation ready.
- Blower-door or diagnostic testing scheduled where required.
- Photos taken before cover.

Do not rush drywall because the room looks almost ready.

**Professional**

Energy and insulation approval is a bridge between rough-in and interior cover. It confirms that thermal, air, vapor, and firestopping details were completed before they vanish behind drywall, plaster, tile, cabinets, or ceilings.

For a high-performance concrete house, continuity matters more than isolated product labels. The inspector may verify code minimums, but the owner-builder should also verify performance intent.

**Code**

Insulation, air sealing, energy documentation, blower-door testing where required, vapor control, firestopping, foam thermal barriers, and cover approval must follow the adopted code, approved energy path, and local inspection process.

Do not cover energy or firestopping work before required approval.

## Final Trade Inspections

**Simple**

Final trade inspections happen when the systems are installed and working.

The rough work was about what is hidden. The final work is about what operates.

**Applied**

Before final trade inspections:

- Fixtures are installed.
- Devices are installed.
- Lights work.
- Fans and ventilation work.
- Equipment starts and shuts down properly.
- Shutoffs are accessible.
- Panels and breakers are labeled.
- GFCI and AFCI protection is tested where present.
- Smoke and CO alarms work.
- Water heater and HVAC equipment are ready.
- Manufacturer instructions and startup documents are available.

Run the house before calling for final inspection.

**Professional**

Final plumbing, electrical, and mechanical inspections should confirm that installed systems are complete, accessible, labeled, safe, and operational. This is where missing escutcheons, unlabeled panels, dead devices, loose fixtures, non-working fans, inaccessible shutoffs, unfinished alarms, and incomplete equipment startup can slow occupancy.

The owner-builder should walk the house by system before scheduling finals.

**Code**

Final trade inspections are controlled by the applicable specialty codes, approved plans, product listings, and local inspection process.

Passing rough inspection does not guarantee passing final inspection.

## Building Final, Temporary Occupancy, And Certificate Of Occupancy

**Simple**

Final building approval is the house saying, "I am safe enough to live in."

The building official decides when that is true.

**Applied**

Before asking for final or temporary occupancy review, confirm:

- Required trade finals are complete or properly addressed.
- Safe egress paths exist.
- Stairs, guards, and handrails are safe where required.
- Required alarms work.
- Required heating, water, sanitation, ventilation, and electrical systems are usable.
- Exterior doors lock and open correctly.
- Required glazing and fall protections are complete.
- Site conditions required for occupancy are addressed.
- Unfinished areas are controlled.
- Inspection records are organized.

Do not move in before the legal occupancy path is approved.

**Professional**

Temporary occupancy can be a useful owner-builder milestone, but it is not a loophole. It is an approval status controlled by the jurisdiction. It may require specific systems, safe access, life-safety items, trade approvals, site conditions, and a plan for remaining work.

The final approval package should make the building official's job straightforward: prior approvals, trade finals, corrections resolved, safe occupancy conditions, and clear remaining items if temporary occupancy is requested.

**Code**

Temporary Certificate of Occupancy, Certificate of Occupancy, partial occupancy, final building inspection, site conditions, life-safety items, and required systems are controlled by the jurisdiction and building official.

Do not assume any old occupancy list is complete or current. Verify before building the move-in plan.

## Corrections, Reinspection, And Records

**Simple**

Corrections are part of building.

Write them down, fix them cleanly, and get the reinspection done.

**Applied**

When corrections are issued:

- Record the exact correction.
- Ask for clarification if the correction is unclear.
- Photograph the condition before repair if useful.
- Fix the actual issue, not just the visible symptom.
- Update the project record.
- Do not cover the correction before reinspection if the inspector needs to see it.
- Schedule reinspection only when the correction is complete.

Treat corrections as data.

**Professional**

Inspection records should include approval dates, correction notices, reinspection results, photos, product data, test results, inspector comments where available, and the plan version used for the inspection.

A clean inspection record supports future maintenance, resale, insurance, warranty claims, and any later remodel.

**Code**

Correction notices, reinspection requirements, fees, stop-work orders, partial approvals, and approval conditions are controlled by the jurisdiction.

If there is disagreement about a correction, resolve it through the official appeal or clarification process, not by hiding the work.
