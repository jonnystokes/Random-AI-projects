# Chapter 8: Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, And Radon

The envelope is the line between inside and outside. It includes the roof, walls, slab edge, below-grade walls, windows, doors, penetrations, air barrier, vapor control, waterproofing, insulation, drainage plane, and soil-gas control.

A concrete house can be strong and still fail if the envelope is wrong. Water gets through weak details. Air leaks carry heat and moisture. Thermal bridges make cold spots. Radon can move through the soil and slab. The cleaner path is to treat the envelope as a set of connected control layers, not as a shopping list of products.

## 8.1 Envelope Purpose

**Simple**

The envelope is the house shell. It keeps rain, groundwater, cold air, hot air, pests, smoke, and soil gas from becoming indoor problems.

Good concrete does not replace a good envelope. Concrete handles structure. The envelope handles water, air, vapor, heat, comfort, and soil gas.

**Applied**

Before choosing materials, divide the envelope into jobs:

| Job | What It Controls | Field Question |
|---|---|---|
| Bulk water | Rain, roof water, groundwater, splashback, leaks | Where does liquid water go when it hits this detail? |
| Air | Drafts, uncontrolled air exchange, hidden moisture movement | Can the air barrier be traced and inspected? |
| Vapor | Slow moisture movement through materials | Which side can the assembly dry toward? |
| Heat | Winter heat loss, summer heat gain, comfort | Is insulation continuous at edges and openings? |
| Soil gas | Radon and other gases moving from ground to house | Is there a sealed rough-in and vent path? |

Each job requires a planned control layer. Do not assume one product does all of them.

**Professional**

Envelope design is control-layer design. The water-control layer, air-control layer, vapor-control strategy, thermal layer, and soil-gas layer must connect across assemblies. The weak points are transitions: slab to wall, wall to roof, wall to window, window to flashing, below-grade to above-grade, and every penetration.

Concrete and masonry are durable, but they are not automatically dry, airtight, insulated, or radon-resistant. Treat the concrete shell as the structural base that the control layers attach to.

**Code**

- ORSC Chapter 11: residential energy efficiency and thermal envelope requirements.
- ORSC R703.1: exterior wall weather protection.
- ORSC R703.8: flashing and water-resistive detailing for masonry veneer where used.
- ORSC R401.3: surface drainage around foundations.
- ORSC R405: foundation drainage.
- ORSC R406: foundation dampproofing and waterproofing.
- ORSC Appendix AF: radon control methods in covered Oregon counties.
- Manufacturer installation instructions: controlling source for listed membranes, windows, doors, flashing products, sealants, tapes, coatings, and roof assemblies.

## 8.2 Oregon Energy Compliance Map For The Envelope

**Simple**

Energy code does not require one exact wall. It defines the performance that must be shown.

For a concrete home, that matters. A concrete roof, double-wythe wall, masonry cavity, slab edge, and unusual insulation location may not fit a simple prescriptive picture.

**Applied**

At permit time, build the energy package before ordering envelope materials:

1. Confirm the adopted ORSC edition in force for the permit.
2. Select the compliance path: prescriptive, UA tradeoff, or another accepted path.
3. Fill out the residential energy additional-measure form required by Oregon BCD.
4. List every envelope component on the drawings: walls, roof, slab edge, basement walls, windows, skylights, exterior doors, and heated slab areas.
5. Show insulation location, R-value, U-factor, air barrier location, vapor-control strategy, foam protection, fenestration labels, and the selected additional measure.
6. Decide whether blower-door testing is part of the compliance path or an above-code quality target.
7. Save the energy forms, calculations, product labels, and final test reports in the project binder.

Under the 2023 ORSC Chapter 11, the baseline envelope table includes these performance values:

| Component | Required Performance | Equivalent Value Or Note |
|---|---|---|
| Above-grade wall | U-0.059 | R-21 intermediate framing equivalent |
| Below-grade wall | C-0.063 | R-15 continuous insulation or R-21 |
| Flat ceiling | U-0.021 | R-49 |
| Vaulted ceiling | U-0.033 | R-30 rafter or R-30A scissor truss |
| Underfloor | U-0.033 | R-30 |
| Slab-edge perimeter | F-0.520 | R-15, minimum 24 inch horizontal or vertical below grade |
| Heated slab interior | N/A | R-10 under entire slab, in addition to perimeter insulation |
| Windows | U-0.27 | Glass doors follow window performance |
| Skylights | U-0.50 | Product label or approved rating path |
| Exterior doors | U-0.20 | Door exceptions depend on the adopted table notes |

Use the adopted code table and BCD forms as the controlling documents. Do not copy an old value from a prior design note into the permit set.

**Professional**

The 2023 ORSC Chapter 11 became effective October 1, 2023, and mandatory April 1, 2024. It uses Table N1101.1(1) as the standard base-case envelope table and Table N1101.1(2) for additional measures. Oregon's residential energy path requires the envelope baseline plus at least one additional measure for new conditioned space. If ducts and air-handling equipment are outside the conditioned space under an exception path, the measure count can change.

A concrete roof is the important caution. A flat-ceiling prescriptive value does not automatically translate into a concrete roof assembly. The concrete deck has very little insulating value by itself. A concrete roof usually requires a UA tradeoff calculation, energy model, or another accepted performance path that accounts for the actual deck, insulation, membrane, penetrations, parapets, and thermal bridges.

Oregon's Table N1101.1(1) lists window U-factor requirements but does not set a mandatory SHGC value in that table. Solar heat gain coefficient is still a real design choice. It affects overheating, passive solar gain, glare, and cooling load, but it should not be written as a statewide Oregon residential code minimum unless the adopted table or approved compliance path requires it.

**Code**

- ORSC N1101.1: residential energy compliance baseline and additional-measure structure.
- ORSC Table N1101.1(1): prescriptive envelope requirements.
- ORSC Table N1101.1(2): additional measures.
- ORSC N1104.1 and Table N1104.1(1): UA tradeoff path.
- ORSC N1104.7: slab-on-grade floor insulation.
- Oregon BCD Form 440-4854j: Residential Energy Additional Measure Selection.
- Oregon BCD residential energy compliance resources: current Chapter 11, forms, technical bulletins, and compliance tools.

## 8.3 Control Layers And Continuity

**Simple**

The house requires continuous layers. If a layer stops at a corner or opening, the problem starts there.

Water should drain out. Air should stay controlled. Heat should stay inside when the house is heated. Soil gas should have a planned path out.

**Applied**

Trace each control layer with a pencil on the drawings:

- Water-control layer from roof to wall to flashing to drainage.
- Air-control layer from slab to wall to roof.
- Thermal layer from under-slab or slab edge to wall to roof.
- Vapor strategy through slab, wall, roof, and interior finishes.
- Soil-gas path from under slab to vent routing.

If you cannot trace the line without lifting the pencil, the detail is incomplete.

Build a transition schedule for the hard spots:

| Transition | What Must Connect |
|---|---|
| Slab edge to wall | Thermal layer, air barrier, vapor retarder, radon seal |
| Below-grade wall to above-grade wall | Waterproofing, drainage, insulation, flashing, protection board |
| Wall to roof | Air barrier, insulation, membrane, parapet or eave flashing |
| Wall to window | Structural opening, flashing, drainage, air seal, insulation |
| Roof drain or scupper | Membrane, slope, overflow, wall penetration, outlet path |
| Service penetration | Sleeve, sealant, firestop where required, air seal, insulation repair |

**Professional**

Continuity failures are usually transition failures. Window rough openings, roof parapets, balconies, hose bibs, wall penetrations, masonry ties, slab edges, below-grade wall tops, sill transitions, and mechanical penetrations require specific details.

The goal is a controlled house. A tight house without reliable ventilation becomes an indoor air quality problem. A highly insulated wall without moisture control can become a condensation problem. A durable waterproofing product without drainage can still be overloaded.

**Code**

- ORSC N1101.4: plans and specifications must show envelope and energy details.
- ORSC N1104.8: air sealing and air barrier requirements.
- ORSC R703.1: exterior wall water resistance.
- ORSC R703.8: flashing for masonry veneer and related water management.
- Approved construction documents: controlling source for project-specific control-layer locations.

## 8.4 Concrete House Thermal Strategy

**Simple**

The best use of concrete is to keep it inside the insulated zone when possible. Then the concrete helps steady the indoor temperature.

Think of the insulation as the protective layer and the concrete as the mass that stays inside that protection.

**Applied**

For this house, the working strategy is:

- Keep insulation continuous where possible.
- Keep the main concrete mass inside the thermal boundary where possible.
- Protect below-grade concrete from water before backfill.
- Make the air barrier simple enough to inspect.
- Seal every sleeve, pipe, conduit, window, door, and service penetration.
- Test the envelope before finishes hide the work.

If a detail interrupts the insulation or air barrier, draw the repair before building it.

**Professional**

The wall concept uses a concrete or masonry structural wall with insulation placed toward the exterior side of the structural mass. The purpose is to reduce thermal bridging, keep indoor surface temperatures stable, and use the mass for thermal damping.

That strategy depends on continuity. A high R-value panel does not fix a broken edge, a wet wall, a poorly flashed window, or a metal connection that bypasses insulation. Whole-assembly performance matters more than catalog R-value.

For a double-wythe or concrete sandwich idea, the insulation decision interacts with tie spacing, drainage path, fire exposure, vapor drying, structural classification, and building official acceptance. Do not finalize the material until the wall section, tie system, flashing, and approval path are drawn together.

**Code**

- ORSC Table N1101.1(1): envelope component performance baseline.
- ORSC N1104.1: thermal performance tradeoff where an assembly does not match prescriptive assumptions.
- ORSC R316: foam plastic insulation and required protection or exceptions.
- TMS 402 and TMS 602: masonry design and construction when the wall assembly uses masonry wythes.
- Approved energy documentation: controlling source for the permitted thermal envelope.

## 8.5 Insulation Materials And Foam Protection

**Simple**

Insulation materials are not interchangeable. The same R-value can behave differently in water, fire, cold weather, sun, load, and a masonry cavity.

Pick insulation for the place it will live.

**Applied**

Before approving an insulation product, check:

- R-value at the thickness shown.
- Long-term R-value or aged R-value where applicable.
- Water exposure.
- Vapor openness or vapor resistance.
- Compressive resistance and long-term creep where loaded.
- Fire classification and required thermal or ignition barrier.
- UV protection.
- Compatibility with sealants, membranes, adhesives, and coatings.
- Tie penetrations, fasteners, washers, and support method.
- Manufacturer installation instructions and product listing.

Use this material map as a starting point:

| Material | Strong Use | Watch First |
|---|---|---|
| EPS | Below grade, slab edge, economical continuous insulation | Lower R per inch, water exposure details, protection at grade |
| XPS | Wet or below-grade locations where accepted | Vapor resistance, long-term thermal drift, environmental concerns |
| Polyiso | Above-grade exterior insulation where protected | Cold-weather R-value reduction, foil-facing vapor control, water exposure |
| High-density mineral wool board | Above-grade cavity or exterior insulation with drying and fire advantages | Support, tie plates, compression, cost |
| Cellular glass | Specialty below-grade or load-sensitive locations | Cost, rigidity, drainage, tie penetrations |
| Closed-cell spray foam | Air seal and insulation in selected protected locations | Exposed foam protection, thickness control, adhesion, fire requirements |

**Professional**

Foam plastic requires fire-protection review. The general rule is separation from occupied space by an approved thermal barrier, often 1/2 inch gypsum board, unless a specific exception applies. One key exception for this project type is foam plastic in a masonry or concrete wall, floor, or roof system where the foam is covered on each face by not less than 1 inch of masonry or concrete. That can make an insulated concrete or masonry sandwich assembly easier to approve than exposed interior foam.

Mineral wool is not foam plastic and does not carry the same R316 foam-plastic protection problem. It is vapor-open and noncombustible, but correct support is still required. In a cavity with wall ties, the support and tie details matter as much as the insulation value.

Below-grade insulation requires a wet-environment and protection plan. Soil may protect buried foam from sunlight, but the above-grade transition requires protection from UV, impact, landscaping work, pests, and damage during backfill.

**Code**

- ORSC R316: foam plastic insulation, thermal barriers, ignition barriers, and exceptions.
- ORSC N1104.2 and N1104.7: envelope insulation and slab-on-grade insulation provisions.
- ASTM C578: rigid cellular polystyrene thermal insulation.
- ASTM C1289: faced rigid cellular polyisocyanurate thermal insulation board.
- ASTM C612: mineral fiber block and board thermal insulation.
- ICC-ES Evaluation Report or product listing: approval path for proprietary insulation systems where required.
- Manufacturer installation instructions: controlling source for facer, adhesive, fastener, coating, UV exposure, and protection limits.

## 8.6 Above-Grade Wall Insulation, Drainage, And Ties

**Simple**

A wall is not just block plus insulation. The wall detail has to keep the assembly dry, hold insulation in place, handle ties and penetrations, and provide a water-exit path if water enters the outer layer.

The outer face can look like masonry or concrete, but comfort depends on the insulation and drainage details.

**Applied**

For above-grade concrete or masonry walls, confirm:

- The insulation type and thickness are listed in the drawings.
- Seams are staggered where layers are doubled.
- Gaps at corners and openings are sealed.
- The air barrier is named and inspectable.
- Wall ties match the structural drawings.
- Wall ties do not create unmodeled thermal bridges.
- The cavity can drain or dry as designed.
- The outer wythe or cladding has weeps, flashing, or another water-exit path where required.
- Interior finishes do not trap moisture against the wrong side of the wall.

Mock up one wall corner before committing to the whole house. Include a window edge, tie, insulation seam, flashing, weep, and air-seal detail.

**Professional**

The double-wythe concept requires final engineering and building-science detailing. The inner wall carries structure. The insulation layer controls heat flow. The outer wythe protects the assembly and creates the visual mass. Ties, shelves, ledgers, openings, flashing, cavity drainage, differential movement, and drying direction must be resolved.

FRP ties can reduce thermal bridging compared with steel or stainless steel ties, but they are not a prescriptive default for masonry cavity walls. A concrete sandwich connector with an ICC-ES report is not automatically approval for a masonry cavity wall. Treat FRP ties as an alternate method requiring manufacturer data, engineer approval, and building official acceptance before installation.

One issue is a high-performance wall detail that cannot be inspected. The cleaner path is to show tie spacing, insulation, air sealing, drainage, flashing, and weeps on the drawings, then photograph representative work before it closes.

**Code**

- ORSC R104.11: alternate materials, design, and methods of construction.
- ORSC R606: masonry construction where applicable.
- TMS 402: masonry design, composite and noncomposite masonry behavior, and masonry veneer where applicable.
- TMS 602: masonry construction requirements.
- ORSC R703.8: masonry veneer flashing and water management where veneer provisions apply.
- ICC-ES Evaluation Report: product-specific approval path for proprietary wall ties or connectors.

## 8.7 Below-Grade Envelope Interface

**Simple**

Below-grade water creates long-term load and leakage risk. A basement wall requires more than strength: drainage, waterproofing or dampproofing, protection, and a discharge path.

Dampproofing slows dampness. Waterproofing is for severe soil-water conditions and water pressure risk.

**Applied**

The foundation, basement, slabs, and concrete placement chapter owns the full foundation drainage and waterproofing sequence. This chapter owns the envelope continuity at the transition.

At the below-grade wall, confirm:

- Surface grade drains away from the house.
- Roof water is routed away from the foundation.
- Dampproofing or waterproofing matches the actual water condition and approved plans.
- Below-grade insulation is approved for wet exposure.
- Protection board or drainage mat protects the membrane where specified.
- The footing drain or other drainage path has a clean outlet.
- Waterproofing, insulation, air barrier, flashing, and above-grade wall protection overlap correctly at grade.
- The grade transition is protected from UV, impact, landscaping work, and insects where relevant.

Do not backfill until waterproofing is inspected, protected, photographed, and recorded.

**Professional**

Below-grade performance is a layered system. Grading reduces load. Roof drainage removes large pulses of water. Dampproofing or waterproofing protects the wall. Drainage mat relieves pressure. Perimeter drainage removes collected water. Backfill controls stored water and lateral load. Protection board keeps equipment and stones from damaging the membrane.

Hydrostatic pressure changes the standard. If water can stand against the wall or slab, the foundation and waterproofing strategy must address pressure, buoyancy, seepage, sump reliability, drain capacity, failure mode, and maintenance. Do not let a product label substitute for a water-control design.

**Code**

- ORSC R401.3: surface drainage away from foundation walls.
- ORSC R405.1: foundation drainage for concrete or masonry foundations enclosing habitable or usable spaces below grade, with code exceptions.
- ORSC R406.1: dampproofing.
- ORSC R406.2: waterproofing where high water table or other severe soil-water conditions are known to exist.
- ORSC R406.3: joints and penetrations in foundation walls.
- Manufacturer installation instructions: membrane surface preparation, primer, thickness, seams, terminations, protection board, drainage mat, and temperature limits.

## 8.8 Slab Edge, Basement Wall, And Under-Slab Continuity

**Simple**

The slab edge is small on the drawing and large in performance. It can leak heat, air, vapor, and soil gas if the details do not meet.

**Applied**

At the slab edge and below-grade transition, trace:

- Under-slab insulation to slab-edge insulation.
- Slab-edge insulation to wall insulation.
- Vapor retarder to wall or footing detail.
- Soil-gas retarder to sealed penetrations.
- Radon rough-in to vent route.
- Air barrier from slab or wall to the above-grade wall.
- Waterproofing and flashing at the grade line.

Before concrete, photograph:

- Slab-edge insulation.
- Vapor retarder laps and sealed penetrations.
- Soil-gas/radon layer.
- Sleeve seals.
- Wall-to-slab or footing transitions.
- Any repair patches.

**Professional**

The slab-edge energy requirement is not only an R-value. It is geometry. Under the 2023 ORSC table, slab-edge insulation has a minimum total horizontal or vertical below-grade distance of 24 inches. Heated slabs require insulation under the full slab in addition to perimeter insulation.

Concrete homes make slab edges more important because the wall mass and slab mass can create a continuous heat-flow path if insulation is interrupted. A clean section should show where the load transfers, where the insulation continues, where water drains, and where soil gas is blocked or vented.

**Code**

- ORSC Table N1101.1(1): slab-edge perimeter insulation and heated slab interior insulation.
- ORSC N1104.7: slab-on-grade floor insulation.
- ORSC R506.2.3: under-slab vapor retarder for concrete floors on ground.
- ASTM E1745: plastic vapor retarders used below concrete slabs.
- ORSC Appendix AF103: radon-control rough-in elements where applicable.

## 8.9 Air Barrier And Air Sealing

**Simple**

Insulation does not work well if air leaks around it. Air leaks carry heat, dust, smoke, moisture, and odors.

A tight house requires planned ventilation. Leaks are not ventilation. Leaks are uncontrolled holes.

**Applied**

Choose one primary air barrier and make it continuous. Common locations can include:

- Interior parge coat or sealed concrete/masonry surface.
- Exterior sheathing or membrane.
- Roof membrane or roof air-control layer.
- Slab and foundation connection.
- Window and door sealant system.

High-risk air leaks include:

- Top-of-wall transitions.
- Slab-to-wall joints.
- Masonry cells and cracks.
- Electrical boxes.
- Plumbing sleeves.
- Duct penetrations.
- Windows and doors.
- Attic or roof transitions.
- Mechanical penetrations.
- Service chases.

Make an air-seal checklist for the crew. Then test the work before finishes hide it.

**Professional**

Concrete and grouted masonry can be airtight, but joints, ungrouted cores, cracks, sleeves, bond beams, service penetrations, window bucks, and roof transitions can leak. Air sealing should be inspected before finishes hide it.

Use compatible sealants, gaskets, tapes, liquid flashing, mortar, parge coats, boots, or collars. Compatibility matters. Some tapes do not bond to dusty masonry. Some sealants do not tolerate wet concrete. Some foams shrink, burn, or fail under UV exposure.

**Code**

- ORSC N1104.8: air sealing and air barrier requirements.
- Oregon BCD Blower Door Results Reporting Checklist: documentation path when blower-door results are used.
- ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380: blower-door test protocol referenced by Oregon BCD reporting.
- Approved plans and energy documents: controlling source for the selected air-sealing compliance path.

## 8.10 Thermal Bridges

**Simple**

A thermal bridge is a shortcut for heat. It is the cold handle on a hot pan.

In a house, thermal bridges happen at metal pieces, concrete edges, wall ties, window frames, roof connections, and slab edges.

**Applied**

Look for thermal bridges at:

- Slab edge.
- Wall ties.
- Lintels.
- Window and door frames.
- Roof-to-wall connection.
- Balcony, canopy, or deck connections.
- Steel ledgers, brackets, plates, or anchors.
- Fasteners that cross the insulation layer.
- Foundation-to-wall transition.
- Parapet walls and roof drains.

Break the bridge where possible. Where it cannot be broken, calculate it, insulate around it, or accept it knowingly in the energy model.

**Professional**

Thermal bridges reduce whole-assembly performance and can create condensation risk at interior surfaces. They are most harmful when they connect high-conductivity material from outside to inside or when they lower an interior surface below the local dew-point condition.

A concrete house requires careful slab-edge, balcony, lintel, tie, parapet, roof edge, and opening details. Continuous insulation helps, but only if structural penetrations are limited, thermally broken, or included in the performance model.

For wall ties, stainless steel can be the durable structural default while still carrying a thermal penalty. FRP or thermally broken ties can reduce the penalty, but the approval path must come first.

**Code**

- ORSC Table N1101.1(1): standard base-case envelope performance.
- ORSC N1104.1: UA tradeoff where thermal bridges or nonstandard assemblies require whole-assembly accounting.
- ORSC R104.11: alternate method approval for nonstandard thermal-break or tie systems.
- Approved energy calculation: controlling source for modeled thermal bridges and assembly performance.

## 8.11 Windows, Doors, Openings, And Flashing

**Simple**

Windows and doors are openings in the shell. They require structure above them, support below them, flashing around them, and durable seals.

Most wall leaks start at openings.

**Applied**

For each window and exterior door, confirm:

- Opening size matches the approved plan.
- Lintel or header design is approved.
- Window or door rating matches the energy documents.
- NFRC label or certified product documentation is saved.
- NAFS or other required product rating is available where applicable.
- Sill slopes or drains outward.
- Flashing directs water out, not into the wall.
- Back dam or interior seal is handled.
- Side flashing ties into the water-control layer.
- Head flashing sheds water over the opening.
- Air seal is continuous on the interior side or designated air-barrier side.
- Insulation fills the rough-opening gap without bowing the frame.
- Emergency escape and rescue openings are provided where required.

Install one opening as a mockup and inspect it before repeating the detail everywhere.

**Professional**

Masonry openings require structure, movement tolerance, drainage, and air sealing. Lintels carry load. Flashing carries water. Sealant handles movement but should not be the only drainage strategy. Backer rod controls sealant shape. Weeps or drainage paths let water leave the cavity.

Window performance includes frame material, glazing package, spacer, U-factor, solar heat gain coefficient, air leakage, water rating, condensation resistance, durability, serviceability, and replacement path. A high-performance window installed badly becomes a water leak with expensive glass.

Oregon's energy table requires windows at U-0.27 in the standard base case. Skylights are U-0.50. Exterior doors are U-0.20, with table-note exceptions that must be checked on the adopted code table. Glass doors comply with window performance. Window SHGC is a design choice unless the selected compliance path or product specification makes it a requirement.

Emergency escape openings are not optional where required. For sleeping rooms, the opening size, sill height, operation, well, ladder, and access details must be shown with the wall and structural opening design.

**Code**

- ORSC Table N1101.1(1): window, skylight, exterior door, and glass-door energy performance.
- ORSC N1104.4.3: fenestration air leakage and product testing/listing path.
- NFRC rating: source for U-factor and other fenestration energy ratings.
- AAMA/WDMA/CSA 101/I.S.2/A440: North American Fenestration Standard product rating path.
- ORSC R310: emergency escape and rescue openings where required.
- ORSC R703.1 and R703.8: exterior water resistance and flashing.
- TMS 402, ACI 318, or approved structural drawings: masonry or concrete lintel and opening reinforcement design.

## 8.12 Roof, Parapet, And Wall Transitions

**Simple**

Roofs commonly fail at edges, corners, drains, penetrations, and transitions. A flat or low-slope roof requires especially careful drainage.

Water should have an obvious path off the roof and away from the walls.

**Applied**

Before roofing work starts, confirm:

- Roof slope and drainage path.
- Primary drains, scuppers, gutters, or downspouts.
- Overflow drainage.
- Parapet cap or wall top protection.
- Roof-to-wall flashing.
- Penetration boots and curbs.
- Solar, antenna, vent, and mechanical mounts.
- Insulation location.
- Vapor-control approach.
- Air barrier tie-in.
- Maintenance access.
- Replacement path for membrane or roof covering.

Do not rely on caulk at a roof edge. Use proper flashing, laps, membranes, counterflashing, caps, and drainage.

**Professional**

Concrete and steel roof options require different envelope details. A concrete roof slab may place insulation above the structural deck, below it, or in a hybrid assembly. A steel deck may require condensation control, acoustic control, fire detailing, and thermal-break planning. Low-slope assemblies must control drainage, vapor drive, membrane compatibility, and penetrations.

For a concrete roof deck, the prescriptive flat-ceiling R-value path does not translate directly. A concrete slab provides mass and structure, not enough insulation by itself. The design should normally use insulation above the deck or another approved assembly and demonstrate compliance through UA tradeoff or an accepted energy path.

Parapets are high-risk details because they combine structure, water, air, heat, and movement. The wall insulation, roof insulation, roof membrane, flashing, coping, and air barrier should connect in a drawn detail. Secondary overflow matters because a clogged primary drain can turn a low-slope roof into a shallow pond.

**Code**

- ORSC Table N1101.1(1): flat-ceiling standard base-case value used for energy comparison.
- ORSC N1104.1 and Table N1104.1(1): UA tradeoff path for nonstandard roof assemblies.
- ORSC R903: weather protection for roofs.
- ORSC R903.4: roof drainage and secondary drainage where required.
- ORSC R905: roof coverings by material and slope.
- ORSC R905.10: built-up roof slope and low-slope membrane context where applicable.
- ASCE 7: snow load, wind uplift, rain load, and ponding stability where engineered design applies.
- Manufacturer listing and installation instructions: controlling source for membrane, fasteners, flashing, terminations, warranty, and substrate limits.

## 8.13 Radon And Soil Gas

**Simple**

Radon is an invisible gas that comes from soil and rock. You cannot smell it or see it. You test for it.

The cheapest time to plan for radon is before the slab is poured.

**Applied**

For a concrete house, build the soil-gas plan before concrete:

- Confirm whether the site is in Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, or Yamhill County.
- Use radon-ready construction in covered counties.
- Consider radon-ready construction voluntarily outside covered counties.
- Provide a gas-permeable layer where required or chosen.
- Provide a soil-gas retarder where required or chosen.
- Seal slab joints, penetrations, sump openings, and other soil-gas entry points.
- Route passive vent piping through the building and above the roof where required or chosen.
- Leave space and a power path for a future fan if test results require activation.
- Label the radon pipe so it is not mistaken for plumbing or abandoned piping.
- Test the lowest livable area after construction.

Install the rough-in during construction. Test after occupancy. Add active mitigation if test results call for it.

**Professional**

Oregon radon applicability is county-based under the code path, not a parcel-by-parcel risk map trigger. ORSC Appendix AF applies in the seven listed counties. Additional counties can be added through the state process described in the statutory authority, not by an informal local map note.

Radon-ready construction is a low-disruption strategy: control soil-gas entry, provide a collection layer, provide a vent path, seal penetrations, and leave a practical path for adding a fan. The final design should coordinate with slab vapor control, sump design, foundation drains, mechanical ventilation, and roof penetrations.

ANSI/AARST RRNC is useful for new-construction rough-in practices. ANSI/AARST CCAH is useful for verification and activation decisions. Oregon Appendix AF is still the binding construction code layer in covered counties.

**Code**

- ORS 455.365: Oregon statutory authority for radon construction standards.
- ORSC Appendix AF101.1: covered Oregon counties for radon-control construction.
- ORSC Appendix AF103.2: gas-permeable layer.
- ORSC Appendix AF103.3: soil-gas retarder.
- ORSC Appendix AF103.4: sealing and caulking.
- ORSC Appendix AF103.6: vent pipe system.
- ORSC Appendix AF103.8: vent pipe termination and routing.
- ORSC Appendix AF103.12: power source for future fan where required.
- ANSI/AARST RRNC: radon-resistant new construction rough-in standard.
- ANSI/AARST CCAH: radon system verification, activation, and homes standard.
- EPA and Oregon Health Authority radon guidance: testing and mitigation guidance, including 4.0 pCi/L action level and consideration of mitigation between 2.0 and 4.0 pCi/L.

## 8.14 Vapor Control And Drying

**Simple**

Vapor is moisture moving through materials as gas. It is slower than a leak, but it can still damage finishes.

A wall, slab, or roof requires a drying plan. Trapping water between layers can damage an otherwise good assembly.

**Applied**

Before choosing membranes and finishes, ask:

- Which side can the assembly dry toward?
- Is the concrete still releasing construction moisture?
- Will interior finishes trap moisture?
- Is the exterior layer vapor-open or vapor-closed?
- Are there wet rooms against exterior walls?
- Does the floor finish require a low-moisture slab?
- Are coatings, sealers, membranes, and adhesives compatible?
- Will roof membrane, insulation, and deck temperature create condensation risk?
- Does the ventilation system control indoor humidity?

Do not install sensitive flooring or coatings until the slab and wall moisture conditions fit the product instructions.

**Professional**

Concrete contains construction moisture. Masonry can store rainwater. Below-grade walls have ground-moisture exposure. High indoor humidity can condense on cold surfaces. Vapor control requires climate, assembly, material, drying direction, and interior humidity context.

The wrong vapor-closed layer can trap water. The right vapor-control layer can protect the assembly. The difference depends on location, temperature profile, material permeability, rain exposure, interior humidity, and whether the assembly can dry.

One issue is a roof or wall with vapor-impermeable layers on both sides and no drying path. The cleaner path is to model or review the hygrothermal behavior before construction, especially where concrete, foam, membranes, and low-slope roofing meet.

**Code**

- ORSC R318: vapor retarder provisions where applicable.
- ORSC R506.2.3: under-slab vapor retarder.
- ASTM E1745: plastic vapor retarder material standard.
- Manufacturer installation instructions: controlling source for flooring, coatings, adhesives, membranes, sealers, and moisture limits.
- Approved energy and envelope details: controlling source for vapor-control layer locations.

## 8.15 Envelope Testing During Construction

**Simple**

Test the envelope before it is covered. A leak found early is a repair. A leak found late can require demolition.

**Applied**

Use envelope testing as a build sequence, not as a final surprise:

| Time | Useful Check | What To Save |
|---|---|---|
| After air sealing, before finishes | Diagnostic blower door | CFM50, ACH50, setup notes, leak list |
| During diagnostic blower door | Smoke pencil or theatrical smoke | Marked leak photos and repair notes |
| During heating or cooling season | Thermal camera scan | Images with visible-light reference photos |
| After rain | Flashing and drainage inspection | Photos of roof edges, openings, drains, and leaks |
| Before final approval where required | Code blower-door report | BCD form or local equivalent |
| After occupancy | Radon test | Test dates, location, result, and follow-up action |

Mark leaks on the wall or drawing. Fix them. Retest before moving on.

**Professional**

Blower-door testing gives a whole-building leakage number and supports diagnostic work. The result is commonly reported as CFM50 and ACH50:

```text
ACH50 = (CFM50 x 60) / conditioned volume
```

The Oregon BCD blower-door checklist gives two important thresholds under the 2023 ORSC path. A standard blower-door compliance result passes at less than or equal to 3.25 ACH50. If the project selects the additional measure for air leakage control and efficient ventilation, the blower-door threshold is less than or equal to 2.75 ACH50, with third-party testing and the required ventilation conditions.

Smoke helps locate leaks. Thermal imaging shows temperature anomalies that may indicate air leakage, missing insulation, wet materials, or thermal bridges. Hot-box and heat-flux testing can support research or assembly verification, but they belong mainly in the testing and commissioning chapter unless the building official or approved compliance path requires them.

One issue is waiting for the final blower-door test to learn that the air barrier failed at every opening. The cleaner path is to run a diagnostic test before interior finishes, repair leaks while they are accessible, and keep the final code test from becoming a rescue operation.

**Code**

- Oregon BCD Blower Door Results Reporting Checklist: reporting form, CO timing, 3.25 ACH50 standard threshold, and 2.75 ACH50 additional-measure threshold.
- ORSC N1104.8.2: sealing and blower-door compliance path.
- ORSC Table N1101.1(2), Additional Measure for air leakage control and efficient ventilation: lower ACH50 threshold and ventilation requirements when selected.
- ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380: standard industry protocol named by the Oregon BCD blower-door reporting form.
- NFRC, NAFS, and manufacturer labels: fenestration product verification.
- ORSC Appendix AF and ANSI/AARST CCAH: radon rough-in and post-construction verification context.

## 8.16 Indoor Air Quality Link

**Simple**

A tight house requires planned fresh air. If the shell is tight and the ventilation is poor, indoor air gets stale.

The envelope and HVAC system work together.

**Applied**

After the envelope is tight, confirm the ventilation plan handles:

- Fresh outdoor air.
- Stale-air exhaust.
- Bathroom and kitchen moisture.
- Cooking particles and odors.
- Filtered outdoor smoke when possible.
- Humidity control.
- Radon pressure effects.
- Maintenance access for filters and fans.
- Commissioning and balancing.

Do not use random leaks as the ventilation strategy.

**Professional**

Airtight construction increases the importance of mechanical ventilation, pressure balance, filtration, source control, and humidity control. Exhaust-only strategies can depressurize the building and may increase soil-gas entry or pull air through assemblies. Balanced ventilation with heat or energy recovery can improve control, but it must be commissioned.

Indoor air monitoring can help identify chronic CO2, particulate, humidity, VOC, or radon problems. Monitoring does not replace source control or ventilation design. Recurring moisture and IAQ review belongs in the documentation, maintenance, and future-upgrades material, but this envelope chapter should make the construction-phase connection clear.

**Code**

- ORSC Chapter 11: energy and envelope path.
- OMSC residential ventilation provisions: mechanical ventilation, exhaust, intake, and equipment requirements.
- ORSC Table N1101.1(2): ventilation requirements tied to selected additional measures where applicable.
- Manufacturer installation instructions: HRV, ERV, fan, damper, filter, and control requirements.

## 8.17 Acceptance Before Interior Work

**Simple**

Before closing the walls and ceilings, the envelope should be ready. Water paths, air seals, insulation, flashing, and penetrations should be checked.

Once finishes go on, the work becomes harder to see and harder to fix.

**Applied**

Before interior cover:

- Energy documents are approved.
- Air barrier is continuous.
- Insulation is complete and not crushed.
- Slab edge and wall insulation transitions are complete.
- Openings are flashed and sealed.
- Roof and wall transitions are complete.
- Below-grade waterproofing is protected where applicable.
- Penetrations are sealed.
- Foam protection plan is understood.
- Blower-door diagnostic testing is complete where useful.
- Thermal scan is complete where useful.
- Radon rough-in is complete where required or chosen.
- Product labels and installation instructions are saved.
- Photos and notes are saved in the project record.

If the envelope is not ready, do not hide it.

**Professional**

Envelope acceptance should combine visual inspection, documentation, diagnostic testing, product verification, and transition review. The most important evidence is not a single final test number. It is proof that the control layers were installed continuously before they disappeared behind finishes.

Record photos of every buried envelope detail: slab edge, below-grade wall, window flashing, roof edge, penetrations, insulation seams, air barrier transitions, soil-gas rough-in, and repair locations.

**Code**

- ORSC R109: required inspections where administered by the jurisdiction.
- ORSC Chapter 11: energy documentation and final compliance requirements.
- Oregon BCD energy forms: additional-measure selection and blower-door reporting where applicable.
- ORSC Appendix AF: radon-control inspection and rough-in details in covered counties.
- Approved permit documents: controlling source for final acceptance before cover and before occupancy.
