# Chapter 5: Soil, Ground Prep, Drainage, And Foundation Risk

Foundation soil is part of the structural system.

Concrete gets the attention because it is visible, expensive, and permanent. Soil carries the load below the surface. Firm, uniform, dry-enough, documented soil simplifies the whole project. Weak, wet, expansive, filled, or unpredictable soil can move even under a well-built foundation.

This chapter is about proving the ground before concrete arrives. A proven site has documented checks, a drainage path, removed topsoil, documented fill, and a foundation design based on evidence instead of guessing.

The detailed testing procedures belong in the testing and commissioning material. This chapter explains what the tests are trying to prove, what the results mean, and when the work has crossed the line from owner-builder screening into geotechnical design.

Even good concrete can fail on weak, wet, expansive, or undocumented ground.

## 5.1 The Ground Is Part Of The Structure

**Simple**

A house does not sit on concrete first. It sits on ground. The footing, slab, basement wall, or mat foundation only works if the soil below it can carry the load without moving too much.

Some soil sinks. Some soil swells. Some soil turns soft when wet. Some soil freezes and lifts. Some soil looks solid until earthquake shaking, groundwater, or excavation reveals the actual condition.

That makes soil part of the design.

**Applied**

Start with the ground before debating the house design.

Walk the site in wet weather if you can. Look for standing water, soft ground, cracked dry clay, leaning trees, patched driveways, cracked nearby foundations, drainage ditches, springs, old fill, buried debris, and places where the ground looks different from the rest of the parcel.

Then build a soil file:

- Save desk research maps.
- Mark the proposed building footprint.
- Photograph the site in dry and wet conditions.
- Dig test pits where safe and allowed.
- Use hand augers or small probes to compare depths.
- Run field tests where they make sense.
- Bag and label representative samples.
- Record groundwater observations.
- Keep every soil decision tied to a drawing, photo, test, report, or inspection.

One issue is waiting until excavation day to discover the site. The cleaner path is to collect site clues, maps, photos, test results, and professional input before the foundation design is fixed.

**Professional**

Foundation design starts with allowable bearing pressure, total settlement, differential settlement, lateral support, groundwater elevation, frost depth, and the expected volume change of fine-grained soils. A concrete house raises the stakes because the dead load is higher and the structure tolerates differential movement poorly.

For shallow foundations, the bearing surface must be accepted at design elevation. The record should identify whether the foundation bears on undisturbed natural soil, engineered fill, improved ground, rock, or a deep-foundation element. If the design uses a presumptive bearing value, the soil classification must support that value. If the design uses a higher or project-specific value, the geotechnical basis belongs in the record.

**Code**

- ORSC Chapter 4: foundations.
- ORSC R401.4 and Table R401.4.1: soil tests, geotechnical evaluation, and presumptive load-bearing values.
- ORSC R403.1: footings must support imposed loads and transmit them to soil within the limits of the soil's character.
- ORSC R301.1.3: engineered design path when the building or element does not conform to prescriptive provisions.
- ORS 455.440: recordation of foundation condition information where required.
- 2023 ORSC adoption status: Oregon BCD identifies the 2023 ORSC as based on the 2021 IRC, with construction provisions mandatory April 1, 2024.

## 5.2 What The Soil Evidence Must Prove

Foundation soil does not have to be perfect. It has to be identified, documented, and matched to the design.

| Soil Question | Plain Meaning | What A Bad Answer Can Do |
|---|---|---|
| Bearing | Can the soil carry the load? | Footings sink, punch, or tilt. |
| Settlement | Will it compress after construction? | Slabs crack, doors bind, walls move. |
| Uniformity | Is it similar across the footprint? | One corner moves differently than another. |
| Drainage | Where does the water go? | Basements leak, clay swells, soil softens. |
| Shrink and swell | Does clay change volume with moisture? | Foundations lift, drop, or crack. |
| Frost | Can freezing water lift the soil? | Slab edges and shallow footings heave. |
| Seismic behavior | What happens during shaking? | Loose saturated soils can lose strength. |
| Excavation behavior | Can it be dug safely? | Excavation walls cave, slump, or flood. |

**Applied**

Write the clue first. Write the decision second.

Observations:

- The test pit has gray soil and seepage at 4 feet.
- A dry clay surface has wide cracks.
- The hand auger hits random brick, wood, ash, or old pipe.
- One corner has much softer DCP readings than the others.
- A nearby foundation has diagonal cracking and patched drainage.

Decisions:

- Move the footprint.
- Remove organic or disturbed soil.
- Use engineered fill.
- Change the foundation type.
- Add lab testing.
- Hire a geotechnical engineer.
- Reject the site.

Do not jump from one clue to one grand conclusion. Collect enough evidence to see the pattern.

**Professional**

The soil investigation should answer these design questions:

| Design Question | Main Evidence | Possible Design Response |
|---|---|---|
| Bearing strength | Soil classification, test pits, DCP, pocket penetrometer, geotechnical report | Footing sizing, mat design, soil replacement, engineered foundation |
| Settlement risk | Organic content, soft clay, loose fill, compressible layers, water table | Over-excavation, preload, controlled fill, deep support |
| Excavation behavior | Soil cohesion, groundwater, depth, adjacent loads | Slope, bench, shore, shield, dewater, sequence |
| Moisture behavior | Surface flow, mottling, seepage, water table, nearby wells | Grading, perimeter drains, capillary break, waterproofing, sump |
| Volume change | Clay plasticity, slickensides, dry cracks, Atterberg limits | Moisture control, soil replacement, stiffened slab, pier-and-grade-beam |
| Seismic response | DOGAMI hazard layers, groundwater, loose sand or silt | Geotechnical analysis, densification, deep support, alternate footprint |
| Frost behavior | Frost depth, soil type, water availability | Footing depth, insulation, drainage, non-frost-susceptible fill |

The professional deliverable is a design basis: allowable bearing pressure, estimated settlement behavior, groundwater elevation used for design, expansive-soil classification, fill acceptance criteria, frost-protection assumption, and any limits on excavation or backfill.

## 5.3 Soil Types And Field Clues

**Simple**

No geology degree is required to start recognizing soil conditions. The first step is knowing the main families.

- Gravel drains well and can support loads when compacted.
- Sand drains well but may cave when excavated.
- Silt feels smooth or floury and can be bad with water and frost.
- Clay sticks together and holds shape, but some clay swells badly.
- Organic soil is not building soil.
- Old fill is suspicious until proven.

Topsoil grows things. It does not hold the house.

**Applied**

Use your hands, eyes, shovel, and camera before you trust a number.

| Field Clue | Likely Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Dark soil with roots | Topsoil or organic layer | Strip it and keep it out of structural fill. |
| Gritty grains visible | Sand or sandy soil | Check density, drainage, and excavation stability. |
| Sticky soil that ribbons | Clay | Check plasticity, drainage, and shrink-swell risk. |
| Smooth flour-like feel | Silt | Check frost susceptibility and water behavior. |
| Gray or blue-gray color | Wet or poorly drained condition | Investigate water table and seasonal saturation. |
| Red or orange mottling | Seasonal wetting and drying | Treat wet-season water as real. |
| Buried concrete, wood, trash, pipe, ash, or random layers | Old fill or disturbed soil | Investigate extent, remove it, or engineer around it. |
| Slick shiny clay faces | Possible expansive clay or old movement surfaces | Sample and test before design confidence. |
| Strong organic or rotten smell | Organic soil or anaerobic wet soil | Do not bear on it. |

Owner-builder field checks that belong in the soil file:

- Visual soil description.
- Ribbon test for clay behavior.
- Jar test for rough sand, silt, and clay proportions.
- Dry strength and toughness checks.
- Test pit profile.
- Hand auger profile.
- Pocket penetrometer readings on cohesive soil.
- DCP profiles for relative strength with depth.

**Professional**

Soil classification should separate what the material is from how it behaves. A clay can have adequate short-term bearing strength and still be a long-term volume-change problem. A sand can drain well and still be a cave-in or liquefaction concern. Gravel can be excellent natural support or terrible uncontrolled fill.

Record each layer by depth, color, texture, moisture, consistency, organics, inclusions, groundwater, and contact with the layer above and below. Sharp changes matter. A natural transition is different from a fill horizon full of debris.

The surface layer does not control the design decision. The bearing layer is the material at foundation elevation after stripping, excavation, replacement, and compaction.

**Code**

ORSC Table R401.4.1 gives presumptive load-bearing values for broad soil and rock categories. These are code categories, not permission to ignore actual site behavior.

| Soil Or Rock Category | Presumptive Bearing Value |
|---|---:|
| Crystalline bedrock | 12,000 psf |
| Sedimentary or foliated rock | 4,000 psf |
| Sandy gravel and gravel | 3,000 psf |
| Sand, silty sand, clayey sand, silty gravel, clayey gravel | 2,000 psf |
| Clay, sandy clay, silty clay, clayey silt, silt, and sandy silt | 1,500 psf |

- ORSC R401.4 and Table R401.4.1: geotechnical evaluation and presumptive load-bearing values.
- ORSC R403.1: footing size and support depend on the soil load-bearing value.
- ASTM D2487: Unified Soil Classification System laboratory classification.
- ASTM D2488: visual-manual soil description and identification.
- Expansive clay, engineered fill, slopes, groundwater, undocumented fill, seismic hazards, and unusual loads can move the project out of simple presumptive design.

## 5.4 Desktop Research Before The Site Visit

Maps do not approve a foundation. They keep you from walking blind onto bad ground.

**Applied**

Before committing serious money, check:

1. NRCS Web Soil Survey for soil series, drainage class, map unit descriptions, and engineering interpretations.
2. DOGAMI HazVu for mapped Oregon geologic hazards.
3. DOGAMI SLIDO for mapped landslides and landslide susceptibility.
4. Local GIS for contours, floodplain layers, wetlands, utilities, old roads, and tax-lot history.
5. FEMA or local floodplain sources used by the jurisdiction.
6. Oregon Water Resources well logs near the site.
7. Oregon Design Criteria Hub for frost line depth, seismic design category, snow, wind, weathering, and related site criteria.
8. County septic records where wastewater disposal affects the buildable area.

Save the maps, screenshots, exported reports, dates, and parcel identifiers. A soil map without the parcel boundary and date is incomplete.

**Professional**

Desktop research should produce a preliminary geologic and geotechnical model, not a final design. Start with soil map units, parent material, landform position, slope gradient, drainage class, hydrologic soil group, depth to restrictive layer, reported depth to seasonal water table, hydric rating, mapped landslide susceptibility, liquefaction susceptibility, flood zone, and local design criteria.

The useful professional output is a desktop risk plan: footprint over contours, suspected cut and fill areas, drainage paths, slope breaks, possible groundwater sources, nearby well-log depths, mapped hazards, floodplain limits, and planned test locations. Mark uncertainty directly on the plan. "Unknown fill boundary" is better than a clean drawing that hides doubt.

A full basement on a sloping wet site requires a different investigation scope than a small slab-on-grade on uniform high ground. The investigation density should follow risk: more test points at slope breaks, old fill, drainage transitions, mapped hazards, and any area where the proposed bearing elevation crosses a different soil unit.

**Code**

- Oregon BCD Residential Structures Code Program: adopted 2023 ORSC and Oregon Design Criteria Hub.
- Oregon Design Criteria Hub: site-specific frost line depth, seismic design category, wind speed, snow load, weathering, and related criteria used with ORSC and OSSC work.
- NRCS Web Soil Survey: soil series, drainage class, hydric soil indicators, and engineering interpretations.
- DOGAMI HazVu: Oregon geologic hazard screening.
- DOGAMI SLIDO: Oregon landslide inventory and susceptibility data.
- FEMA Flood Map Service Center or local floodplain administrator: mapped floodplain information.
- Oregon Water Resources Department well logs: nearby subsurface clues.

These are screening sources. They do not approve the foundation.

## 5.5 Field Investigation Workflow

**Applied**

Use the same discipline every time. Do not test only the pretty corner.

### Phase A: Walk The Site

- Visit during wet weather if possible.
- Walk high ground, low ground, drainage paths, and the planned driveway.
- Look at nearby houses, retaining walls, driveways, and outbuildings.
- Photograph cracks, seepage, low spots, fill areas, and slope movement clues.
- Talk to neighbors about water, basement leakage, springs, past earthwork, and seasonal access.

### Phase B: Mark The Footprint

- Stake the proposed building corners.
- Mark the rough foundation perimeter.
- Mark the driveway, utility trench routes, septic area, well area, and drainage outlets if known.
- Test where the house will sit, not where digging is easy.

### Phase C: Strip Topsoil At Test Locations

- Remove the organic layer at each test point.
- Keep topsoil separate from structural fill.
- Start serious soil identification on subsoil, not surface duff.

### Phase D: Run Shallow Field Checks

- Identify soil by look and feel.
- Run ribbon tests where clay is suspected.
- Run jar tests on representative samples.
- Use a pocket penetrometer where cohesive soil makes it meaningful.
- Bag and label samples by location and depth.

### Phase E: Run Deeper Checks

- Use DCP tests at multiple locations in the building footprint.
- Use hand auger samples between test points.
- Dig test pits where results are unclear or deeper foundation work is planned.
- Observe groundwater immediately and again later if water table is a concern.

### Phase F: Make The Design Decision

- Compare field results with the foundation design assumptions.
- If the soil is ordinary and uniform, continue with documented verification.
- If the soil is variable, wet, expansive, soft, filled, or unstable, get professional help before final foundation design.

### Phase G: Verify During Excavation

- Watch every layer as it comes out.
- Stop when the soil is not what the design expected.
- Verify prepared subgrade and engineered fill before concrete.

**Professional**

The investigation should generate a soil profile, not a pile of notes. Tie each test point to the proposed footprint. Record elevation where possible, sample depth, groundwater elevation, soil description, DCP penetration profile, pocket penetrometer readings where valid, and any lab sample ID.

Increase test density when the site has slope breaks, mapped hazards, old fill, variable DCP profiles, perched water, planned basement walls, or foundation loads that exceed ordinary prescriptive assumptions.

## 5.6 Owner-Builder Soil Kit And Record Package

The tools do not make the decision for you. They make the ground visible.

**Applied**

A practical owner-builder soil kit includes:

| Tool | Use |
|---|---|
| Shovel and digging bar | Strip topsoil, expose shallow layers, check roots and fill. |
| Hand auger | Sample soil below shovel depth without opening a large pit. |
| DCP | Compare resistance with depth across the footprint. |
| Pocket penetrometer | Check cohesive soil strength where the soil is suitable for the tool. |
| Clear jars | Run rough sediment tests and compare samples. |
| Sample bags | Save representative soil by location and depth. |
| Tape measure | Record layer depths, pit dimensions, and water levels. |
| Stakes and flags | Mark test locations, footprint corners, drainage paths, and hazards. |
| Camera or phone | Photograph every layer before it is disturbed or covered. |
| Field notebook | Tie photos, samples, test points, dates, and decisions together. |

The record matters as much as the test. A loose pile of photos is not a soil investigation. Each photo should connect to a location, depth, date, and observation.

At minimum, record:

- Test location.
- Date and recent weather.
- Approximate elevation or reference point if known.
- Depth of topsoil.
- Soil layers by depth.
- Moisture condition.
- Water seepage or standing water.
- Roots, organics, debris, or fill.
- Ribbon result for clay.
- Pocket penetrometer readings where valid.
- DCP profile where used.
- Decision made from the evidence.

Do not display a form in the book. Build the project record in the binder.

**Professional**

The soil record should let a later reader reconstruct the field condition. A geotechnical engineer, building official, inspector, future buyer, or future version of you should be able to see where the test happened, what material was found, what was accepted, and what was corrected.

This is especially important for a long-life concrete house. Concrete hides ground decisions. The record keeps those decisions visible.

## 5.7 Test Pits, Augers, And Samples

**Simple**

A DCP gives resistance. A test pit gives understanding. An auger gives a narrow look at depth. Samples let you compare the soil later.

Use them together.

**Applied**

Test pits are useful because you can see the profile:

- Topsoil thickness.
- Layer changes.
- Roots.
- Organics.
- Old fill.
- Buried debris.
- Cobbles and boulders.
- Seepage.
- Mottling.
- Soft pockets.
- Smells that suggest contamination or anaerobic wet soil.

Do not climb into an unsafe pit. Keep people away from unstable edges. Call Oregon 811 before digging. Keep equipment, spoils, and trucks back from excavation edges.

Hand augers are useful between pits. They are slower, cleaner, and less disruptive. They help compare layers across the footprint without opening a large hole every time.

Label samples like this:

- Site name or parcel.
- Test point.
- Depth.
- Date.
- Soil description.
- Moisture condition.
- Person who collected it.
- Photos tied to the same location.

Do not put unlabeled soil in a bag and expect it to help later.

**Professional**

The test pit is the field log you can see. Record station or offset from the footprint, surface elevation or benchmark reference, pit depth, layer thickness, contact type, color, moisture, USCS visual-manual description, consistency or relative density, organics, debris, roots, mottling, slickensides, groundwater seepage elevation, and sample interval.

DCP and auger data become much more valuable when tied to visible layers in a pit. A DCP refusal might be bedrock, a cobble, a buried slab, or a dense gravel lens. A pit or auger sample tells which one is likely. A hand auger can extend the profile between pits, but it will not show lateral continuity, fill seams, root mats, boulders, or open voids the way an exposed face can.

If groundwater is a design question, a pit observed for five minutes is not enough. Water can seep in slowly. Leave a safe observation point open only when it can be protected, barricaded, and managed without creating a hazard.

**Code**

- ORS 757.541 through ORS 757.562: Oregon utility-notification law.
- Oregon 811: one-call utility notification before excavation.
- OSHA 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P: excavation safety.
- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.651: excavation requirements, including protection of underground installations and safe access.
- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.652: protective systems for excavations.

If the investigation exposes contamination, abandoned utilities, tanks, unstable ground, or unexpected buried structures, stop and bring in the right authority or professional.

## 5.8 Screening Tests And What They Can Actually Prove

Field tests are screening tools. They do not approve a foundation by themselves.

| Tool Or Test | What It Helps Prove | Good Use | Do Not Use It For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visual walk-through | Water, slope, vegetation, distress clues | First risk screen | Final foundation approval |
| Ribbon test | Clay content and plasticity warning | Deciding whether clay needs lab attention | Exact Plasticity Index |
| Jar test | Rough sand, silt, and clay proportions | Comparing samples and documenting layers | Bearing capacity |
| Hand auger | Soil profile at depth | Checking between pits and DCP locations | Seeing broad excavation conditions |
| Test pit | Layers, water, fill, roots, debris | Understanding the real profile | Safe entry without excavation precautions |
| Pocket penetrometer | Strength of cohesive soil | Quick clay strength check | Sands, gravels, or mixed coarse soils |
| DCP | Relative strength with depth | Comparing locations and detecting layers | Stand-alone bearing design |
| Atterberg limits | Lab plasticity of fine-grained soil | Expansive-clay decisions | Field-only guessing |
| Proctor test | Density and moisture target for fill | Fill specification and compaction control | Native soil approval by itself |

**Professional**

Field tests should answer a decision question:

- Is this soil ordinary enough for prescriptive design?
- Is there a weak layer under a stiff crust?
- Is one corner different from the others?
- Is clay plastic enough to require lab testing?
- Is fill controlled or random?
- Is groundwater part of the foundation design?
- Do the results point to a different footprint or foundation type?

Professional screening should separate index testing from design testing. DCP gives penetration resistance with depth. Pocket penetrometer gives an unconfined compressive strength estimate for cohesive soil only. Atterberg limits give Liquid Limit, Plastic Limit, and Plasticity Index. Proctor testing gives maximum dry density and optimum moisture content for a specific fill material.

Those values do not become a foundation design by themselves. They define whether presumptive design still makes sense or whether the project needs a geotechnical report.

**Code**

- ASTM D2487: laboratory soil classification using USCS.
- ASTM D2488: visual-manual soil description.
- ASTM D4318: Atterberg limits.
- ASTM D6951/D6951M: Dynamic Cone Penetrometer test method.
- ASTM D698: Standard Proctor compaction.
- ASTM D1557: Modified Proctor compaction.
- ORSC R401.4: soil evaluation path for foundation support.
- ORS 455.440: recordation of foundation conditions where required.

## 5.9 DCP Results Without Overclaiming

**Simple**

A Dynamic Cone Penetrometer, or DCP, is a steel rod with a cone at the bottom and a sliding hammer at the top. You drop the hammer and record how far the cone moves into the ground.

Firm soil resists the cone. Soft soil lets it move faster. A DCP is a flashlight, not a stamp.

**Applied**

Use DCP readings to find patterns:

- Low penetration per blow usually means firmer material.
- High penetration per blow usually means softer material.
- A sudden weak layer matters.
- A stiff crust over soft material matters.
- Matching profiles across the footprint are better than one strong location.
- Weak readings under one corner need more investigation.
- DCP results on fill are useful only when tied to the specific material and compaction target.

Practical screening bands for natural ground:

| DCP Pattern | Working Interpretation | Next Action |
|---|---|---|
| Consistently low penetration, matching soil type, no water concern | Likely competent for ordinary residential screening | Confirm with soil classification and approved design path. |
| Mixed readings, layer changes, or one weak zone | Site is variable | Add pits, augers, lab tests, or geotechnical review. |
| Very high penetration, soft layers, wet loose soil, or poor results near bearing depth | Do not trust prescriptive assumptions | Stop and get geotechnical input before foundation design. |

Do not publish or use a DCP-to-bearing-pressure conversion table for this project. The research file is clear: DCP is mainly a pavement-rooted tool. It is useful for layer detection, relative comparison, and calibrated compaction quality control. It should not produce the allowable bearing pressure that goes into footing sizing.

**Professional**

DCP penetration index is usually reported in millimeters per blow. DCP-to-CBR correlation is a pavement tool. CBR-to-shallow-foundation-bearing conversion is a second empirical step. Stacking those correlations creates a number that looks precise but is not a residential footing design method.

Use DCP data with soil classification, test pits, groundwater observations, lab tests when needed, and the structural design. It can show:

- Layer boundaries.
- Relative density changes.
- Soft pockets.
- Differences between cut and fill areas.
- Differences between house footprint and utility trench routes.
- Whether compacted fill is behaving consistently after calibration.

The useful professional output is a penetration profile: depth versus penetration index, plotted at multiple locations and compared against soil type, moisture, groundwater, and layer changes. The red flags are high penetration rate near bearing elevation, abrupt slope changes in the profile, refusal that looks like a cobble instead of rock, and inconsistent profiles across the footprint.

**Code**

- ASTM D6951/D6951M: Dynamic Cone Penetrometer test method.
- ASTM D2487 and ASTM D2488: soil classification support for interpreting DCP results.
- ORSC Table R401.4.1: presumptive soil load-bearing values.
- ORSC R403.1: footing design depends on soil support, not a DCP number by itself.

DCP is supporting evidence unless the engineer of record and building official accept a project-specific interpretation.

## 5.10 Pocket Penetrometer, Ribbon Test, And Atterberg Limits

**Simple**

Clay is tricky because it can be strong one day and still move badly over seasons. The goal is not just to know whether clay is hard. The goal is to know whether it changes size when moisture changes.

**Applied**

Use the ribbon test as a warning sign:

| Ribbon Result | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Less than 1 inch | Low clay or low plasticity | Continue screening and verify drainage. |
| 1 to 2 inches | Moderate clay | Consider Atterberg limits if the foundation is critical or other warning signs exist. |
| More than 2 inches | High clay content | Get Atterberg limits before treating the foundation as ordinary. |

Use a pocket penetrometer only on cohesive soil:

- Soil should ribbon or hold shape.
- The reading should be taken on a clean cohesive face or sample.
- Do not use it on sand, gravel, cobbles, mixed debris, dry crust, or partially saturated coarse material.
- Take several readings and record the range.

A practical cohesive-soil field rule from the research file:

| Pocket Penetrometer Reading | Approximate Field Meaning For Cohesive Soil |
|---|---|
| Less than 0.5 tsf | Soft clay. Stop and investigate. |
| 0.5 to 1.0 tsf | Stiff clay range. May still be below ordinary bearing assumptions. |
| 1.0 to 2.0 tsf | Very stiff clay range. Often compatible with ordinary residential support if plasticity and water are controlled. |
| More than 2.0 tsf | Hard clay range. Do not ignore plasticity or code limits. |

For cohesive soils only, the common field estimate is:

```text
allowable bearing in psf is about 1500 x pocket penetrometer reading in tsf
```

That estimate is not for sand, gravel, fill with debris, or any soil where the tool is reading grain interference instead of cohesive strength.

**Professional**

Atterberg limits provide the clean answer for plasticity. The Plasticity Index, or PI, is the Liquid Limit minus the Plastic Limit. PI is one of the main ways to determine whether clay is merely clay or an expansive foundation problem.

Practical project screening thresholds:

| PI Range | Working Meaning | Project Response |
|---|---|---|
| PI below 15 | Low swell concern for design screening | Prescriptive design may remain possible if other conditions are ordinary. |
| PI 15 to 20 | Screening concern | Treat moisture control as part of foundation design and consider engineering input. |
| PI 20 to 25 | Serious concern | Mitigation discussion belongs in the design. Engineered foundation likely. |
| PI 25 to 35 | High concern | Engineered foundation required for this project standard. |
| PI above 35 | Active mitigation territory | Use full engineered approach, deep support, soil replacement, stabilization, or other site-specific strategy. |

For a long-life concrete home, PI 15 is the discussion trigger. PI 20 is the point where guessing should stop.

**Code**

- ASTM D4318: Atterberg limits, including Liquid Limit, Plastic Limit, and Plasticity Index.
- ASTM D2487 and ASTM D2488: soil classification and visual-manual identification.
- ASTM D4829: Expansion Index testing.
- ORSC R401.4.2: compressible or shifting soils must be removed or stabilized as required for stable foundation support.
- ACI 332 Chapter 12: residential concrete provisions for expansive soils when used by the design professional.

## 5.11 Soil Screening Matrix

Soil screening should end in action. If a test does not change the next step, it is only trivia.

| Evidence | Finding | Meaning | Next Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ribbon test | Less than 1 inch | Low clay signal | Continue with normal screening and drainage checks. |
| Ribbon test | 1 to 2 inches | Moderate clay signal | Use pocket penetrometer where valid and consider Atterberg limits if other warning signs exist. |
| Ribbon test | More than 2 inches | High clay signal | Get Atterberg limits before treating the foundation as ordinary. |
| Pocket penetrometer on cohesive soil | Less than 0.5 tsf | Soft cohesive soil | Stop. Investigate, remove, redesign, or engineer. |
| Pocket penetrometer on cohesive soil | 0.5 to 1.0 tsf | Stiff but possibly low for foundation assumptions | Add evidence and do not rely on it alone. |
| Pocket penetrometer on cohesive soil | 1.0 to 2.0 tsf | Very stiff cohesive soil | May support ordinary residential assumptions if plasticity, water, and uniformity are controlled. |
| Pocket penetrometer on cohesive soil | More than 2.0 tsf | Hard cohesive soil | Still verify plasticity, water, and code limits. |
| DCP profile | Consistent low penetration across footprint | Relative evidence of competent material | Confirm with soil classification and approved design assumptions. |
| DCP profile | Sudden weak layer | Hidden soft layer or transition | Add pits, augers, or professional review before design confidence. |
| DCP profile | One weak corner | Footprint variability | Increase testing and investigate why that corner differs. |
| DCP profile | Very high penetration near bearing depth | Loose or weak material | Do not use ordinary assumptions without geotechnical input. |
| Test pit | Organic soil, roots, debris, ash, or random layers | Topsoil, old fill, or disturbed ground | Remove, map extent, replace correctly, or engineer. |
| Test pit | Seepage or stable water level | Groundwater or perched water | Treat water as a design condition. |
| Test pit or auger | Loose dry silt, ash-like soil, or sudden collapse when wetted | Possible collapsible soil | Investigate wetting behavior and compaction strategy. |
| Test pit or site walk | Silt plus poor drainage near frost depth | Frost-susceptible condition | Drain, replace, insulate, or use accepted frost protection. |
| DOGAMI or field pattern | Loose saturated sand or silt in seismic area | Liquefaction concern | Get geotechnical input before relying on shallow support. |
| Field results across footprint | Different soil layers or strength trends | Variable bearing and settlement risk | Increase testing and design for variation. |
| Slope walk | Scarps, leaning trees, hummocky ground, seepage, or tension cracks | Slope-instability concern | Avoid the area or hire geotechnical help before design. |
| Wet-season visit | Water in pits, gray soil, seepage, or ponding | High water table or perched water | Raise, drain, redesign, or reject the site. |
| Pit, auger, or refusal pattern | Cavities, abrupt loss, abrupt refusal, soluble rock, or lava-tube clues | Void or karst-like concern | Get specialty geotechnical review. |
| Atterberg limits | PI below 15 | Low swell concern | Prescriptive path may remain possible if other conditions are ordinary. |
| Atterberg limits | PI 15 to 20 | Plasticity concern | Add moisture-control strategy and consider engineering input. |
| Atterberg limits | PI 20 or higher | Expansive-clay design concern | Use engineered foundation review or accepted mitigation. |
| Proctor test | Fill target established | Fill can be specified and tested | Compact in lifts and verify against the specified target. |
| No Proctor or fill spec | "Compacted" is undefined | No reference density exists | Do not treat the fill as engineered. |

**Applied**

Use the matrix at the table, not in your head:

1. Mark every test result on the site plan.
2. Circle every weak, wet, filled, or clay-heavy location.
3. Draw the likely boundary of each problem area.
4. Add more pits or auger holes at the edges of the problem.
5. Write one action beside each problem: remove, drain, test, engineer, move, or reject.
6. Do not finalize the foundation layout until every circled area has an action.

Soil screening ends with a soil class and a risk level. Foundation design begins with that soil class and risk level. Do not merge those decisions into one guess.

**Code**

- ORSC R401.4 and Table R401.4.1: soil evaluation and presumptive bearing values.
- ORSC R301.1.3: engineered design path when prescriptive provisions are exceeded.
- ASTM D4318, ASTM D6951, ASTM D698, and ASTM D1557: testing standards that support the screening matrix.

The matrix is a screening tool. It does not create a code-approved foundation design.

## 5.12 Drainage Comes Before Foundation Confidence

**Simple**

Water changes soil performance over time.

Water makes soft soil softer. Water makes some clay swell. Water feeds frost heave. Water pushes against basement walls. Water turns a small grading mistake into a permanent maintenance problem.

Before designing the foundation, decide where the water goes.

**Applied**

Do this before the foundation plan hardens:

1. Walk the site in rain and mark every place water flows or stands.
2. Mark uphill water that points toward the building area.
3. Sketch swales, ditches, driveway runoff, roof discharge points, and any daylight drain outlet.
4. Move the house if the best building pad sits in a bowl.
5. Grade finished soil away from the foundation.
6. Pipe roof water away from the house before backfill.
7. Install footing drains and cleanouts where the design requires them.
8. Put a capillary break and vapor retarder below slabs.
9. Use a sump only when gravity drainage cannot do the job.

Roof water is not small. A 3,000 square foot roof receiving 1 inch of rain sheds about 1,870 gallons of water. If that water lands beside the foundation, the foundation becomes part of the drainage system. It should not be.

**Professional**

Drainage design starts with the site water budget, not the footing drain. Roof runoff, overland flow, perched groundwater, capillary rise, and seasonal groundwater are separate loads. A perimeter drain handles one part of the problem. It does not fix bad surface grading, a perched water source above the wall, or a foundation elevation below the seasonal groundwater table.

For a basement, the system usually includes site grading, roof water control, wall waterproofing, drainage mat or drainage board, free-draining backfill or drainage gravel, filter fabric, footing-level drain, cleanout access, and a reliable discharge point. A daylight outlet should be protected from crushing, erosion, freezing, roots, rodents, and blockage. A sump should be treated as permanent mechanical equipment with backup power, alarm, access, and maintenance.

For slab-on-grade, the moisture strategy is compacted subgrade, capillary break aggregate, vapor retarder continuity, sealed penetrations, edge drainage, and no standing water beside or under the slab. For below-grade walls, drainage mat, free-draining backfill, filter fabric, footing drain slope, cleanouts, and discharge capacity are part of the system.

**Code**

- ORSC R401.3: surface drainage at foundations.
- ORSC R405.1: foundation drainage for concrete or masonry foundations enclosing habitable or usable spaces below grade, with Group I soil exception.
- ORSC R406.1: dampproofing for below-grade foundation walls where waterproofing is not triggered.
- ORSC R406.2: waterproofing where high water table or severe soil-water conditions are known to exist.
- ORSC R506.2.3: under-slab vapor retarder for concrete floors on ground.
- ASTM E1745: plastic vapor retarder material performance.

## 5.13 Basement, Slab-On-Grade, Or Another Foundation Path

**Simple**

A basement is useful, but it adds serious site requirements. You are digging a big hole, building walls below grade, keeping water out for decades, and relying on soil support on all sides.

A slab-on-grade is simpler, but it still requires clean support, moisture control, frost protection, and good detailing.

Site conditions may determine the foundation before preference does.

**Applied**

A site suited to a basement usually has:

- Firm soil at basement footing depth.
- No high groundwater near the basement floor elevation.
- Excavation walls that can be made safe.
- A practical drainage outlet.
- No major expansive clay.
- No active slope problem.
- Enough access for excavation, concrete, waterproofing, and backfill.

A poor basement site may have:

- High water table.
- Bedrock or boulders at shallow depth.
- Soft organic soil.
- Major expansive clay.
- No way to drain to daylight.
- Mapped or visible landslide risk.
- Loose saturated sand or silt.
- Tight access that makes safe excavation unrealistic.

When a basement requires too much mitigation, consider slab-on-grade, conditioned crawl space, pier-and-grade-beam, mat foundation, or another engineered strategy.

**Professional**

Basement design combines vertical bearing, active and at-rest lateral earth pressure, surcharge loads, hydrostatic head, wall fixity, footing restraint, drainage capacity, waterproofing continuity, and backfill compaction. Saturated backfill adds water pressure to earth pressure. Expansive clay can add lateral swell pressure and slab heave. Loose granular soil can require shoring, shielding, or wide cut slopes during construction.

Slab-on-grade shifts the risk toward modulus of subgrade reaction, differential support, capillary break continuity, vapor transmission, edge frost behavior, and shrinkage crack control. It often reduces risk on wet or difficult sites, but only if the building pad is prepared as a system.

**Code**

- ORSC R403.1: footing support and load transfer to soil.
- ORSC Table R403.1: minimum footing widths by construction type and soil bearing value.
- ORSC R403.1.4: minimum footing depth and frost protection.
- ORSC R403.3: frost-protected shallow foundations for heated buildings.
- ASCE 32-01: frost-protected shallow foundation design outside the basic prescriptive path.
- ORSC R405: foundation drainage.
- ORSC R406: dampproofing and waterproofing.
- ORSC R506: concrete floors on ground.

## 5.14 Excavation Confirms The Ground

**Simple**

Excavation is an inspection point. It shows whether the actual ground matches the investigation.

If the excavation shows something different from the investigation, stop. Do not cover the condition with forms and concrete.

**Applied**

Stop and reassess if you see:

- Water rising in the bottom of the excavation.
- Soil slumping, cracking, or falling into the cut.
- Soil much softer than expected.
- Buried wood, trash, concrete, pipe, tanks, ash, or debris.
- Fuel, chemical, sulfur, or rotting smells.
- Deep black organic soil below the surface layer.
- Blue-gray saturated soil.
- A sudden soft layer under one corner.
- Bedrock or boulders where the design expected soil.
- Utility lines not shown on the plan.
- Old trenches crossing bearing areas.

Before digging, contact Oregon 811 and wait for utilities to be marked. Do not treat a neighbor's recollection or a missing map as proof that the ground is clear.

**Professional**

Excavation safety is separate from bearing strength. A soil can have adequate allowable bearing pressure and still be Type C for excavation protection. Soil that stands vertically in the morning can fail after rain, vibration, surcharge loading, seepage, or equipment movement near the edge.

Keep spoils, pallets, trucks, and equipment back from excavation edges. Provide safe access where workers enter. Use sloping, benching, shoring, or shielding when required. Assign a competent person where OSHA excavation rules apply.

**Code**

- OSHA 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P: excavation safety.
- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.650: excavation definitions.
- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.651: excavation requirements.
- OSHA 29 CFR 1926.652: protective systems.
- ORS 757.541 through ORS 757.562: Oregon utility notification.
- Oregon 811: utility locate process before digging.

## 5.15 Compaction And Engineered Fill

**Simple**

Compaction is not smashing dirt flat.

Good compaction means the right material, placed in thin lifts, at the right moisture, with the right equipment, and checked before the next lift covers it.

Bad fill can look fine from above and still settle later.

**Applied**

Use this fill discipline:

- Remove topsoil, roots, stumps, organics, debris, and soft pockets.
- Keep topsoil out of structural fill.
- Use approved fill material from a known source.
- Place fill in lifts thin enough for the equipment to compact.
- Keep moisture near the target for the material.
- Compact each lift before adding the next.
- Test before hiding the lift.
- Stop if the fill pumps, ruts, smears, or moves under the compactor.
- Record material source, lift thickness, moisture condition, equipment, date, weather, and test results.

Imported granular material from a known supplier is usually easier to control than random site soil.

**Professional**

Engineered fill is a constructed material. The specification should identify the fill material, gradation or source, lift thickness, compaction target, moisture range, testing method, and acceptance criteria. Where a Proctor test is used, the Proctor result defines maximum dry density and optimum moisture content.

There is no universal DCP-to-percent-Proctor table. A DCP value that works for one fill material does not automatically prove another material. DCP can support compaction quality control only when it is calibrated to the material, target density, and moisture condition.

The owner-builder cannot self-certify engineered fill. For structural fill, the clean path is a one-day geotechnical consultation before fill placement, a written fill specification, field density or calibrated acceptance testing, and a final acceptance record.

**Code**

- ORSC R401.4: soil evaluation and foundation support.
- ORSC R401.4.2: compressible or shifting soils.
- ORSC R403.1: footing support on acceptable soil or engineered fill.
- ORS 455.440: recordation of foundation condition information where required.
- ASTM D698: Standard Proctor maximum dry density and optimum moisture.
- ASTM D1557: Modified Proctor maximum dry density and optimum moisture.
- ASTM D6938: nuclear density gauge field density testing.

Imported rock does not become engineered fill because a truck delivered it. It becomes engineered fill when material, placement, compaction, and verification match the approved requirements.

## 5.16 Soil Problems And Practical Responses

**Simple**

The screening matrix tells you what the clues mean. This section tells you how soil problems are usually fixed.

Most soil fixes follow a few patterns. You avoid the problem, remove the problem, drain the problem, compact the problem, bridge over it, or bypass it with a deeper foundation.

**Applied**

The easiest soil fix is often moving the house footprint. The second easiest is removing a shallow bad pocket and replacing it correctly. The hardest fix is forcing a basement into wet, unstable, or expansive ground because the drawing looked good.

Use this hierarchy:

1. Avoid the bad area.
2. Move the footprint.
3. Remove localized bad material.
4. Improve drainage.
5. Use controlled fill.
6. Change the foundation type.
7. Use engineered ground improvement.
8. Reject the site.

If the best fix is uneconomic, change the footprint, change the foundation, or choose a different site.

**Professional**

Each soil problem has a failure mechanism. The response must match that mechanism.

| Problem | Mechanism | Professional Response |
|---|---|---|
| Weak bearing | Applied footing pressure exceeds acceptable bearing for the soil condition. | Reduce bearing pressure, increase footing area, improve or replace soil, use a mat, or transfer load deeper. |
| Excess settlement | Immediate settlement, primary consolidation, or secondary compression exceeds movement tolerance. | Remove compressible material, preload, wait for consolidation, bridge variable areas, or bypass the layer. |
| Differential settlement | Support stiffness varies across the footprint. | Increase investigation density, stiffen the foundation, improve weak zones, or use deep support. |
| Expansive clay | Moisture change in the active zone drives shrink-swell movement. | Control moisture, remove active clay where practical, use stiffened slab, pier-and-grade-beam, deep piers, or other engineered response. |
| Collapsible soil | Loose metastable soil loses structure when wetted under load. | Wet-collapse testing, moisture conditioning, compaction, removal, or foundation redesign. |
| Frost heave | Frost-susceptible soil plus water plus freezing forms ice lenses. | Drain water, replace fines, place footings below frost depth, or use accepted frost-protected shallow foundation design. |
| Liquefaction | Cyclic shaking raises pore pressure and reduces effective stress in loose saturated soil. | Check triggering and settlement, densify, drain, bypass, or move the footprint. |
| Slope instability | Driving forces exceed resisting shear strength along a slope or slide plane. | Analyze factor of safety, control groundwater, set back, buttress, reinforce, or avoid. |
| High groundwater | Hydrostatic head, uplift, seepage, and reduced effective stress change the load case. | Raise the building, design drainage and waterproofing, check uplift, or avoid below-grade space. |
| Voids or lava tubes | Support is discontinuous below the bearing zone. | Map voids, bridge, grout, use deep support, or move the foundation. |

Use the right parameter for the problem: allowable bearing pressure for bearing, settlement estimate for compression, Plasticity Index and active-zone depth for expansive clay, hydraulic conductivity and groundwater elevation for water, factor of safety for slopes, and cyclic resistance for liquefaction. If the needed parameter is unknown, the design is not ready.

More gravel is not a universal answer.

## 5.17 Soil Improvement Choices

Soil improvement can be the right choice, but it can also consume the budget quickly. Choose the smallest documented intervention that solves the actual problem.

| Soil Improvement Method | Best Fit | Watch The Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Move footprint | Local bad area, drainage conflict, slope edge | Requires site flexibility. |
| Over-excavate and replace | Shallow weak pockets, organic layers, limited bad fill | Requires controlled fill and verification. |
| In-place compaction | Loose near-surface granular soil | Does not fix organics, wet clay, or deep soft layers. |
| Drainage correction | Wet soil, perched water, slope seepage | Must have a discharge path and maintenance access. |
| Lime or cement stabilization | Clayey or weak soils where appropriate | Specialty design, mixing, curing, and testing. |
| Preloading or surcharging | Compressible soil with time available | Slow and engineered. |
| Stone columns or aggregate piers | Soft or variable soil needing reinforcement | Specialty contractor and engineered design. |
| Helical piers | Bypass weak upper soils to deeper support | Requires engineering, torque records, and inspection. |
| Driven piles | Deep weak soil, high load, poor near-surface support | Vibration, access, noise, and engineering. |
| Drilled shafts or caissons | Deep support, heavy loads, special site requirements | Groundwater and cave-in risk can complicate drilling. |
| Pier-and-grade-beam | Expansive or weak upper active zone | Requires structural design and careful drainage. |

**Applied**

Before choosing an improvement method, answer:

- How deep is the bad layer?
- Is the bad material local or site-wide?
- Is water causing the problem?
- Can the footprint move?
- Can the site drain by gravity?
- Is the improvement permanent or maintenance-dependent?
- Who will document the work?
- What will the inspector or engineer accept?

The cheapest soil fix is often moving the house.

**Code**

- ORSC R301.1.3: engineered design path.
- ORSC R401.4 and Table R401.4.1: soil evaluation and bearing assumptions.
- ORSC R401.4.2: compressible or shifting soils.
- ORSC R403.1: footing support.
- ORS 455.440: foundation condition recordation where required.
- ASTM D698, ASTM D1557, and ASTM D6938: fill compaction target and field verification standards.

If the fix changes bearing material, foundation type, drainage, slope stability, or structural support, update the approved documents before construction hides the condition.

## 5.18 High Water Table

**Simple**

A high water table means groundwater is close enough to affect the foundation. It is one of the fastest ways for a normal build to become an engineering project.

Water enters weak points. It pushes on basement walls. It lifts slabs. It softens soil. It makes excavation harder. It turns pumps into permanent equipment.

There is no benefit in under-designing a wet site.

**Applied**

Check for high water table before buying or designing:

- Visit in winter or spring.
- Dig safe test pits and observe seepage.
- Check nearby well logs.
- Look for gray or blue-gray soil.
- Look for orange and red mottling.
- Check low spots after rain.
- Ask neighbors about basement water and springs.
- Compare site elevation to nearby creeks, ditches, ponds, and wetlands.

A basement on a high-water-table site requires an exceptional reason. It can require engineered walls and slab, permanent drainage, premium waterproofing, dewatering during construction, backup pumping, alarms, inspection access, and maintenance for the life of the house.

A slab-on-grade is often the better move, but it still requires elevation, capillary break, vapor control, drainage, and a subgrade that does not stay wet.

**Professional**

High groundwater changes the load case. Hydrostatic pressure increases with water depth at 62.4 pcf. A wall with 8 feet of water against it sees roughly 500 psf of water pressure at the base before earth pressure is added. Buoyancy acts upward on basement slabs when design groundwater is above the slab. Saturated soil loses effective stress, which can reduce bearing capacity and slope stability.

Dewatering is a design operation, not just pumping a hole dry. The method depends on hydraulic conductivity, fines content, excavation depth, discharge limits, and nearby structures. In fine sands and silts, poorly planned dewatering can move fines and cause settlement.

If the design depends on pumps, say that plainly. A pump-based solution is not wrong, but it is a mechanical dependency. It requires power, backup, alarm, access, replacement planning, and a discharge path that stays legal and functional.

**Code**

- ORSC R405.1: foundation drainage for below-grade habitable or usable spaces.
- ORSC R406.1: dampproofing where hydrostatic pressure does not occur.
- ORSC R406.2: waterproofing where high water table or other severe soil-water conditions exist.
- ORSC R406.3: sealing joints and penetrations against water entry.
- ASTM D5084: hydraulic conductivity testing for saturated porous materials when used by the geotechnical design professional.

## 5.19 Oregon-Specific Ground Risks

**Simple**

Oregon is not one soil condition. The coast, Willamette Valley, Cascade foothills, high desert, river valleys, volcanic areas, and mountain sites behave differently.

One county can contain good gravel, wet clay, old landslide deposits, floodplain silt, shallow rock, and undocumented fill.

**Applied**

Watch for these Oregon patterns:

| Area Or Setting | Common Ground Concerns |
|---|---|
| Coast and Coast Range | Heavy rain, slopes, landslides, high groundwater, marine clays, corrosion exposure, tsunami planning in coastal zones. |
| Willamette Valley | Silt, clay, seasonal groundwater, alluvial deposits, shrink-swell behavior, liquefaction in some alluvial flats. |
| Cascade foothills | Slope, springs, weathered rock, variable bedrock depth, drainage complexity. |
| High Cascades and volcanic areas | Pumice, ash, shallow rock, lava tubes in some areas, severe frost at elevation. |
| Eastern and Central Oregon | Frost depth, expansive soils in some areas, dry-season cracking, loess, basalt, wide moisture swings. |
| River corridors | Floodplain limits, soft alluvium, liquefaction susceptibility, high groundwater. |
| Older developed sites | Undocumented fill, old utilities, buried structures, changed drainage. |

Use official maps for screening. Then field test the actual footprint.

**Professional**

DOGAMI hazard layers should be treated as geologic screening data. A mapped hazard does not automatically condemn a site, and a blank map does not guarantee safety. The question is whether mapped geology, surface evidence, water behavior, and proposed foundation design are compatible.

When the site has mapped landslide, liquefaction, floodplain, slope, coastal, or volcanic hazard exposure, bring the relevant professional into the design before the architectural plan is fixed.

**Code**

- DOGAMI HazVu: mapped Oregon geologic hazards.
- DOGAMI SLIDO: landslide inventory and susceptibility.
- NRCS Web Soil Survey: soil series, drainage class, hydric soil, and engineering interpretations.
- Oregon Design Criteria Hub: site design criteria used with ORSC and OSSC projects.
- FEMA flood mapping and local floodplain ordinance: floodplain review.
- Oregon Water Resources Department well logs: nearby subsurface and groundwater clues.

## 5.20 Expansive Clay And Frost

**Simple**

Expansive clay moves with moisture. Frost heave moves soil when freezing water forms ice lenses. Both problems are slow until they are not.

Clay can lift a slab in winter, shrink away in summer, and repeat the cycle for years. Frost can lift shallow edges when frost-susceptible soil, water, and freezing temperatures meet.

**Applied**

Field clues for expansive clay:

- Long ribbon.
- Sticky soil that polishes under pressure.
- Wide dry cracks.
- Slickensides.
- Seasonal movement in nearby flatwork.
- Doors or slabs in nearby buildings that move seasonally.
- Deep clay layers in test pits or auger samples.

Field clues for frost risk:

- Silty soil.
- Poor drainage.
- Shallow groundwater.
- Standing water near foundation areas.
- Local frost-depth requirements that reach the planned foundation zone.
- Construction scheduled during freezing weather.

Good responses:

- Keep water away from the foundation.
- Avoid irrigation beside the house.
- Use capillary breaks and drainage.
- Replace frost-susceptible soil where needed.
- Put foundations below required frost depth or use an accepted frost-protection design.
- Use engineered foundation design for expansive clay that crosses the project threshold.

**Professional**

Expansive clay design is not one detail. It is a system: USCS classification, Atterberg limits, Plasticity Index, Expansion Index where used, active-zone depth, moisture variation, foundation stiffness, edge restraint, drainage, landscaping water, and acceptance criteria.

Frost heave requires freezing temperature, frost-susceptible soil, and water. Silts are especially dangerous because capillary rise can feed ice lenses. Clean granular material with low fines is usually less frost-susceptible. Frost-protected shallow foundations use insulation to control heat loss at the edge; conventional frost protection uses depth below the local frost line.

**Code**

- ASTM D4318: Atterberg limits.
- ASTM D4829: Expansion Index.
- ORSC R401.4.2: compressible or shifting soils.
- ORSC R403.1.4: frost protection and minimum footing depth.
- ORSC R403.3: frost-protected shallow foundations.
- ASCE 32-01: design and construction of frost-protected shallow foundations.
- ACI 332 Chapter 12: residential concrete expansive-soil provisions when used by the design professional.

For project-level screening, PI 15 triggers discussion and PI 20 triggers engineered-foundation review unless the soil is removed, replaced, or otherwise mitigated through an accepted design.

## 5.21 Go, Modify, Or Walk Away

At the end of soil investigation, make a decision. Do not leave the project in a fog.

| Level | Soil Condition | Typical Decision |
|---|---|---|
| A | Firm, uniform, dry, ordinary soil with clear drainage | Proceed with normal documentation. |
| B | Mostly good soil with manageable local issues | Proceed with targeted fixes and verification. |
| C | Significant water, clay, weak soil, fill, or variation | Get geotechnical and structural input. |
| D | Major site-wide problems | Compare repair cost against finding better land. |
| E | Active landslide, severe water, organic ground, severe instability, or uneconomical risk | Walk away unless a qualified team proves otherwise. |

**Applied**

Your soil decision record should answer:

- What soil was found?
- Where was it found?
- At what depth?
- What tests were run?
- What problems were identified?
- What was corrected?
- Who accepted the correction?
- What foundation assumptions remain?

Do not display a fill-in form in the book. Keep the actual record in the project binder.

**Professional**

The site decision should connect investigation data to design action. If the soil is uniform and strong, state the basis for using the planned foundation. If the soil is variable, state how the foundation, subgrade prep, and testing will control that variation. If groundwater is present, state whether the design avoids it, drains it, resists it, or depends on pumps. If expansive clay is present, state the moisture-control and structural strategy.

The decision record is the bridge between field conditions and approved construction documents.

**Code**

- ORSC R301.1.3: engineered design where prescriptive provisions do not cover the condition.
- ORSC R401.4: soil evaluation.
- ORSC R403.1: foundation support and footing design.
- ORS 455.440: foundation condition recordation where required.
- Local permit revision process: required when field conditions change the approved design.

## 5.22 Before Concrete

**Simple**

Concrete day is too late to debate the soil.

**Applied**

Before footing, mat, or slab concrete:

- Topsoil and organics are removed.
- Soft pockets are corrected.
- Water is controlled.
- Utilities are located.
- Subgrade matches the approved design elevation.
- Fill is placed and compacted as required.
- Compaction or bearing verification is documented.
- Excavation is safe for workers.
- Drainage paths are understood.
- Required inspections are complete.
- The engineer or building official has been involved where required.

Once the truck arrives, the job moves at concrete speed. Soil questions should already be closed.

**Professional**

Subgrade acceptance is the last gate before foundation work. Walk the prepared footprint with the excavation contractor, concrete contractor, and responsible designer or inspector as needed. Probe soft areas. Check elevations. Confirm that the soil profile matches the investigation. Confirm that no water, loose material, organics, debris, or unapproved fill remains in bearing areas.

If there is a mismatch, stop. Correct the ground before reinforcing steel, vapor retarder, insulation, forms, or concrete turn the problem into a hidden defect.

**Code**

- ORSC R109.1.1: foundation inspection before concrete placement where required.
- ORSC R403.1: footing support and foundation load transfer.
- ORSC R403.1.4: minimum footing depth and frost protection.
- ORSC R405.1: foundation drainage where required.
- ORSC R406: dampproofing and waterproofing.
- ORSC R506.2.3: vapor retarder below concrete floors on ground.
- NEC 250.52(A)(3) and ORSC foundation inspection practice: concrete-encased electrode coordination where used.

Do not cover inspection points.

The ground should be proven before concrete arrives.
