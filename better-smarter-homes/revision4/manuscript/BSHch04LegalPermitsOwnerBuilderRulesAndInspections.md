# Chapter 4: Legal, Permits, Owner-Builder Rules, And Inspections

The legal path is part of the build.

Concrete, pipe, wire, insulation, and drainage all matter. So do permits, licenses, inspections, ownership, occupancy, and records. A strong house with a broken legal path is still a broken project.

The goal is not to be scared of the rules. The goal is to know which lane each decision belongs in before the work gets buried.

## 4.1 The Legal Path Is Construction Work

**Simple**

The legal path defines who can do the work, which permits are required, when inspections happen, when the house can be occupied, and which records must exist when the project is finished.

This is not paperwork after the build. It is part of the build.

**Applied**

Put permits and inspections on the schedule before the work starts.

For each phase, know:

- which permit covers the work
- who is allowed to do the work
- what documents must be submitted
- what the inspector must see
- what cannot be covered yet
- what correction process applies if the work fails
- what record goes into the project binder

The worst time to discover a permit problem is after concrete is poured, a trench is backfilled, insulation is installed, or drywall is up.

**Professional**

Legal compliance and construction sequencing should be coordinated from the beginning. Oregon residential work can involve land-use review, structural building permits, plumbing permits, electrical permits, mechanical permits, septic approvals, driveway access, erosion-control conditions, local planning conditions, inspection approvals, and certificate-of-occupancy requirements.

The clean project is not the one with the most paper. It is the one where the paper matches the work.

**Code**

- ORS Chapter 455: Oregon building-code authority, owner-built dwelling provisions, permits, inspections, and occupancy framework.
- ORS Chapter 701: construction contractor licensing, exemptions, owner-builder posture, and intent-to-sell issues.
- OAR Chapter 918 Division 480: one- and two-family dwelling specialty-code administration, including occupancy rules.
- ORSC Chapter 1: residential code administration, permits, inspections, certificate of occupancy, and temporary certificate of occupancy.
- Local building department procedures: local submittal, inspection scheduling, correction, and approval workflow.

## 4.2 Owner-Builder Means Responsibility

An owner-builder is not skipping responsibility. The owner-builder is taking responsibility.

**Applied**

If using the homeowner path, build around these habits:

- apply as the property owner where allowed
- read and sign the required homeowner acknowledgement before permit work starts
- keep the permit and approved plans on site
- schedule inspections before covering work
- resolve correction notices before moving on
- hire licensed contractors when required or when the work is beyond your skill
- keep subcontractor, permit, inspection, and product records in the binder

Do not treat friends, helpers, or paid labor casually. A helper can become a legal problem faster than a bad measurement can become a layout problem.

**Professional**

The owner-builder path changes who carries the coordination burden. The owner becomes the permit holder, document keeper, inspection scheduler, correction manager, and first person responsible for proving that the work stayed inside the approved path.

Oregon's homeowner acknowledgement process makes that responsibility explicit. It applies to residential building, electrical, mechanical, and plumbing permits submitted by property owners who are not licensed with the Construction Contractors Board.

**Code**

- ORS 701.325(3): Information Notice to Property Owners About Construction Responsibilities.
- BCD homeowner acknowledgement or Property Owner Statement: state form used with owner-permit applications. The form is referenced here, not reproduced.
- ORS 455.320: owner-built dwelling structural-exemption framework, including owner occupancy and unpaid friends or relatives language.
- ORS 479.540: electrical owner or immediate-family licensure exemption, where the statutory conditions fit.
- ORS 693.020: plumbing owner licensure exemption, where the statutory conditions fit.

Keep the signed acknowledgement with the project records.

### Owner-Builder Path Compared With Licensed Contractor Path

This project is written around the owner-builder path, but the other path should be understood.

| Path | What it means | Where it works | Where it does not work |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owner-builder | The owner builds for personal occupancy under the homeowner path. | Best fit for a slow, careful, self-built home where learning, testing, and direct control matter. | Bad fit for building inventory, flipping, or construction for other people. |
| Licensed residential contractor | A licensed contractor builds for compensation or for sale. | Best fit for commercial construction activity, repeated builds, client work, or fast resale. | Does not automatically let the owner self-perform licensed trade work. |
| Hybrid owner-builder | The owner holds the main homeowner role and hires licensed trades where needed. | Useful when the owner wants control but wants help on high-risk or high-skill work. | Must be structured cleanly so permits, licenses, insurance, and supervision match the work. |

Becoming a licensed plumber or electrician is not a quick shortcut for this project. Those licenses are trade careers, not weekend paperwork. The practical question is not "Can I become every trade?" The practical question is "Which work can I legally and competently self-perform, and which work should be hired out?"

## 4.3 The Oregon Resale Timing Myth

There is no statewide Oregon rule that simply says an owner-builder must wait three years before selling.

The common three-year shortcut is not the rule. Oregon uses intent, occupancy, exemption limits, and disclosure rules. It is not one simple clock.

**Simple**

The strongest position is factual: own the land personally, build the house for yourself, move in when occupancy is approved, actually live there, and keep normal proof that it was your home.

Do not build a flip and call it an owner-builder project.

**Applied**

Use this practical posture:

1. Hold title in the correct personal ownership structure before relying on the homeowner path.
2. Pull permits as the homeowner where allowed.
3. Build because you intend to occupy the home.
4. Do not list or market the house for sale while owner-builder work is still being completed.
5. Move in only after the building official allows occupancy.
6. Keep ordinary occupancy records: utilities, mail, insurance, tax address, voter registration, and daily-life records.
7. If plans change later, document the real reason.
8. Before sale, lease, trust transfer, LLC involvement, or tax planning, get professional advice.

The strong position is not a guessed number of months. It is genuine owner occupancy with records that match the facts.

**Professional**

The Oregon owner-builder issue is an intent framework. The contracting exemption becomes vulnerable when the owner constructs, remodels, repairs, or arranges construction with intent to offer the structure for sale before, upon, or after completion. Non-occupancy after completion can be used as evidence of intent to sell.

Separate timing rules exist, but they control different things:

| Rule type | What it controls |
|---|---|
| CCB licensing intent test | Whether the work looks like owner occupancy or construction for sale. |
| Five-year structural exemption cooldown | How often certain owner-built structural-code exemptions can be used. |
| Rural-county exemption limit | How often a qualifying exempt owner-built dwelling permit can be issued in participating rural counties. |
| Seventy-five-day homebuyer protection notice | Certain lien-notice obligations around very recent completion and sale. |
| Federal capital-gains rules | Tax treatment, separate from Oregon construction licensing. |

For this project, the clean strategy is owner occupancy first. If resale happens later, it should look like a real homeowner selling a real home, not a disguised developer selling inventory.

**Code**

- ORS 701.010(5): contractor-licensing owner exemption and intent-to-sell test.
- ORS 455.320: owner-built dwelling structural-exemption cooldown.
- ORS 455.335: rural-county exempt owner-built dwelling permit frequency limit where that rural exemption path applies.
- ORS 87.007 and ORS 87.093: homebuyer-protection and lien-notice timing around recent completion and sale.
- ORS 87.045: substantial-completion definition used in construction-lien timing.
- ORSC R110 and OAR 918-480-0140: CO/TCO occupancy framework.

CO or TCO is the gate to legal occupancy. It is not the start of a statewide owner-builder resale clock.

If anyone gives a bright-line resale rule, ask for the ORS or OAR number.

## 4.4 Ownership Structure, Spouses, Trusts, And LLCs

The owner-builder path depends on who owns the property and who is doing the work.

**Applied**

Before relying on the homeowner path, confirm the ownership structure:

- individual ownership
- spouse or co-owner on title
- revocable trust
- irrevocable trust
- LLC ownership
- future transfer plan
- financing or insurance requirements

Do this before buying land in the wrong name. Fixing title after the project starts can be harder than choosing the clean structure at the beginning.

**Professional**

The safest owner-builder posture is a natural person who owns the property, signs the homeowner statement, intends to occupy, performs or properly contracts the work, and keeps records.

LLC ownership is different. An LLC that owns property and arranges construction with intent to sell can look like a contractor or developer, not a homeowner. Trust ownership is also fact-specific. A revocable living trust where the owner-occupant controls the trust is easier to explain than an irrevocable trust, business trust, or entity structure where the occupant is not the clear owner-builder.

Do not let business structure outrun the legal exemption.

**Code**

- ORS 701.005: contractor, developer, and residential contractor definitions.
- ORS 701.010: exemptions from contractor licensure and the intent-to-sell owner exemption.
- ORS 93.180: Oregon tenancy by the entirety rule for spouse transfers, where spouse ownership is part of the title question.
- Tandem Properties LLC v. Construction Contractors Board, 184 Or App 28 (2002): Oregon case warning on LLC ownership, construction activity, and intent to sell.

If title will be held by a trust, LLC, spouse arrangement, or other entity structure, get local confirmation before relying on the homeowner path.

## 4.5 Who Can Do The Work

Owner-builder does not mean every job is automatically open.

Some work can be done by the owner with permits and inspections. Some work requires licensed contractors. Some work has a separate licensing system outside the homeowner path.

**Applied**

Use this first-pass map before deciding to self-perform:

| Work type | Owner-builder treatment |
|---|---|
| General residential building work | Often possible under the homeowner path, with permits and inspections. |
| Plumbing | Owner work can be allowed on the owner's own building, with permit and inspection. |
| Electrical | Narrower owner or immediate-family exemption, tied to property not intended for sale, exchange, lease, or rent. |
| Mechanical | Often available through homeowner permit practice, but verify local scope before work starts. |
| Natural gas piping | Homeowner work can be allowed in some Oregon residential permit paths, but the jurisdiction needs to identify the permit path before planning gas work. |
| LP gas | Hard stop. Do not self-perform unless properly licensed through the State Fire Marshal path. |
| Septic | Separate DEQ or county approval path. Owner installation can be possible only under that permit path. |
| Water well | Separate Oregon Water Resources path. A landowner permit can be possible, but this is its own system. |
| Refrigerant work | EPA Section 608 certification applies when refrigerant is handled, even when the homeowner installs equipment. |
| Boiler or pressure-vessel work | Separate licensing can apply. |

Before doing trade work, verify four things:

1. who pulls the permit
2. who is allowed to perform the work
3. what inspection or test is required
4. what record proves approval

If you hire a licensed trade, use them deliberately. Good uses include:

- layout review before rough work starts
- on-site walk before inspection
- difficult terminations, equipment setup, or specialty connections
- full trade installation where the risk is not worth learning on this house

A short paid consultation before rough-in can save a week of correction work. That is not failure. That is using the right tool.

**Professional**

The contracting-side exemption and the trade-side exemptions are separate. CCB licensing rules do not automatically answer electrical, plumbing, mechanical, LP gas, septic, well, refrigerant, boiler, or fire-sprinkler questions.

Plumbing and electrical have separate statutory exemption language. Electrical is narrower because the owner or immediate-family exemption is tied to property that is not intended for sale, exchange, lease, or rent. Plumbing has different language and still requires code compliance, permits, and inspections.

LP gas is the major hard stop. Propane piping, tank, fitting, vent, and flue work belong under the State Fire Marshal licensing path. Do not treat propane like ordinary homeowner mechanical work.

**Code**

- ORS 693.020: plumbing licensure exemption for an owner working on the owner's own building, with permit and inspection still required.
- ORS 479.540: electrical licensure exemption for owner or immediate-family work on qualifying property not intended for sale, exchange, lease, or rent.
- ORS 480.432 and OAR Chapter 837 Division 30: LP gas licensing path through the State Fire Marshal.
- OAR Chapter 340 Division 71: onsite wastewater rules.
- OAR Chapter 690 and ORS 537.747: water-supply-well constructor and landowner well path.
- EPA Section 608: refrigerant-handling certification when refrigerant is handled.

The entity doing the work normally needs to be the entity signing for the permit. If a licensed contractor performs the work, the contractor normally signs or pulls the appropriate permit for that work.

### Trade-Scope Notes

**Plumbing:** Owner work can be allowed on the owner's own building, but the work still has to meet the plumbing code, permit requirements, and inspections. Water supply, DWV, water heaters, fixtures, sewer line, water service, backflow devices, and related systems should be treated as regulated work unless the local authority says the specific replacement is exempt.

**Electrical:** The owner or immediate-family path is narrower. It is tied to property not intended for sale, exchange, lease, or rent. Permanent wiring, new circuits, panel work, service equipment, and low-voltage systems require permit attention. Do not assume low voltage means no rules.

**Mechanical:** Heating, cooling, ventilation, exhaust, gas piping, ductwork, heat pumps, mini-splits, equipment replacement, and dryer exhaust are permit issues. Refrigerant-handling work requires EPA certification even when the owner is allowed to install other parts of the system.

**LP gas:** Treat propane as a separate licensing world. The homeowner path does not make propane tank, piping, fitting, vent, or flue work open to self-performance.

**Septic and wells:** These are site-system paths, not normal building-trade paths. Septic belongs to the DEQ or county onsite wastewater process. Wells belong to the Oregon Water Resources path. Each has its own application, inspection, and closeout records.

## 4.6 Paid Help, Friends, Family, And Subcontractors

Free help and paid help are not the same thing.

A friend helping for free is one kind of risk. A paid worker is another. A licensed subcontractor is another. Mixing them up can create tax, insurance, workers' compensation, licensing, and permit problems.

**Applied**

Before anyone touches the job, write down the labor plan:

- who will work
- whether they are paid
- what they will do
- what license they hold, if any
- which permit covers the work
- who supervises the work
- what insurance covers the jobsite

If someone is paid and is not a properly licensed contractor, slow down before they work. Paid labor can make the homeowner an employer.

If a licensed subcontractor is hired, verify the license before work starts and keep the license record, contract, insurance certificate, permit record, and inspection result.

**Professional**

The cleanest owner-builder posture is the owner doing the work personally, with truly unpaid family or friends where allowed, and licensed contractors used for work that requires licensing, exceeds the owner's skill, or creates unacceptable risk.

Do not create a half-licensed jobsite where the homeowner permit is used, but paid unlicensed workers are doing regulated work. That is exactly the arrangement the homeowner acknowledgement process warns about.

**Code**

- ORS 455.320: owner-built dwelling language allowing the owner and unpaid friends or relatives in specific exemption contexts.
- ORS 701.325: homeowner responsibility notice tied to unlicensed labor, subcontractor licensing, and permit responsibility.
- ORS 656.027: workers' compensation subject-worker exceptions and limits.
- OAR 812-002-0275: CCB family-member definition for contractor-side context.
- ORS 316 and federal employment-tax rules: withholding and tax duties when paid workers become employees.

If paid help is part of the plan, resolve the employment and licensing questions before the work arrives on site.

### Legal Risk Triage

Use the same sorting habit here that belongs in land feasibility. Not every legal issue has the same severity.

| Risk level | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Hard stop | Do not proceed until this is solved. | LP gas work without proper licensing, no occupancy approval, construction through the wrong entity. |
| Pause and verify | The path might work, but the answer needs a source. | Trust ownership, LLC involvement, paid helpers, resale or lease plan, rural exemption use. |
| Permit gate | Work can proceed only inside the permit path. | Structural work, plumbing, electrical, mechanical, septic, well, grading, driveway access. |
| Record issue | The work might be correct, but proof is required. | Inspection approvals, contractor license information, product instructions, photos, correction notices. |
| Strategy choice | More than one legal path can work. | Owner-builder, licensed contractor, hybrid owner-builder with licensed trades. |

## 4.7 Permit Rhythm

A permit is permission to start regulated work. It is not a trophy for the end of the job.

**Applied**

The basic permit rhythm is:

1. Confirm zoning and land-use path.
2. Finish enough design work for plan review.
3. Apply with plans, specifications, and supporting documents.
4. Answer plan review comments and receive approval.
5. Post the permit and keep approved plans on site.
6. Build only the approved scope and schedule inspections before covering work.
7. Keep approvals and correction notices in the binder.

Oregon's ePermitting path can support online application, status tracking, fee payment, document upload, inspection scheduling, and inspection-result review where the local jurisdiction participates.

**Professional**

For this project, the permit set can touch several scopes:

- residential structural building permit
- plumbing permit
- electrical permit
- mechanical permit
- septic or sewer approval
- well or water-supply path
- driveway or access permit
- grading, erosion control, floodplain, wildfire, or local planning conditions

Permits are not interchangeable. A building permit does not clear septic. A septic permit does not clear electrical. A homeowner permit does not make unlicensed paid trade work legal.

Here is the permit-family map:

| Permit or approval family | What it controls |
|---|---|
| land-use or zoning approval | whether the project is allowed on the parcel |
| structural building permit | foundation, walls, floors, roof, stairs, guards, egress, and main building work |
| plumbing permit | water supply, DWV, water heater, fixtures, building sewer, and related tests |
| electrical permit | service, panel, grounding, branch circuits, devices, equipment power, and required protection |
| mechanical permit | heating, cooling, ventilation, exhaust, ductwork, combustion, and some gas-piping work |
| septic approval | site evaluation, system design, installation, inspection, and final septic approval |
| well or water-source approval | well construction, alteration, abandonment, logs, and water-source records |
| driveway or access approval | road approach, emergency access, grades, drainage, and private-road conditions |
| grading, erosion, floodplain, wildfire, or local planning conditions | site-specific work required before or during construction |

The owner-builder should know which lane owns each question before calling the office. The faster you can name the lane, the faster you can get a useful answer.

**Code**

- ORSC R105: permit requirements for residential work.
- ORSC R106: construction documents submitted for review.
- ORSC R109: inspections.
- ORSC R110: certificate of occupancy and temporary certificate of occupancy.
- OAR 918-480-0140: Oregon residential CO/TCO and contractor-information disclosure rule.
- Oregon ePermitting: online application, status, document, fee, and inspection tools where the jurisdiction participates.

Permits can expire if work does not start or if work is suspended beyond the allowed time. Permits are not transferable to another person, company, or contractor.

## 4.8 Inspection Gates

The inspector cannot approve what is already hidden.

**Applied**

Use this rule: if concrete, dirt, insulation, drywall, finishes, or backfill will hide the work, schedule the inspection first.

Common approval gates for this design include:

| Gate | What must stay visible |
|---|---|
| site and setback | building location, setbacks, site controls |
| septic and well work | system location, pipe, tank, trench, well records, approval documents |
| footing and foundation | excavation, bearing surface, forms, reinforcement, grounding electrode, embeds |
| underground utility trench | pipe material, depth, bedding, slope, separation, warning tape, tracer wire |
| underslab | gravel, vapor barrier, insulation, tubing, reinforcement, penetrations |
| structural or framing | walls, reinforcement, grout, anchors, floor and roof structure |
| rough plumbing | water pipe, DWV, vents, supports, tests, cleanouts |
| rough electrical | boxes, cables, grounding, service equipment, panel, protection, support |
| rough mechanical | ducts, exhausts, equipment, condensate, ventilation, combustion or refrigerant-related items |
| insulation and air sealing | insulation R-value, continuity, gaps, air-sealing work |
| final trade inspections | fixtures, devices, equipment, labeling, operation |
| final building inspection | safety, drainage, access, stairs, guards, site conditions, completion records |

Do not schedule concrete, backfill, insulation, drywall, or finish work until the required approval is clear.

**Professional**

Inspection planning is quality control. Concrete construction makes this more important because many defects become permanent once concrete is placed or work is backfilled.

The owner-builder should prepare for each inspection with the approved plans, permit card, relevant product instructions, test records, photos, and a clean work area. The goal is not to debate the inspector. The goal is to make compliance visible.

A useful inspection rhythm is site, foundation, underground work, structure, rough trades, insulation, final trades, final building, and occupancy. The exact inspection names and grouping can change by jurisdiction, but the physical logic does not change. Inspect before the work disappears.

**Code**

- ORSC R109: required residential inspections and inspection authority.
- ORSC R109.1.1: footing and foundation inspection before concrete placement where applicable.
- ORSC R110: final approval and occupancy path.
- Specialty-code inspection provisions: electrical, plumbing, mechanical, onsite wastewater, well, and other permit-specific inspection paths.
- Project-specific permit conditions: local inspection sequencing, correction notices, reinspection requirements, and approval records.

If an inspection fails, correct the work and get reinspection or written approval before covering the work or moving into the next dependent phase.

## 4.9 Certificate Of Occupancy And Move-In

A house is not legally ready to live in just because it has walls, heat, and working lights.

**Applied**

Plan for occupancy from the beginning:

- finish required life-safety work
- finish required sanitation and utility work
- pass final building inspection
- pass final electrical inspection
- pass final plumbing inspection
- pass final mechanical inspection
- pass required site-development or local-condition inspections
- resolve correction notices
- submit required contractor or license information forms
- close septic, well, or deferred submittal items when applicable
- keep proof of approvals
- do not move in until the local authority allows occupancy

Temporary occupancy can be possible when the building official determines that the occupied portion can be used safely. Do not plan on it without asking early.

**Professional**

For a new residential dwelling, certificate-of-occupancy approval depends on inspection approval and required documentation. Temporary certificate of occupancy is discretionary. The building official sets the conditions, expiration, unfinished-work requirements, and extension path.

CO/TCO also matters outside the building department. It can affect lending, insurance, tax records, utilities, and principal-residence timing.

**Code**

- ORSC R110.1: occupancy is not allowed until the building official issues the required certificate of occupancy.
- ORSC R110.3: certificate-of-occupancy issuance after inspection shows no code or law violations enforced by the department.
- ORSC R110.4: temporary certificate of occupancy authority.
- OAR 918-480-0140: Oregon residential CO/TCO administration and contractor-information disclosure.
- BCD Form 4823 or local equivalent: contractor license information disclosure before CO where required.

CO/TCO is the gate to legal occupancy. It is not the start of any statewide owner-builder resale clock.

## 4.10 Better Smarter Homes LLC And Brand Separation

The safest arrangement is simple: the person owns and builds the house. Better Smarter Homes is the design, research, education, and publishing brand.

Do not make the brand look like the contractor, developer, seller, architect, or engineering company unless the right licenses and professionals are actually in place.

**Applied**

Use safer language:

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| engineered by Better Smarter Homes | designed by Better Smarter Homes |
| Better Smarter Homes engineering | Better Smarter Homes design research |
| stamped by Better Smarter Homes | Better Smarter Homes maker's mark or brand mark |
| Better Smarter Homes-built homes for sale | owner-built home using Better Smarter Homes design principles |
| construction management by Better Smarter Homes | design method documented by Better Smarter Homes |
| Better Smarter Homes-certified engineering | Better Smarter Homes documented design method |

If a licensed Oregon professional engineer is involved, name the engineer accurately: structural engineering by the licensed engineer, in collaboration with Better Smarter Homes.

**Professional**

The separation works only if the activities stay separated. The individual owner owns the land, pulls the homeowner permit, builds the home, occupies the home, and later sells as the owner. The LLC owns intellectual property, video content, written content, plan concepts, research records, trademarks, and educational material.

The word "engineered" is a legal pivot. In commercial speech, marketing, plan sales, listing language, or brand material, it can imply professional engineering practice. Without a licensed Oregon professional engineer responsible for that work, the safer brand language is "designed," "researched," "tested," "documented," or "built using Better Smarter Homes design principles."

Do not use a stamp-shaped mark that looks like a professional seal. A maker's mark is fine. A mark that resembles a professional seal creates avoidable legal risk.

**Code**

- ORS 672.007 and ORS 672.020: practicing or offering to practice engineering and prohibition on unregistered engineering practice.
- ORS 671.020 and ORS 671.030: architecture registration limits and single-family dwelling design exemptions.
- ORS 701.005 and ORS 701.010: contractor, developer, exemption, and intent-to-sell definitions.
- OAR 812-002-0160: construction-management definition.
- OAR 812-002-0680: speculative construction definition.
- Tandem Properties LLC v. Construction Contractors Board, 184 Or App 28 (2002): LLC ownership and intent-to-sell warning.

Keep Better Smarter Homes out of the construction stream unless it is properly licensed for the activity. The brand should not hold title to the land, sign construction contracts, manage construction for compensation, receive payment for construction services, or imply professional engineering or architectural licensure that it does not have.

## 4.11 What To Have Ready For Any Inspection

Inspectors do not need a speech. They need visible work, approved documents, and a site that is ready to inspect.

**Applied**

Before the inspector arrives:

- permit is posted or available as required
- approved plans are on site
- work area is clean enough to inspect
- required parts are visible
- ladders or access are safe
- product instructions are available when relevant
- test gauges or test setup are holding if required
- photos are taken before work is covered
- questions are written down
- no concrete, backfill, insulation, drywall, or finish work is scheduled until approval is clear

After the inspection:

- save the approval result
- save correction notices
- photograph corrected work before reinspection
- record the inspector name, date, and permit number
- do not cover the work until approval is clear

Inspection is not only permission to continue. It is the record that hidden work was checked before it disappeared.

## 4.12 The Clean Legal Posture

The clean path is not complicated. It is disciplined.

**Applied**

Before work starts, the project should be able to say:

- the land ownership matches the homeowner path
- the permit applicant matches the work being performed
- Better Smarter Homes is not acting as the contractor
- paid workers and subcontractors have been sorted legally
- self-performed trade work is allowed for that trade and that property
- special systems have their own permit or license path
- inspections are scheduled before work is hidden
- CO/TCO is treated as occupancy permission, not a resale clock
- records are saved as the work happens

If the legal path is unclear, stop before the work becomes permanent.

**Code**

- ORS 701.010: owner exemption and intent-to-sell test.
- ORS 455.320 and ORS 455.335: owner-built dwelling exemption paths and five-year limits.
- ORS 479.540 and ORS 693.020: electrical and plumbing owner licensure exemptions.
- ORS 480.432: LP gas licensing hard stop.
- ORS 672.007 and ORS 672.020: engineering title, holding-out, and practice limits.
- OAR 918-480-0140 and ORSC R110: CO/TCO occupancy framework.
- ORS 87.007, ORS 87.093, and ORS 87.045: post-completion lien-notice timing and substantial completion.

Build the house so the records match the concrete.
