# Chapter 7: Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement

This chapter covers the connection between the house and the ground.

The soil work verifies the ground. The structural work shows how the loads move. This chapter converts both into built concrete: excavation, subgrade acceptance, foundation type, slab stack, vapor control, radon readiness, waterproofing, drains, reinforcement, concrete delivery, placement, curing, testing, backfill, and records.

A foundation is not only the first pour. It is the connection between the house and the site.

## 7.1 What The Foundation Must Do

**Simple**

The foundation holds the house, spreads the weight, limits movement, resists water pressure where designed, and ties into the structure above it.

It also hides many of the most important pieces of the build. Soil, drains, vapor control, radon piping, insulation, plumbing, rebar, grounding, anchors, and concrete all meet here.

Concrete conceals work quickly. Check the hidden pieces before placement.

**Applied**

Before the foundation is chosen, answer these questions in writing:

- What soil or engineered fill will support the foundation?
- What foundation type is being used?
- Where does water go during winter?
- Is the building pad higher than the surrounding drainage?
- Is the frost-protection path conventional depth, frost-protected shallow foundation, solid rock, or engineered design?
- Will the house have a basement, crawl space, slab-on-grade, mat foundation, grade beam, or pier-supported foundation?
- Where do plumbing, radon piping, electrical grounding, sleeves, drains, anchor bolts, and embeds go?
- Which inspections happen before concrete?
- Who can approve a field change?

If the answer changes in the field, stop. Do not pour concrete over a new question.

**Professional**

Foundation work integrates bearing pressure, settlement, differential movement, frost, groundwater, hydrostatic pressure, lateral earth pressure, uplift, drainage, vapor drive, soil gas, reinforcement detailing, penetrations, concrete durability, inspection timing, and backfill sequencing.

A concrete house raises the stakes because the dead load is high and the intended service life is long. Foundation design cannot be a trade-by-trade pileup. The subgrade, aggregate, vapor retarder, insulation, soil-gas layer, reinforcement, concrete, wall connection, waterproofing, drains, and backfill have to be coordinated as one system.

Hidden coordination errors can become permanent foundation defects.

**Code**

- ORSC R401.3: drainage around foundations.
- ORSC R401.4: soil tests when required.
- ORSC R403: footings.
- ORSC R404: foundation and retaining walls.
- ORSC R405: foundation drainage.
- ORSC R406: foundation dampproofing and waterproofing.
- ORSC R506: concrete floors on ground.
- ORSC R109.1.1: foundation and concrete slab inspection timing where administered by the jurisdiction.
- ACI 332: residential concrete.
- ACI 318: engineered structural concrete where required.

## 7.2 Choosing The Foundation Type

**Simple**

The site helps pick the foundation.

A basement is not always better. A slab is not always cheaper. A crawl space is not always easier. The best foundation is the one that fits the soil, water, frost, access, structure, and budget.

**Applied**

Use the site conditions to sort the options:

| Site Condition | Strong Candidate | Watch First |
|---|---|---|
| Dry, firm, uniform soil | Basement, slab-on-grade, or mat foundation | Drainage and frost protection |
| High seasonal groundwater | Slab-on-grade or raised foundation | Waterproofing, sub-slab drainage, finished-floor elevation |
| Expansive clay | Engineered slab, pier-and-grade-beam, or soil replacement | Moisture control and movement |
| Sloping site | Daylight basement, stepped foundation, or retaining design | Excavation, shoring, drainage, wall pressure |
| Shallow rock | Slab, shallow foundation, or redesigned layout | Excavation cost and drainage |
| Loose or variable fill | Removal, engineered fill, mat, grade beams, or deep support | Settlement documentation |
| Poor access | Simpler pour and excavation strategy | Pump reach, truck route, staging |

Do not force a preferred foundation onto the wrong site. That is how square footage becomes a repair plan.

**Professional**

Foundation selection is a structural, geotechnical, water-management, energy, access, and maintenance decision. A mat or raft can distribute load and help reduce differential movement, but it still requires engineered thickness, reinforcement, edge beams, bearing assumptions, subgrade modulus, joints, penetrations, and placement sequencing.

Conventional footings and stem walls can be efficient where loads and soil are straightforward. Basements add lateral earth pressure, waterproofing, drainage, and excavation stability. Crawl spaces improve access but create vapor, insulation, ventilation, and pest-control questions. Pier and grade-beam systems can bypass poor shallow soil but require engineering, layout discipline, and lateral bracing.

**Code**

- ORSC R403.1: footing design and bearing.
- ORSC Table R403.1: footing sizing where prescriptive tables apply.
- ORSC R404.1: concrete and masonry foundation walls.
- ORSC R408: under-floor spaces and crawl spaces.
- ORSC R301.1.3: engineered design path when the foundation system exceeds prescriptive limits.
- ACI 318 Chapter 13: engineered concrete foundations.
- ACI 332: residential concrete foundations and slabs.

## 7.3 Basement Or Slab-On-Grade

**Simple**

A basement gives space, storage, shelter, and mechanical flexibility. It also puts the house into the wet part of the site.

A slab-on-grade stays closer to the surface. It is usually simpler to drain, but the ground below it must be clean, firm, and even.

**Applied**

Use a basement only when the site can carry it:

- Excavation can be made safe.
- Footing soil is documented and accepted.
- Water table is below the basement system with room to spare.
- Site drainage has a legal outlet.
- Wall design accounts for soil pressure.
- Waterproofing can be installed and protected.
- Perimeter drains can reach daylight or a reliable sump.
- Backfill can be placed without crushing the drainage system.
- Egress, ceiling height, light, ventilation, and mechanical routes are planned.

Use slab-on-grade when it gives the better risk profile:

- Groundwater is high.
- Basement excavation would be unstable.
- Drainage to daylight is weak or impossible.
- Budget does not support below-grade waterproofing risk.
- A simpler, more inspectable foundation makes the whole project better.

The right house on the wrong basement is still the wrong project.

**Professional**

Basement work combines excavation safety, wall stability, footing bearing, lateral earth pressure, hydrostatic pressure, wall-to-slab joint detailing, drain-board continuity, membrane protection, perimeter drains, sump reliability, vapor control, radon routing, and backfill pressure.

Slab-on-grade shifts the risk. It depends on subgrade uniformity, base course, vapor retarder continuity, capillary break, insulation support, frost protection, soil-gas routing, slab-edge detailing, reinforcement, jointing, curing, and floor-finish moisture tolerance.

A wet basement is not a waterproofing product failure by itself. It is often a system failure: grade, roof water, backfill, drains, membrane, penetrations, sump, and maintenance did not work together.

**Code**

- ORSC R305: minimum ceiling height for habitable space.
- ORSC R310: emergency escape and rescue openings where sleeping rooms require them.
- ORSC R404: foundation and retaining wall requirements.
- ORSC R405: drainage for foundations enclosing habitable or usable spaces below grade.
- ORSC R406.1: dampproofing.
- ORSC R406.2: waterproofing where high water table or severe soil-water conditions are known.
- ORSC R506: concrete floors on ground.
- ORSC R408: crawl space requirements.

## 7.4 Oregon Foundation Inspection Rhythm

**Simple**

Inspection timing matters because concrete covers proof.

The inspector must see the work while it is still open.

**Applied**

Confirm the local inspection sequence before work starts. Jurisdictions use the same code framework, but scheduling names and combined inspections vary.

Typical hold points:

| Hold Point | Work Must Be Ready Before |
|---|---|
| Footing inspection | Concrete is placed in footings or thickened edges |
| Stem wall or foundation wall inspection | Concrete, masonry grout, or backfill hides the work |
| Underfloor or pre-slab inspection | Slab concrete covers vapor retarder, insulation, plumbing, conduit, radon pipe, or reinforcement |
| Foundation drainage inspection | Drain tile, stone, filter fabric, membrane protection, and outlet are buried |
| Pre-backfill inspection | Waterproofing, dampproofing, drains, wall bracing, and wall strength are accepted |

Use this readiness check before calling for inspection:

- Footings bear on accepted undisturbed soil or approved engineered fill.
- Loose soil, mud, standing water, debris, and ice are removed.
- Footing depth and frost-protection path match the approved plan.
- Forms are level, square, braced, and clean.
- Reinforcement size, spacing, lap, hook, grade, and cover match drawings.
- Anchor bolts, hold-downs, and embed plates are secured.
- Concrete-encased electrode work is coordinated before pour.
- Slope setbacks and excavation clearances are respected.
- Top of foundation and finished grade relationship match drainage requirements.
- Drain tile and waterproofing inspection timing is known before backfill.

Call the local building department early and ask:

- What inspections are required for this foundation type?
- Which inspections are combined?
- How far ahead do inspections need to be scheduled?
- What must be visible?
- What photos or reports are accepted?
- What cannot be covered before approval?

Do not schedule the truck before the inspection path is real.

**Professional**

Inspection readiness is a construction-control problem. Footing excavations, forms, reinforcement, vapor retarders, insulation, plumbing, soil-gas routing, concrete-encased electrodes, anchor bolts, drain tile, waterproofing, and backfill each have a concealment point.

The mistake is not only missing an inspection. The mistake is sequencing the site so the inspection becomes impossible without tearing work apart. A good foundation schedule puts inspection gates before delivery commitments.

**Code**

- ORSC R106: construction documents.
- ORSC R109.1.1: foundation inspection.
- ORSC R109.1.2: concrete slab or under-floor inspection where administered by the jurisdiction.
- ORSC R403: footing and foundation provisions.
- ORSC R404: foundation walls.
- ORSC R405: foundation drainage.
- ORSC R406: dampproofing and waterproofing.
- ORSC R401.3: surface drainage around foundations.
- OESC/NEC 250.52(A)(3): concrete-encased electrode.
- Marion County Residential Footing and Foundation Checklist: Oregon jurisdiction checklist model for footing and foundation readiness.
- Oregon BCD "Cite It, Write It" checklist: Oregon plan and inspection correction-reference model.

## 7.5 Excavation And Subgrade Acceptance

**Simple**

Concrete does not fix bad ground. It conceals it.

Strip the junk. Shape the pad. Fix the soft spots. Keep water out. Do not pour on mud.

**Applied**

Prepare the foundation area in this order:

1. Strip topsoil and organic material.
2. Remove roots, stumps, trash, old fill, debris, and soft pockets.
3. Excavate to the design elevation.
4. Keep excavation water out of the work.
5. Compare the exposed soil to the expected soil profile.
6. Repair wet spots, soft pockets, and disturbed zones.
7. Place fill only where the approved design allows it.
8. Compact fill in controlled lifts.
9. Verify the surface before covering it.
10. Protect the accepted surface from rain, rutting, freezing, and traffic.

Do not place concrete over:

- topsoil
- roots
- organic soil
- loose fill
- frozen soil
- standing water
- mud
- unapproved fill
- an excavation that does not match the design

If the ground changes, the foundation decision changes with it.

**Professional**

Subgrade acceptance should confirm elevation, bearing material, moisture condition, uniformity, compaction, drainage, and correction of localized defects. The goal is not only strength. The goal is predictable support across the footprint.

Compaction depends on lift thickness, equipment type, soil gradation, and moisture content near optimum. Thick lifts can mislead. The surface can feel hard while the lower half remains loose. Wet soil pumps under compaction. Dry soil bounces. Organic material decays and settles.

For engineered fill, field density testing, moisture-density relationship, placement records, and engineer acceptance may be required. Imported rock does not become engineered fill because a truck delivered it.

**Code**

- ORSC R401.4: soil tests where required.
- ORSC R403.1: foundations supported on undisturbed natural soils or engineered fill.
- ORSC R403.1.4: frost protection.
- ORSC R506.2.1: fill under concrete floors.
- ASTM D698: Standard Proctor moisture-density relationship.
- ASTM D1557: Modified Proctor moisture-density relationship.
- ASTM D6938: nuclear density and moisture testing, where used by qualified personnel.
- ASTM D1556: sand-cone density test, where used.

## 7.6 Compaction And Base Course

**Simple**

Loose soil keeps moving after you build on it. Compaction squeezes the particles together before the house gets there.

The slab requires steady support, not surprise settlement.

**Applied**

For compacted fill and base course:

- Place material in lifts.
- Match lift thickness to the equipment.
- Keep the material damp enough to compact.
- Do not soak it.
- Make overlapping passes.
- Compact edges and corners.
- Recheck low spots.
- Document each lift where fill is structural.

Typical field guide:

| Equipment | Better Use | Typical Lift Discipline |
|---|---|---|
| Hand tamper | Tiny repairs and corners | Very thin lifts only |
| Jumping jack rammer | Trenches and tight zones | Thin controlled lifts |
| Plate compactor | Granular fill and base course | Moderate lifts, overlapping passes |
| Roller | Larger pads | Requires room and a planned pattern |

Around foundation walls, slow down:

- Use granular backfill where specified.
- Keep lifts thin.
- Use lighter equipment near waterproofing.
- Do not let compaction push the wall.
- Do not rip the membrane.
- Do not crush the drain.

**Professional**

Compaction quality is a relationship between soil type, moisture content, maximum dry density, lift thickness, compactive effort, and verification method. Granular soils respond well to vibration. Cohesive soils often need kneading action and tighter moisture control.

Base course under slabs should create uniform support and drainage without creating a capillary path. Clean stone works as a capillary break and, where radon-ready construction applies, a gas-permeable collection layer. Dense-graded base can be useful for leveling and support but does not perform the same drainage function as clean open-graded stone.

The base should be flat enough to protect insulation and vapor retarder. Sharp rock under thin membrane is not durability.

**Code**

- ORSC R506.2.2: base for concrete floors on ground.
- ORSC R506.2.1: fill under concrete floors.
- ASTM D698: Standard Proctor.
- ASTM D1557: Modified Proctor.
- ASTM D6938: in-place density and moisture by nuclear gauge.
- ASTM D6951: Dynamic Cone Penetrometer, useful for comparative field screening when calibrated and interpreted correctly.
- Approved geotechnical report: controlling source for engineered fill requirements.

## 7.7 Slab Stack Order

**Simple**

A slab is not concrete on dirt.

From bottom to top, the lower layers support and drain. The middle layers control moisture, heat, and soil gas. The top layer is the concrete people see.

**Applied**

For a slab-on-grade in this Oregon concrete home, use this working order unless the approved design says otherwise:

| Order | Layer | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Compacted subgrade | Supports everything |
| 2 | Clean aggregate or approved base | Levels, supports, drains, and can collect soil gas |
| 3 | Soil-gas retarder where required | Part of the radon-ready system |
| 4 | Rigid insulation | Reduces heat loss and supports the slab |
| 5 | Vapor retarder | Stops slab moisture drive |
| 6 | Reinforcement, radiant tubing, sleeves, and embeds | Carries design and systems into the pour |
| 7 | Concrete slab or mat | Becomes the hard base of the house |

For the preferred path, place the vapor retarder directly below the slab. Do not put a sand blotter layer between the vapor retarder and concrete unless the approved design specifically accepts that moisture risk.

If radiant tubing is used, keep the vapor retarder intact below it. Radiant heat pushes moisture upward. A torn vapor retarder under a heated slab is a mistake with a heating system attached to it.

Use the stack as a checklist, not as a sketch:

| Variant | Added Detail | Field Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Standard conditioned slab | Concrete over vapor retarder over insulation over aggregate over subgrade | Vapor retarder sits directly under slab |
| Radiant slab | Tubing and reinforcement are coordinated above the vapor retarder | Pressure-test tubing before pour |
| Radon county slab | Gas-permeable layer, soil-gas retarder, passive vent pipe, sealed joints | Install vent pipe before slab, not later |
| Garage or heavy-load slab | Higher foam compressive resistance or local thickening | Do not let point loads crush insulation |
| Basement slab | Moisture, water table, sump, and wall joint matter more | Treat wall-to-slab joint as a water detail |

**Professional**

Slab layer order controls drying, curling, finishing, floor-covering moisture, radon collection, thermal performance, puncture risk, capillary movement, and construction sequencing. ACI 302 guidance now favors vapor retarder placement directly below the slab where moisture-sensitive floor finishes or conditioned space are involved.

Rigid insulation below the vapor retarder keeps the membrane close to the concrete and protects the insulation from the highest upward vapor drive. The aggregate layer below supports drainage and radon collection. In radon counties, soil-gas control has to be coordinated with vapor control rather than treated as a later add-on.

Some slab assemblies include second vapor barriers, sand blinding, or fixed foam choices. Use them only if the approved detail calls for them. The stack belongs in drawings and specifications, with materials and sequence recorded.

**Code**

- ORSC R506.1: concrete floor slab minimum construction.
- ORSC R506.2.2: base for concrete floors on ground.
- ORSC R506.2.3: vapor retarder below concrete floors on ground.
- ASTM E1745: plastic water vapor retarders used in contact with soil or granular fill under concrete slabs.
- ACI 302.1R: guide for concrete floor and slab construction.
- ACI 302.2R: guide for concrete slabs that receive moisture-sensitive flooring materials.
- ORSC Appendix AF: radon control methods in covered Oregon counties.

## 7.8 Vapor Retarder And Soil-Gas Control

**Simple**

Water vapor can move up through the ground and through concrete. Soil gas can also move up through cracks and gaps.

The slab assembly must block both before the concrete hides the work.

**Applied**

For conditioned space, use a durable vapor retarder:

- Use ASTM E1745 Class A material.
- Use at least the code minimum thickness.
- Consider 15 mil as the project durability default.
- Lap seams at least 6 inches.
- Tape seams with manufacturer-approved tape.
- Seal pipe penetrations.
- Turn the membrane up at walls and footings where the detail requires it.
- Patch tears before reinforcement covers them.
- Keep traffic off the membrane when possible.
- Photograph the membrane before the pour.

For Oregon radon counties, integrate radon into the slab work:

- Confirm whether the site is in Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, or Yamhill County.
- Install a gas-permeable aggregate layer.
- Install a soil-gas retarder.
- Install the passive vent pipe before the slab.
- Seal slab joints and penetrations.
- Cover sumps with gasketed lids where required.
- Route the vent so a fan can be added later.
- Provide future fan power where required.

Do not treat radon piping as a finish-stage task. It belongs below the slab.

**Professional**

Vapor and soil-gas control are continuity problems. The material matters, but performance depends on the seams, tears, edges, penetrations, pipe boots, joint sealants, sump covers, and wall transitions.

The vapor retarder and soil-gas retarder can be separate products, but a single higher-grade membrane can serve both roles when specified and detailed that way. The drawings should say exactly what function the membrane serves, where it laps, how it seals, and how damage gets repaired.

Radon-ready work is cheap before the pour and painful after the pour. The pipe, aggregate, membrane, slab seals, and future fan path should be treated as part of the foundation build.

**Code**

- ORS 455.365: Oregon statutory authority for radon construction standards.
- ORSC Appendix AF: radon control methods.
- ORSC AF103.2: gas-permeable layer.
- ORSC AF103.3: soil-gas retarder.
- ORSC AF103.4: sealing and caulking.
- ORSC AF103.6: vent pipe system.
- ORSC AF103.8: vent pipe termination and routing.
- ORSC AF103.12: power source for future fan where required.
- ORSC R506.2.3: vapor retarder under concrete floors on ground.
- ASTM E1745: vapor retarder material standard.

## 7.9 Under-Slab Insulation And Frost Protection

**Simple**

The ground can steal heat from the slab. Frost can move soil at the edge of the building.

Insulation helps with both, but only when it is the right material in the right place.

**Applied**

Before installing under-slab insulation:

- Confirm material type.
- Confirm compressive resistance.
- Confirm R-value.
- Confirm layer thickness.
- Stagger seams if using multiple layers.
- Keep boards tight.
- Protect edges from damage.
- Protect foam from traffic before concrete.
- Seal or trim around penetrations as detailed.
- Confirm slab-edge insulation and frost wings before backfill.

Do not substitute foam because the sticker R-value looks close. Under-slab foam also has to survive load, water, construction traffic, and time.

Use the compressive-strength conversation early:

| Location | Better Question Than "What R-Value?" |
|---|---|
| Normal living-area slab | Can the foam handle the slab and residential loads for the life of the building? |
| Garage or equipment pad | Can it handle point loads and vehicle loads without long-term creep? |
| Slab edge near walls | Is load transferred to footing or bearing soil instead of crushing insulation? |
| Frost wing | Can it survive wet soil, backfill, UV during construction, and future landscaping work? |
| Radiant slab | Is the R-value high enough that heat goes into the room, not the ground? |

For frost protection, confirm:

- Local frost depth or approved frost-protected shallow foundation path.
- Heated versus unheated space classification.
- Vertical insulation.
- Horizontal wing insulation where required.
- Corner insulation.
- Slope away from the foundation.
- Protection from UV and physical damage above grade.

**Professional**

Under-slab insulation affects heat loss, radiant slab response, slab support, compressive stress, long-term creep, moisture exposure, frost behavior, and edge thermal bridging. Average residential floor load is low, but wall lines, equipment pads, garage edges, point loads, and thickened slab zones require separate checks.

Frost-protected shallow foundation design depends on air freezing index, soil type, building heat flow, vertical insulation, horizontal insulation, corner conditions, snow cover, drainage, and whether the adjacent space is heated. Insulation wings are not decorative. They are part of the frost design.

Generous continuous insulation can be useful, but final material, thickness, and compressive resistance must come from the approved foundation and energy details.

**Code**

- ORSC R403.1.4: frost protection.
- ORSC R403.3: frost-protected shallow foundations for heated buildings.
- ASCE 32: Design and Construction of Frost-Protected Shallow Foundations.
- ORSC N1104.7: slab-on-grade floor insulation.
- ORSC Table N1101.1(1): Oregon residential envelope performance requirements.
- ASTM C578: rigid cellular polystyrene thermal insulation.
- ICC-ES Evaluation Report for selected insulation product: product-specific compliance path.

## 7.10 Reinforcement, Sleeves, Grounding, And Embeds

**Simple**

Concrete is hard to change after it cures. Put the important pieces in the right place first.

Rebar, anchors, sleeves, drains, conduit, radon pipe, radiant tubing, floor boxes, grounding, and blockouts all require planned locations before the pour.

**Applied**

Before concrete placement, use the drawings during a field walk:

- Forms are straight, braced, clean, and at the correct elevation.
- Subgrade or base is accepted.
- Vapor retarder is intact.
- Insulation is complete and protected.
- Reinforcement matches size, spacing, laps, hooks, and cover.
- Chairs or spacers hold reinforcement at the correct height.
- Dowels align with future walls.
- Anchor bolts and embed plates are secured.
- Hold-downs are tied in place where required.
- Plumbing sleeves are located and protected.
- Floor drains slope correctly.
- Radiant tubing is pressure-tested if installed.
- Electrical conduit and grounding electrode work are coordinated.
- Radon piping is installed where required or desired.
- Blockouts and penetrations are formed.
- Inspection approval is recorded.
- Curing materials are on site.

If it will be inside concrete, verify it before concrete.

**Professional**

The highest-risk pre-pour errors are reinforcement elevation, insufficient cover, missing dowels, wrong lap locations, misplaced anchor bolts, punctured vapor retarder, unsupported radiant tubing, blocked drains, sleeves cutting reinforcement, missing concrete-encased electrode coordination, and field changes that never reach the drawings.

Penetrations are structural and moisture events. A pipe sleeve can interrupt reinforcement, create a crack initiator, leak path, radon path, or termite path. Group penetrations only where the structural and waterproofing details allow it.

**Code**

- ORSC R403.1.3: footing reinforcement in seismic design categories where required.
- ORSC R403.1.6: foundation anchorage.
- ORSC R506: concrete floors on ground.
- ORSC R109.1.1 and R109.1.2: inspection before concealment.
- OESC/NEC 250.52(A)(3): concrete-encased electrode.
- ASTM A615/A615M: standard deformed reinforcing bar.
- ASTM A706/A706M: low-alloy reinforcing bar.
- Approved structural drawings: controlling source for bar size, spacing, cover, laps, dowels, embeds, anchors, and sleeves.

## 7.11 Basement Walls, Dampproofing, And Waterproofing

**Simple**

A basement wall holds back soil and keeps water out. Water looks for cracks, joints, pipe holes, and weak spots.

Dampproofing slows dampness. Waterproofing is for real water pressure. They are not the same thing.

**Applied**

Treat below-grade water control as a sequence:

1. Shape the site so surface water moves away.
2. Keep roof water away from the wall.
3. Prepare the wall surface.
4. Apply dampproofing or waterproofing as required.
5. Detail corners, seams, and penetrations.
6. Add protection board or drainage mat where specified.
7. Install perimeter drain at the footing.
8. Surround drain with clean stone.
9. Wrap stone with filter fabric where specified.
10. Connect drain to daylight or sump.
11. Inspect before backfill.
12. Backfill with material that will not destroy the system.

Do not backfill against exposed membrane without protection. Do not bury a crushed drain. Do not let downspouts dump beside the wall.

Use the right word for the water condition:

| Condition | Minimum Concept | Typical Materials Or Parts |
|---|---|---|
| Damp soil, no severe water condition | Dampproofing | Bituminous coating, acrylic-modified cement, surface-bonding cement, or approved waterproofing material |
| High water table or severe soil-water condition | Waterproofing | Code-listed waterproofing membrane or coating with detailed seams and penetrations |
| Masonry wall below grade | Parging plus dampproofing or waterproofing path | Exterior parge coat before coating where required |
| Basement with water risk | Waterproofing plus drainage | Membrane, protection, drain board, footing drain, sump or daylight |
| Pipe penetration | Sealed opening, not a smear of hope | Boot, collar, sealant, sleeve, or manufacturer-approved detail |

**Professional**

Dampproofing is the default treatment for below-grade walls that retain earth and enclose interior spaces where severe soil-water conditions are not present. Waterproofing is required where high water table or severe soil-water conditions are known. The distinction is not cosmetic. It changes the membrane standard, detailing burden, inspection attention, and failure consequences.

Waterproofing should be paired with drainage. A membrane that blocks water while the drain system fails becomes a pressure vessel. Dimple mat, drainage board, free-draining backfill, footing drain, cleanouts, and a reliable outlet reduce hydrostatic pressure before the wall carries unnecessary water load.

Hydrostatic pressure can enter through one weak detail.

**Code**

- ORSC R406.1: foundation dampproofing.
- ORSC R406.2: foundation waterproofing where high water table or severe soil-water conditions are known.
- ORSC R405.1: concrete or masonry foundation drains for habitable or usable spaces below grade.
- ORSC R405.2: sump pits where required.
- ASTM C887: surface-bonding cement where used under R406.1.
- Manufacturer installation instructions: controlling source for membrane surface preparation, primers, seams, terminations, penetrations, protection board, and temperature limits.

## 7.12 Foundation Drainage And Water Outlet

**Simple**

Water requires a discharge path.

If water reaches the foundation and cannot leave, drainage becomes a permanent maintenance problem.

**Applied**

Build drainage in layers:

| Drainage Layer | What It Does | Field Check |
|---|---|---|
| Site grading | Moves surface water away | Grade falls away from foundation |
| Roof drainage | Moves roof water away | Downspouts do not discharge at wall |
| Upslope interception | Catches water before it reaches house | Swales, drains, or ditches have outlet |
| Foundation drain | Collects water at footing | Pipe slope, stone, filter fabric, outlet |
| Drainage mat | Gives water a path down the wall | Mat reaches drain zone |
| Sump or daylight | Removes collected water | Outlet works and cannot freeze or clog |
| Sub-slab drainage where needed | Relieves water below slab | Connected to outlet or sump |

Daylight drainage is usually preferred when the site allows it. It has no motor, switch, battery, or alarm.

If a sump is required, make it a real system:

- Primary pump.
- Backup pump.
- Check valve.
- High-water alarm.
- Backup power.
- Accessible lid.
- Discharge away from the foundation.
- Maintenance schedule.

The sump is not a detail. It is a permanent machine keeping water out of the house.

**Professional**

Drainage reduces hydraulic load. It does not replace waterproofing, and waterproofing does not replace drainage. Free-draining backfill and drain board reduce the time water spends against the wall. Perforated pipe at or below the protected elevation collects water. Outlet design determines whether the system remains a drain or becomes a buried pond. Filter fabric reduces fines migration.

Clay backfill stores water and expands. Large rock can damage membrane. Silty soil can clog stone. Surface grading and roof discharge often matter more than the drain pipe because they control the volume of water delivered to the foundation zone.

**Code**

- ORSC R401.3: drainage.
- ORSC R405.1: foundation drainage.
- ORSC R405.2: sump pits.
- ORSC R406.2: waterproofing in severe soil-water conditions.
- ORSC R903.4: roof drainage and secondary drainage.
- Local stormwater requirements: control legal discharge location, erosion, dry wells, infiltration systems, and neighbor impacts.

## 7.13 Ordering Concrete And Reading Batch Tickets

**Simple**

The batch ticket is the receipt for the concrete. It records what the plant says it sent.

Read it before the concrete disappears into the work.

**Applied**

For every truck, photograph and save the batch ticket. Record:

- Supplier.
- Plant.
- Truck number.
- Ticket number.
- Mix design ID.
- Specified strength.
- Aggregate size.
- Slump target.
- Air target where specified.
- Admixtures.
- Batch time.
- Arrival time.
- Discharge start time.
- Discharge finish time.
- Cubic yards delivered.
- Water added at plant.
- Water added on site.
- Who approved any water added on site.

Reject or pause a load when:

- The mix design does not match the order.
- The truck is too late for the specification.
- Water was added without approval.
- Slump, air, or temperature is outside the accepted range.
- The concrete looks segregated, partially set, or otherwise wrong.

Concrete is cheaper in the truck than in the wrong place.

**Professional**

Ready-mix quality control depends on specified mix, batching accuracy, transport time, water addition, admixture timing, temperature, slump retention, air stability, and discharge sequence. The batch ticket is not a perfect proof of concrete quality, but it is the first project record tying the delivered material to the design.

High-performance durability goals require supplier coordination. Low water-cementitious ratio, supplementary cementitious materials, corrosion inhibitors, air entrainment, pumpability, finishing behavior, curing demand, and set time all affect placement. Do not order a theoretical mix that the crew cannot place.

**Code**

- ASTM C94/C94M: ready-mixed concrete.
- ACI 301: specifications for structural concrete where used by project documents.
- ACI 318 Chapter 26: construction documents and construction requirements for structural concrete.
- ACI 332: residential concrete.
- ASTM C143/C143M: slump test.
- ASTM C172/C172M: sampling freshly mixed concrete.
- Approved project specifications: controlling source for mix design, delivery limits, field water addition, and acceptance.

## 7.14 Pour Day Planning And Placement

**Simple**

Concrete day is not planning day.

Once the trucks arrive, the job moves at concrete speed.

**Applied**

One to three days before the pour:

- Confirm inspection approvals.
- Confirm mix design with supplier.
- Confirm truck count and delivery spacing.
- Confirm pump or chute access.
- Confirm crew roles.
- Confirm vibration tools work.
- Confirm screed points and elevations.
- Confirm finishing tools.
- Confirm test kit or testing lab.
- Confirm cylinder storage location.
- Confirm weather plan.
- Confirm curing method.
- Confirm cleanup water and washout location.

Just before the first truck:

- Photograph forms, reinforcement, embeds, vapor retarder, insulation, and sleeves.
- Record weather and start time.
- Walk the pour route.
- Keep testing tools near the discharge point.
- Keep curing materials ready.
- Decide who has authority to stop placement.

During placement:

- Place concrete close to its final location.
- Keep a steady sequence.
- Do not bury reinforcement out of position.
- Consolidate around bars, corners, sleeves, and embeds.
- Watch forms for movement.
- Watch anchor bolts and dowels.
- Do not add water without approval.
- Start curing as soon as the finish allows.

Keep a pour station separate from the finishing work:

- Clipboard or tablet for logs.
- Phone or camera for batch tickets and photos.
- Watch or timer.
- Thermometer.
- Slump cone and rod if testing.
- Air meter if testing air-entrained concrete.
- Cylinder molds if casting samples.
- Labels and marker.
- Shaded protected initial-cure spot for cylinders.
- Clean water for tools and safety.

**Professional**

Placement planning should account for access route, pump setup, hose path, truck spacing, lift height, cold joint risk, consolidation method, finishing sequence, lighting, weather, bleed water, set time, crew fatigue, and emergency stop criteria.

Concrete can fail because the design was wrong, but it can also fail because the site was not ready. A missing vibrator, delayed truck, rainstorm, blocked pump, or exhausted crew can turn good concrete into weak, porous, honeycombed concrete.

**Code**

- ORSC R109.1.1 and R109.1.2: inspection before placement where required.
- ACI 318 Chapter 26: construction requirements for structural concrete.
- ACI 332: residential concrete construction.
- ACI 301: project specification framework where used.
- ASTM C94/C94M: ready-mixed concrete delivery.
- Approved construction documents: control pour sequence, reinforcement, embed placement, testing, and inspection requirements.

## 7.15 Fresh Concrete Checks

**Simple**

Fresh concrete can look fine and still be wrong.

Testing checks whether the truck matches the order before the concrete becomes part of the house.

**Applied**

Use these field checks at major pours:

| Check | Standard | What It Tells You |
|---|---|---|
| Batch ticket | ASTM C94 framework | What the plant says was loaded |
| Slump | ASTM C143 | Workability and consistency |
| Sampling | ASTM C172 | Whether the test sample represents the load |
| Air content | ASTM C231 or C173 | Freeze-thaw air system where specified |
| Temperature | ASTM C1064 | Concrete condition and weather risk |
| Cylinders | ASTM C31 and C39 | Later compressive strength |
| Beams | ASTM C78 or C1609 | Flexural behavior where specified |

For each test, record:

- Pour ID.
- Truck number.
- Time.
- Location.
- Test result.
- Target range.
- Tester.
- Decision made.

Do not perform testing as theater. If a result fails, the project team must make a real decision before placement continues.

**Professional**

Testing frequency depends on whether the work is prescriptive residential concrete, engineered structural concrete, local inspection requirement, or a project-specific quality plan. ACI 332 does not automatically make every residential pour a laboratory-testing event. ACI 318 testing requirements become important where engineered concrete, project specifications, or authority requirements call for them.

For this project, the practical middle path is stronger than the minimum: use batch tickets for every truck, perform fresh checks on major pours, and cast cylinders for major structural placements when the project team accepts that plan. ACI Grade I Field Testing Technician certification is a useful owner-builder upgrade if the owner wants defensible field data.

For major pours, a practical voluntary testing set is:

- Slump on each truck or on the trucks selected by the testing plan.
- Air content on the first truck and when appearance or slump changes if air is specified.
- Concrete temperature at the first truck and when weather changes.
- One cylinder set for each major pour when testing is part of the plan.
- One early cylinder, two 28-day cylinders, and one hold cylinder when using a four-cylinder set.
- Laboratory breaks by a qualified lab.

This is not a claim that every Oregon residential pour requires lab testing. It is the project quality posture when the owner wants proof.

**Code**

- ASTM C143/C143M: slump of hydraulic-cement concrete.
- ASTM C172/C172M: sampling freshly mixed concrete.
- ASTM C231/C231M: air content by pressure method.
- ASTM C173/C173M: air content by volumetric method.
- ASTM C1064/C1064M: temperature of freshly mixed concrete.
- ASTM C31/C31M: making and curing concrete test specimens in the field.
- ASTM C39/C39M: compressive strength of cylindrical concrete specimens.
- ASTM C78/C78M: flexural strength by third-point loading.
- ASTM C1609/C1609M: flexural performance of fiber-reinforced concrete.
- ACI 318 Section 26.12: concrete evaluation and acceptance when the project path requires ACI 318 testing.

## 7.16 Consolidation, Finishing, And Curing

**Simple**

Placing concrete is not enough. It has to fill the space, wrap the steel, finish correctly, and cure without drying out too fast.

Concrete hardens through hydration, a chemical reaction with water. Keep enough moisture available for the curing method.

**Applied**

During placement and finishing:

- Place concrete close to its final location.
- Avoid long dragging that separates rock from paste.
- Vibrate walls, footings, and congested reinforcement where required.
- Do not over-vibrate.
- Watch corners, sleeves, embeds, and anchor zones.
- Screed slabs to the planned elevation.
- Let bleed water leave before final finishing.
- Do not add water to the surface to make finishing easier.
- Protect from rain, freezing, hot sun, wind, and early traffic.
- Start curing as soon as the finish allows.

Use a curing method chosen before the pour:

- wet curing
- curing compound
- plastic sheet
- insulated blankets
- evaporation reducer
- heated protection in cold weather
- other method approved for the mix and finish

Keep curing records. A smooth finish over poorly cured concrete is still poorly cured concrete.

**Professional**

Consolidation removes entrapped air and improves contact around reinforcement and embeds. Under-consolidation leaves voids, honeycombing, reduced bond, and leak paths. Over-consolidation can segregate aggregate, drive paste upward, and weaken uniformity.

Finishing and curing depend on mix design, slab thickness, ambient temperature, wind, humidity, sun exposure, admixtures, supplementary cementitious materials, floor finish, and schedule. Low water-cementitious ratio mixes and pozzolanic mixes can require more disciplined curing than ordinary residential flatwork.

Cold weather, hot weather, wind, and rapid drying all change the job. A tight schedule does not change curing, protection, or weather requirements.

**Code**

- ACI 318 Chapter 26: structural concrete construction requirements.
- ACI 332: residential concrete construction.
- ACI 301: specification framework for placement, finishing, and curing where used.
- ACI 302.1R: concrete floor and slab construction guidance.
- ASTM C94/C94M: ready-mixed concrete delivery and discharge.
- Manufacturer instructions: curing compounds, sealers, coatings, membranes, and floor-finish compatibility.

## 7.17 Backfill And Foundation Protection

**Simple**

Backfill can ruin good foundation work.

A machine can tear waterproofing, crush drains, push a wall, or dump the wrong soil against the house.

**Applied**

Before backfill:

- Dampproofing or waterproofing is complete.
- Membrane protection is installed where required.
- Drainage mat is in place.
- Perimeter drain is sloped and surrounded by stone.
- Filter fabric is placed where specified.
- Drain outlet or sump works.
- Pipe penetrations are sealed.
- Inspections are complete.
- Wall strength is ready for backfill.
- Bracing is installed where required.
- Backfill material matches the design.

During backfill:

- Place material in lifts.
- Keep heavy equipment away from walls.
- Use lighter compaction near waterproofing.
- Keep large rocks away from membranes.
- Do not crush drain pipe.
- Maintain grade away from the foundation.
- Keep roof water away from the backfill zone.

Do not make the foundation waterproof and then attack it with a loader.

**Professional**

Backfill creates lateral pressure and construction loads. Wet clay, expansive soil, frozen chunks, construction debris, and large rock can damage walls and waterproofing. Granular backfill drains better but still requires compaction control and filter separation where fines migration is possible.

Foundation walls may rely on floor diaphragms, slabs, or bracing for lateral support. Backfilling too early can move or crack the wall. The backfill plan should respect wall cure, wall support, waterproofing protection, drain function, compaction effort, and equipment stand-off distance.

**Code**

- ORSC R404: foundation and retaining wall design.
- ORSC R405: foundation drainage.
- ORSC R406: dampproofing and waterproofing.
- ORSC R109.1.1: inspection before concealment where required.
- ACI 318: structural concrete strength, construction, and loading considerations under engineered path.
- Manufacturer instructions: membrane protection, drain board, backfill timing, and compatible materials.

## 7.18 Pour Records And Foundation Acceptance

**Simple**

The foundation should be accepted before the house moves upward.

That means the concrete is placed, cured, inspected, documented, drained, protected, and ready for the next work.

**Applied**

Every major concrete pour should produce a one-page operational record plus attachments:

- Pour ID.
- Date.
- Location.
- Supplier.
- Mix design.
- Truck numbers.
- Batch ticket photos.
- Arrival and discharge times.
- Cubic yards placed.
- Weather.
- Concrete temperature.
- Slump results.
- Air results where measured.
- Cylinder or beam IDs where made.
- Water added on site.
- Placement notes.
- Consolidation notes.
- Finishing notes.
- Curing method.
- Photos before, during, and after placement.
- Problems and who approved the response.

Use one pour ID everywhere:

| Record | Same Pour ID Goes Here |
|---|---|
| Batch tickets | Ticket photo file name and binder entry |
| Field test record | Slump, air, temperature, sampling notes |
| Cylinder labels | Mold label and lab chain |
| Placement photos | Before, during, after |
| Curing log | Method, start time, duration, weather |
| Inspection record | Approval tied to the pour location |
| Correction note | Who approved a change and why |

Before starting wall construction:

- Inspection approvals are saved.
- Concrete records are filed.
- Test reports are pending or received.
- Anchor bolts, dowels, and embeds are located correctly.
- Slab and wall elevations are checked.
- Waterproofing and drains are protected.
- Cracks, voids, honeycombing, or defects are evaluated.
- The next trade has a clean layout surface.

If something is wrong, fix it while the foundation is still accessible.

**Professional**

Foundation acceptance is structural, moisture-related, and documentary. The work should match the drawings, inspection approvals, test records, as-built measurements, and field decisions. Any deviation should have a responsible decision attached to it.

The foundation record becomes part of the permanent house file. Future wall layout, remodels, waterproofing repairs, radon activation, equipment anchorage, resale review, and structural questions may depend on proof created before the first wall course is placed.

The concrete is permanent. The records should be permanent too.

**Code**

- ORSC R104: duties and powers of the building official.
- ORSC R106: construction documents.
- ORSC R109: inspections.
- ACI 318 Section 26.12: concrete evaluation and acceptance under the approved project path.
- ASTM C31/C31M: field specimens.
- ASTM C39/C39M: compressive strength reports.
- ASTM C143/C143M, C172/C172M, C231/C231M, C173/C173M, and C1064/C1064M: fresh concrete field test records.
- Approved permit documents: controlling source for final acceptance before the next concealed stage.
