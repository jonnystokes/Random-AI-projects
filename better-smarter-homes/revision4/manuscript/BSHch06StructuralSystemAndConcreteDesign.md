# Chapter 6: Structural System And Concrete Design

The structure turns the drawings into load-carrying work.

This house works best when the structure is plain in the right places: regular shape, stacked walls, clear load paths, reinforced concrete and masonry where they belong, service routes that do not damage the structure later, and drawings that match the building in the field.

Concrete provides mass, fire resistance, durability, and permanence. Masonry provides wall geometry and repeatability. Steel reinforcement provides tensile capacity. Engineering makes all of it legal, inspectable, and defensible.

A heavy house is not automatically a strong house. Weight without a load path is just a more expensive mistake.

## 6.1 The Structural Goal

**Simple**

A house must carry weight down to the ground and resist forces from the side. Gravity pulls down. Wind and earthquakes push sideways. Soil and water push against basement walls. People, furniture, snow, and the roof all add load.

The goal is not vague strength. The goal is a structure that can be traced.

**Applied**

Before the structural design is finished, make the load path visible on paper:

- Mark the bearing walls.
- Mark the floor supports.
- Mark the roof supports.
- Mark the stair openings.
- Mark the large window and door openings.
- Mark the wall lines that resist wind and earthquake forces.
- Mark where the foundation receives the loads.
- Mark every planned utility route through or around structure.

Then ask the hard question for each load: where does it go next?

If the answer is not obvious, the design is not ready.

**Professional**

The structural system requires a complete gravity and lateral load path. Dead load, live load, roof live load, snow load, wind load, seismic load, soil pressure, hydrostatic pressure, and construction loads have to be collected, transferred, resisted, and anchored.

For this project, the preferred structural language is regularity: aligned bearing walls, continuous reinforced wall lines, floor diaphragms tied to wall elements, bond beams or ring beams at key levels, controlled openings, and foundation elements designed from site-specific ground information.

The building should be easy to trace in section. Roof to wall. Floor to wall. Wall to foundation. Foundation to soil. Lateral force to diaphragm. Diaphragm to shear wall. Shear wall to foundation.

Structure should be explainable in the drawings and visible in the field.

**Code**

- ORSC R301: design criteria for residential buildings.
- ORSC R301.1.3: engineered design or nonprescriptive design path when structural elements exceed prescriptive limits.
- ORSC R301.2: location-specific climatic and geographic design criteria.
- ORSC R301.2.2: seismic provisions.
- ORSC R301.5: minimum uniformly distributed live loads.
- ASCE 7: minimum design loads and load combinations where referenced by the adopted code or engineered design path.
- Oregon Design Criteria Hub: Oregon site-specific design criteria source used with the ORSC.

## 6.2 Engineered Design Is The Default

**Simple**

This is a concrete and masonry house. That is not the same thing as a normal prescriptive wood house with different wall material.

The safe assumption is simple: the structural system gets engineered.

**Applied**

Give the structural engineer a clean package instead of a loose concept:

- Site address and tax lot.
- Soil and ground information from the soil chapter work.
- Proposed footprint.
- Number of levels.
- Roof type.
- Floor type.
- Wall assembly concept.
- Basement or crawl space decision.
- Stair and large-opening locations.
- Service-layer concept.
- Desired solar, roof-deck, or rooftop-equipment loads.
- Target durability goals.
- Any materials the owner wants to use or avoid.

Do this early. Engineering should shape the design before the floor plan is committed.

**Professional**

Most concrete and CMU versions of this house in Oregon should be treated as engineered design under ORSC R301.1.3. Prescriptive values are useful references, but they should not be assembled into a structural recipe for a nonstandard concrete home.

The governing issues are seismic design category, above-grade concrete wall provisions, reinforced masonry limits, diaphragm irregularities, nonstandard wall assemblies, concrete floor systems, roof mass, foundation-soil interaction, and the two-wythe wall classification. Any one of those can move the project out of simple prescriptive construction. Together, they make engineering the normal path.

Prescriptive tables still matter. They give vocabulary, minimum expectations, and sanity checks. They do not replace the engineer of record when the actual house is outside their limits.

**Code**

- ORSC R301.1.3: structural elements exceeding ORSC prescriptive limits must be designed by accepted engineering practice.
- ORSC R301.2.2.5: concrete construction provisions in seismic categories where above-grade concrete walls require PCA 100 or ACI 318 design path.
- ORSC R301.2.2.6: irregular building conditions that trigger engineered analysis.
- ORSC R608: prescriptive exterior above-grade concrete wall provisions, limited by scope and seismic category.
- ORSC R606: prescriptive masonry wall provisions, limited by scope, height, reinforcement, and seismic category.
- PCA 100: prescriptive design of exterior concrete walls for one- and two-family dwellings when applicable.
- ACI 318: structural concrete design standard.
- ACI 332: residential concrete standard.
- TMS 402 and TMS 602: masonry design and construction standards.

## 6.3 Shape, Grid, And Load Path

**Simple**

The easiest house to make strong is the one where the walls line up. Weight travels down like a stack of books.

The harder house is the one where an upstairs wall sits over empty space. That can be done. It just stops being simple.

**Applied**

Start with a square or near-square footprint unless the land, view, sunlight, or program gives a real reason not to. A clean grid makes the structure easier to understand, engineer, inspect, and build.

Use this grid check while sketching:

| Question | Good Sign | Warning Sign |
|---|---|---|
| Do upper walls land on lower walls? | Most wall lines stack | Walls float over open rooms |
| Are large rooms planned early? | Beams, slabs, or openings are designed | Bearing walls get deleted late |
| Are stairs located early? | Openings are trimmed into the structure | Holes are cut after the slab is designed |
| Do utilities have routes? | Chases and service zones are drawn | Holes are drilled wherever convenient |
| Does the roof load land cleanly? | Supports line up with wall lines | Roof loads jump through hidden transfers |
| Can an inspector see the system? | Reinforcement and anchors are visible before cover | Critical work is buried too soon |

Do not move a bearing wall in the field because it makes a room feel better. Move it on the drawings, with the load path redrawn.

**Professional**

Regular geometry reduces torsional response, simplifies diaphragm load distribution, improves collector and chord continuity, and reduces transfer girder requirements. It also helps masonry work: coursing is cleaner, openings repeat, reinforcement is easier to inspect, and grout paths are less congested.

Structural regularity is not only about appearance. It reduces the number of places where force has to change direction. Every load transfer is a design event. Transfer beams, discontinuous shear walls, reentrant corners, large diaphragm openings, offset wall lines, and irregular stiffness all require more analysis and more field discipline.

The best split is simple structural logic with livable rooms above it.

**Code**

- ORSC R301.2.2.6: irregular building provisions, including diaphragm discontinuities and other irregularities.
- ORSC R301.3: story height limits and design implications.
- ORSC R301.5: live loads used for floors, balconies, decks, guards, attics, and similar areas.
- ASCE 7: seismic and wind load analysis where the engineered path is used.
- Approved structural drawings: controlling source for load path, wall layout, diaphragms, collectors, chords, anchors, and hold-downs.

## 6.4 Concrete, Masonry, And Steel Roles

**Simple**

Concrete carries compression well. Steel carries tension well. Masonry block defines the wall geometry. Grout locks reinforcement inside the block.

None of those parts is the whole structure by itself. The system works when the parts work together.

**Applied**

Use each material for the job it does best:

| Material | What It Does | What To Check Before It Gets Hidden |
|---|---|---|
| Ready-mix concrete | Slabs, walls, footings, beams, mass | Ticket, slump, placement time, consolidation, curing |
| Reinforcing bar | Tension, crack control, continuity | Size, spacing, laps, hooks, cover, support |
| CMU block | Wall geometry and compression | Layout, plumb, bond pattern, clean cells |
| Grout | Bonds rebar into masonry cells | Mix, lift height, flow, consolidation, cleanouts |
| Mortar | Bonds masonry courses | Type, joint thickness, tooling, weather protection |
| Wall ties | Connect wythes or facing | Material, spacing, embedment, corrosion path |
| Embed plates and anchors | Connect later work to structure | Location, tolerance, protection, inspection |

Most structural failures start small. A bar is missing. A cell is dirty. A bolt moves. A wall gets grouted before anyone looks inside. Check the work while it is still fixable.

**Professional**

Reinforced concrete depends on strain compatibility between concrete and steel. Concrete carries compression. Reinforcement carries tension and controls crack width when detailed with proper development length, splice length, cover, support, and confinement. The concrete must also protect the reinforcement from carbonation, chloride exposure, moisture, and fire.

Reinforced masonry depends on the same basic idea with different parts. The masonry unit, mortar, grout, vertical bars, horizontal bars, bond beams, lintels, dowels, and diaphragm anchors have to create one designed wall system. Hollow block is not automatically a reinforced wall. Structural capacity comes from the designed reinforced cells, grouted bond beams, lintels, anchors, and connections.

Steel belongs in the building where it improves the system: reinforcement, anchors, embeds, joists, beams, deck, grating, connection plates, and roof attachments. It should be specified for corrosion exposure and connected so it can be inspected.

**Code**

- ACI 318: reinforced concrete design, detailing, development, cover, exposure, and anchorage.
- ACI 332: residential concrete construction and design provisions.
- ASTM A615/A615M: deformed carbon-steel reinforcing bars.
- ASTM A706/A706M: low-alloy reinforcing bars with weldability and ductility advantages.
- ASTM C94/C94M: ready-mixed concrete.
- ASTM C150/C150M: portland cement.
- ASTM C260/C260M: air-entraining admixtures.
- ASTM C494/C494M: chemical admixtures.
- ASTM C90: loadbearing concrete masonry units.
- ASTM C476: grout for masonry.
- TMS 402: masonry design.
- TMS 602: masonry construction specification.

## 6.5 The Wall Assembly

**Simple**

The wall idea has three jobs:

- The inside wall carries the house.
- The middle space insulates and manages moisture.
- The outside face protects the building and gives it character.

Do not count the outside face as structural unless the drawings say so.

**Applied**

Before masonry begins, mark the wall assembly in the drawings:

- Inner structural wythe.
- Insulation cavity.
- Drainage air space where required.
- Exterior wythe, facing, or veneer.
- Wall tie type and spacing.
- Reinforced cells.
- Grouted cells.
- Bond beam courses.
- Lintels over openings.
- Weeps, flashing, and drainage paths.
- Utility chases that are allowed.
- Utility chases that are forbidden.

On site, check the wall before grout:

- Dowels land in the correct cells.
- Vertical bars are tied and centered.
- Horizontal bars are in the bond beams.
- Cells are clean enough for grout.
- Ties are embedded and spaced correctly.
- Insulation is not crushed, missing, or blocking drainage.
- Flashing and weeps are installed before they become unreachable.

The wall is where durability, structure, insulation, and water control all meet. It requires careful planning before production masonry begins.

**Professional**

The cleanest structural classification for this wall concept is a hybrid engineered wall assembly: reinforced masonry structural wythe, insulated cavity, and exterior wythe or facing that carries its own self-weight and weather role unless the engineer assigns it more. That avoids treating the assembly as a simple veneer and avoids claiming composite action that the insulation cavity prevents.

If the wythes are expected to act compositely, the collar joint, tie system, shear transfer, and absence of insulation between acting wythes become central design issues. If the wythes are noncomposite, each wythe is designed for its assigned load, and ties transfer out-of-plane forces without assuming full composite behavior.

The name matters. "Cavity wall" alone is too vague. "Veneer" may be too narrow. "Reinforced masonry, noncomposite cavity wall, engineered design per TMS 402" is clearer. The engineer of record should make the final classification in the permit drawings.

**Design Alternative: FRP Ties**

FRP ties can reduce thermal bridging. They are not the default for this book.

Use stainless steel ties as the default structural and durability path. Treat FRP ties as an engineered alternate requiring manufacturer data, ICC-ES documentation where available, engineer approval, and building official acceptance. A thermal upgrade that cannot pass plan review is not ready for construction.

**Code**

- TMS 402 Chapter 5: composite and noncomposite masonry wall behavior.
- TMS 402 Chapter 12: masonry veneer when the exterior wythe is designed as veneer.
- TMS 602: construction requirements for masonry.
- ORSC R606: masonry construction provisions.
- ORSC R301.1.3: engineered design where the assembly is outside prescriptive limits.
- ASTM A580/A580M or ASTM A240/A240M: stainless steel material paths for corrosion-resistant tie components.
- ASTM A951/A951M: steel wire joint reinforcement.
- ICC-ES Evaluation Reports: approval path for proprietary or alternate tie systems.
- ORSC R104.11: alternate materials, design, and methods of construction, where accepted by the building official.

## 6.6 Wall Ties, Corrosion, And Thermal Bridges

**Simple**

Wall ties are small, but they matter. They help keep the outside wall connected to the wall behind it.

A tiny part can become a big problem if it rusts away inside a wall.

**Applied**

Use a tie schedule on the drawings. Do not leave tie choice to jobsite shopping.

For the default wall assembly:

- Use stainless steel ties unless the engineer approves another system.
- Use Type 316 stainless near coastal salt or high-chloride exposure.
- Keep the tie type consistent. Do not mix stainless parts with carbon steel parts where they touch.
- Install ties at the specified spacing.
- Keep ties embedded in the mortar or grout as required.
- Keep insulation tight around ties without leaving drainage blocked.
- Photograph tie installation before the cavity closes.

If someone proposes FRP or thermally broken ties, ask for:

- Product data.
- Structural capacity.
- Fire behavior.
- Corrosion data.
- ICC-ES report or equivalent approval path.
- Engineer approval.
- Building official acceptance before installation.

**Professional**

The tie system must resist out-of-plane load, maintain wythe spacing, tolerate differential movement, and survive the exposure environment. Stainless steel reduces corrosion risk but still needs compatible fasteners, anchors, and joint reinforcement. Galvanic coupling between stainless and carbon steel can make corrosion worse at the wrong contact point.

Tie stiffness also affects movement. A tie that is too flexible may not provide the intended lateral support. A tie that is too rigid can restrain differential movement and crack finishes or masonry. Thermal bridging is real, but it is not the first design variable. Structural capacity, corrosion resistance, embedment, movement, fire behavior, and approval path come first.

**Code**

- TMS 402: tie spacing, tie size, and multi-wythe masonry provisions.
- TMS 602: masonry construction and installation requirements.
- ASTM A580/A580M: stainless steel wire.
- ASTM A240/A240M: stainless steel plate, sheet, and strip.
- ASTM A951/A951M: masonry joint reinforcement.
- ICC-ES AC04 or AC11: acceptance criteria commonly used for masonry anchors and ties, depending on product type.
- ICC-ES ESR for proprietary thermally broken or FRP tie products: required documentation when using a nonstandard system.

## 6.7 Reinforcement, Grout, And Bond Beams

**Simple**

Rebar only works when it is in the right place. Grout only works when it fills the right spaces.

A wall can look finished from the outside and still be wrong inside.

**Applied**

Before grout day, walk the wall slowly:

- Compare bar size to the drawings.
- Compare bar spacing to the drawings.
- Check that dowels land in the correct cells.
- Check that bars are tied so they will not drift.
- Check lap locations and lap lengths.
- Check bond beam bars.
- Check lintel bars.
- Check jamb reinforcement at openings.
- Check cleanouts where required.
- Remove mortar droppings from cells.
- Confirm grout type and delivery.
- Confirm inspection approval before grout.
- Keep a camera on the job. Photograph before cover.

During grout:

- Place in lifts that match the approved plan.
- Consolidate without moving reinforcement.
- Watch for blowouts.
- Watch for grout bridging.
- Stop if cells are not filling.
- Do not hide a bad lift under the next lift.

**Professional**

Reinforced masonry capacity depends on the specified compressive strength of masonry, grout strength, bar size, bar spacing, development length, lap splice length, clearances, cover, wall height, axial load, out-of-plane demand, in-plane shear, and seismic detailing. Bar placement tolerance matters because the effective depth of reinforcement drives flexural capacity.

Bond beams are not decorative. They provide horizontal reinforcement, distribute diaphragm reactions, tie wall segments together, and create collectors or chords where designed. Lintels over openings require bearing, shear, flexure, deflection, and continuity checks. Jamb bars have to connect opening loads back into the wall.

Grout lift height is not just a convenience issue. Tall lifts increase pressure on masonry, increase the chance of voids, and make consolidation harder. Cleanout requirements, special inspection triggers, and cell cleanliness can determine whether the wall is accepted.

**Code**

- ORSC R606: masonry construction.
- ORSC R606.2.8.3: mortar requirements in seismic design categories where lateral resistance matters.
- ORSC R606.3.5.1: grout placement and lift-height requirements.
- TMS 402 Section 6.1.6: reinforcement development and lap splice requirements.
- TMS 602: masonry construction, placement, tolerances, and grouting.
- ASTM C476: grout for masonry.
- ASTM C270: mortar for unit masonry.
- ASTM A615/A615M: standard deformed reinforcing bar.
- ASTM A706/A706M: low-alloy deformed reinforcing bar.

## 6.8 Foundation Interface In Structural Terms

**Simple**

The foundation transfers house loads into the ground. The structure above can be excellent, but the bearing material below still has to support the load.

Concrete day is too late to debate the soil.

**Applied**

Keep the foundation discussion split:

- The soil work documents what the ground can support.
- The structural drawings show how loads reach the foundation.
- The foundation and concrete placement material explains the build sequence.
- This chapter checks that the system connects.

Before foundation concrete, verify:

- Bearing surface is accepted.
- Excavation matches drawings.
- Thickened edges, grade beams, ribs, piers, or footings match drawings.
- Dowels match the wall layout.
- Anchor bolts and embeds match the wall and roof plan.
- Sleeves do not cut required reinforcement.
- Radon, drainage, plumbing, and grounding work are coordinated.
- Inspection approval is recorded before placement.

If a sleeve, pipe, trench, or last-minute change cuts the load path, stop and ask before pouring.

**Professional**

Foundation design is the connection between geotechnical assumptions and structural demand. Bearing pressure, differential settlement, frost depth, expansive soil movement, groundwater, lateral earth pressure, sliding, overturning, uplift, and seismic load transfer all meet at the foundation.

A mat or raft concept can be useful where loads are distributed and differential settlement control matters, but the phrase "mat slab" does not define the design. A mat requires checks for flexure, one-way shear, punching shear at concentrated loads, edge beams, subgrade modulus, reinforcement continuity, construction joints, penetrations, and concrete shrinkage.

The foundation should not be treated as a concrete floor that got thicker. It is a structural member in contact with imperfect ground.

**Code**

- ORSC R401.4: soil tests where required by the building official.
- ORSC R403: footings.
- ORSC R403.1.4: frost protection.
- ORSC R403.1.8: expansive soil provisions.
- ORSC R404: foundation and retaining walls.
- ORSC R405: foundation drainage.
- ORSC R406: foundation dampproofing and waterproofing.
- ACI 318 Chapter 13: foundations.
- ACI 332: residential concrete foundations and slabs.
- ASCE 32: frost-protected shallow foundation design where used.

## 6.9 Floor System And Diaphragm Strategy

**Simple**

Floors do more than hold people and furniture. In a concrete or masonry house, floors can help tie the walls together.

A floor is a plate in the structure, not just a surface to walk on.

**Applied**

Choose the floor family before the plans get detailed:

| Floor Family | Best Use | Watch First |
|---|---|---|
| Cast-in-place reinforced concrete slab | Shorter spans, full concrete philosophy, custom shape | Formwork, weight, shoring, reinforcement congestion |
| Hollow-core precast plank with concrete topping | Medium spans, faster build, strong concrete identity | Crane access, bearing, shop drawings, topping connection |
| Concrete on metal deck with steel beams or joists | Longer spans or hybrid practicality | Corrosion, fire rating, beam depth, vibration, connections |
| Precast joist or double-tee style system | Specialized spans and supplier-driven design | Availability, cost, transport, bearing details |
| Wood I-joist or LVL with concrete topping | Transitional or budget-driven design | Least aligned with concrete-house philosophy |

For this concrete house, a strong default direction is hollow-core precast with cast-in-place topping where spans and site access fit. Cast-in-place concrete is the clean alternative where spans are shorter and formwork makes sense.

Keep these floor-system ideas in the design conversation, but do not treat preliminary dimensions as final design:

| Floor-System Idea | Useful Intent | Required Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Block-on-side course at floor levels | A continuous ring element at floors is a good structural instinct | Use an engineered bond beam, ring beam, bearing detail, or manufacturer-approved plank bearing detail |
| Ribbed cast-in-place slab | Ribs can reduce span demand and add stiffness | Engineer the rib depth, spacing, shear, deflection, and reinforcement |
| Steel joists assisting a concrete floor | Hybrid systems can solve longer spans | Treat as structural steel design with fire, corrosion, vibration, and connection details |
| Fiber-reinforced floor slab | Fibers can improve crack behavior and toughness | Do not let fibers replace primary reinforcement unless engineered and approved |
| High floor load ambition | Future flexibility is a real design value | State the target loads to the engineer instead of inventing slab thickness |

Before choosing, check:

- Clear span.
- Bearing wall locations.
- Crane or pump access.
- Floor openings.
- Service-layer needs.
- Diaphragm action.
- Fire and sound goals.
- Final load goals.
- Shop drawing requirements.

Do not cut big holes through a concrete floor after the fact. Plan openings before reinforcement or planks are placed.

**Professional**

The floor system must satisfy gravity support and lateral diaphragm behavior. Cast-in-place one-way or two-way slabs, hollow-core planks with composite topping, composite metal deck systems, and precast systems each handle continuity differently.

Hollow-core plank can give excellent durability and speed, but diaphragm action depends on topping, grouted keyways, chord reinforcement, collector detailing, bearing length, and connection to walls or bond beams. Cast-in-place slabs give monolithic continuity but require formwork, shoring, deflection checks, punching or one-way shear checks, crack control, and construction-joint planning.

Fiber reinforcement can help plastic shrinkage cracking, impact resistance, abrasion resistance, and toughness. It does not automatically replace primary flexural reinforcement. Fibers are a supplement unless the engineer of record designs and accepts them as part of the structural system.

**Code**

- ORSC R301.5: residential live loads.
- ORSC R301.2.2.6: diaphragm irregularity triggers.
- ACI 318: structural concrete slabs, beams, diaphragms, development, shear, and anchorage.
- ACI 332: residential concrete when the final floor or slab detail fits its scope.
- ACI 544.4R: guide for design with fiber-reinforced concrete.
- ASTM C1609/C1609M: flexural performance of fiber-reinforced concrete.
- ASTM C39/C39M: compressive strength of cylindrical concrete specimens.
- ASTM C78/C78M: flexural strength of concrete using simple beam with third-point loading.
- Approved shop drawings: controlling source for precast plank layout, bearing, topping, lifting, camber, and connection details.

## 6.10 Openings, Stairs, And Structural Interruptions

**Simple**

A stair is not just a stair. It is a hole through a floor.

The same is true for big windows, doors, shafts, and mechanical openings. Every opening interrupts something.

**Applied**

Locate structural openings early:

- Stairs.
- Elevator or future lift shafts.
- Large windows.
- Exterior doors.
- Mechanical shafts.
- Plumbing risers.
- Chimney or flue paths.
- Roof drains.
- Solar or equipment penetrations.

For each opening, mark:

- Size.
- Location in the grid.
- Nearby bearing walls.
- Reinforcement around the edge.
- Header, lintel, or trim requirement.
- Guard and handrail needs.
- Fire and smoke separation needs.
- Furniture movement path.

A compact stair can save floor area and create decades of inconvenience. A generous stair costs space but makes moving, aging, maintenance, and daily life easier.

Use stair type as a livability and structure decision, not just a shape preference:

| Stair Type | Why It Works | What To Watch |
|---|---|---|
| Straight stair | Simple framing, easy to understand, easy to carry loads around | Long run of floor area |
| L-shaped stair | Fits corners and gives a landing | More edge reinforcement and landing coordination |
| U-shaped stair | Compact in plan, good for stacked circulation | Larger opening block and more trim reinforcement |
| Zigzag or scissor stair | Flexible for unusual plans | More landings, more openings, more coordination |
| Spiral or circular stair | Very small footprint | Poor furniture movement and often not acceptable as the main stair |

**Professional**

Openings interrupt diaphragm shear flow, slab reinforcement, chord continuity, collector paths, wall shear capacity, and local gravity load distribution. Large openings require trim reinforcement, edge beams, chord bars, collector detailing, lintels, jamb reinforcement, anchorage, and crack-control detailing.

In concrete diaphragms, openings may require diagonal bars, boundary reinforcement, additional bars equivalent to interrupted reinforcement, and collector/chord design around the opening. In reinforced masonry walls, lintels and jambs have to transfer load around the opening and back into the reinforced wall lines.

Do not let the stair designer, window schedule, HVAC layout, and structural drawings solve the same hole four different ways.

**Code**

- ORSC R301.2.2.6: irregularity triggers for diaphragm openings and structural discontinuities.
- ORSC R311.7: stairways.
- ORSC R311.7.5: treads and risers.
- ORSC R311.7.8: handrails.
- ORSC R312: guards.
- ORSC R310: emergency escape and rescue openings where sleeping rooms require them.
- ACI 318: slab openings, reinforcement, anchorage, and diaphragm design under the engineered path.
- TMS 402: masonry lintels, reinforced masonry wall design, and opening reinforcement.

## 6.11 Service Layer Without Structural Confusion

**Simple**

The service layer is for pipes, wires, ducts, controls, and future repair. It should protect the structure from later damage.

It is not unclassified extra space. The permit drawings have to say what it is.

**Applied**

A crawlable service layer between living levels can make pipes, ducts, wiring, and controls reachable without cutting structure later. The problem is classification. A big service space can start looking like a story, attic, mezzanine, crawl space, or mechanical level depending on height, access, and use.

Choose the code identity before drawing the final section:

| Service Strategy | What It Means | Main Tradeoff |
|---|---|---|
| Shallow concealed service zone | Limited height, access panels, not a walk-in space | Harder maintenance, cleaner story treatment |
| Designed mechanical level | Real access, lighting, ventilation, support, fire treatment | May affect story count and cost |
| Crawl-space style service area below lowest occupied floor | Utility access below the main floor | Requires crawl-space code treatment |
| Middle-height crawlable zone between floors | Tempting for maintenance | Plan-review trap unless classified clearly |

Do not rely on a non-habitable label without plan-review support. Put the intended classification on the drawings.

If a crawlable service layer survives plan review, detail it like a real maintenance space:

- Show access hatches or doors.
- Show lighting and service receptacles where required.
- Show the walkway or grating support.
- Label the walkway as maintenance access if it is not part of the primary structure.
- Use noncombustible grating where practical.
- Isolate rattles and thermal movement at metal supports.
- Keep plumbing, ducts, wiring, and controls reachable.
- Keep required electrical working space clear.
- Fireblock and draftstop the concealed spaces.
- Photograph every service run before closing the ceiling.

**Professional**

The service layer intersects structural design in three ways. First, it affects story-height and building-height interpretation. Second, it creates penetrations, supports, hangers, access openings, and fireblocking conditions. Third, it can create a false sense that utilities are structurally harmless.

Utility support loads, suspended equipment, pipe movement, duct vibration, condensate, firestopping, access panels, working clearances, and maintenance platforms need assigned structure. Catwalks and grating should be nonstructural maintenance elements unless designed otherwise. Hangers should attach to approved structure, not random masonry webs or field-drilled anchors in critical zones.

A 4 to 5 foot service-layer concept should be treated as a design option needing plan-review confirmation, not as a guaranteed way to add invisible maintenance space.

**Code**

- ORSC R202: definitions of story, habitable space, attic, and related terms.
- ORSC R302.11: fireblocking in concealed spaces.
- ORSC R302.12: draftstopping.
- ORSC R305: ceiling height.
- ORSC R310: emergency escape and rescue openings when sleeping rooms require them.
- ORSC R311: means of egress.
- ORSC R408: under-floor space and crawl space requirements.
- OMSC provisions: mechanical equipment access, working space, duct routing, and plenum restrictions.
- OESC provisions: electrical working space and access for equipment.

## 6.12 Roof Structure Choice

**Simple**

The roof sees sun, rain, wind, snow, heat, cold, and maintenance traffic every year.

The roof must be strong, drainable, repairable, and clear about its service life.

**Applied**

Keep three roof families in the design conversation:

| Roof Family | Why It Fits | What Can Go Wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete deck with protected membrane roof | Best match for long-life concrete structure, replaceable membrane, rooftop equipment or solar potential | Heavy, requires slope, overflow drainage, leak detection, careful detailing |
| Concrete deck with sloped metal roof above | Keeps concrete as structure, sheds water more visibly | More parts, more height, more coordination, more maintenance points |
| Engineered sloped truss or steel roof on concrete walls | Familiar to trades, lighter, cheaper | Shorter roof-life path if cheap roofing is used, more material mismatch |

The default direction for this house should be concrete deck with protected membrane roof unless site, snow, budget, or local construction conditions indicate a better option. The membrane is replaceable. The concrete deck remains the structural roof.

Before choosing, check:

- Snow load.
- Wind uplift.
- Seismic mass.
- Drain locations.
- Overflow scuppers or secondary drains.
- Solar mounting.
- Rooftop equipment.
- Maintenance access.
- Insulation location.
- Leak detection and future replacement.

Roof drainage and waterproofing require specific details. Water collects at low spots, weak terminations, and missing overflow paths.

Keep these roof families in the design conversation:

| Roof Family | Useful Intent | Required Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete roof slab with parapet and internal drains | Strong match for long-life structure and rooftop use | Engineer the deck, slope, drains, overflow, parapet, guard, waterproofing, and maintenance access |
| Steel deck or metal roof on engineered supports | Lighter, familiar, easier to shed water | Engineer uplift, corrosion, condensation, acoustics, fasteners, and solar attachment |

**Professional**

A concrete roof deck changes structural demand through dead load, seismic mass, diaphragm stiffness, parapet force, ponding risk, thermal movement, waterproofing continuity, and equipment load. A protected membrane roof can be durable because the membrane and insulation can be renewed while the deck remains structural.

Low-slope concrete roofs require positive drainage, secondary drainage, ponding stability review, membrane termination detailing, parapet movement detailing, roof-drain coordination, and maintenance access. If the roof is occupiable, the live load, guard loads, walking surface, egress, fall protection, and waterproofing system all change.

Standing-seam metal over a concrete deck can simplify water shedding but adds attachment, condensation, thermal movement, and corrosion questions. A conventional framed roof can reduce first cost but moves the project away from the long-life structural goal.

**Code**

- ORSC R301.2: wind, snow, seismic, and climate design values.
- ORSC R301.5: roof live loads and related live-load criteria.
- ORSC R301.6: roof load provisions.
- ORSC R903: weather protection for roofs.
- ORSC R903.4: roof drainage and secondary drainage where required.
- ORSC R905: roof covering provisions by material and slope.
- ASCE 7: snow load, wind uplift, seismic load, and ponding stability where engineered design applies.
- IPC 1106 or adopted plumbing code roof-drain sizing provisions.
- Approved roof assembly listing and manufacturer instructions: controlling source for membrane, fasteners, flashing, and warranty requirements.

## 6.13 Concrete Durability And Mix Discipline

**Simple**

Concrete can be strong and still be bad concrete.

Too much water, poor curing, bad consolidation, or missing cover can turn an expensive pour into a future repair. The truck does not deliver permanence. The whole process does.

**Applied**

Before any structural pour, know the concrete order:

- Specified compressive strength.
- Maximum water-cementitious ratio if specified.
- Slump range.
- Air content where freeze-thaw exposure applies.
- Cementitious materials.
- Admixtures.
- Aggregate size.
- Chloride restrictions.
- Delivery time limits.
- Testing plan.
- Curing method.
- Who can accept or reject a truck.

At the pour:

- Check the ticket before discharge.
- Do not add water casually.
- Take required samples.
- Record weather.
- Place in the planned sequence.
- Vibrate or consolidate where required.
- Protect the cure.
- Keep curing materials on site before the truck arrives.

Once placement starts, planning time is over.

Preserve the old durability ambition, but make it a design conversation instead of a recipe:

| Durability Lever | Why It Matters | Who Locks It In |
|---|---|---|
| Lower water-cementitious ratio | Reduces permeability when placed and cured correctly | Engineer, supplier, specifications |
| Supplementary cementitious materials | Can improve permeability and long-term strength gain | Engineer and ready-mix supplier |
| Silica fume or similar fine material | Can densify the paste and reduce permeability | Engineer and supplier |
| Corrosion inhibitor | Can protect reinforcement in higher-risk exposure | Engineer and supplier |
| Air entrainment | Protects exposed concrete in freeze-thaw exposure | Supplier and testing plan |
| Extra cover where appropriate | Slows corrosion and protects reinforcement | Engineer and drawings |
| No chloride accelerators | Reduces corrosion risk to steel | Specifications and batch ticket |
| Curing discipline | Lets the concrete become what was ordered | Crew and inspection records |

**Professional**

Durability design is not the same as strength design. Compressive strength, permeability, water-cementitious ratio, cementitious replacement, air-void system, curing, consolidation, cover, crack control, exposure class, jointing, reinforcement corrosion risk, and shrinkage all matter.

For long-life concrete, the design should favor low permeability, adequate cover, proper curing, controlled cracking, clean reinforcement, sound consolidation, and mix chemistry suited to the exposure. Supplementary cementitious materials can improve permeability and long-term performance, but they change early strength gain, curing sensitivity, finishing behavior, and cold-weather placement.

Fiber reinforcement belongs in the right sentence. Polypropylene microfibers help plastic shrinkage cracking. Macro-synthetic and steel fibers can improve toughness and post-crack behavior. They do not erase the need for primary reinforcement unless the engineer designs that system and the code path accepts it.

**Code**

- ACI 318: structural concrete material requirements, durability, cover, reinforcement, and acceptance.
- ACI 332: residential concrete construction requirements.
- ACI 544.4R: guide for design with fiber-reinforced concrete.
- ASTM C94/C94M: ready-mixed concrete.
- ASTM C143/C143M: slump test.
- ASTM C172/C172M: sampling freshly mixed concrete.
- ASTM C31/C31M: making and curing concrete test specimens in the field.
- ASTM C39/C39M: compressive strength testing of cylinders.
- ASTM C1064/C1064M: temperature of freshly mixed concrete.
- ASTM C231/C231M or ASTM C173/C173M: air content testing, depending on method.
- ASTM C1609/C1609M: flexural performance of fiber-reinforced concrete.

## 6.14 Testing, Mockups, And Owner-Built Research

**Simple**

Breaking small samples can teach a lot. It does not make you the engineer.

Testing converts questions into evidence.

**Applied**

Keep the useful part of owner-built testing:

- Build mockups before the real work.
- Test concrete samples.
- Practice grouting cells.
- Practice vibrating congested reinforcement.
- Try the tie system before a full wall.
- Build sample wall corners.
- Build sample waterproofing transitions.
- Break sample beams or panels if you are learning.
- Record mix, age, dimensions, cure, load, crack pattern, and failure mode.

Use testing to learn, compare, and improve the design conversation. Do not use garage testing to bypass code, engineering, ready-mix quality control, or approved lab testing.

Useful mockup and test variables include:

| Test Variable | What It Can Teach | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Water-cementitious ratio | Workability, strength trend, permeability trend | Not a license to override the approved mix |
| Fly ash, slag, or silica fume | Finish timing, early strength, density, permeability | Supplier and engineer must approve |
| Steel or synthetic fibers | Crack behavior, toughness, finishability | Does not automatically replace bars |
| Rebar size and spacing in samples | Constructability and congestion | Final reinforcement comes from design |
| Slab or beam thickness samples | Relative stiffness and failure behavior | Not a permit design by itself |
| Grout mockups | Cell filling, lift height, consolidation | Does not replace required inspection |
| Wall-corner mockups | Tie placement, insulation fit, flashing, weeps | Useful before production masonry |

**Professional**

Owner-built testing can be valuable as research and prototyping. It can compare mixes, fibers, curing regimes, reinforcement placement, consolidation methods, and mockup details. It can also reveal constructability problems before the real pour.

It is not a substitute for structural calculations, approved material specifications, required special inspection, or accredited laboratory testing where those are required. A reaction frame in a garage can produce useful data, but the data quality depends on calibration, specimen geometry, curing control, loading rate, boundary conditions, instrumentation, statistical sample size, and documentation.

The best use is practical: test to learn how the detail behaves, then bring that evidence to the engineer and supplier. A failed sample is cheaper than a hidden failure.

**Code**

- ASTM C39/C39M: compressive strength of cylindrical concrete specimens.
- ASTM C78/C78M: flexural strength of concrete using third-point loading.
- ASTM C1609/C1609M: flexural performance of fiber-reinforced concrete.
- ASTM C31/C31M: field-made and field-cured concrete test specimens.
- ASTM C172/C172M: sampling freshly mixed concrete.
- ACI 318: acceptance, strength, and construction requirements for structural concrete.
- ACI 544 series: fiber-reinforced concrete guidance.
- Approved project specifications: controlling source for required testing, acceptance criteria, lab qualifications, and report format.

## 6.15 Story Height, Service Height, And The Vertical Stack

**Simple**

Height changes the building. A taller wall changes more than room size. It changes structure, stairs, fire, comfort, cost, and code review.

**Applied**

Before locking the vertical section, draw the whole stack:

- Finished floor thickness.
- Clear ceiling height.
- Structural floor depth.
- Service zone depth.
- Roof structure depth.
- Insulation depth.
- Parapet height if used.
- Stair rise and run.
- Total building height.
- Story classification.

Then compare that stack to:

- Code story-height limits.
- Stair headroom.
- Service access.
- Mechanical duct depth.
- Window and door heights.
- Wall bracing or engineered wall design.
- Exterior proportions.

Do not let a service layer quietly consume the story-height margin.

Use preliminary vertical targets as planning inputs, not as permission to build:

| Planning Item | Useful Baseline | Required Check |
|---|---|---|
| Clear room height | 8 foot living-space ceiling | Still a reasonable comfort baseline |
| Service layer height | 4 to 5 foot crawlable utility zone | Must be classified before design is trusted |
| Structural floor thickness | Roughly 8 inches as an early placeholder | Final value depends on the floor system |
| Masonry coursing | 8 inch CMU courses made the vertical math clean | Keep the coursing discipline, but engineer the wall |
| Story-height margin | A 13 feet 7 inch target was an early masonry planning limit | Confirm current ORSC path, wall type, seismic category, and engineered design status |

**Professional**

A 4 foot service layer with a masonry story-height target requires classification before it becomes final design. In engineered concrete and masonry design, wall clear height, unbraced height, diaphragm spacing, slenderness, axial load, out-of-plane load, seismic detailing, and story definition all matter.

For a two-wythe or reinforced CMU system, wall height also affects reinforcement spacing, bond beam frequency, grout sequencing, cleanouts, scaffold access, lifting, bracing during construction, and inspection timing. Story height is a structural and code decision, not only an interior design decision.

**Code**

- ORSC R202: definition of story and habitable space.
- ORSC R301.3: story height and structural limits.
- ORSC R305: ceiling height.
- ORSC R311.7.2: stair headroom.
- ORSC R606: masonry wall construction and prescriptive limits.
- ORSC R608: limited prescriptive path for above-grade concrete walls.
- TMS 402: masonry wall height, slenderness, and design requirements under engineered path.

## 6.16 What Must Be Engineered

**Simple**

Paint color is preference. Reinforcement is not.

When a choice changes how the house carries weight, resists earthquakes, holds back soil, or survives water pressure, it belongs in design review.

**Applied**

Treat these as engineering items:

| Item | Why It Requires Design |
|---|---|
| Above-grade concrete walls | Seismic, wind, reinforcement, openings, connections |
| Reinforced CMU structural walls | Bar layout, grout, lateral resistance, wall height |
| Two-wythe loadbearing or hybrid walls | Classification, ties, load sharing, cavity limits |
| Concrete floors | Span, reinforcement, diaphragm action, openings |
| Hollow-core plank systems | Shop drawings, bearing, topping, diaphragm connection |
| Concrete roofs | Dead load, ponding, snow, seismic mass, drainage |
| Large stair openings | Diaphragm interruption, trim reinforcement, chords |
| Basement and retaining walls | Soil pressure, water pressure, sliding, overturning |
| Foundations on weak, expansive, or engineered fill | Bearing, settlement, movement, documentation |
| FRP reinforcement or ties | Alternate material approval and product data |
| Field changes to bars, walls, embeds, or openings | Changes the approved load path |

If the crew asks, "Can we just move this bar?" the answer is not a feeling. Check the drawings or call the responsible designer.

**Professional**

The permit set should identify which details are prescriptive, which are engineered, which are proprietary, and which are owner preference. The dangerous middle is a detail that feels ordinary in the field but is actually carrying diaphragm force, wall load, uplift, shear, or soil pressure.

For this concrete home, assume the structural system as a whole requires a licensed structural engineer. The engineer should own the structural drawings, calculations, critical connection details, reinforcement schedules, special inspection notes where required, and responses to structural field changes.

- ORSC R301.1.3: engineered design path.
- ORSC R301.2.2.6: irregularity triggers.
- ORSC R404: retaining and foundation wall design requirements.
- ORSC R606: masonry construction limits and requirements.
- ORSC R608: above-grade concrete wall provisions.
- ACI 318: structural concrete.
- ACI 332: residential concrete.
- TMS 402: masonry design.
- ORS Chapter 672: Oregon professional engineering licensure law.
- OAR Chapter 820: Oregon State Board of Examiners for Engineering and Land Surveying rules.

## 6.17 Structural Hold Points

**Simple**

Concrete hides mistakes. Grout hides mistakes. Check before they arrive.

**Applied**

Use hold points. Do not let the job slide past them because everyone is busy.

| Hold Point | Stop Until This Is True |
|---|---|
| Before footing or mat placement | Soil is accepted, forms are ready, reinforcement is inspected |
| Before wall grout | Cells are clean, bars are correct, inspection is approved |
| Before floor placement | Shoring, bearing, reinforcement, openings, embeds, and test plan are ready |
| Before roof placement | Supports, slope, drainage, parapets, anchors, and access are coordinated |
| Before covering structure | Photos, inspection records, drawings, and correction notes are saved |

Before structural concrete or grout:

- Approved drawings are on site.
- Correct bar size is installed.
- Bar spacing matches drawings.
- Laps and hooks match drawings.
- Bars are tied and supported.
- Cover is maintained.
- Cells and forms are clean.
- Anchor bolts, embeds, sleeves, and blockouts are placed.
- Openings match the plan.
- Inspection is complete where required.
- Concrete or grout order matches the specification.
- Testing supplies and lab coordination are ready.
- Curing materials are on site.

During placement:

- Watch for shifting bars.
- Consolidate around congested reinforcement.
- Keep forms and walls under observation.
- Do not add water without approval.
- Document truck tickets, samples, weather, and placement time.

The inspector cannot approve what is already hidden.

**Professional**

Structural quality control is a sequence: design, drawings, procurement, layout, formwork, reinforcement, inspection, placement, consolidation, curing, testing, documentation, and correction. Failure in one step can defeat the whole design.

Hold points reduce momentum risk by creating a shared pause for the engineer, inspector, owner-builder, and crew before work becomes expensive to uncover.

**Code**

- ORSC R109: inspections, where adopted and administered by the jurisdiction.
- ORSC R106: construction documents and information on construction documents.
- ORSC R104.4 and R104.11: building official authority and alternate methods.
- ACI 318 Chapter 26: construction documents, inspection, and construction requirements for structural concrete.
- TMS 602: masonry construction inspection and quality requirements.
- Approved permit documents: controlling source for inspection sequence and required approvals.

## 6.18 The Structural Standard

**Simple**

The structure should be traceable.

Obvious load paths. Good materials. Clean inspections. No hidden guesses.

**Applied**

For every structural decision, write down:

- What carries the load?
- Where does the load go next?
- What keeps it from moving sideways?
- What happens in an earthquake?
- What happens when water gets near it?
- How will it be inspected?
- How will it be photographed before cover?
- How will it be repaired later?

If those questions are hard to answer, the detail is not ready.

**Professional**

The final structural design should be understandable, redundant, inspectable, durable, and documented. It should not rely on undocumented field judgment, hidden reinforcement changes, proprietary parts with no approval path, or assumptions that cannot be checked later.

Ambitious structural ideas require drawings, calculations, and an approval path.

The approved permit set authorizes the system. The engineer of record owns the engineered structural design where required. The inspector verifies the work. The project records preserve proof after the concrete and grout hide the evidence.
