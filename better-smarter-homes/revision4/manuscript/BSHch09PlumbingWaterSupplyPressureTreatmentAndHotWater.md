# Chapter 9: Plumbing: Water Supply, Pressure, Treatment, And Hot Water

This chapter covers clean water coming into the house, moving through the house, staying safe to drink, staying under control, and becoming hot water where needed.

Waste piping, sewage, drain slope, venting, traps, and septic or sewer connections belong in the DWV chapter. Keep that boundary clean. Potable water is pressure work. DWV is gravity and vent work.

## Plumbing Boundary For This Chapter

**Simple**

Plumbing has two big sides:

- Clean water comes in under pressure.
- Dirty water leaves by gravity or pumping.

This chapter is the clean-water side.

**Applied**

Use this chapter for:

- Water source.
- Water entry.
- Shutoffs.
- Pressure control.
- Pipe routing.
- Hot and cold distribution.
- Water treatment.
- Water heaters.
- Hot-water recirculation.
- Backflow prevention.
- Outdoor potable and nonpotable water connections.

Use the DWV chapter for drains, vents, sewer, septic, basement sewage, traps, and slope.

**Professional**

The potable-water system should be designed separately from the sanitary drainage system, even when they share the same wet wall or service chase. Pressure, flow, backflow, thermal expansion, scald protection, treatment, disinfection, and fixture supply are the governing issues here.

The system should be accessible. A concrete house is difficult to alter after the fact, so the service layer should keep valves, unions, filters, regulators, manifolds, mixing valves, pumps, tanks, sensors, and clean service points reachable.

**Code**

Oregon uses the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code for plumbing systems. The adopted OPSC applies to installation, alteration, repair, relocation, and replacement of plumbing systems in residential and commercial buildings.

Use the current adopted OPSC, approved plans, product listings, manufacturer instructions, and local inspection process. Do not size water piping from memory or from a generic internet chart.

## Wet-Zone Planning

**Simple**

Put bathrooms, kitchen, laundry, water heater, and mechanical plumbing close together when possible. Short pipes are easier to build, easier to repair, and faster to deliver hot water.

A spread-out plumbing plan can work, but it costs more and hides more failure points.

**Applied**

The preferred layout is a wet zone:

- Bathrooms stacked or close together.
- Kitchen near the plumbing core if the floor plan allows.
- Laundry near the same service area.
- Main water shutoff easy to reach.
- Water heater near the major hot-water loads.
- Service layer used for horizontal runs.
- Valves labeled and reachable.

If a bathroom or kitchen moves far away, treat it as a deliberate design tradeoff. It adds pipe length, hot-water wait time, more penetrations, more insulation work, and more places to inspect.

**Professional**

Wet-zone planning reduces pipe length, pressure loss, heat loss, fixture wait time, penetrations, and hidden joints. It also simplifies future repairs because supply, treatment, pumps, recirculation, and drains can be concentrated in a controlled service path.

For a concrete structure, plumbing layout should be coordinated before sleeves, blockouts, slab penetrations, and wall chases are formed. Do not cut the structure later because the plumbing layout was not settled.

**Code**

Plumbing rough-in must match the approved plans and pass inspection before cover. Pipes through concrete, masonry, floors, walls, and foundations need protection, sleeves, clearance, or corrosion separation where required by code and product instructions.

## Water Source Options

**Simple**

The house may get water from a public utility, a private well, stored water, or a combination. The source changes the design.

City water usually arrives treated. Well water belongs to the site and needs testing.

**Applied**

Before designing the plumbing system, identify:

| Question | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Is the source public water or a private well? | Changes permits, treatment, pressure, and backup planning. |
| What flow can the source deliver? | Sets tank, pump, and pipe design. |
| What pressure arrives at the building? | Determines regulator or booster needs. |
| What does the water test show? | Determines filtration, softening, or treatment. |
| Is there a nonpotable source too? | Requires full separation from drinking water. |
| What happens during power loss? | A well pump and booster pump may stop. |

Do not assume the source can feed luxury fixtures directly. Measure or document source pressure and flow.

**Professional**

Public water design starts with service size, meter capacity, static pressure, residual pressure, utility requirements, backflow conditions, and fixture-unit load. Private well design starts with well report, yield, static water level, pump curve, pressure tank, treatment, storage, disinfection, and power resilience.

A storage and booster system can separate source flow from peak demand. The source fills a tank slowly. The house draws from the tank through a controlled pump system. This can improve pressure stability and resilience, but it adds water-quality, overflow, backflow, pump, maintenance, and inspection obligations.

**Code**

Public water, private wells, water storage, and treatment systems have different approval paths. Oregon Water Resources Department oversees well construction and well reports. Oregon Health Authority provides domestic well testing guidance. The plumbing code controls the building distribution system and backflow protection.

If a private well is part of the project, keep the well report, pump data, water test, treatment design, and plumbing permit record in the project file.

## Water Quality And Treatment

**Simple**

Clear water is not automatically safe water. Well water can look clean and still have bacteria, nitrate, arsenic, iron, sulfur, hardness, or other problems.

Test first. Treat the actual problem.

**Applied**

For private water, plan these steps:

1. Test the raw water.
2. Identify health issues first.
3. Identify equipment issues second.
4. Pick treatment for the test result, not for guesswork.
5. Retest after treatment.
6. Put filters and treatment equipment where they can be serviced.
7. Label bypass valves and flow direction.
8. Record filter sizes and replacement intervals.

Do not install a random filter stack and call the water safe.

**Professional**

Water treatment is source-specific. Common treatment targets can include sediment, iron, manganese, hardness, sulfur odor, arsenic, nitrate, bacteria, pH, tannins, total dissolved solids, and disinfection byproducts. The treatment train should be ordered so one device does not ruin the next device.

Examples: sediment can foul a carbon filter. Hardness can scale water heaters. Iron can foul softeners and fixtures. UV disinfection needs clear water ahead of the lamp. Reverse osmosis wastes water and should usually be targeted, not applied blindly to every gallon.

**Code**

Oregon Health Authority domestic well guidance identifies arsenic, nitrate, and coliform bacteria testing in real estate transaction rules, with samples analyzed by an accredited laboratory. That is not necessarily the full test list for a new build, but it is a useful minimum warning set.

Treatment devices must be listed, installed as directed, protected from freezing, and kept accessible. If treatment creates waste discharge, drain discharge, brine, or bypass water, coordinate with the approved plumbing and wastewater design.

## Storage Tanks And Booster Pumps

**Simple**

A tank stores water. A pump moves water. A pressure tank smooths pump cycling.

This can make the house feel strong and steady at the fixtures, but it must be designed carefully.

**Applied**

A storage and booster system can help when:

- A well has low flow but steady recovery.
- Public water pressure is low.
- The house has high peak demand.
- The owner wants backup water.
- The site needs treatment contact time.
- The plumbing design includes outdoor utility water.

Before using this strategy, confirm tank material, overflow, venting, drain, cleaning access, disinfection, pump sizing, pressure tank sizing, power backup, low-water shutoff, leak detection, and bypass mode.

**Professional**

Pump design should be based on a fixture-unit calculation, desired simultaneous use, static lift, pressure loss, pipe length, fittings, filters, treatment pressure drop, water heater loss, elevation, and minimum residual pressure at the fixture. A variable-speed pump can improve stability, but it still needs correct sizing and protection.

Parallel pumps increase available flow and redundancy. Series pumps increase pressure. Both approaches need controls, check valves, isolation valves, unions, pressure relief, electrical coordination, vibration isolation, and service clearance.

**Code**

Booster pumps, pressure tanks, stored potable water, cross-connection control, overflow, drains, disinfection, access, and electrical equipment must meet the approved plumbing and electrical path. If a pressure-reducing valve, check valve, backflow preventer, or pump creates a closed system, thermal expansion must be addressed.

Do not exceed fixture, pipe, appliance, valve, water heater, or code pressure limits. If the design uses higher pressure upstream of regulators, it needs a clear engineered and inspected path.

## Distribution Pipe Layout

**Simple**

Big pipes can move more water. Small pipes are cheaper and easier to run. The right pipe size depends on the fixtures, distance, pressure, and material.

More pipe is not always better. Wrongly sized pipe can waste money, slow hot-water delivery, and make the system harder to maintain.

**Applied**

A good supply layout should show:

- Water service entry.
- Main shutoff.
- Pressure regulator if needed.
- Backflow device if needed.
- Treatment equipment.
- Water heater.
- Hot and cold trunks.
- Branch lines.
- Manifolds.
- Fixture shutoffs.
- Hose bibs.
- Recirculation return.
- Drain points.
- Isolation valves.

The water-distribution concept favors generously sized trunks and smaller branches. Keep the concept, but size the actual system from the fixture count, pressure, length, and code tables.

**Professional**

The original large-trunk concept is a performance strategy, not a universal rule. It can reduce pressure drop and support high simultaneous demand, but it increases water volume in the pipe. That can increase hot-water wait, recirculation losses, material cost, stagnant volume, support requirements, and coordination space.

Use calculations. Compare trunk-and-branch, home-run manifold, hybrid manifold, distributed water heaters, recirculation loops, and point-of-use heaters before freezing the layout.

**Code**

The OPSC water-distribution sizing path uses fixture demand, available pressure, developed length, elevation, material, and pressure loss. The approved plan should show the sizing basis, not just pipe labels.

Minimum and maximum pressures, approved materials, fixture protection, and pipe support are code and product issues. Verify with the current OPSC and manufacturer data.

## Pipe Materials And Compatibility

**Simple**

Copper, PEX, stainless, CPVC, and other approved materials can all move water. Each has strengths and weaknesses.

The best material is the one that fits the temperature, pressure, water chemistry, access, cost, and repair plan.

**Applied**

For each pipe material, check:

- Is it approved for potable water?
- Is it approved for hot water?
- Is it rated for the pressure and temperature?
- Does the water chemistry attack it?
- Can it touch concrete or masonry?
- Does it need sleeves?
- How is it supported?
- How are joints made?
- Can it be repaired from the service layer?
- Does it need protection from UV, freezing, or abrasion?

Do not bury a material in concrete unless the approved detail says it can be buried and protected that way.

**Professional**

Material selection affects pressure loss, velocity, expansion, water hammer, noise, corrosion, scale, dezincification, chlorine resistance, oxygen diffusion, fire behavior, penetrations, hangers, fittings, and long-term repair. Mixed-metal systems need attention to galvanic corrosion and dielectric separation.

PEX may be efficient for branches and service-layer routing. Copper may be attractive for accessible mains and high-temperature service. CPVC may be approved but less attractive where impact, heat, or long-life durability is a concern. The final choice should be based on the approved code path, water chemistry, and manufacturer limits.

**Code**

Use approved potable-water materials and fittings. Follow joining methods, support spacing, protection from damage, corrosion separation, firestopping, and manufacturer limits. Lead-containing materials and unapproved fittings do not belong in potable-water work.

## Pressure Control

**Simple**

Pressure is the push. Flow is how much water moves. A house can have high pressure and still poor flow if the pipe is too small or clogged.

Good plumbing controls both.

**Applied**

Measure these conditions:

- Static pressure with no fixtures running.
- Flow pressure while fixtures run.
- Pressure drop through filters and treatment equipment.
- Pressure at the farthest fixture.
- Pressure before and after regulators.
- Pump cut-in and cut-out or variable-speed setpoint.

Tune the system after fixtures are installed. A perfect number on paper is not enough.

**Professional**

Pressure control should prevent excessive static pressure, water hammer, fixture damage, pump short cycling, filter collapse, heater stress, and poor fixture performance. Regulators need accessible strainers, isolation valves, gauges, unions, and maintenance clearance.

Per-fixture pressure control can make a luxury system feel refined, but it adds many parts. Use it where it solves a real problem. The simpler version is a main regulator, good fixture valves, and correct pipe sizing. The advanced version adds local regulators for high-flow or sensitive fixtures.

**Code**

The current Oregon plumbing code includes pressure control requirements for excessive water pressure and pressure-regulator installations. Verify the exact threshold, regulator standard, expansion-tank requirement, access, and protection requirements in the current OPSC before final design.

## Water Hammer, Noise, And Movement

**Simple**

Water hammer is the bang that happens when moving water stops suddenly. It can shake pipes, damage valves, and make the house sound cheap.

Prevent it before the walls close.

**Applied**

Control water hammer with:

- Correct pipe sizing.
- Reasonable flow velocity.
- Good pipe support.
- Smooth valve operation.
- Hammer arrestors where required or useful.
- Isolation from framing or masonry.
- Secure pump starts and stops.
- Expansion loops or allowances for hot piping.

Listen during testing. A quiet plumbing system is not an accident.

**Professional**

Noise control is pressure, velocity, support, material, valve, pump, and building-structure coordination. Concrete can transmit vibration if pipes are rigidly coupled to the structure. Use isolation, sleeves, supports, and service-layer routing to keep movement and noise controlled.

Fast-closing solenoid valves on washing machines, dishwashers, ice makers, irrigation valves, and equipment connections are common hammer sources. Booster pumps and recirculation pumps need vibration isolation and service access.

**Code**

Pipe support, protection, water hammer control, equipment listing, and installation instructions apply. Where a manufacturer requires a hammer arrestor, expansion allowance, support spacing, or pressure limit, follow that requirement.

## Hot Water Generation

**Simple**

Hot water is a comfort system and a safety system. Too cold can be unsanitary. Too hot can burn people.

The system should heat water, control temperature, and protect the building if something leaks.

**Applied**

Before choosing water heaters, define:

- Number of people.
- Fixture count.
- Simultaneous shower demand.
- Tub fill demand.
- Laundry and dishwasher timing.
- Recirculation strategy.
- Fuel or electric source.
- Available electrical service.
- Floor drain or safe drain pan path.
- Service clearance.
- Replacement path.

Do not buy water heaters before the fixture schedule and energy plan are settled.

**Professional**

Water heater selection should compare storage tank, heat pump water heater, tankless, multiple tanks, indirect tank, solar preheat, point-of-use heaters, and hybrid systems. The best choice depends on peak demand, recovery rate, energy source, ventilation, space temperature, noise, maintenance, equipment room layout, and resilience.

Storage temperature, delivery temperature, recirculation temperature, bacterial control, scald control, mixing valves, expansion control, and fixture limits must be designed together.

**Code**

Water heaters must be listed and installed according to the manufacturer's instructions and the current plumbing code. Temperature-pressure relief valves, discharge piping, drain pans where needed, seismic support, combustion or electrical requirements, expansion control, and access all matter.

## Hot Water Delivery And Recirculation

**Simple**

Hot water sitting in a long pipe cools off. When you open the faucet, you wait while the cold water in the pipe runs out.

A good design either keeps hot runs short or uses recirculation.

**Applied**

Use one of these strategies:

| Strategy | Best Use |
|---|---|
| Short runs | Small wet zone, fixtures close to heater |
| Timed recirculation | Predictable daily use |
| Demand recirculation | Push button, motion, smart control, or fixture call |
| Continuous recirculation | High convenience, higher standby loss |
| Point-of-use heater | Remote sink or isolated load |

Insulate hot-water piping. Do not recirculate through uninsulated pipe and then wonder why the water heater runs all day.

**Professional**

Recirculation is a comfort tradeoff. It reduces wait time and wasted draw water, but it can increase heat loss, pump energy, pipe temperature exposure, and system complexity. A demand-controlled loop can reduce losses compared with constant circulation.

The return line, check valves, balancing valves, pump, aquastat or controller, mixing valve placement, and cross-over prevention must be drawn clearly. Without a clear drawing, recirculation systems often get piped wrong.

**Code**

Hot-water pipe insulation, water-heater efficiency, recirculation controls, and energy provisions can affect the accepted design. Follow the adopted energy and plumbing provisions, product listings, and manufacturer instructions.

## Scald Protection And Temperature Control

**Simple**

Hot water can burn skin quickly. Children, older people, and guests are especially vulnerable.

Use mixing valves and approved fixtures so hot water feels good without becoming dangerous.

**Applied**

Control temperature at several points:

- Water heater storage setting.
- Master mixing valve if used.
- Recirculation loop temperature.
- Shower or tub valve.
- Point-of-use tempering where needed.
- Outdoor hot-water connection.
- Appliance connection.

Label any line that is hotter than normal domestic hot water. Do not put high-temperature water where someone can touch it by accident.

**Professional**

Temperature control is not one device. A master mixing valve can temper the distribution system. Shower valves can compensate for pressure or temperature changes. Point-of-use valves can protect lavatories, tubs, or special fixtures. Storage temperature can be higher than delivery temperature when bacterial control strategy calls for it.

The design should avoid dead legs, stagnant warm water, unbalanced mixing, failed check valves, and unmaintainable tempering devices.

**Code**

Anti-scald, water-heater, mixing-valve, shower-valve, and fixture temperature requirements depend on the current plumbing code and listed devices. Use approved ASSE-listed devices where required and install them exactly as directed.

## Backflow And Cross-Connection Control

**Simple**

Backflow is dirty water moving backward into clean water. It can happen when pressure drops or when a hose sits in dirty water.

Backflow prevention protects the drinking water.

**Applied**

High-risk places include:

- Hose bibs.
- Irrigation systems.
- Hot tubs.
- Pools.
- Boiler fill lines.
- Water treatment drains.
- Pressure washer feeds.
- Rainwater systems.
- Any connection between potable and nonpotable water.

Use an air gap where possible. Where an air gap is not practical, use the approved backflow device for the hazard.

**Professional**

Cross-connection control is hazard classification. Low-hazard and high-hazard conditions require different protection. A device that is acceptable for a hose bib may not be acceptable for chemical injection, irrigation, boiler makeup, or nonpotable water makeup.

Backflow devices need access, testing where required, freeze protection, drainage, support, and replacement clearance. Do not bury them behind finishes.

**Code**

The OPSC requires potable-water protection against contamination. Backflow devices, air gaps, vacuum breakers, double-check assemblies, reduced-pressure assemblies, testability, and installation location depend on the hazard and device type.

Nonpotable rainwater or graywater systems must stay separated from potable water except through an approved protection method.

## Nonpotable Water And Outdoor Utility Water

**Simple**

Rainwater can be useful for irrigation, washing, and outdoor work. It is not drinking water unless it is treated and approved as drinking water.

Keep nonpotable water separate and labeled.

**Applied**

A nonpotable outdoor water system should include:

- Gutters and downspouts.
- Leaf screens.
- First-flush or sediment control where used.
- Cistern or tank.
- Overflow to a safe discharge.
- Separate pump.
- Separate piping.
- Nonpotable labels.
- Backflow or air-gap protection from potable water.
- Winter protection.

Do not connect rainwater directly to the potable system.

**Professional**

Rainwater collection affects stormwater, roof materials, storage, stagnation, mosquito control, sediment, filtration, pump reliability, overflow routing, potable makeup, and freeze protection. It also must stay away from septic drainfields and foundation drainage problems.

Outdoor utility water can be valuable for washing tools, vehicles, equipment, concrete, and site work. Hot outdoor water needs scald protection, backflow protection, freeze protection, clear labeling, and an equipment-use boundary.

**Code**

Rainwater, graywater, nonpotable water, irrigation, and outdoor fixtures can trigger plumbing, health, wastewater, cross-connection, and local rules. Use approved separation from potable water and do not route overflow or wastewater toward the foundation, septic drainfield, or neighboring property.

## Freeze Protection And Concrete Penetrations

**Simple**

Water pipes do not like freezing. Concrete is also hard to fix after the fact.

Protect pipes before winter and before concrete closes around them.

**Applied**

Before cover, verify:

- Pipes are inside the conditioned space where possible.
- Exterior walls and service layers keep pipes warm.
- Hose bibs are frost-protected where needed.
- Penetrations through concrete are sleeved.
- Dissimilar metals are separated.
- Pipes can move as they expand and contract.
- Shutoffs and drains are accessible.
- Heat tape, if used, is listed and accessible.

Do not run water pipes in risky exterior locations just because the route is shorter.

**Professional**

Freeze protection is routing, insulation, air sealing, heat source, pipe material, flow, and access. A pipe in a warm room can still freeze if outdoor air leaks through a sleeve or rim detail. A pipe in concrete can fail from corrosion, movement, abrasion, or lack of sleeve clearance.

Concrete penetrations should be coordinated with structure, waterproofing, firestopping, air sealing, vapor control, radon control, and pest control.

**Code**

Water piping must be protected from freezing, physical damage, corrosion, and incompatible contact. Pipes passing through concrete, masonry, or foundations need approved protection and inspection before cover.

## Inspection, Testing, And Documentation

**Simple**

Plumbing should be tested before it is hidden. A leak found before drywall is annoying. A leak found after finishes is expensive.

**Applied**

Before cover, document:

- Approved rough plumbing inspection.
- Pressure test results.
- Pipe material and size.
- Valve locations.
- Regulator settings.
- Water heater model and listing.
- Mixing valve model.
- Expansion tank location if used.
- Backflow device locations.
- Filter and treatment model numbers.
- Photos of buried or hidden piping.

Keep the record with the permanent house file.

**Professional**

Testing should prove that the system is leak-free, protected, supported, accessible, isolated, labeled, and built to the approved plan. The plumbing record should let a future owner find every shutoff, regulator, backflow device, filter, pump, tank, mixing valve, recirculation pump, and serviceable fitting.

The final system should be commissioned under real use: multiple fixtures running, hot-water delivery checked, pressure drop observed, water hammer checked, heater recovery confirmed, recirculation balanced, treatment flow verified, and leaks inspected.

**Code**

Rough and final plumbing inspections, pressure testing, water-heater installation, backflow protection, material approval, support, protection, and fixture installation must satisfy the current OPSC and local inspection process. Do not cover plumbing before required inspection approval.
