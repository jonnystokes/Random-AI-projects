# Chapter 12: Electrical: Branch Circuits, Wire Size, Voltage Drop, Lighting, Low Voltage, And Future Power

This chapter covers the circuits after the service backbone: room circuits, kitchen circuits, bathroom and laundry circuits, receptacles, lighting, switches, wire routing, voltage-drop planning, low-voltage wiring, network pathways, and future circuit capacity.

The service, panels, grounding, bonding, surge protection, backup power, solar readiness, and EV service capacity are handled in the electrical service chapter. This chapter is about getting power and signal safely to the rooms.

## Branch-Circuit Scope

**Simple**

The service brings power into the house. Branch circuits carry that power from a panel to the rooms, lights, outlets, appliances, pumps, tools, and controls.

A good branch-circuit plan means one problem does not take down half the house.

**Applied**

Build the circuit plan room by room:

- List every room.
- List every appliance.
- Mark wet areas.
- Mark work areas.
- Mark outdoor outlets.
- Mark future equipment.
- Decide which loads need their own circuit.
- Keep lighting from depending on the same circuit as heavy plug loads where practical.
- Label the plan before rough-in.

The goal is simple: when a breaker trips, the problem should be easy to find.

**Professional**

This design favors more branch-circuit granularity than a minimum-code house. That means separate circuits for rooms, dedicated circuits for predictable loads, separate lighting where useful, and a service-layer routing strategy that allows future conductor replacement.

That approach costs more in breakers, conductors, boxes, raceway, and panel space. It buys better fault isolation, better maintenance, less nuisance shutdown, and easier upgrades.

**Code**

Branch circuits, required circuits, conductor ampacity, overcurrent protection, receptacle placement, lighting outlets, AFCI protection, GFCI protection, boxes, conduit, low-voltage separation, and communication circuits are controlled by the adopted Oregon Electrical Specialty Code, based on the NEC with Oregon amendments.

Use the adopted code at permit time, the approved electrical drawings, product listings, and the local inspection process. Do not size a circuit from memory.

## Circuit Granularity

**Simple**

More circuits make the house easier to live with. If the office has its own circuit, a problem in the office does not turn off the bedroom.

That is the point. Keep problems small.

**Applied**

Use this as the planning direction:

| Area | Planning Goal |
|---|---|
| Small bedrooms | Keep outlets predictable and easy to isolate. |
| Primary bedroom | Consider more than one outlet circuit if loads or room size justify it. |
| Office | Treat computers, printers, networking, and chargers as real loads. |
| Living room | Plan for entertainment equipment, lamps, chargers, and future furniture layouts. |
| Hallways | Keep lighting dependable and easy to switch from both ends where needed. |
| Mechanical rooms | Separate equipment loads from general receptacles where practical. |

Do not design the whole floor as one giant general-purpose circuit.

**Professional**

Circuit granularity should follow load type, use pattern, fault consequence, and maintainability. A concrete house is harder to modify after finish work, so oversimplified branch-circuit layouts create long-term friction.

The preferred strategy is not to add circuits randomly. Group loads by use, risk, and serviceability. Lighting, receptacles, kitchen appliances, laundry, bathrooms, mechanical equipment, network equipment, outdoor work areas, and future loads should each be visible in the panel schedule.

**Code**

The code sets minimum required branch circuits and protection rules. A design may exceed those minimums when the approved load calculation, panel capacity, conductor sizing, and installation method support it.

Extra circuits still require correct conductor sizing, overcurrent protection, box fill, raceway fill, derating, grounding, labeling, and inspection.

## Dedicated Circuits

**Simple**

Some things should not share. A refrigerator, dishwasher, microwave, washer, dryer, water heater, pump, heat pump, and network rack can each create problems if they are tied to the wrong circuit.

Give important equipment a clear path back to the panel.

**Applied**

Make a dedicated-circuit list before rough-in:

- Refrigerator and freezer.
- Dishwasher.
- Disposal, if used.
- Microwave or built-in cooking appliance.
- Range, cooktop, oven, or other large cooking equipment.
- Laundry equipment.
- Water heater.
- Well pump or booster pump.
- Sump pump or sewage ejector, if used.
- Heat pump and air handler.
- Ventilation equipment.
- Network rack and smart-home equipment.
- Outdoor utility bay.
- Shop or workbench areas.
- Future EV, if it is not already covered in the service plan.

Then mark the circuit on the plan, panel schedule, and as-built record.

**Professional**

Dedicated circuits are not only about amperage. They also protect uptime. A freezer should not lose power because a countertop appliance trips a breaker. A network rack should not share with a shop tool. A sewage pump should not be hidden on a general basement circuit.

The final list depends on the selected appliances, nameplate ratings, manufacturer instructions, current code, and load calculation.

**Code**

Required dedicated branch circuits and required appliance circuits must follow the adopted electrical code and manufacturer instructions. Appliances may require specific circuit ratings, conductor sizes, disconnecting means, GFCI protection, AFCI protection, and receptacle types.

Use nameplate data and the approved plans, not a generic table.

## Kitchen Circuits

**Simple**

Kitchens use a lot of electricity. Coffee makers, toasters, microwaves, refrigerators, dishwashers, ovens, and cooktops should not all fight for one circuit.

The kitchen needs several circuits.

**Applied**

Plan the kitchen by zones:

- Countertop work zones.
- Island or peninsula.
- Refrigerator or freezer.
- Dishwasher.
- Disposal.
- Microwave.
- Range, cooktop, or oven.
- Lighting.
- Under-cabinet lighting.
- Coffee bar or appliance garage, if planned.

Put receptacles where people actually use appliances. Do not leave a finished counter dependent on extension cords.

**Professional**

This design uses more kitchen circuits than a minimum layout so cooking, refrigeration, cleaning, and countertop use stay isolated. That is a good direction for a high-use kitchen.

The exact number and rating of kitchen circuits should be set after the appliance schedule is chosen. Electric cooking, induction, wall ovens, warming drawers, beverage centers, steam ovens, and built-in coffee equipment can change the circuit plan quickly.

**Code**

Kitchen branch circuits, small-appliance circuits, countertop receptacles, island or peninsula receptacles, appliance circuits, GFCI protection, AFCI protection, receptacle ratings, and appliance disconnects are code-controlled.

Verify the current OESC and approved appliance data before setting exact circuit ratings or receptacle spacing.

## Bathroom, Laundry, Basement, And Outdoor Circuits

**Simple**

Water and electricity need extra protection. Bathrooms, laundry rooms, basements, garages, and outdoor areas need careful circuit design.

That is where shock risk is higher.

**Applied**

Treat these as special zones:

- Bathrooms: receptacles, lighting, fans, heaters, heated floors, tubs, and bidet seats.
- Laundry: washer, dryer, utility sink, ironing, exhaust, and possible heat-pump equipment.
- Basement: mechanical equipment, shop tools, storage, sump or sewage pumps, and general use.
- Garage or carport: tools, chargers, door openers, freezers, lighting, and future EV.
- Outdoors: receptacles, lighting, pumps, gates, cameras, holiday outlets, and utility bay equipment.

Use weather-rated equipment outdoors. Keep wet-area circuits easy to identify at the panel.

**Professional**

Wet and outdoor circuits need coordination between load, environment, protection device, enclosure rating, receptacle type, cover type, equipment grounding, and inspection access. A circuit can be electrically adequate and still be wrong for the location.

The outdoor utility bay should remain as a planned work zone, but exact receptacle count, circuit ratings, and equipment layout need current code, product, and use-case verification.

**Code**

Bathrooms, laundry areas, basements, garages, crawl spaces, outdoor receptacles, wet locations, damp locations, weather-resistant devices, in-use covers, and equipment grounding are controlled by the adopted electrical code.

Use listed devices and enclosures rated for the location. Do not assume an indoor device is acceptable because it is under a roof.

## GFCI And AFCI Protection

**Simple**

GFCI protection helps protect people from shock. AFCI protection helps reduce fire risk from arcing faults.

They are different protections. Many modern circuits need one or both.

**Applied**

Plan protection at the same time as the circuit:

- Mark wet-area circuits.
- Mark outdoor circuits.
- Mark kitchen circuits.
- Mark bathroom and laundry circuits.
- Mark bedroom, living, dining, hall, and office circuits.
- Decide where protection will be provided: breaker, receptacle, device, or listed equipment.
- Keep the panel schedule clear.
- Test protection devices after installation and during maintenance.

Do not wait until final inspection to figure out which breaker type is needed.

**Professional**

GFCI and AFCI strategy affects breaker selection, panel type, troubleshooting, nuisance-trip risk, device placement, appliance compatibility, and long-term maintenance. Combination protection can be clean, but it must match the panel and code path.

Critical loads need special attention. Do not defeat required protection, but do design the circuit so a nuisance trip is visible, traceable, and correctable.

**Code**

GFCI and AFCI locations and methods are controlled by the adopted OESC. Requirements have changed across NEC editions, and Oregon amendments may affect the final answer.

Verify current requirements before specifying breaker type, receptacle type, appliance circuit protection, dishwasher protection, outdoor protection, garage protection, basement protection, or EV charging protection.

## Receptacle Placement

**Simple**

Outlets should be where people need them. A room with too few outlets becomes a room full of extension cords.

Extension cords are not a wiring plan.

**Applied**

Walk each room on paper:

- Where does the bed go?
- Where does the desk go?
- Where does the sofa go?
- Where does the TV or projector go?
- Where do chargers land?
- Where will cleaning equipment plug in?
- Where will holiday lights or exterior decorations plug in?
- Where will future furniture move?

Add receptacles while the walls are open. It is cheaper now.

**Professional**

Receptacle placement should satisfy code spacing, room use, furniture layout, accessibility, extension-cord avoidance, cleaning paths, future flexibility, and device density. More receptacles are usually easier to live with, but they still need correct boxes, conductors, protection, and panel capacity.

For concrete walls, decide receptacle locations before masonry, conduit, furring, or finishes lock the route in place.

**Code**

Dwelling-unit receptacle spacing, countertop receptacles, bathroom receptacles, laundry receptacles, basement and garage receptacles, hallway receptacles, outdoor receptacles, tamper-resistant devices, and weather-resistant devices are code-controlled.

Use the current code for exact spacing and required locations.

## Lighting And Switching

**Simple**

Lighting should make the house easy to use. You should not have to cross a dark room to turn on a light.

Good lighting is safety, comfort, and daily usefulness.

**Applied**

Plan lighting by task:

| Area | Lighting Job |
|---|---|
| Kitchen | Food prep, cooking, cleaning, island work, under-cabinet task light. |
| Bathroom | Vanity, shower, night use, exhaust control. |
| Hallways and stairs | Safe movement and switching from both ends. |
| Bedrooms | General light, bedside use, closet lighting. |
| Office | Glare control, task light, video-call light if needed. |
| Basement and service layer | Bright maintenance lighting. |
| Exterior | Entry safety, utility work, cameras, and motion detection. |

Separate lighting from heavy receptacle loads where it improves safety and troubleshooting.

**Professional**

Lighting design should coordinate fixture type, fixture rating, dimming compatibility, color temperature, switching location, control method, serviceability, energy-code compliance, and future smart control. LED is the practical default, but product selection still matters.

A good lighting plan is not just fixture count. It is control, glare, maintenance access, wet-location rating, insulation contact rating where applicable, and clear scene logic.

**Code**

Lighting outlets, switch requirements, luminaire installation, fixture ratings, damp and wet location fixtures, closet lighting, energy-code lighting efficacy, and required exterior or stair lighting are code-controlled.

Verify the current residential energy requirements and the adopted electrical code before writing exact fixture-count or efficacy requirements.

## Service-Layer Wiring Routes

**Simple**

Concrete makes bad wiring routes expensive. Use the service layer and conduit so wires can be reached later.

The building should not have to be broken to replace a wire.

**Applied**

Use this route preference:

1. Service-layer conduit where accessible.
2. Interior partition walls where changes are likely.
3. Planned concrete-wall sleeves or conduits where the route is fixed.
4. Concealed slab runs only when there is no better option and the design team approves it.

Put pull strings in empty conduit. Label both ends.

**Professional**

This design treats conduit as part of the long-life strategy. That is especially important in a concrete house because electrical components will change long before the structure wears out.

Branch-circuit routes should account for conductor replacement, pull length, bend count, junction-box access, box fill, raceway fill, support spacing, physical protection, firestopping, air sealing, and separation from plumbing and low-voltage systems.

**Code**

Raceway type, conductor type, box fill, conduit fill, bend limits, pull boxes, support, fittings, grounding, wet-location conductors, underground wiring, firestopping, and accessibility are code-controlled.

Do not bury junction boxes. Keep pull points accessible.

## Low-Voltage, Network, And Communications Wiring

**Simple**

Low-voltage wiring carries data, cameras, alarms, doorbells, controls, speakers, and sensors. It does not need the same power as an outlet, but it still needs a clean path.

Wireless is useful. Wires are still the backbone.

**Applied**

Plan low-voltage runs for:

- Internet service entry.
- Network rack or closet.
- Wireless access points.
- Office drops.
- Entertainment locations.
- Camera locations.
- Doorbell or intercom locations.
- Thermostats and HVAC controls.
- Security sensors, if wired.
- Gate, driveway, or exterior equipment.
- Future fiber or spare pathway.

Home-run important data cables to the network rack. Label both ends before closing walls.

**Professional**

This design strongly favors hardwired networking with a central rack, structured cabling, PoE-capable devices, camera pathways, and local-first automation infrastructure. That direction should stay, but exact cable category, drop count, rack size, switch size, UPS runtime, access-point spacing, and camera count should be set by the final network design.

Keep line-voltage and low-voltage systems separated as required. Do not mix power conductors and communications cabling casually in the same raceway.

**Code**

Class 1, Class 2, Class 3, remote-control, signaling, communications, coax, fiber, fire alarm, and power-limited circuits have their own code rules. Separation from power wiring, grounding, listing, plenum or riser ratings, exterior transitions, and penetrations must be verified.

Low voltage is not no-code.

## Smart-Home Electrical Groundwork

**Simple**

A smart home is easier when the wiring is ready. Put power, neutral conductors, deep boxes, network pathways, and spare conduit where future controls may need them.

Do not make the house depend on one brand.

**Applied**

Prepare for smart controls by planning:

- Neutral conductors at switch boxes where required.
- Deep boxes where smart switches or dimmers may be installed.
- Extra space in boxes with multiple devices.
- Network power for access points and cameras.
- A dedicated circuit for network equipment if justified.
- Local control for important lights.
- Manual fallback for doors, gates, pumps, heat, and water.
- Spare conduit from the service layer to key rooms.

Smart should add control, not remove basic function.

**Professional**

This design favors local-first smart-home infrastructure. In branch-circuit terms, that means reliable power for the network rack, room for larger control devices, documented switch legs, accessible junctions, PoE strategy, and separation between platform choices and permanent wiring choices.

Do not hardwire the house into a product ecosystem that may be obsolete before the concrete cures through its first decade.

**Code**

Switches, neutral conductors, box fill, device ratings, low-voltage control wiring, Class 2 circuits, lighting controls, alarms, cameras, access control, and equipment listings must follow the adopted code and manufacturer instructions.

Smart devices do not exempt the installation from electrical rules.

## Wire Schedule And Voltage Drop

**Simple**

Wire size matters. Long runs, high loads, heat, conduit fill, and equipment type all affect what wire is correct.

This is not a guessing job.

**Applied**

The final wire schedule should list:

| Item | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Circuit name | Tells the builder and inspector what the circuit serves. |
| Load served | Connects the circuit to the appliance or room. |
| Breaker or overcurrent device | Protects the conductor and equipment. |
| Conductor material | Copper and aluminum are not interchangeable without design checks. |
| Conductor insulation | Temperature and location ratings matter. |
| Raceway or cable type | Changes ampacity, fill, protection, and installation method. |
| Circuit length | Needed for voltage-drop review. |
| Protection type | GFCI, AFCI, combination, equipment protection, or special device. |
| Notes | Manufacturer instructions, future use, or inspection concerns. |

Keep the wire schedule with the panel schedule.

**Professional**

The planning material includes practical wire-size and distance tables. Those tables should not be treated as installation instructions until checked against the current OESC, conductor material, insulation type, temperature rating, termination rating, raceway fill, ambient temperature, number of current-carrying conductors, continuous load rules, equipment nameplate data, and voltage-drop target.

The intent is clear: the book needs a usable wire schedule. The exact table should be added only after the values are verified.

**Code**

Branch-circuit conductor sizing, feeder sizing, overcurrent protection, continuous loads, conductor ampacity, temperature correction, adjustment factors, raceway fill, voltage-drop guidance, grounding conductors, and equipment instructions must be checked together.

The code does not allow a generic wire table to override the actual installation conditions.

## Inspection, Testing, And Documentation

**Simple**

Before walls close, take photos. Label wires. Check boxes. Save inspection approvals.

Future you will need that record.

**Applied**

Before cover, verify:

- Boxes are mounted and accessible.
- Conductors are protected from damage.
- Conduit and cable routes match the plan.
- Wet-location devices are rated for the location.
- Outdoor covers match the use.
- GFCI and AFCI protection is installed where required.
- Smoke, CO, and alarm wiring is coordinated.
- Low-voltage wiring is separated and labeled.
- Panel schedules match field wiring.
- Photos are taken before cover.
- Inspection approval is recorded.

Fix wiring before finishes hide it.

**Professional**

Branch-circuit commissioning should include panel schedule review, breaker type review, receptacle polarity and grounding checks, GFCI and AFCI testing, lighting control testing, switch-leg verification, low-voltage continuity testing, network certification where specified, camera and access-point pathway verification, and as-built documentation.

Record deviations. Undocumented electrical changes become expensive diagnostics later.

**Code**

Rough electrical inspection, final electrical inspection, required testing, product listings, equipment instructions, and local inspection approvals control when work may be covered or energized.

Do not cover branch-circuit wiring until the required inspection path allows it.
