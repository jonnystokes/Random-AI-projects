# Chapter 13: HVAC, Ventilation, Humidity, And Air Quality

This chapter covers how the house is heated, cooled, ventilated, exhausted, dehumidified, filtered, controlled, and commissioned. The design direction is a concrete house with high thermal mass, deliberate ventilation, mostly electric equipment, accessible service routes, and a mechanical room that can be maintained without tearing into finished space.

Exact heating loads, cooling loads, loop lengths, duct sizes, ventilation rates, equipment capacities, refrigerant details, and exhaust values must come from the final design, manufacturer instructions, adopted code, and commissioning results.

## HVAC Scope

**Simple**

HVAC means heating, ventilation, and air conditioning. In this house, it also means humidity control, exhaust, fresh air, filtering, and comfort.

The house should not depend on random leaks for fresh air.

**Applied**

Plan HVAC as a system, not as separate equipment:

- Heat source.
- Cooling source.
- Hydronic loops or other heat distribution.
- Ventilation.
- Kitchen exhaust.
- Bathroom exhaust.
- Dryer exhaust.
- Makeup air.
- Humidity control.
- Filtration.
- Controls.
- Access for service.
- Commissioning.

If one part changes, the others may need to change too.

**Professional**

The core direction is to keep the concrete thermal mass useful, make ventilation deliberate, keep ducts accessible, avoid unnecessary combustion, and design the mechanical room as a maintainable service zone.

HVAC should be coordinated with the envelope, plumbing, electrical service, branch circuits, controls, and documentation. A tight concrete house can be comfortable and efficient, but only if ventilation, humidity, and exhaust are designed instead of guessed.

**Code**

Oregon BCD lists the 2025 Oregon Mechanical Specialty Code as the current mechanical code, based on the 2024 International Mechanical Code and International Fuel Gas Code, with mandatory use beginning April 1, 2026. Check the adopted code at permit time because code editions and local requirements change.

Mechanical work, exhaust systems, gas piping, venting, heating and cooling equipment, and certain appliances may require permits and inspections.

## Heat The Masses, Not Just The Air

**Simple**

Concrete can store heat. If the floor and walls stay comfortable, the room feels better even when the air is not blasting from a vent.

That is the main idea: use the building mass instead of fighting it.

**Applied**

Design choices that support this:

- Put heating and cooling where the mass can help.
- Keep the envelope tight and insulated.
- Do not oversize equipment just because the house is large.
- Use room-by-room control where practical.
- Keep controls simple enough to troubleshoot.
- Leave access to pumps, valves, manifolds, filters, and sensors.

The equipment should work steadily, not violently.

**Professional**

Radiant systems, hydronic distribution, thermal mass, and air-side ventilation can work well together. The comfort target is not only air temperature. Mean radiant temperature, floor temperature, air movement, humidity, and surface condensation risk all matter.

This concept uses hydronic radiant heating, possible radiant cooling, per-room zoning, balanced ventilation, and a high-performance envelope. The final design must come from load calculations and product-specific engineering.

**Code**

Mechanical design must comply with the adopted mechanical code, residential energy provisions, manufacturer instructions, equipment listings, and inspection requirements. If a system depends on engineered sizing or nonstandard assemblies, those documents need to be part of the approved plan set.

## Heating And Cooling Plant

**Simple**

The heating and cooling plant is the machine side of the system. It makes hot water, cold water, or conditioned air.

Choose it late enough that the final site and technology are known, but early enough that the house has space, power, drains, and piping ready.

**Applied**

Keep the plant as a defined interface:

- What does it need for power?
- What does it need for piping?
- What does it need for condensate drainage?
- What does it need for service clearance?
- How much noise does it make?
- Where will it be replaced from in the future?
- Does it need outdoor equipment?
- Does it affect the electrical load calculation?

Reserve the space before the mechanical room fills up.

**Professional**

This design treats the ground-loop heat exchanger as a black box. That is a useful planning method. The final plant could be ground-source, air-to-water, air-source with hydronic interface, or another verified system available at build time.

The rest of the house should care about interfaces: supply water temperature range, cooling strategy, pumps, controls, electrical demand, maintenance access, condensate, noise, and equipment replacement path. Do not lock the building around one product too early.

**Code**

Heat pumps, hydronic equipment, refrigerant systems, condensate disposal, electrical connections, equipment access, and product installation requirements are controlled by the adopted codes and listings.

Refrigerant work is regulated. Do not write homeowner refrigerant procedures into the book.

## Radiant Heating And Hydronic Zoning

**Simple**

Radiant heating warms the floor or another surface. The room feels warm because the surfaces are warm, not because a fan is blasting hot air.

Per-room zones let different rooms hold different temperatures.

**Applied**

For a radiant plan, document:

- Which rooms get radiant heating.
- Where manifolds go.
- How tubing reaches each room.
- Which rooms share a manifold.
- Where thermostats go.
- How loops are labeled.
- How leaks are found.
- How air is purged.
- How the system is pressure-tested.
- How finished floors affect heat transfer.

Photograph tubing before it is covered.

**Professional**

Radiant heating needs load calculation, loop layout, tubing material, oxygen-barrier strategy, manifold access, pump sizing, water temperature, balancing valves, expansion control, air removal, insulation below the active layer, and finish-floor coordination.

Per-room zoning is a comfort feature, but it adds controls, valves, wiring, balancing, and future troubleshooting. Design the hydronic layout so a failed valve or actuator can be reached without demolition.

**Code**

Hydronic piping, heating equipment, pressure testing, materials, pumps, valves, insulation, clearances, and controls must follow the adopted mechanical code, manufacturer instructions, and approved plans.

Exact loop lengths, spacing, pressures, water temperatures, and flow rates need verified design documents.

## Radiant Cooling And Condensation Risk

**Simple**

Radiant cooling can help cool a room, but cold floors can create condensation if the air is too humid.

Water on a floor is not comfort. It is a failure.

**Applied**

Before using radiant cooling, plan:

- Dew point sensing.
- Humidity control.
- Automatic shutoff or temperature reset.
- Backup cooling for peak days.
- Floor finish compatibility.
- Alarm or notification if condensation risk rises.
- A way to test the system before summer.

Radiant cooling should not run blind.

**Professional**

Radiant cooling is limited by dew point, surface temperature, latent load, ventilation humidity, internal gains, solar gains, and control response. It can reduce sensible cooling load, but it does not remove moisture by itself.

For this house, radiant cooling should be treated as one tool alongside dehumidification, ventilation control, shading, envelope performance, and optional fan coils where needed.

**Code**

Cooling equipment, hydronic piping, condensate control, controls, and equipment installation are code and listing issues. If the design uses radiant cooling, the approved plans should address condensation prevention and control logic.

## Ventilation Strategy

**Simple**

A tight house needs planned fresh air. Open windows are nice, but they are not a whole-house ventilation system.

Fresh air should enter on purpose. Stale air should leave on purpose.

**Applied**

Plan ventilation paths:

- Fresh air to bedrooms and living areas.
- Exhaust from bathrooms.
- Exhaust from laundry or wet rooms where needed.
- Kitchen background ventilation separate from the cooking hood, if used.
- Transfer paths under doors or through grilles.
- Filters where they can be reached.
- Outdoor intake away from exhaust, smoke, dust, and pests.
- Controls that occupants can understand.

Balance the system after installation.

**Professional**

Balanced ventilation with heat or energy recovery fits this house because the envelope is intended to be tight. The HRV versus ERV decision should be based on climate, humidity, radiant cooling strategy, occupant load, equipment availability, maintenance, and commissioning.

Ventilation ducts should be short, smooth, accessible, and inside the conditioned envelope where practical. Duct leakage inside the envelope is less damaging than duct leakage outside the envelope, but leakage still reduces control and balance.

**Code**

Ventilation requirements are controlled by the adopted mechanical code, residential energy provisions, and referenced standards such as ASHRAE 62.2 where applicable.

Use the current code and approved ventilation calculation for exact airflow rates.

## Humidity And Indoor Air Quality

**Simple**

Too much moisture creates condensation, mold risk, comfort problems, and poor air quality. Too little moisture can also feel bad.

Humidity needs control, not guesswork.

**Applied**

Watch the moisture sources:

- Showers.
- Cooking.
- Laundry.
- Wet boots and coats.
- Plants.
- Occupants.
- Outdoor air.
- Basement storage.
- Any water leak.

Use ventilation, exhaust, dehumidification, drainage, and maintenance together.

**Professional**

Humidity control must serve both comfort and building durability. It also protects radiant cooling. If the indoor dew point rises too high, the cooling system must respond before condensation forms.

Filtration and source control belong in the same conversation. A good HVAC system cannot fix stored chemicals, wet materials, bad exhaust, dirty filters, or combustion products released indoors.

**Code**

Ventilation, exhaust, equipment installation, condensate disposal, and some indoor air quality requirements are code-controlled. Filtration level, dehumidifier capacity, humidity target, and sensor strategy should be verified against the final design and equipment instructions.

## Kitchen Exhaust And Makeup Air

**Simple**

Cooking makes smoke, grease, steam, odors, and particles. A real hood sends those outside.

In a tight house, a strong hood also needs replacement air.

**Applied**

Plan the range hood before cabinets:

- Cooktop type.
- Hood capture area.
- Exhaust route.
- Duct size from the hood design.
- Exterior termination.
- Grease filter access.
- Noise level.
- Makeup air path.
- Interlock between hood and makeup air if required.
- Location away from ventilation intake.

Do not let cabinet design force a bad duct route.

**Professional**

Kitchen exhaust is source control. Capture efficiency matters more than decorative appearance. A high-output hood without makeup air can depressurize a tight house, pull air backward through other exhaust paths, and disturb combustion appliances if any exist.

This design should favor a ducted hood, short exhaust path, accessible duct, cleanable filters, and planned makeup air where required or needed by the pressure strategy.

**Code**

Domestic cooking exhaust, hood ducting, terminations, grease handling, airflow, and makeup air are controlled by the adopted mechanical code and product instructions.

Verify the current makeup-air trigger and interlock requirements before writing exact hood airflow numbers.

## Dryer Exhaust

**Simple**

A vented dryer sends hot, wet, lint-filled air outside. Lint can burn. Dryer ducts must stay short, clean, and accessible.

A heat-pump dryer can avoid an exterior dryer vent.

**Applied**

Decide early:

- Vented electric dryer.
- Heat-pump dryer.
- Gas dryer, if combustion is allowed.
- Dryer location.
- Exterior vent route.
- Lint access.
- Condensate drain if using a heat-pump dryer.
- Makeup-air effect in a tight house.
- Maintenance access.

Do not bury a long dryer duct with hidden elbows.

**Professional**

Dryer exhaust should be direct, smooth, cleanable, and terminated correctly. A heat-pump dryer fits the airtight-envelope strategy because it removes a wall penetration and reduces exhaust imbalance, but it changes drying time, condensate handling, and appliance selection.

Gas dryers add combustion and venting issues. They do not fit the all-electric default unless the owner intentionally chooses combustion.

**Code**

Dryer exhaust ducts, materials, lengths, terminations, booster fans, gas appliance requirements, and installation instructions are code-controlled.

Use the dryer manufacturer instructions and adopted mechanical code for exact duct sizing and maximum length.

## Combustion Strategy

**Simple**

Combustion means burning fuel inside or attached to the house. It can make heat, but it can also make carbon monoxide, moisture, and exhaust problems.

The simplest tight-house strategy is all electric.

**Applied**

If combustion is considered, decide before design is complete:

- Gas cooking or induction.
- Wood stove, pellet stove, gas fireplace, or electric fireplace.
- Backup heat need.
- Combustion air path.
- Flue or chimney route.
- Clearance to combustibles.
- Carbon monoxide alarms.
- Makeup air.
- Fire and ash handling.
- Fuel storage.

Combustion should never be added casually after the envelope is finished.

**Professional**

The design direction is an all-electric default with combustion only by deliberate choice. That is sound for a tight concrete house. It avoids gas service, combustion air conflicts, flue penetrations, indoor combustion byproducts, and CO risk from heating equipment.

If a wood stove, pellet stove, gas fireplace, gas cooktop, or other combustion appliance is retained, use sealed or direct-vent equipment where possible, dedicated combustion air where required, manufacturer clearances, and verified CO detection.

**Code**

Combustion appliances, chimneys, vents, gas piping, combustion air, clearances, makeup air, and carbon monoxide alarms are code-controlled. Some fuel-gas and fire-related work may require licensed trades or separate inspection paths.

Do not use a fireplace or stove as a workaround for incomplete HVAC design.

## Ductwork And Air Distribution

**Simple**

This house should not need big heating ducts if radiant systems handle most heating and cooling. The ducts mainly move fresh air and exhaust stale air.

Keep ducts accessible.

**Applied**

Good duct planning means:

- Ducts inside conditioned space where practical.
- Short routes.
- Smooth ducts.
- Sealed joints.
- Accessible dampers.
- Accessible filters.
- Cleanout access where needed.
- Quiet air speeds.
- No crushed flex duct.
- Register locations that do not blow on people.

Label dampers after balancing.

**Professional**

Ventilation-only ductwork can be much smaller and simpler than forced-air distribution, but it still needs real design. Supply air, exhaust air, transfer air, pressure balance, noise, filtration, cleaning, and balancing all matter.

The service layer should make ducts inspectable and modifiable. That is one of the advantages of this house.

**Code**

Duct construction, duct materials, joint sealing, duct insulation, support, access, firestopping, exhaust separation, terminations, and balancing requirements are controlled by code and standards.

If ductwork crosses unconditioned space or rated assemblies, additional requirements apply.

## Mechanical Room

**Simple**

Put the complicated equipment in one serviceable place. Leave room to stand in front of it, open panels, change filters, and replace equipment.

Do not build a mechanical room that only works on paper.

**Applied**

Reserve space for:

- Heat pump or plant equipment.
- Manifolds.
- Pumps.
- Expansion tank.
- ERV or HRV.
- Dehumidifier.
- Filters.
- Condensate drain.
- Water heater if located there.
- Water treatment if located there.
- Electrical equipment with required working space.
- Controls.
- Tools and service access.
- Wall diagram and maintenance log.

Keep water and electrical clearances straight.

**Professional**

Mechanical-room design is layout, replacement path, service clearance, condensate management, noise control, vibration isolation, floor drain strategy, electrical working space, lighting, combustion separation if any, and documentation.

The room should support future equipment changes. A heat pump, ERV, water heater, dehumidifier, and controls may all be replaced during the life of the building.

**Code**

Mechanical equipment access, service space, condensate disposal, working clearances, electrical clearances, equipment installation, combustion appliance restrictions, and manufacturer instructions control the final mechanical-room layout.

## Controls And Monitoring

**Simple**

Controls should make the system easier to use, not harder. If the internet is down, the house should still heat, cool, ventilate, and protect itself.

Local control matters.

**Applied**

Monitor the basics:

- Room temperature.
- Room humidity.
- System mode.
- Filter status.
- Pump or fan faults.
- Water leaks.
- Condensate problems.
- Ventilation boost.
- Range hood and makeup air status.
- Dehumidifier runtime.
- Outdoor temperature.

Use alerts for real problems, not noise.

**Professional**

The control stack should separate required equipment operation from optional smart-home features. Equipment must run locally. Smart-home integration can add dashboards, logging, schedules, and alerts, but it should not become a single point of failure.

For hydronic zoning, ventilation, dehumidification, and radiant cooling, control logic matters as much as hardware. Poor controls can cause short cycling, humidity drift, condensation risk, or comfort complaints even when equipment is sized correctly.

**Code**

Controls, sensors, thermostats, safety devices, equipment interlocks, makeup-air interlocks, combustion safety controls, and listed equipment instructions must be followed. If controls affect required ventilation, exhaust, safety shutdown, or equipment protection, they are not optional gadgets.

## Commissioning And Seasonal Tuning

**Simple**

The HVAC system is not finished when the equipment turns on. It needs testing, balancing, and adjustment.

Commissioning is how you prove it works.

**Applied**

Before accepting the system, verify:

- Equipment matches the approved design.
- Filters are installed.
- Ducts are sealed and accessible.
- Hydronic loops are labeled.
- Manifolds are accessible.
- Pumps run correctly.
- Valves open and close.
- Thermostats control the right zones.
- Ventilation supplies and exhausts are balanced.
- Range hood and makeup air work together.
- Dryer exhaust is clear.
- Condensate drains.
- Controls and alarms work.
- As-built drawings are updated.

Retest after the first winter and first summer.

**Professional**

Commissioning should document hydronic pressure tests, flow balance, air balance, control sequence, sensor calibration, ventilation boost, dehumidifier operation, condensation protection, equipment clearances, filter access, condensate drainage, alarm behavior, and seasonal performance.

Keep the first year as a tuning period. The building mass, occupants, ventilation settings, humidity loads, and control logic all reveal themselves over time.

**Code**

Mechanical inspection, energy-code compliance, ventilation commissioning, equipment startup, manufacturer startup procedures, and local approval determine when the system can be accepted.

Do not cover or accept mechanical work until required inspections and commissioning records are complete.
