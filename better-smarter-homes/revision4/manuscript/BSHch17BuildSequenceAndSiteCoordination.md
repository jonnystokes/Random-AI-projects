# Chapter 17: Build Sequence And Site Coordination

This chapter explains the order of the build: how an empty site becomes a dried-in concrete house, how rough systems are coordinated, how finishes follow, and how the project moves toward occupancy without losing control of quality.

The build should happen in useful stages: pace the work for a real owner-builder life, hire help where time or skill matters, document everything, and treat occupancy as a major milestone rather than the end of the project.

Exact phase durations, costs, inspection timing, permit timing, contractor scope, driveway requirements, temporary utility requirements, curing times, weather windows, and occupancy requirements must be verified for the actual site, jurisdiction, season, approved plans, and building official.

## Build Sequence Scope

**Simple**

A house is not built all at once.

It moves through stages: land, design, permits, dirt work, foundation, walls, roof, windows, rough systems, insulation, finishes, inspections, and move-in.

The order matters because one mistake can trap the next trade.

**Applied**

Use this chapter as the project map:

| Stage | Main Goal |
|---|---|
| Site and feasibility | Prove the land can support the house. |
| Design and permits | Turn the idea into approved documents. |
| Site preparation | Make the land buildable and serviceable. |
| Foundation | Create the base and below-grade protection. |
| Structure | Build the concrete and masonry body. |
| Roof and openings | Dry in the house. |
| Rough systems | Install plumbing, electrical, HVAC, and low-voltage. |
| Insulation and cover | Close assemblies after inspection. |
| Finishes | Make the house livable. |
| Occupancy | Prove the required systems are safe and usable. |

Keep a current version of the sequence in the project binder.

**Professional**

The build sequence is a dependency map. Gravity drains need slope before finishes hide them. Electrical boxes need location before drywall. Waterproofing needs inspection before backfill. Radiant tubing needs photos before topping slabs. Windows need opening verification before ordering. Roof work needs weather planning. Occupancy needs a coherent life-safety and utility state.

This chapter should not replace the technical chapters. It tells when to use them.

**Code**

Building sequence is controlled by permits, inspections, approved plans, utility requirements, safety rules, weather protection, manufacturer instructions, and the building official.

Do not cover, backfill, energize, pressurize, or occupy work before the required approval path is satisfied.

## Timeline Reality And Sustainable Pace

**Simple**

An owner-builder project takes time.

That is not failure. It is the tradeoff for control, learning, cost management, and building something carefully.

The project has to fit real life or it will grind the owner down.

**Applied**

Plan a rhythm, not a fantasy:

- Use weeknights for ordering, drawings, calls, cleanup, and planning.
- Use weekends for physical work.
- Use planned time off for big pours, roof work, utility work, or crew-heavy events.
- Build rest into the schedule.
- Stop before tired work becomes bad work.
- Keep a short list of next tasks so the next workday starts fast.

Every work session should end with the site safer and clearer than it started.

**Professional**

A professional crew compresses labor with people, equipment, and repetition. An owner-builder compresses cost with patience, planning, and selective hiring.

The risk is not a long timeline. The risk is an unmanaged timeline: exposed materials, missed inspections, unpaid storage, weather damage, lost motivation, stale quotes, life conflicts, and quality decline.

**Code**

Permits can expire, inspections can require sequencing, temporary erosion control may need maintenance, and some approvals may be tied to current code editions or permit conditions.

Verify permit duration, extension rules, inspection scheduling, temporary occupancy rules, and required site protections with the jurisdiction.

## DIY, Hire Out, And Work Alongside

**Simple**

Some jobs are good owner-builder jobs. Some are not.

The goal is not to prove you can do everything. The goal is to get the house built correctly.

**Applied**

Sort tasks into three groups:

| Group | Good Fit |
|---|---|
| DIY | Repetitive, learnable, not time-critical, safe with normal tools and training. |
| Hire out | Licensed, high-risk, equipment-heavy, time-critical, or specialized. |
| Work alongside | A pro sets the method, then the owner repeats the work. |

Good work-alongside candidates include first masonry layout, first window, first manifold, first tile area, and first cabinet run.

**Professional**

Hire decisions should weigh licensing, safety, equipment, time compression, inspection risk, repeatability, tolerance, weather exposure, and visible quality. Excavation, septic, well drilling, large pours, heavy lifts, refrigerant work, roofing, specialty waterproofing, and high-end drywall finishing often justify professional help.

DIY is strongest where the task is repetitive and inspection-friendly: layout support, block work after coaching, routing, labeling, insulation, documentation, selected finish work, site cleanup, and maintenance access.

**Code**

Owner-builder allowances, licensed trade requirements, septic rules, well rules, electrical rules, plumbing rules, mechanical rules, refrigerant rules, safety requirements, and inspection acceptance vary by task and jurisdiction.

Confirm what the owner can legally perform before scheduling it as DIY work.

## Site Selection And Due Diligence

**Simple**

The build starts before buying land.

A bad site can make a good house impossible or too expensive. Check water, septic, access, zoning, drainage, slope, utilities, soil, hazards, and title before falling in love with the view.

**Applied**

Before purchase, check:

- Zoning and allowed use.
- Setbacks and buildable area.
- Legal access.
- Driveway feasibility.
- Utility availability.
- Well or water supply.
- Septic feasibility or sewer connection.
- Flood, landslide, wildfire, and other hazards.
- Soil and drainage risk.
- Easements, covenants, and title exceptions.
- Internet and cellular service.

Put contingencies in the offer when due diligence is not complete.

**Professional**

Site due diligence should be treated as design work. The land controls excavation, foundation, septic, water, utility trenching, access, fire response, driveway grade, erosion control, material delivery, crane access, concrete truck access, solar orientation, and weather exposure.

A buildable parcel is not only legal. It is practical.

**Code**

Zoning, land use, septic, well, driveway access, floodplain, erosion control, environmental constraints, fire access, utility service, and building permits can each have separate approval paths.

Do not rely on a real estate listing for buildability. Verify with the proper authorities and professionals.

## Design, Permits, And Pre-Construction

**Simple**

Before digging, the house needs approved plans and a ready jobsite plan.

This is where drawings, engineering, permits, suppliers, contractors, utilities, and money get organized.

**Applied**

Pre-construction tasks:

- Finalize the site plan.
- Finalize the floor plan and elevations.
- Complete structural design.
- Coordinate mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and energy strategy.
- Submit permits.
- Coordinate septic, well, driveway, and utility approvals.
- Set up supplier accounts.
- Identify hired trades.
- Build the procurement schedule.
- Build the inspection calendar.
- Set up the project binder and digital folder.

Do not let a permit set and a construction plan drift apart.

**Professional**

Pre-construction is where many delays are created or prevented. Structural drawings, energy compliance, site drainage, utility routing, material lead times, trade availability, and inspection sequencing should be coordinated before site work begins.

The owner-builder should know which documents are design intent, which are approved permit documents, and which are field notes. Mixing them creates mistakes.

**Code**

Permit requirements depend on jurisdiction, site, occupancy, scope, trade work, septic or sewer, well or water service, driveway access, energy path, and engineered design.

Use the approved permit documents as the construction baseline. If the field design changes, update the approval path before building the change.

## Site Preparation, Utilities, And Staging

**Simple**

Site prep makes the land ready for work.

Survey, clearing, driveway, erosion control, water, power, toilet, staging, and storage all matter before concrete trucks show up.

**Applied**

Set up the site:

- Survey and stake the building.
- Protect areas that should not be disturbed.
- Clear only what needs clearing.
- Build safe access for trucks.
- Install erosion control.
- Establish temporary power.
- Establish water for work and cleanup.
- Provide sanitation.
- Create material staging.
- Create tool storage.
- Create a waste and recycling plan.
- Mark underground hazards.

The site should be organized enough that delivery trucks know where to go.

**Professional**

Site preparation should support the whole build sequence. Access affects excavation, ready-mix delivery, pump placement, crane work, truss or steel delivery, window delivery, septic work, fire access, and emergency response.

Staging should account for theft, weather, mud, slope, drainage, material weight, handling distance, and future excavation. Do not store expensive finish materials on an unsecured rough site.

**Code**

Survey, erosion control, driveway access, temporary power, temporary sanitation, well work, septic work, utility trenching, excavation safety, fire access, and stormwater controls can all be regulated.

Verify temporary site requirements before work starts.

## Foundation And Sub-Slab Work

**Simple**

The foundation is the hardest part to fix later.

Get the soil, drainage, insulation, vapor control, radon path, reinforcement, sleeves, forms, and concrete placement right before anything is covered.

**Applied**

Foundation sequence:

- Confirm layout.
- Excavate safely.
- Prepare subgrade.
- Place drainage layer.
- Place radon or soil-gas components where used.
- Place under-slab insulation where used.
- Place vapor barrier.
- Place sleeves and penetrations.
- Tie reinforcement.
- Set forms and embeds.
- Call required inspection.
- Place concrete with the right crew and equipment.
- Cure and protect.
- Waterproof and drain before backfill.

Photograph everything before concrete and before backfill.

**Professional**

Foundation work combines geotechnical risk, structural work, moisture control, thermal control, radon strategy, utility sleeves, inspection timing, concrete logistics, weather, and crew coordination. A mat slab or basement foundation is not a solo improvisation on pour day.

The owner-builder can do valuable work in prep, verification, documentation, and cleanup, but large pours need enough skilled labor to place, consolidate, finish, and cure concrete on concrete time.

**Code**

Excavation, footing, foundation, reinforcement, concrete placement, soil conditions, drainage, waterproofing, insulation, radon, vapor barrier, utility sleeves, backfill, and inspection requirements must follow approved plans and adopted code.

Do not pour or backfill before required inspection approval.

## Masonry Walls

**Simple**

Masonry is slow, visible, and important.

Every course depends on the course below it. The first course matters a lot.

**Applied**

Masonry work rhythm:

- Confirm layout.
- Set corners and first course carefully.
- Keep courses level and plumb.
- Keep reinforcement in the right cells.
- Keep openings square.
- Install flashing, weeps, ties, and insulation as the wall rises.
- Keep mortar consistent.
- Clean as you go.
- Inspect before grout where required.
- Document reinforcement and embedded items before filling.

Hire coaching for the first critical layout if needed.

**Professional**

Masonry wall work is owner-builder-friendly because it is repeatable, but it is not casual. Alignment, reinforcement, grout cells, bond beams, lintels, cavity drainage, wall ties, insulation continuity, opening dimensions, and top-of-wall conditions affect later structure and envelope work.

Large grout placements, heavy lintels, high work, and structural transitions may need crew help or equipment.

**Code**

Masonry materials, reinforcement, grout, mortar, wall ties, lintels, bond beams, openings, wall height, bracing, and inspections must follow approved structural design and adopted code.

Do not grout required reinforced cells before the inspection path allows it.

## Floor Slabs And Service Layers

**Simple**

The service layer is where the house stays repairable.

Plumbing, electrical, HVAC, low-voltage, and controls need routes that can be inspected, labeled, and reached later.

**Applied**

Before a floor slab or service layer closes:

- Confirm structural support.
- Confirm openings and chases.
- Route gravity drains first where possible.
- Route ducts with access in mind.
- Route conduit and low-voltage cleanly.
- Label every run.
- Photograph everything.
- Keep junctions, valves, cleanouts, dampers, filters, and manifolds accessible.
- Call required inspections before cover.

Do not make a beautiful ceiling that hides an unserviceable system.

**Professional**

Floor and service-layer work is the coordination heart of this design. Structure, penetrations, drains, ventilation, branch circuits, feeders, network cabling, hydronic piping, acoustic control, firestopping, and access panels all compete for space.

The sequence should favor least-flexible systems first: gravity plumbing, large ducts, major conduits and feeders, then smaller branches and low-voltage paths.

**Code**

Structural slabs, steel deck, joists, penetrations, firestopping, plumbing, electrical, mechanical, low-voltage, access, and inspections are code-controlled.

Keep required boxes, cleanouts, valves, panels, dampers, and equipment accessible.

## Roof, Weather Protection, And Dry-In

**Simple**

The roof changes the whole project.

Before the roof, rain controls the schedule. After dry-in, interior work becomes much more predictable.

**Applied**

Roof planning should cover:

- Structural system.
- Crane or lift needs.
- Fall protection.
- Weather window.
- Deck or slab preparation.
- Roof insulation.
- Vapor and air control.
- Waterproofing or roofing membrane.
- Penetrations.
- Drains, scuppers, gutters, or downspouts.
- Solar-ready attachments if planned.
- Inspection before concealment where required.

Temporary tarps are not a long-term roof plan.

**Professional**

Roof work combines structure, waterproofing, thermal design, safety, access, drainage, penetrations, and weather timing. A concrete roof or steel deck and metal roof direction can both work as concepts. Either path needs professional-level detailing and careful installation.

The owner-builder should treat roof completion as a major schedule gate. Once the roof is reliable, interior work can proceed through weather that would have stopped the project earlier.

**Code**

Roof structure, roof covering, insulation, vapor control, drainage, fire classification, penetrations, fall protection, equipment supports, solar attachments, and inspections must follow approved plans, adopted code, product listings, and manufacturer instructions.

Do not invent roof details in the field.

## Windows, Exterior Doors, And Dry-In Verification

**Simple**

Windows and doors are holes in the shell.

They need to fit, flash, seal, drain, lock, and open correctly.

**Applied**

Before and during installation:

- Verify rough openings before ordering.
- Confirm sill, jamb, and head flashing sequence.
- Confirm shims and support.
- Confirm fasteners.
- Confirm air seal.
- Confirm drainage path.
- Confirm lock and latch operation.
- Water-check risky openings before trim hides the work.
- Photograph flashing before cover.

Do not rush the part that keeps rain out.

**Professional**

Window and exterior door installation is both envelope work and finish work. The opening needs structural support, tolerances, flashing, drainage, air seal, water seal, manufacturer fastening, interior finish planning, exterior finish planning, and future service access.

The build should not move into interior finish work until dry-in is real, not assumed.

**Code**

Windows, exterior doors, egress, safety glazing, air leakage, flashing, landings, locks, emergency escape openings, energy performance, and manufacturer installation requirements can all apply.

Use the approved window and door schedule and installation instructions.

## Rough-In: Plumbing, Electrical, HVAC, And Low Voltage

**Simple**

Rough-in is when the hidden systems go into the house.

This is not the time to guess. After rough-in is covered, mistakes become expensive.

**Applied**

Rough-in order usually follows constraints:

- Drains and vents before flexible systems.
- Main trunks before branches.
- Large ducts before small conduits.
- Panels and equipment locations before final branch routes.
- Low-voltage after major pathways are clear.
- Firestopping and air sealing before cover.
- Pressure tests and inspections before closing walls.

Label everything before moving on.

**Professional**

Rough-in is a coordination phase, not four separate trades ignoring each other. DWV slope, vent routing, supply manifolds, electrical panels, branch circuits, low-voltage cable, HVAC ducts, hydronic manifolds, controls, access panels, and firestopping all need space.

The service-layer concept helps only if access is preserved and documentation is serious.

**Code**

Rough plumbing, rough electrical, rough mechanical, low-voltage, firestopping, pressure tests, grounding, bonding, duct sealing, and required inspections must follow adopted codes and approved plans.

Do not insulate, pour topping slabs, or install wall cover before rough inspections and required tests are complete.

## Insulation, Air Sealing, And Topping Slabs

**Simple**

This phase locks in comfort.

Insulation, air sealing, vapor control, radiant tubing, and topping slabs all affect how the house feels for decades.

**Applied**

Before cover or topping:

- Confirm insulation type and location.
- Seal penetrations.
- Confirm vapor-control layer.
- Confirm radiant tubing layout.
- Pressure-test radiant loops where required.
- Photograph loops before cover.
- Protect tubing during pour.
- Call required inspections.
- Cure topping slabs before finish flooring.

Do not install finish floors over a slab that is not ready for that product.

**Professional**

Insulation and topping work ties the envelope, HVAC, interiors, and structure together. Radiant tubing location, slab moisture, floor finish compatibility, air leakage, vapor drying direction, acoustic control, and inspection timing all matter.

The work should be paced so tests and photos happen before the next layer hides the evidence.

**Code**

Insulation, energy compliance, air sealing, vapor control, firestopping, radiant systems, hydronic pressure testing, concrete topping, and flooring readiness may have code, standard, manufacturer, and inspection requirements.

Keep energy and insulation documentation available for inspection.

## Drywall, Interior Cover, And Wall Finishes

**Simple**

Drywall and interior cover make the house feel close to finished.

They also hide mistakes. Only cover work that is ready to be hidden.

**Applied**

Before drywall or cover:

- Finish rough inspections.
- Finish required tests.
- Photograph walls and ceilings.
- Mark backing locations.
- Confirm access panels.
- Confirm wet-area backing.
- Confirm ceiling and wall penetrations are sealed.
- Confirm boxes and fixtures are set correctly.
- Confirm no material is wet or damaged.

Drywall finishing is worth hiring when visible quality matters.

**Professional**

Interior cover is a quality gate. It locks in layout, device boxes, plumbing locations, duct locations, blocking, backing, firestopping, and air sealing. A small rework item before drywall can become demolition afterward.

Wet areas need special attention because tile can look good while the water-control system behind it is wrong.

**Code**

Interior covering, fire-rated assemblies, wet-area backing, foam thermal barriers, smoke and CO alarms, electrical boxes, plumbing access, mechanical access, and inspections can affect when cover is allowed.

Do not cover required access or unapproved work.

## Finishes, Fixtures, And Final Systems

**Simple**

Finishes make the house livable.

This is floors, tile, cabinets, counters, trim, paint, doors, fixtures, lights, appliances, and hardware.

**Applied**

Finish sequence usually follows this order:

- Wet-area waterproofing and tile.
- Cabinets and built-ins.
- Counter templating and installation.
- Flooring.
- Doors and trim.
- Paint touch-ups.
- Plumbing fixtures.
- Light fixtures and plates.
- Appliances.
- Smart-home devices.
- Final cleaning.
- Punch list.

Keep finish materials protected until the dirty work is done.

**Professional**

Finish sequencing is about preventing damage and rework. Tile wants stable substrates. Cabinets want finished layout and level floors. Counters need cabinets installed. Trim usually follows floors. Final devices follow paint. Appliances need power, water, drain, ventilation, clearance, and access.

The owner-builder should keep a punch list by room and system, not one giant memory pile.

**Code**

Final plumbing, final electrical, final mechanical, alarms, egress, guards, handrails, glazing, ventilation, fixtures, appliances, and energy items may need inspection before occupancy.

Do not treat final inspection as a decoration review. It is a safety and compliance review.

## Occupancy Push And Life After Move-In

**Simple**

Moving in is a major milestone.

It does not always mean every detail is finished. It means the house is safe and approved for the occupancy path the building official allows.

**Applied**

Before pursuing occupancy, confirm:

- Required inspections are passed.
- Required utilities work.
- Required bathroom and kitchen functions are accepted.
- Heat and ventilation are accepted.
- Water heater works.
- Egress paths are safe.
- Required alarms work.
- Stairs, guards, and handrails are safe where required.
- The occupied area is separated from unfinished work where needed.
- Tools, dust, chemicals, and hazards are controlled.

After move-in, keep a weekly work plan.

**Professional**

Temporary occupancy can be a powerful owner-builder milestone because it reduces commute time and puts the owner on site every day. It can also create stress if unfinished work, dust, safety issues, family needs, and code obligations are not controlled.

The move-in plan should separate immediate habitability, remaining finish work, exterior work, maintenance startup, warranty tracking, and final certificate work.

**Code**

Temporary Certificate of Occupancy, Certificate of Occupancy, final inspection, partial occupancy, required systems, required life-safety items, and unfinished-area separation are controlled by the building official and jurisdiction.

Do not move in before the legal occupancy path is approved.
