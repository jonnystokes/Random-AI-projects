# Chapter 10: Plumbing: DWV, Sewage, Venting, Slope, Distance, And Fixtures

This chapter covers the dirty-water side of plumbing: drain, waste, vent, sewage, fixture drains, traps, cleanouts, sewer or septic outlet, basement wastewater decisions, and fixture placement.

Clean water is pressure work. DWV is gravity, air, slope, and maintenance access. A good DWV system is quiet, vented, cleanable, and simple enough to inspect.

## DWV Purpose

**Simple**

DWV means drain, waste, and vent.

- Drains carry wastewater away.
- Waste pipes carry dirty water and solids.
- Vents let air into the system so water flows and traps do not get siphoned dry.

If drains are not vented correctly, the house can gurgle, smell, clog, or pull sewer gas past the traps.

**Applied**

The DWV plan should show:

- Every fixture.
- Every trap.
- Every trap arm.
- Every vent.
- Every vertical stack.
- Every horizontal drain.
- Every cleanout.
- The building drain.
- Sewer or septic connection.
- Backwater protection where needed.
- Basement fixture strategy.

Draw the DWV system before cutting or sleeving anything in concrete.

**Professional**

DWV performance depends on hydraulic loading, pipe diameter, slope, developed length, fitting geometry, vent location, trap seal protection, cleanout access, fixture-unit load, stack behavior, and outlet elevation. The system should drain by gravity wherever possible and use pumps only where gravity cannot work.

DWV should be treated as a separate system from water supply. Keep that separation in the drawings and in the inspection record.

**Code**

The OPSC governs sanitary drainage, venting, traps, cleanouts, backwater protection, fixture connections, sewer connection, and alternative plumbing systems. Exact pipe sizes, slopes, developed lengths, trap-arm limits, vent sizes, and cleanout locations must come from the current adopted code and approved plans.

## Wet-Zone And Stack Planning

**Simple**

The easiest drain is short, straight, sloped, and easy to clean.

Stacking bathrooms and keeping wet rooms near each other makes the whole plumbing system easier.

**Applied**

Use a wet-zone plan when the floor plan allows:

- Stack bathrooms vertically.
- Keep laundry near the main plumbing wall.
- Keep the kitchen near the plumbing core if practical.
- Put the main stack where it can rise cleanly.
- Keep horizontal drains short.
- Keep vents simple.
- Keep cleanouts reachable.
- Avoid random pipes buried in concrete.

If a fixture moves far away, trace its drain, vent, cleanout, and hot-water wait time before approving the layout.

**Professional**

Wet-zone planning reduces fixture-branch length, vent complexity, roof penetrations, slab penetrations, cleanout access problems, and future repair difficulty. It also reduces coordination conflicts with structure, insulation, air sealing, and sound control.

For a concrete house, the stack location should be settled before foundation, slab, wall, and service-layer details are finalized. It is cheaper to move a bathroom on paper than to core concrete later.

**Code**

Plumbing drawings should identify fixture locations, drainage route, vent route, pipe sizes, cleanouts, and connection to the approved sewer or onsite wastewater system. Inspectors need access before cover.

## Fixture Drain Sizing

**Simple**

Every fixture needs a drain big enough for the water and solids it sends out.

A toilet, shower, sink, tub, and washing machine do not all use the same drain size.

**Applied**

Use fixture sizing as a code-table task:

| Fixture Group | Design Check |
|---|---|
| Lavatory and hand sink | Trap, trap arm, drain, vent, cleanout access |
| Kitchen sink | Grease, food particles, trap, cleanout access |
| Shower | Drain body, flow rate, trap access, waterproofing |
| Tub | Fill and drain rate, overflow, trap access |
| Toilet | Waste line, closet flange, vent, clearances |
| Laundry | Standpipe, trap, vent, overflow risk |
| Floor drain | Trap primer, venting, cleanout, backwater risk |

Do not copy a size table blindly. Use the approved code table and fixture schedule.

**Professional**

Fixture-unit loading, developed length, slope, fitting selection, venting, and cleanout access work together. Oversizing a drain is not always better because low flow can reduce scouring. Undersizing creates slow drainage and clog risk.

Luxury fixtures can change the calculation. Large tubs, multiple shower heads, laundry equipment, utility sinks, floor drains, and outdoor service sinks should be included before the drain layout is finalized.

**Code**

The OPSC drainage tables and fixture-unit rules control final sizing. Common residential sizes should be treated as starting points until the current code tables and fixture specifications are checked.

## Slope And Flow

**Simple**

Drains need slope. Too flat and water stalls. Too steep and water can leave solids behind.

Slope is not decoration. It is what makes gravity plumbing work.

**Applied**

Before installing horizontal drains:

- Mark pipe elevations.
- Confirm direction of flow.
- Confirm pipe slope.
- Use the correct fittings for drainage.
- Support the pipe so it does not sag.
- Keep bellies out of the run.
- Keep cleanouts reachable.
- Protect pipe during backfill or concrete work.

Check slope before cover, not after the floor is finished.

**Professional**

DWV slope should maintain self-cleansing velocity without outrunning solids. The correct slope depends on pipe size, fixture load, material, fitting layout, and code table. Long flat runs, too many direction changes, sagging supports, and poor bedding create chronic clog points.

Where a drain must pass through or below concrete, sleeve and protect it, then document it with photos and measurements before the pour.

**Code**

The current OPSC controls minimum slope, fittings, materials, cleanouts, and installation. Do not write final slope values into the build set until the pipe size and fixture load are confirmed.

## Traps And Trap Seals

**Simple**

A trap is the U-shaped part under a fixture. It holds water. That water blocks sewer gas from entering the house.

If the trap dries out or gets siphoned, sewer gas can enter the room.

**Applied**

For each trap, confirm:

- Correct trap size.
- Correct trap location.
- Vent connection within the allowed distance.
- No hidden trap that cannot be serviced.
- No double-trapping.
- Trap protected from freezing.
- Trap primer where needed for floor drains.
- Access to clean the fixture drain.

Unused drains can dry out. Plan how floor drains, basement drains, and rarely used fixtures keep their trap seals.

**Professional**

Trap seal protection is one of the main reasons venting exists. A trap can fail from siphonage, backpressure, evaporation, capillary action, momentum, pressure transients, or improper fixture layout. Vent placement and trap-arm length matter.

Floor drains, utility drains, and infrequently used fixtures deserve special attention because they can dry out silently.

**Code**

Trap size, trap-arm length, vent connection, floor drain treatment, interceptors, primers, and prohibited trap arrangements are code-controlled. Use the current OPSC tables and accepted details.

## Venting Strategy

**Simple**

Drains need air. Without air, water pulls on the trap like a straw and can empty it.

A vent gives the drain system a safe air path.

**Applied**

The preferred durable strategy is simple:

- Give fixtures real vent paths where practical.
- Keep vents accessible during rough-in.
- Group vents into a clean stack where allowed.
- Minimize roof penetrations without making venting fragile.
- Avoid relying on mechanical vent devices as the first choice.
- Keep at least one true exterior vent path where required.

If a vent is hard to draw, it will probably be hard to build and inspect.

**Professional**

Individual vents are robust and easy to understand, but they use more pipe. Common vents, wet vents, circuit vents, and other code-approved arrangements can reduce pipe when designed correctly. Air-admittance valves can solve difficult layouts, but they are mechanical devices with access and replacement concerns.

The final design should balance durability, simplicity, code acceptance, roof and wall penetrations, service access, and inspection clarity.

**Code**

Venting method, vent size, vent connection height, trap-arm limits, vent termination, and air-admittance valve use are controlled by the current OPSC and local interpretation. Do not assume a sidewall vent or AAV is accepted for a specific location until checked.

## Vent Terminations

**Simple**

Vents carry sewer gas outside. They should not end near a window, door, fresh-air intake, roof deck, or place where people gather.

The vent needs to leave the building safely.

**Applied**

Before choosing roof or sidewall termination, check:

- Distance to windows and doors.
- Distance to outdoor air intakes.
- Distance to balconies, decks, patios, and occupied roof areas.
- Distance to property lines.
- Snow and ice exposure.
- Roof flashing risk.
- Insect or debris protection where allowed.
- Future roof maintenance.

The cleanest route on paper is not always the cleanest route in weather.

**Professional**

Roof penetrations are common and code-familiar, but every roof penetration is a flashing and maintenance point. Sidewall terminations can reduce roof penetrations, but they need strict clearance control and local acceptance.

Vent routing should coordinate with the air barrier, roof membrane, wall flashing, pest control, and makeup-air intake locations.

**Code**

Vent termination clearances, heights, materials, screens, location restrictions, and protection from frost or obstruction are code-controlled. Verify the current OPSC before using exact clearance values or sidewall termination details.

## Cleanouts And Service Access

**Simple**

Cleanouts are access points for clearing clogs. If a drain clogs and there is no cleanout, the repair gets ugly fast.

A cleanout hidden behind a finished wall is not really a cleanout.

**Applied**

Provide cleanout access at:

- Base of stacks.
- Long horizontal runs.
- Major direction changes.
- Building drain exit.
- Kitchen drain routes.
- Laundry drain routes.
- Basement and lower-level drain routes.
- Any location where a future cable or camera would need access.

Keep a service path to every cleanout. Label it in the project record.

**Professional**

Cleanout layout should anticipate actual maintenance. A code-minimum cleanout can still be miserable if it faces the wrong direction, lacks working clearance, sits behind equipment, or is buried under finish work.

In a concrete house, cleanout access should be treated like an architectural and maintenance detail. It belongs in the service layer, utility room, cabinet plan, or access panel plan.

**Code**

Cleanout location, size, clearance, access, and underfloor treatment are controlled by the current OPSC. Recent Oregon amendment summaries include cleanout clearance and underfloor cleanout details, so final access dimensions should be checked during the code pass.

## Sewer Or Septic Connection

**Simple**

Wastewater leaves the house through either a public sewer connection or an onsite septic system.

The site decides which path is available.

**Applied**

Confirm early:

- Is public sewer available?
- If sewer is available, where is the lateral?
- If septic is needed, has the site been evaluated?
- Where can the septic tank and drainfield go?
- What fixtures sit below the sewer or septic connection elevation?
- Does the building drain have enough slope?
- Is a pump needed?
- Is backwater protection needed?

Do not design basement plumbing until the outlet elevation is known.

**Professional**

Sewer and septic are site-geometry decisions as much as plumbing decisions. The building drain must reach the approved connection with slope, depth, cleanouts, frost protection, traffic protection, and proper material. Septic adds soil, setback, reserve area, tank, drainfield, slope, groundwater, and DEQ approval issues.

The building footprint, basement depth, driveway, well, property lines, water bodies, trees, and future additions can all affect the wastewater layout.

**Code**

Public sewer work follows the plumbing code, utility requirements, and local jurisdiction requirements. Onsite wastewater treatment systems in Oregon are regulated through DEQ onsite wastewater rules and permitting. New onsite rules are effective January 1, 2026, so use current DEQ material during final design.

## Basement Fixtures And Backwater Risk

**Simple**

Basement plumbing is risky because it may sit below the sewer or septic outlet. Water does not flow uphill by itself.

If sewage backs up, the basement is usually where it appears first.

**Applied**

Before putting any wet fixture in the basement, answer:

- Can it drain by gravity?
- Is it below the next upstream sewer or septic control elevation?
- Does it need a sewage ejector?
- Does it need a backwater valve?
- Can the pump, basin, check valve, and alarm be serviced?
- What happens during power failure?
- Where does handwash water go?
- Is the fixture allowed by the local approval path?

If those answers are not clear, keep the basement dry or use an approved waterless toilet strategy.

**Professional**

Basement fixtures create three main problems: gravity outlet elevation, sewage backup exposure, and mechanical dependence. A sewage ejector can solve elevation but adds a basin, pump, check valve, gate or ball valve, venting, electrical supply, alarm, odor control, and maintenance. A backwater valve can reduce backup risk but must remain accessible and maintained.

Avoiding wastewater below the slab is reasonable for durability. If basement plumbing is added, it should be designed as a serviceable system, not a buried surprise.

**Code**

Fixtures below the level of the upstream sewer control point can require backwater protection or pumping. Sewage ejector discharge piping, valves, fittings, access, venting, and alarms must follow the current OPSC and local requirements.

## Basement Toilet Options

**Simple**

A basement toilet is not automatically simple. It needs a safe waste path.

The main choices are: no basement toilet, gravity drain if the site allows it, sewage pump, macerating system, composting toilet, or incinerating toilet.

**Applied**

Compare options before choosing:

| Option | Main Advantage | Main Risk |
|---|---|---|
| No basement toilet | Simplest and lowest failure risk | Less convenience |
| Gravity flush toilet | Normal use, no pump | Only works if outlet elevation allows |
| Sewage ejector | Works below sewer elevation | Mechanical system, power, maintenance |
| Macerating toilet | Smaller discharge path | Mechanical equipment and approval path |
| Composting toilet | Low water use | Approval, maintenance, odor control |
| Incinerating toilet | No sewage drain | Electrical load, venting, approval |

Do not route graywater or toilet waste into the ground without an approved system.

**Professional**

Waterless and alternative toilets need a real approval path, ventilation plan, maintenance plan, handwash-water plan, and user plan. They should not be treated as a loophole around wastewater rules. Composting and incinerating systems can be useful, but product listing, DEQ treatment, local approval, solids handling, graywater disposal, and resale understanding matter.

A Venturi-style concept for lifting basement wastewater remains unresolved research, not a build instruction. Use a conventional approved sewage ejector, macerating system, gravity drain, or approved alternative toilet unless the Venturi approach is verified and approved.

**Code**

Alternative toilets, non-water-carried sewage, graywater, dry wells, sewage ejectors, and septic connections can trigger OPSC, DEQ, product-listing, and local approval requirements. Use current official sources before selecting the basement toilet path.

## Graywater And Alternate Wastewater

**Simple**

Graywater is used water from places like showers, tubs, bathroom sinks, kitchen sinks, and laundry. It is not toilet waste.

Graywater still needs rules. It can carry dirt, soap, food, grease, and germs.

**Applied**

Do not mix these categories:

- Potable water.
- Nonpotable rainwater.
- Graywater.
- Blackwater from toilets.
- Sewage.
- Stormwater.
- Foundation drainage.

Each category needs its own approved path. Label nonpotable systems clearly.

**Professional**

Graywater reuse can reduce potable demand, but it adds treatment, storage, exposure control, setback, irrigation, maintenance, monitoring, and user behavior requirements. Kitchen and laundry water can be more difficult than bathroom sink or shower water because of grease, food, chemicals, lint, and detergent chemistry.

For this house, graywater should stay optional until the final site, landscape, septic, and permitting strategy are settled.

**Code**

Oregon DEQ regulates graywater reuse and disposal under Oregon Administrative Rules for graywater. DEQ identifies graywater types based on treatment level and sets requirements for permitted uses, treatment, monitoring, setbacks, access, exposure control, and site management.

## Fixture Placement And Clearances

**Simple**

Fixtures need room around them. A toilet squeezed against a wall is not just annoying. It may fail inspection.

Show the fixture, the drain, the water supply, the vent, and the service access together.

**Applied**

For each bathroom, kitchen, laundry, and utility area, confirm:

- Fixture centerlines.
- Clear floor space.
- Door swing.
- Trap location.
- Vent route.
- Shutoff access.
- Drain route.
- Cleanout access.
- Waterproofing and floor slope where needed.
- Access panel if a serviceable part is hidden.

Do the layout at full scale before rough-in.

**Professional**

Fixture placement is coordination between plumbing, framing or masonry, waterproofing, accessibility, finishes, cabinets, doors, electrical, ventilation, and service access. A technically code-sized fixture can still be bad if it cannot be cleaned, serviced, or used comfortably.

The fixture plan should be reviewed before slab sleeves, wall cores, masonry openings, and cabinet rough-ins are fixed.

**Code**

Fixture clearances, fixture fittings, water-conservation requirements, trap requirements, venting, cleanouts, and access are controlled by the current plumbing code and approved plans. Accessibility requirements may apply depending on project scope and owner goals.

## Inspection, Testing, And Documentation

**Simple**

Test drains before hiding them. Once concrete, cabinets, tile, and drywall cover the work, repairs are harder.

**Applied**

Before cover, document:

- Rough plumbing approval.
- Drain and vent layout.
- Pipe sizes.
- Slopes.
- Cleanout locations.
- Vent routes and terminations.
- Basement pump or backwater valve locations if used.
- Septic or sewer approval documents.
- Photos of buried pipes.
- Changes from the approved drawing.

Keep the record with the permanent house file.

**Professional**

DWV commissioning should confirm that fixtures drain quickly, traps hold seal, vents do not gurgle, cleanouts are accessible, pumps operate correctly, alarms work, backwater valves are reachable, and no fixture creates pressure behavior that affects another fixture.

The system should be serviceable by someone who did not build it. That means drawings, photos, labels, access panels, cleanout maps, and a clear record of sewer or septic connection.

**Code**

Drainage, venting, backwater protection, sewage ejectors, septic connection, sewer connection, traps, cleanouts, fixture installation, and required inspections must satisfy the current OPSC, DEQ rules where applicable, and local inspection process. Do not cover DWV work before required approval.
