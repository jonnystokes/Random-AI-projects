# Chapter 14: Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, And Livability

This chapter covers the surfaces and rooms people touch every day: floors, walls, ceilings, doors, trim, cabinets, counters, bathrooms, closets, pantry, mudroom, lighting feel, accessibility, exterior doors, furniture fit, and move-in readiness.

The concrete structure is meant to last. The finishes are meant to serve life inside the structure. They should be durable, repairable, beautiful, and replaceable without tearing apart the permanent shell.

Exact room dimensions, door sizes, stair details, guard details, accessibility clearances, fire-separation ratings, wet-area assemblies, product ratings, fixture clearances, and finish specifications must come from the approved plans, adopted code, selected products, and manufacturer instructions.

## Interior Scope And Finish Philosophy

**Simple**

The structure is the long-life part. The finishes are the living part.

Concrete, reinforcement, foundation, drainage, and waterproofing should not be treated like decoration. Paint, flooring, cabinets, trim, counters, fixtures, and furniture can change over time.

That is good. A house can stay strong for a very long time and still let the inside change with the people who live there.

**Applied**

Plan every finish as a layer:

| Layer | Job |
|---|---|
| Structure | Carries load and keeps the house standing. |
| Control layers | Manage water, air, vapor, heat, soil gas, and fire separation. |
| Service layer | Carries wiring, plumbing, controls, and access paths. |
| Finish layer | Makes the house usable, cleanable, comfortable, and good to live in. |

Do not bury inspection points, valves, junction boxes, cleanouts, control panels, dampers, manifolds, filters, or service shutoffs behind permanent finish work.

**Professional**

The finish strategy should protect the permanent assembly. Interior finishes need to respect air barriers, vapor behavior, thermal mass, radiant heating, acoustics, service access, fire separation, and future replacement.

Treat finishes as long-lived but not sacred. A polished concrete floor may last for generations. Paint, faucets, appliances, and some light fixtures will not. Cabinets, tile, stone, doors, built-ins, and trim sit in the middle: worth doing well, but still replaceable.

**Code**

Interior work can touch code in several places: room size, light, ventilation, egress, stairs, guards, smoke alarms, carbon monoxide alarms, garage separation, interior coverings, foam thermal barriers, electrical device locations, plumbing fixture clearances, mechanical ventilation, and energy documentation.

Use the adopted code, approved plans, product listings, and inspection requirements. Do not let a decorative decision create a code problem.

## Finish Layers And Replacement Cycles

**Simple**

Not everything in a house has the same life span.

Some things should never need replacement. Some things should last decades. Some things are supposed to wear out and be changed.

Designing with that in mind makes the house less frustrating later.

**Applied**

Use this durability model when choosing materials:

| Tier | Expected Role | Examples | Design Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Permanent | Do not cover problems. | Concrete, reinforcement, foundation, masonry, structural steel. | Keep inspection and repair logic clear. |
| Semi-permanent | Worth investing in. | Doors, cabinets, tile, stone, hardwood, built-ins. | Install well, document products, make replacement possible. |
| Consumable | Planned future refresh. | Paint, carpet if used, appliances, faucets, basic fixtures, some lighting. | Do not overbuild items that will be replaced. |

Keep samples, model numbers, finish names, paint formulas, and photos in the project record.

**Professional**

Finish durability is not only material strength. It includes moisture tolerance, heat transfer, abrasion, cleaning chemistry, UV exposure, repairability, attachment method, replacement path, and compatibility with the substrate.

The wrong finish can damage a good assembly. Vapor-closed flooring over a wet slab, tile without proper movement planning, unvented wet rooms, poorly rated shower backing, or inaccessible service points can turn interior design into building failure.

**Code**

Interior coverings, wet-area materials, fire-rated assemblies, thermal barriers over foam plastic, closet lighting, electrical boxes, plumbing fixtures, mechanical exhaust, and product listings may be code-controlled.

If a finish is part of a rated, waterproofed, insulated, or mechanically ventilated assembly, follow the approved assembly and manufacturer instructions.

## Floor Finishes And Radiant Compatibility

**Simple**

The floor is not just what you walk on. In this house, the floor may also help heat the room.

Some floors work well with radiant heat. Tile, stone, polished concrete, and many engineered wood products are good candidates when designed correctly. Thick carpet and some wood or vinyl products can fight the system.

**Applied**

Choose floor finishes by room use:

| Room Type | Good Direction |
|---|---|
| Great room, kitchen, hallways | Polished or stained concrete where the slab can be the finish. |
| Bathrooms, laundry, mudroom, entry | Tile or another water-tolerant surface. |
| Bedrooms and office | Engineered wood, cork, or another warmer floor that is compatible with radiant heat. |
| Basement and shop areas | Polished concrete or another durable, moisture-tolerant finish. |

Before ordering flooring, confirm:

- Slab moisture condition.
- Radiant heat compatibility.
- Maximum floor temperature allowed by the product.
- Underlayment requirements.
- Adhesive or mortar requirements.
- Movement joint requirements.
- Cleaning and maintenance limits.

**Professional**

Polished concrete is the cleanest fit for a concrete house with radiant slabs. It preserves thermal coupling, reduces finish thickness, avoids trapped moisture under another floor, and can be refinished instead of replaced. The tradeoff is hardness, acoustics, visible cracking, and the need for good concrete placement, curing, polishing, and sealing.

Tile and stone also work well with thermal mass, but they need proper substrate preparation, movement accommodation, waterproofing where needed, crack isolation where appropriate, grout selection, and slip-resistance review. Engineered wood and cork can soften bedrooms and offices, but product selection matters.

**Code**

Floor finishes can interact with fire classification, wet-location rules, electrical heating products, radiant systems, accessibility, egress paths, and manufacturer listings. Wet floors and shower floors need special attention because slip resistance and drainage are safety issues.

Do not publish or rely on a generic floor table as a code approval. Confirm the finish system against the current code, selected product data, and approved floor assembly.

## Wall Finishes And Interior Surface Systems

**Simple**

A concrete wall can stay visible, or it can be covered.

Visible concrete feels honest and strong. Drywall, plaster, tile, wood paneling, and trim can make rooms warmer and more familiar.

The important rule is simple: do not hide a problem behind something pretty.

**Applied**

Common wall directions:

| Wall Type | Finish Direction |
|---|---|
| Interior face of concrete or masonry exterior wall | Exposed, painted, plastered, or covered with a service/furring layer and drywall. |
| Interior partition | Drywall over metal or wood framing, masonry, or another planned partition system. |
| Bathroom wet zone | Tile over an approved wet-area backing and waterproofing system. |
| Utility or service area | Durable, cleanable, accessible finish. |
| Accent room | Plaster, wood, tile, stone, or exposed masonry where it does not block service access. |

Before closing a wall, photograph and document wiring, piping, blocking, backing, shutoffs, ducts, dampers, and any embedded hardware.

**Professional**

Several valid wall-finish paths can work: exposed CMU or concrete, drywall on furring, plaster veneer, metal-stud partitions, and selected masonry partitions for sound or fire separation. The right choice depends on thermal mass goals, acoustics, cost, skill availability, future access, and the desired room feel.

Wet-area walls should be treated as systems. Tile is not waterproof by itself. The waterproof layer, backing board, fasteners, seams, corners, penetrations, slope, drain connection, and movement joints need to work together.

**Code**

Interior wall covering, gypsum board, plaster, cementitious backing, fire separation, foam thermal barriers, garage separation, bathroom ventilation, electrical boxes, and plumbing penetrations may be controlled by the adopted code and product standards.

Follow the approved wall assembly. If the wall is rated, wet, structural, air-sealed, insulated, or part of a required separation, do not substitute finish materials casually.

## Ceilings, Acoustics, And Overhead Services

**Simple**

Ceilings change how a room feels.

Exposed concrete can look strong and clean, but it can make sound bounce. Drywall can make a room feel softer and more ordinary. Plaster can feel warmer and more crafted.

Pick the ceiling based on the room, not just looks.

**Applied**

Decide ceiling finish early:

- Exposed concrete needs surface-mounted, pendant, track, or planned-in lighting.
- Drywall ceilings can hide small services and some recessed fixtures.
- Plaster ceilings need skilled prep and repair planning.
- Service rooms may need accessible panels or removable finishes.
- Hard ceilings may need rugs, furniture, acoustic panels, or other sound control.

Do not cut or chase structural concrete for a light fixture after the fact.

**Professional**

Ceiling design should coordinate structure, lighting, acoustic control, smoke and CO alarm placement, mechanical supply or return points, sprinkler planning if applicable, penetrations, and service access.

Exposed concrete is visually powerful and durable. It also reflects sound and exposes forming quality. Drywall is easier for recessed lighting and conventional room feel. Drop ceilings belong only where access matters more than appearance, such as selected utility or shop areas.

**Code**

Ceiling coverings, fire-rated assemblies, lighting outlet boxes, recessed luminaire ratings, smoke alarm placement, carbon monoxide alarm placement, mechanical registers, and penetrations must follow the adopted code and product listings.

If a ceiling is part of a rated assembly, an air barrier, or a separation, keep the assembly intact.

## Interior Doors, Trim, And Built-Ins

**Simple**

Doors and trim are not decoration only. They affect sound, privacy, daily use, and how solid the house feels.

Cheap hollow doors make a good house feel cheap. Better doors, simple trim, and good hardware make the house feel finished without making it fussy.

**Applied**

Set door and trim rules before ordering:

- Prefer solid-core interior doors where budget allows.
- Use lever handles where accessibility and daily use matter.
- Use privacy hardware where it belongs.
- Avoid keyed locks on interior doors unless the approved code path allows them.
- Use pocket or sliding doors only where their sealing limits are acceptable.
- Install blocking before walls close for future grab bars, shelves, built-ins, handrails, and heavy mirrors.
- Keep trim profiles consistent unless a room has a deliberate reason to differ.

Record door size, swing, hardware finish, hinge type, lock function, and trim profile in the project record.

**Professional**

Door design should coordinate clear opening, swing conflicts, acoustic goals, accessibility goals, return-air transfer, privacy, hardware durability, and emergency egress. A solid-core hinged door behaves differently than a pocket door or surface slider. Sliders save space, but they usually do not seal sound, light, odor, or air as well.

Trim and built-ins should cover movement joints and finish transitions without trapping moisture or blocking inspection. Built-ins need attachment backing, electrical planning, lighting, ventilation where needed, and a future removal path.

**Code**

Interior door operation, egress, garage-to-dwelling separation, fire-rated doors where required, smoke and CO alarm behavior, electrical clearances, and accessibility goals can affect door and built-in design.

If an attached garage is added later, the door and separation between garage and dwelling must be handled as a code-controlled assembly, not as an ordinary interior opening.

## Kitchens, Cabinets, Counters, And Appliance Fit

**Simple**

The kitchen should work on a normal day when someone is cooking, cleaning, unloading groceries, making coffee, and talking to another person at the same time.

Good cabinets and counters matter because the kitchen gets used hard.

**Applied**

Plan the kitchen by work zones:

| Zone | What To Coordinate |
|---|---|
| Cooking | Range, cooktop, oven, hood, landing space, power, exhaust, and fire safety. |
| Cleaning | Sink, dishwasher, trash, recycling, hot water, and lighting. |
| Food storage | Refrigerator, freezer, pantry, dry goods, and backup storage. |
| Prep | Counter space, outlets, lighting, knives, cutting boards, and small appliances. |
| Serving | Island, dining path, warming, coffee, and beverage storage. |

Before cabinets are ordered, freeze appliance models, sink location, outlet locations, lighting locations, hood route, plumbing rough-in, and floor finish thickness.

**Professional**

Cabinet quality should show up in boxes, hardware, drawer slides, hinges, shelves, finish, moisture resistance, attachment, and service access. Plywood boxes, durable drawer construction, quality soft-close hardware, stone or quartz counters, and a butcher-block island can all support that goal when they fit the budget and final design.

Counter materials should be chosen by use. Quartz is low-maintenance but heat-sensitive. Granite is durable and heat-tolerant but may need sealing. Marble can be beautiful and useful for baking, but it stains and etches. Wood is warm and repairable, but it needs maintenance and edge protection.

**Code**

Kitchen design touches electrical circuits, countertop receptacles, lighting, plumbing, dishwasher connection, disposal if used, range or cooktop installation, exhaust, makeup air where required, combustion or electric appliance rules, clearances, and product listings.

Do not finalize cabinet layout before the electrical, plumbing, and HVAC exhaust paths are coordinated.

## Bathrooms And Wet Areas

**Simple**

Bathrooms fail when water goes where it should not.

Tile, glass, stone, and nice fixtures are the visible part. Waterproofing, ventilation, drainage, slope, backing, and access are the part that protects the house.

**Applied**

Treat each bathroom as a water-control room:

- Slope shower floors to the drain.
- Use approved wet-area backing where tile is in a wet zone.
- Waterproof the shower assembly before tile.
- Protect penetrations.
- Vent the room correctly.
- Put access where valves, traps, pumps, controls, and shutoffs may need service.
- Coordinate heated floors, towel warmers, fans, lighting, GFCI protection, and controls before tile starts.

Do not use pretty tile to hide an unresolved water detail.

**Professional**

The source direction favors curbless showers, tiled wet zones, quality glass, good lighting, strong ventilation, durable vanity construction, stone or quartz tops, and accessibility features such as benches, hand showers, and future grab-bar backing.

Curbless showers are a system, not a style choice. They need floor recess or build-up planning, slope, drain placement, waterproofing continuity, glass layout, heating coordination, door swing or open-entry splash control, and finished floor transitions.

**Code**

Bathrooms touch plumbing fixture clearances, shower valves, temperature control, drainage, venting, exhaust, lighting, electrical protection, wet-location fixture ratings, glazing safety, smoke and CO alarm coordination outside sleeping areas, and accessibility goals.

Exact fixture clearances, drain sizes, venting, flow limits, shower dimensions, fan rates, glazing requirements, and electrical protection need current code, product data, and approved plans.

## Storage, Pantry, Mudroom, Laundry, And Utility Areas

**Simple**

Storage is not leftover space. It is what keeps the house from turning into clutter.

A good pantry, mudroom, closet, laundry room, and basement storage area can make the whole house feel calmer.

**Applied**

Plan storage by mess type:

| Storage Area | What It Handles |
|---|---|
| Entry or mudroom | Shoes, coats, wet gear, bags, keys, chargers, and daily carry items. |
| Pantry | Dry food, small appliances, bulk storage, kitchen overflow, and backup supplies. |
| Closets | Clothes, bedding, luggage, seasonal items, and personal storage. |
| Laundry | Cleaning supplies, folding, hanging, air drying, utility sink, and leak control. |
| Basement or shop | Tools, seasonal equipment, project materials, and deep storage. |

Give each area light, power where needed, durable flooring, ventilation where needed, and shelves that match the load.

**Professional**

Storage is a major livability feature. Built-in storage reduces dependence on furniture and keeps circulation clear. Pantry and mudroom planning should happen before final electrical, plumbing, HVAC, and cabinet decisions because these rooms often need lighting, receptacles, water, drains, exhaust, charging shelves, or special doors.

Closet lighting deserves care. Storage and light fixtures can become a heat and fire issue if the wrong fixture is placed too close to stored material.

**Code**

Closet lighting, laundry plumbing, laundry exhaust, utility sinks, electrical receptacles, GFCI protection, appliance circuits, dryer ducts, combustion appliance separation if any, and floor drains may be code-controlled.

If storage is near mechanical, electrical, plumbing, or fire-rated assemblies, keep required access and clearances open.

## Lighting, Controls, And Daily Usability

**Simple**

Good lighting makes a house easier to live in.

You need bright light for work, soft light for evenings, safe light for stairs, and quiet light for nights.

**Applied**

Use three lighting layers:

| Layer | Job |
|---|---|
| Ambient | General room light. |
| Task | Counters, mirrors, desks, laundry, shop, and reading areas. |
| Accent | Art, shelves, toe-kicks, coves, stairs, and mood lighting. |

Coordinate lighting with furniture, doors, cabinets, mirrors, fans, smoke alarms, sprinklers if any, beams, exposed concrete, and ceiling finish.

**Professional**

Lighting design belongs partly in the electrical chapter and partly here. The interior side is about use: glare, shadows, switching logic, dimming, color temperature, fixture maintenance, night paths, mirror lighting, stair visibility, and how rooms feel after dark.

Do not place lighting from an empty room plan only. Place it from the furnished room, the work surface, and the person using it.

**Code**

Required lighting outlets, switch locations, closet fixtures, stair lighting, exterior entry lighting, wet-location fixtures, energy requirements, dimmers, controls, and fixture listings are code and product issues.

Coordinate interior lighting choices with the electrical branch-circuit and smart-home plans. A beautiful fixture still needs the correct box, circuit, listing, support, and control method.

## Accessibility And Aging-In-Place

**Simple**

Accessibility is not only for a wheelchair. It helps with injuries, aging, visitors, children, moving furniture, carrying groceries, and bad days.

The best time to add accessibility is before the house is built.

**Applied**

Low-regret accessibility planning:

- Use lever handles where practical.
- Plan wider and easier door paths where the layout allows.
- Reduce unnecessary thresholds.
- Make at least one entry route easier to use.
- Put blocking in bathroom walls for future grab bars.
- Use curbless or low-threshold shower planning where practical.
- Keep hallways, turns, and furniture layouts from becoming tight traps.
- Put switches, controls, outlets, and windows where real people can reach them.
- Plan strong lighting at entries, stairs, halls, bathrooms, closets, and exterior paths.

These choices are cheap early and expensive later.

**Professional**

Accessibility should be treated as good design rather than as a medical add-on. It should be integrated into door hardware, shower design, bathroom blocking, toilet location, lighting, stair visibility, future elevator or lift provisions if desired, entry thresholds, exterior paths, and single-floor living options where the plan supports them.

Accessibility standards can provide dimensions and best practices even when a private single-family dwelling is not required to follow the full public-access framework. Use those standards carefully and state whether a feature is required or voluntary.

**Code**

Private single-family accessibility requirements depend on jurisdiction, occupancy, funding, rental or sale context, and project type. Voluntary accessibility features should still be built safely and consistently.

Before publishing exact accessibility clearances, door widths, turning spaces, grab-bar locations, shower sizes, ramp slopes, switch heights, outlet heights, stair details, or handrail dimensions, verify the current code path and chosen accessibility standard.

## Exterior Doors And Entry Transitions

**Simple**

Exterior doors work hard. They keep out weather, help keep heat in, handle daily use, and protect the house.

They also have to feel good. The front door is one of the first things people touch.

**Applied**

For every exterior door, coordinate:

- Rough opening.
- Structural support.
- Sill and threshold.
- Flashing.
- Air sealing.
- Drainage path.
- Lockset and deadbolt.
- Weatherstripping.
- Exterior landing.
- Interior flooring transition.
- Lighting.
- Doorbell, camera, or access control if used.
- Maintenance access and adjustment.

Install exterior doors as part of the envelope, not as loose finish carpentry.

**Professional**

The source direction favors solid wood, insulated steel, fiberglass, or steel-and-glass doors with durable frames, strong hardware, quality weather seals, and thoughtful daylight from sidelights or transoms. The final choice should balance thermal performance, security, maintenance, exposure, aesthetics, and repairability.

Concrete and masonry openings need accurate layout. A door can be expensive and still perform badly if the sill is wrong, flashing is incomplete, the frame is distorted, the air seal is broken, or the lock hardware does not align.

**Code**

Exterior doors, egress doors, landings, thresholds, glazing, locks, emergency operation, weather sealing, air leakage, safety glazing, electrical lighting, and access control are code-controlled topics.

If a smart lock or electronic access system is used, the door still needs safe manual operation from the inside where required.

## Color, Palette, Furniture, And Move-In Readiness

**Simple**

The house should feel like one house, not a pile of unrelated choices.

Use a small number of woods, metals, stones, tiles, paint colors, and hardware finishes. Then repeat them with purpose.

**Applied**

Before ordering finish materials, build a finish record:

| Item | Record |
|---|---|
| Paint | Brand, color, sheen, room, primer, touch-up location. |
| Flooring | Product, lot, underlayment, adhesive or mortar, maintenance guide. |
| Tile | Product, grout, trim, waterproofing system, spare tile location. |
| Cabinets | Maker, finish, hardware, hinge and slide model. |
| Counters | Material, sealer if any, care limits. |
| Doors and hardware | Door type, swing, lockset, hinge, finish. |
| Lighting | Fixture, lamp type if replaceable, driver, dimmer compatibility. |

Move-in readiness means every normal activity works without extension cords, missing lights, awkward storage, blocked furniture paths, or unfinished privacy needs.

**Professional**

The source palette favors natural materials, warm woods, stone or quartz, matte tile, polished concrete, restrained trim, consistent hardware, and a small set of paint colors. That supports a long-life house because it avoids fragile trends and makes replacement easier.

Furniture planning should happen before final electrical and lighting placement. Beds, sofas, dining tables, desks, entertainment walls, reading chairs, and dressers all affect outlets, data, lighting, switches, windows, rugs, and circulation.

**Code**

Color is not usually code. Furniture placement usually is not code. The problems appear when furnishings or finishes block egress paths, cover required devices, obstruct electrical panels, block HVAC flow, trap moisture, interfere with smoke or CO alarms, or overload shelves and built-ins.

Move-in readiness should include final approval items, safe paths, working lighting, required alarms, finished guards and handrails where required, working plumbing fixtures, ventilation, secure exterior doors, and clear access to service equipment.

## Inspection, Documentation, And Maintenance

**Simple**

Finishes are the last thing people see, but they should not be the last thing planned.

Once walls, floors, tile, cabinets, and trim go in, many mistakes become expensive to reach.

**Applied**

Before covering work, document:

- Wall and ceiling cavities.
- Blocking locations.
- Plumbing shutoffs.
- Valve access.
- Cleanouts.
- Electrical boxes.
- Conduit paths.
- Low-voltage pathways.
- Radiant tubing or heating elements.
- Waterproofing layers.
- Shower pan or wet-area tests where required.
- Product labels.
- Inspection approvals.

After finish installation, keep spare tile, flooring, paint, hardware, grout, sealer, and product manuals in the project record.

**Professional**

Interior completion should include both visual quality and hidden-system proof. Check doors for swing and latch, cabinets for level and secure attachment, counters for support and seams, tile for slope and movement joints, floors for moisture compatibility, bathrooms for exhaust and water control, lighting for control logic, and service points for access.

A concrete house is forgiving in structure but unforgiving in hidden routing. Photograph before cover, label before final, and keep the record good enough that a future repair does not require guessing.

**Code**

Interior final approval can involve building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, energy, fire separation, alarms, egress, stairs, guards, handrails, exhaust, glazing, and documentation.

The finish phase is not only cosmetic. It is where the project proves that the house is safe, usable, maintainable, and ready for occupancy.
