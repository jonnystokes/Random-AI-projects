# Chapter 11: Electrical: Service, Panels, Grounding, And Load Planning

This chapter covers the electrical backbone: utility service, meter, disconnects, panels, grounding, bonding, surge protection, service capacity, future solar, battery, generator, EV, and large-load planning.

Branch circuits, wire sizes, voltage drop, receptacles, lighting, low-voltage wiring, and detailed future conduit layouts belong in the branch-circuit chapter. Service first. Circuits second.

## Electrical Scope And Safety

**Simple**

Electricity is not forgiving. A bad connection can shock someone, start a fire, ruin equipment, or hide inside a wall for years before failing.

The electrical system needs permits, inspections, correct equipment, and clean documentation.

**Applied**

For this chapter, focus on the backbone:

- Utility service.
- Meter location.
- Exterior disconnect.
- Main panel.
- Subpanels.
- Grounding electrode system.
- Bonding.
- Surge protection.
- Load calculation.
- Space for future solar, battery, generator, EV, shop, pumps, and large appliances.

Do not mix this work with branch-circuit layout yet. Service capacity determines what the house can support.

**Professional**

Electrical planning should start with the service load, utility interface, grounding electrode system, equipment location, available fault current where relevant, future DER integration, and serviceability. A concrete house makes routing and future alteration harder, so the service-layer and conduit strategy matters more than in a conventional stud-wall house.

The design goal is not simply a large service. The goal is an electrical backbone that can be inspected, upgraded, isolated, and maintained without cutting concrete.

**Code**

Oregon uses the Oregon Electrical Specialty Code, which is based on the National Electrical Code with Oregon amendments. The current adopted edition must be checked at permit time. Oregon BCD notes that the 2026 OESC adoption process is underway, so do not assume the code edition will stay fixed.

Electrical work needs the proper permit and inspection path. Oregon residential permit guidance says installing or altering permanent wiring or electrical devices requires a permit.

## Concrete-House Electrical Strategy

**Simple**

Do not bury the electrical system where it cannot be reached. Concrete is strong, but it is not friendly to future wiring changes.

Put wires in planned routes. Leave spare paths for later.

**Applied**

The working strategy is:

- Bring utilities to one controlled exterior area.
- Keep the main electrical equipment close to the service entry.
- Use service layers and conduits for accessible routing.
- Avoid random holes through the concrete envelope.
- Put panels where they can be reached and inspected.
- Leave spare panel spaces and spare conduit paths.
- Label everything.

Future work should feel planned, not hacked in.

**Professional**

The electrical concept uses a single exterior interconnect area, a main panel inside the basement near the service entry, floor-level subpanels, accessible service-layer routing, and future provisions for solar, EV charging, batteries, outdoor utility loads, and smart-home infrastructure.

That concept is sound, but the final equipment sizes and conductor sizes must come from the load calculation, utility requirements, adopted code, and product listings.

**Code**

The approved electrical drawings should show service entry, meter, disconnects, panels, feeders, grounding electrode system, bonding, working clearances, major loads, and future provisions that affect the permit. Do not rely on a narrative description for service equipment.

## Utility Service And Meter Location

**Simple**

Power starts outside the house at the utility connection and meter. The utility must be able to reach its equipment.

The house design should make that simple.

**Applied**

Coordinate early with the utility:

- Overhead or underground service.
- Meter location.
- Transformer location or capacity.
- Service lateral route.
- Trenching path.
- Required clearances.
- Meter-main or meter-plus-disconnect style.
- Future solar or generator interconnection rules.
- Temporary power during construction.

Do not design the utility wall after the foundation is poured.

**Professional**

Service location affects trenching, conductor length, voltage drop, meter access, emergency disconnect location, grounding electrode conductor route, future solar interconnection, generator transfer equipment, and exterior equipment ratings. It also affects aesthetics and site access.

The interconnect area should be structurally capable of supporting equipment, weather-protected where required, accessible to the utility, and coordinated with water, communications, grounding, and any future energy equipment.

**Code**

Utility requirements and electrical code requirements both apply. The utility controls its service standards. The electrical code controls service equipment, disconnecting means, grounding, bonding, conductor protection, working space, and inspected installation.

## Exterior Interconnect Area

**Simple**

The exterior interconnect area is the service wall. It is where power, communications, water, and future energy equipment can be organized instead of scattered around the house.

It should not be an afterthought.

**Applied**

The interconnect area can provide space for:

- Meter.
- Exterior service disconnect.
- Utility communication equipment.
- Future solar disconnect.
- Future generator inlet or transfer equipment.
- Future battery or energy-management equipment, if allowed outdoors.
- Outdoor utility subpanel, if used.
- Spare conduit sleeves into the service layer.

Keep water equipment and electrical equipment separated as required. A neat wall still has to satisfy clearances and equipment ratings.

**Professional**

The exterior interconnect concept should be designed with listed equipment and clear separation between systems. A large exterior box can become too crowded if meter, disconnect, water, pressure equipment, communications, solar, EV, and generator gear are all forced into one cabinet.

Use the interconnect area as an organized utility zone, not as a single box that solves every conflict.

**Code**

Outdoor electrical enclosures must be rated for the exposure. Equipment working space, utility access, disconnect access, conductor protection, wet-location rules, and physical separation from plumbing or gas equipment apply.

## Service Size And Load Planning

**Simple**

Service size is the electrical capacity of the house. A small service can run out of room when the house adds EV charging, heat pumps, shop tools, a hot tub, solar, batteries, or electric water heating.

Bigger is not automatically better. The service must be calculated.

**Applied**

Make a load inventory:

| Load Group | Include |
|---|---|
| Base house | Lighting, receptacles, required small-appliance and laundry loads |
| Kitchen | Range, oven, cooktop, dishwasher, disposal, refrigerator, freezer |
| Water | Well pump, booster pump, recirculation pump, water heater, treatment equipment |
| HVAC | Heat pump, air handler, ventilation, backup heat if any |
| Outdoor | Utility bay, pumps, lighting, tools, gates, irrigation |
| Future | EV charging, shop, hot tub, sauna, solar, battery, generator interface |

Run the official load calculation before buying service equipment.

**Professional**

A larger-than-standard service may make sense because the house anticipates electric heating, hot water, pumps, EV charging, solar, battery, outdoor utility loads, and future expansion. That direction may be right, but the final answer should come from a dwelling load calculation and utility confirmation.

A modern all-electric house can use load management instead of simply increasing service size. EV chargers, batteries, smart panels, heat pump water heaters, and demand controls can reduce peak demand when designed intentionally.

**Code**

Service, feeder, and load calculations are governed by the adopted electrical code. Use the correct dwelling calculation method for the project, not an informal sum of breaker handles. Utility transformer capacity and service lateral requirements must also be confirmed.

## Main Panel And Subpanels

**Simple**

The main panel is the control center. Subpanels spread power closer to the rooms and equipment they serve.

Panels should be easy to reach, easy to label, and not used as storage walls.

**Applied**

Panel planning should show:

- Main panel location.
- Subpanel locations.
- Feeder routes.
- Working space.
- Lighting at panels.
- Spare breaker spaces.
- Large-load breakers.
- Generator, solar, battery, or EV breaker strategy.
- Clear labels.

Do not put panels behind doors, inside crowded closets, above sinks, behind appliances, or in places that will become storage.

**Professional**

Floor-level subpanels can reduce branch-circuit length and simplify maintenance. They also add feeder design, neutral-ground separation, labeling, available fault current considerations, and coordination between panels. The advantage is accessibility and shorter routing. The tradeoff is more equipment and more planning.

A concrete house benefits from subpanels when they reduce long runs through hard-to-change paths. Final feeder and panel sizing belongs in the electrical calculation set.

**Code**

Panelboards, feeders, overcurrent protection, working space, labeling, access, neutral-ground separation, grounding conductors, and enclosure ratings must follow the adopted electrical code and listed equipment instructions.

## Working Space And Equipment Location

**Simple**

Electrical panels need open working space in front of them. That space is for a person working safely, not for shelves, boxes, coats, or tools.

**Applied**

Before placing equipment, check:

- Can the panel door open fully?
- Can someone stand in front of it?
- Is there enough headroom?
- Is the area dry?
- Is there lighting?
- Is the path to the panel clear?
- Is the panel protected from impact?
- Is the equipment outdoors, damp, or wet?
- Is it away from plumbing leaks and mechanical damage?

If the space looks tight on the drawing, it will be worse in real life.

**Professional**

Working clearance is an installation and maintenance requirement, not a suggestion. Service equipment, panels, disconnects, transfer equipment, battery gear, inverters, and utility equipment all need physical space. Future equipment needs space too.

For this house, reserve wall zones for electrical equipment before the mechanical room fills with plumbing, pumps, filters, batteries, networking gear, and storage.

**Code**

Working space, access, dedicated equipment space, illumination, enclosure rating, and location restrictions are code-controlled. Use the current code and manufacturer instructions before final placement.

## Grounding And Bonding

**Simple**

Grounding gives the electrical system a connection to earth. Bonding connects metal parts together so a fault has a low-resistance path back to the breaker.

Grounding and bonding are different jobs, but they work together.

**Applied**

Plan grounding before concrete:

- Identify the grounding electrode system.
- Decide how the concrete-encased electrode will be made accessible.
- Bring a grounding connection point to an accessible location.
- Bond metal water piping where required.
- Bond gas piping where required.
- Bond structural metal where required.
- Keep neutral and grounding conductors separated in subpanels.
- Photograph grounding connections before cover.

Do not wait until after the slab is poured to ask where the grounding electrode is.

**Professional**

A concrete-encased electrode can be an excellent grounding electrode when installed and connected correctly. A concrete house may also contain extensive reinforcing steel, metal piping, steel lintels, equipment racks, exterior metal, and future lightning or solar equipment. The bonding plan should be drawn, not improvised.

The foundation reinforcing system creates a grounding opportunity. Keep that direction, but verify continuity, connection method, corrosion compatibility, accessibility, and code requirements before the pour.

**Code**

Grounding electrode systems, concrete-encased electrodes, grounding electrode conductors, bonding jumpers, water-pipe bonding, structural-metal bonding, main bonding jumper location, and subpanel neutral-ground separation are controlled by the adopted electrical code.

## Surge Protection And Lightning Planning

**Simple**

A power surge can damage electronics, appliances, pumps, controls, and network equipment.

Use whole-house surge protection and protect important low-voltage lines too.

**Applied**

Plan surge protection in layers:

- Service-level surge protective device.
- Protection for sensitive equipment.
- Protection for network, coax, antenna, gate, and outdoor camera lines.
- Bonding to the same grounding system.
- Replacement access.
- Status indicator visible or checked during maintenance.

If the house has lots of electronics, do not treat surge protection as optional decoration.

**Professional**

Surge protection depends on short conductor paths, correct device type, correct installation location, bonding, grounding, and maintenance. Low-voltage lines can bring surges in just like power conductors.

Lightning protection is separate from surge protection. Rural, exposed, hilltop, tall, or isolated sites may need a formal lightning protection evaluation. A lightning protection system must be bonded correctly to the electrical grounding system.

**Code**

The adopted electrical code includes surge-protection requirements for dwelling services and rules for surge protective devices. Lightning protection, if installed, follows listed systems and applicable standards. Do not mix lightning conductors, solar equipment, communications grounding, and service grounding without a coordinated design.

## Backup Power, Batteries, And Transfer

**Simple**

Backup power is not just a generator plug. It needs a safe way to prevent backfeeding the utility and shocking a line worker.

Plan it before the outage.

**Applied**

Decide what backup power should support:

- Refrigerator and freezer.
- Well pump or booster pump.
- Heat pump or backup heat.
- Ventilation.
- Water treatment.
- Sump or sewage pump if used.
- Network and communications.
- Lighting.
- Medical or critical loads if any.

Then choose the strategy: portable generator, standby generator, battery system, solar with battery, critical-loads panel, or whole-house transfer.

**Professional**

Backup power design is load prioritization, transfer equipment, grounding and bonding, generator neutral configuration, fuel supply, ventilation, exhaust, battery location, fire safety, utility interconnection, and inspection. A critical-loads panel can be cleaner than trying to back up everything.

For this house, water and wastewater equipment can become critical loads. A well pump, booster pump, sump pump, sewage ejector, heat pump, water heater, network rack, and ventilation system should be considered in the backup-power plan.

**Code**

Generators, transfer switches, interlocks, inlets, batteries, energy storage systems, and solar interconnections are code-controlled and utility-controlled. Backfeeding without listed transfer equipment is not acceptable.

## Solar And Battery Readiness

**Simple**

Solar and batteries are easier to add if the house is ready for them. Empty conduit and reserved wall space are cheap during construction and expensive later.

**Applied**

Plan for:

- Solar array location.
- Conduit path from array area to electrical equipment.
- Inverter or combiner location.
- Exterior disconnect location if required.
- Battery location if planned.
- Structural roof coordination.
- Utility interconnection path.
- Panel capacity or load-side connection strategy.
- Space for labels and access.

Do not drill a finished roof or concrete wall later if a sleeve can be planned now.

**Professional**

Solar readiness should not assume a specific future product. Provide physical pathways, equipment zones, structural coordination, and service capacity flexibility. The final solar design will depend on module technology, inverter type, battery type, rapid shutdown requirements, utility rules, roof assembly, grounding, and fire access.

Reserved space and conduit for solar and batteries should be treated as infrastructure planning, not as a fixed final electrical design.

**Code**

Solar photovoltaic systems, batteries, rapid shutdown, disconnects, interconnection, overcurrent protection, labeling, grounding, and utility approval are governed by the adopted electrical code, product listings, and utility requirements.

## EV And Future Large Loads

**Simple**

EV chargers, hot tubs, shops, welders, pumps, and electric heating can use a lot of power.

Plan space and capacity now, even if the equipment comes later.

**Applied**

Future-load planning should include:

- EV charger locations.
- Outdoor work area.
- Shop or garage.
- Hot tub or spa.
- Well or booster pump.
- Sewage or sump pumps.
- Electric water heating.
- Heat pump equipment.
- Solar and batteries.
- Spare conduit routes.
- Spare panel space.

A reserved path is useful even when the wire is not installed yet.

**Professional**

Large loads should be handled through service sizing, load calculation, load management, demand controls, future conduits, equipment location, and utility coordination. EV charging in particular can be controlled dynamically rather than sized as if every charger always runs at full output.

High future capacity goals are useful planning prompts, but they must be verified with actual equipment, utility service, and code-compliant load calculations.

**Code**

EV charging, spas, pumps, welders, outbuildings, solar, batteries, and large appliances each have specific code and listing requirements. Do not reserve breaker spaces as a substitute for a real load calculation.

## Inspection, Testing, And Documentation

**Simple**

Electrical work should be inspected before it is hidden. Keep records of what was installed and where.

**Applied**

Before cover and final approval, document:

- Service size and equipment model.
- Meter and disconnect locations.
- Panel schedules.
- Subpanel feeders.
- Grounding electrode connection.
- Bonding points.
- Surge protection.
- Future conduit routes.
- Solar, battery, generator, and EV reserved spaces.
- Photos before cover.
- Inspection approvals.

Labels matter. A future repair should not require guessing.

**Professional**

Electrical documentation should include one-line diagram, panel schedules, load calculation, grounding and bonding record, equipment cut sheets, service coordination notes, inspection approvals, as-built conduit paths, photos of buried work, and future-load provisions.

Commissioning should confirm correct labeling, panel directory accuracy, disconnect operation, surge-protection status, grounding and bonding continuity where tested, transfer equipment operation where installed, and accessibility.

**Code**

Electrical service, grounding, bonding, panelboards, feeders, disconnects, surge protection, transfer equipment, solar readiness, battery readiness, EV provisions, and inspections must satisfy the adopted OESC, utility requirements, listed equipment instructions, and the local inspection process. Do not energize or cover work before required approval.
