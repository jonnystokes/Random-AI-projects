# Chapter 19: Testing, Commissioning, And Quality Control

This chapter explains how the house proves itself. Testing checks assumptions before work is buried. Commissioning proves completed systems work together. Quality control keeps the build from drifting away from the approved design.

Testing involves many possible procedures. This chapter keeps the testing program, but it does not turn unverified procedure values into final instructions. Exact test methods, equipment, timing, target values, acceptance criteria, lab requirements, and code compliance claims must be verified against current standards, manufacturer instructions, approved plans, and inspection requirements.

The goal is practical: test early enough that the result can still change the work.

## Testing Scope

**Simple**

Testing answers one question:

Did the house actually do what the design said it would do?

Do not wait until move-in to find out.

**Applied**

The testing program covers:

| Track | What It Checks |
|---|---|
| Soil and site | Bearing, drainage, compaction, clay risk, water table, and site surprises. |
| Concrete | Workability, strength, curing, cracking, consolidation, and mix behavior. |
| Structure | Mockups, reinforcement, masonry quality, floor and wall behavior. |
| Envelope | Air leakage, thermal bridges, moisture, insulation, windows, and doors. |
| Plumbing | Pressure, leaks, flow, drainage, hot water, water quality, and shutoffs. |
| Electrical | Grounding, polarity, protection devices, labeling, voltage, and circuits. |
| HVAC | Airflow, ventilation, hydronic flow, controls, comfort, humidity, and startup. |
| Smart home | Local control, alerts, cameras, locks, automations, backups, and failure modes. |
| Occupancy | Whole-house readiness, records, alarms, access, and final operation. |

Every test should produce a record.

**Professional**

Testing should be built into the schedule, not added after a problem appears. The program should define the question, method, equipment, responsible person, acceptance basis, documentation, and action if the result fails.

A test without a decision is entertainment. A test that changes the work is quality control.

**Code**

Some tests are required by code, inspection, manufacturer instructions, utility rules, or approved plans. Other tests are owner quality control. Keep those categories separate.

Owner testing does not replace required inspection, required professional testing, or required commissioning.

## Quality Control Layers

**Simple**

Quality control has layers.

Design review catches paper mistakes. Testing catches physical mistakes. Inspections catch code issues. Commissioning catches systems that do not work together.

**Applied**

Use four layers:

| Layer | Timing |
|---|---|
| Design review | Before work starts. |
| Owner quality checks | Before, during, and after each phase. |
| Jurisdiction inspection | Before required work is covered or approved. |
| Commissioning | After systems are installed and before acceptance. |

Do not ask one layer to do every job.

**Professional**

Design review, mockups, field tests, inspections, commissioning, and post-occupancy monitoring should support each other. They are not duplicates. They catch different failure modes.

The most important tests happen when correction is still cheap: before the pour, before backfill, before drywall, before equipment startup, before occupancy, and before the warranty window closes.

**Code**

The adopted code and approved plans decide which tests and inspections are mandatory. The owner-builder may add more testing, but cannot remove required testing.

Where a licensed professional, certified tester, or approved lab is required, do not substitute a DIY test.

## Test Plan And Records

**Simple**

Write down what you tested.

A test that is not recorded becomes a memory. Memories are not good building records.

**Applied**

For each test, record:

- What was tested.
- Why it was tested.
- Who performed it.
- Date and weather if relevant.
- Equipment used.
- Setup condition.
- Result.
- Photos.
- Pass, fail, or needs review.
- Correction taken.
- Retest result.
- Where the record is stored.

Keep raw data, not only summaries.

**Professional**

The project should have a test matrix. It should connect each test to a system, construction phase, acceptance basis, and decision point. The matrix prevents duplicate testing while making sure critical questions are not missed.

Good records should let a future person understand what was installed, what was tested, what failed, what was corrected, and what remained as an owner decision.

**Code**

Inspection records, lab reports, equipment startup reports, pressure tests, energy reports, air leakage reports, water quality results, and other documents may be required for approval, warranty, or resale.

Store official test records with the permanent project file.

## Owner Testing And Professional Testing

**Simple**

Some tests are safe and useful for an owner-builder. Some need a lab, licensed trade, certified technician, or engineer.

Know the difference before trusting the result.

**Applied**

Owner-friendly tests can include:

- Visual inspection.
- Photos before cover.
- Layout checks.
- Mockups.
- Simple water checks.
- Leak observation.
- Thermal camera walkthroughs.
- Basic outlet testing after final device installation where allowed.
- Sensor and automation verification.
- Post-occupancy monitoring.

Professional or lab testing may be needed for structural, code, equipment, or safety-critical acceptance.

**Professional**

DIY testing is valuable when it is used for learning, screening, comparison, and early detection. It becomes dangerous when it pretends to certify code compliance, structural capacity, material strength, combustion safety, refrigerant work, electrical safety, or public-health requirements without proper authority.

The test plan should identify which results are advisory and which results are acceptance documents.

**Code**

Use certified labs, licensed trades, approved testers, engineers, or manufacturer-authorized technicians when required by code, approved plans, permit conditions, equipment startup, warranty, or inspection.

Do not use an owner test as the official record unless the authority having jurisdiction accepts it.

## Soil And Site Testing

**Simple**

The site decides how hard the foundation has to work.

Soil that looks fine at the surface can hide clay, water, fill, organic material, rock, or soft layers.

**Applied**

Soil and site checks can include:

- Desktop soil and hazard review.
- Site walk after rain.
- Drainage observation.
- Test pits where appropriate and safe.
- Hand identification of soil layers.
- Clay screening.
- Compaction checks.
- Bearing screening.
- Water table observation.
- Septic and well information.
- Geotechnical review when risk is high.

Treat owner tests as screening unless a professional accepts them.

**Professional**

The testing program can include field soil identification, ribbon testing, jar testing, hand auger sampling, test pits, pocket penetrometer use, DCP testing, compaction verification, settlement monitoring, Atterberg limits, and Proctor testing.

Those are useful tools, but exact correlations between field tests and design bearing, settlement, compaction, and seismic behavior need careful support. For now, owner testing should identify risk and inform professional questions.

**Code**

Foundation design must satisfy approved plans, adopted code, site conditions, and engineering requirements where applicable. Problem soils, fill, expansive clay, high water table, landslide risk, liquefaction risk, steep slopes, or unusual conditions may require professional geotechnical input.

Do not convert screening tests into final design values without verification.

## Concrete Testing

**Simple**

Concrete is not just gray liquid that gets hard.

The mix, water, aggregate, admixtures, fibers, placement, vibration, finishing, curing, and weather all affect the final result.

**Applied**

Concrete quality checks can include:

- Mix submittal review.
- Trial batches where useful.
- Delivery ticket review.
- Workability observation.
- Placement observation.
- Consolidation observation.
- Finishing timing.
- Curing protection.
- Cylinder or beam testing where required or useful.
- Crack and surface review after curing.

If the concrete is wrong at delivery, do not fix it casually with water.

**Professional**

The source testing program included slump, air content, cylinder casting, compressive strength testing, flexural testing, fiber-reinforced concrete testing, pour-day workflow, and in-place testing.

Those tests should be tied to the approved mix design and the role of the concrete. Foundation concrete, wall grout, floor slabs, topping slabs, mockups, and nonstructural pads do not all need the same acceptance logic.

**Code**

Concrete testing, mix design, strength acceptance, air content, specimen handling, lab testing, field sampling, curing, and placement requirements may be controlled by approved plans, referenced standards, engineering documents, and inspection requirements.

Use qualified testing when the result is needed for acceptance.

## Mockups And Structural Proofing

**Simple**

A mockup lets you make mistakes small.

Build a piece of the wall, opening, flashing, insulation, or finish before doing it across the whole house.

**Applied**

Useful mockups:

- Wall assembly section.
- Window opening and flashing.
- Waterproofing transition.
- Masonry corner.
- Service-layer access panel.
- Tile wet-area assembly.
- Polished concrete sample.
- Cabinet and trim detail.
- Exterior door threshold.

Test the mockup for fit, buildability, water control, appearance, and service access.

**Professional**

A full-scale wall mockup is especially useful for this house because the wall assembly combines concrete or masonry, insulation, ties, openings, flashing, air sealing, water control, thermal bridging, and aesthetics.

Mockups are not only visual samples. They are assembly rehearsals. They reveal tool needs, sequencing problems, tolerances, material compatibility, and field mistakes before the main build locks them in.

**Code**

Mockups do not replace approved assemblies, engineering, manufacturer instructions, or required testing unless specifically accepted.

If a mockup result changes the design, update the approved documents before building the changed assembly.

## Envelope And Air Testing

**Simple**

The envelope keeps outside outside.

Air leaks, water leaks, thermal bridges, wet materials, and bad window seals can make a strong house uncomfortable.

**Applied**

Envelope tests can include:

- Visual control-layer inspection.
- Window and door water checks where appropriate.
- Thermal camera walkthroughs.
- Smoke or tracer checks for air leaks.
- Blower-door testing.
- Insulation inspection.
- Moisture checks.
- Radon testing.
- Post-occupancy comfort monitoring.

Test before finishes hide the control layers.

**Professional**

Envelope and air testing can include blower-door testing, smoke visualization, thermal imaging, hot-box testing, indoor air monitoring, radon testing, water quality, mold and moisture inspection, and annual indoor-air verification.

For this chapter, the important structural idea is timing. Run diagnostic tests while the leaks are still reachable. A final result is useful, but a mid-build diagnostic can save far more work.

**Code**

Air leakage testing, energy compliance, insulation inspection, radon requirements, moisture control, ventilation verification, window installation, and final approval may be controlled by adopted code, local rules, product instructions, and the approved energy path.

Use accepted test methods and qualified testers where required.

## Water, Plumbing, And Drainage Testing

**Simple**

Water tests are not optional in spirit.

If water is supposed to stay in a pipe, prove it. If water is supposed to drain away, prove that too.

**Applied**

Plumbing and water checks can include:

- Water supply pressure test.
- DWV test.
- Fixture shutoff checks.
- Water heater startup review.
- Pump and tank checks where used.
- Backflow device verification where required.
- Flow checks at important fixtures.
- Drainage observation.
- Hot-water delivery observation.
- Water quality testing.
- Leak sensor testing.

Fix leaks before finishes arrive.

**Professional**

Plumbing commissioning should connect rough tests, final fixture operation, water quality, hot-water performance, recirculation behavior, pump operation, shutoff access, leak detection, and documentation.

The plumbing system should not be accepted only because it holds pressure once. It should also be usable, serviceable, labeled, and understandable.

**Code**

Plumbing tests, pressure tests, DWV tests, backflow tests, water quality requirements, fixture installation, pump systems, water heaters, and final approval must follow adopted code, approved plans, product listings, and inspection requirements.

Do not remove test setups before required inspection if the inspector needs to see them.

## Electrical And Grounding Testing

**Simple**

Electrical testing checks whether power is safe and predictable.

A light turning on is not enough.

**Applied**

Electrical checks can include:

- Panel label review.
- Circuit identification.
- Receptacle polarity check.
- Grounding check.
- GFCI test where present.
- AFCI test where present.
- Voltage check.
- Continuity checks where appropriate.
- Smoke and CO alarm test.
- Lighting control test.
- Low-voltage cable test.
- Backup power behavior where installed.

Label before the system gets complicated.

**Professional**

Electrical testing can include receptacle polarity, grounding, GFCI testing, AFCI testing, voltage verification, continuity, insulation resistance testing, and grounding system verification.

Some of these are homeowner-observable checks. Others require proper equipment, training, and safety controls. The test plan should not encourage unsafe live-panel work.

**Code**

Electrical testing, energizing, panel work, grounding, bonding, protection devices, insulation resistance testing, service equipment, alarms, and final approval must follow the adopted electrical code, product listings, utility requirements, and inspection process.

Use licensed or qualified people where required or where the hazard warrants it.

## HVAC, Hydronic, And Ventilation Commissioning

**Simple**

HVAC is not finished when the equipment turns on.

It has to heat, cool, ventilate, dehumidify, drain, balance, and respond to controls.

**Applied**

HVAC commissioning can include:

- Equipment startup.
- Filter access check.
- Condensate drainage check.
- Ventilation airflow check.
- Exhaust fan check.
- Kitchen hood behavior.
- Dryer exhaust check.
- Hydronic pressure test.
- Pump and valve operation.
- Zone control check.
- Thermostat and sensor check.
- Humidity behavior.
- Noise and vibration review.

Record settings before adjusting them.

**Professional**

HVAC testing can include airflow measurement and balancing, heat pump commissioning, hydronic commissioning, ERV commissioning, radiant response testing, and seasonal performance monitoring.

For this house, commissioning should prove interaction: envelope tightness, ventilation, humidity, radiant floors, heat pump operation, exhaust, makeup air, controls, and indoor air quality.

**Code**

Mechanical commissioning, equipment startup, ventilation testing, exhaust verification, hydronic testing, condensate, refrigerant work, combustion equipment if any, and controls must follow adopted code, manufacturer instructions, product listings, and inspection requirements.

Refrigerant work is not an owner DIY procedure.

## Smart Home And Controls Verification

**Simple**

A smart home should fail gracefully.

Test what happens when the internet goes down, power flickers, a hub reboots, a sensor battery dies, or a phone is not available.

**Applied**

Smart-home verification can include:

- Local dashboard works.
- Wall switches still work.
- Automations run locally.
- Cameras record locally.
- Door locks have manual fallback.
- Alerts reach the right people.
- Sensors report correctly.
- Network backup works.
- UPS behavior is tested.
- Remote access is secure.
- HVAC controls keep safe fallback behavior.
- Water shutoff automation is tested carefully.

Do not test a safety automation for the first time during an emergency.

**Professional**

Smart-home commissioning should separate convenience from safety. Lighting scenes, dashboards, media, and reminders can fail without major consequence. Locks, alarms, water shutoff, HVAC controls, access control, cameras, and pumps need more deliberate fallback tests.

Document automations in plain language so a future owner can understand what the house is doing.

**Code**

Smart-home systems cannot replace required life-safety systems unless listed and approved for that purpose. Controls, locks, alarms, pumps, mechanical equipment, water shutoff devices, and electrical loads must follow listings and manufacturer instructions.

Do not let automation override required manual operation or safe default behavior.

## Whole-House Commissioning

**Simple**

Whole-house commissioning asks one big question:

Does the house work as a house?

**Applied**

Before acceptance, test:

- Water on and no leaks.
- Drains working.
- Hot water working.
- Heat and ventilation working.
- Electrical devices working.
- Required alarms working.
- Lighting and controls working.
- Exterior doors working.
- Windows working.
- Exhaust working.
- Panels labeled.
- Shutoffs labeled.
- Filters accessible.
- Service points accessible.
- Documentation complete enough to use.

Walk the house like a tired person coming home at night.

**Professional**

Whole-house commissioning should verify interactions. A kitchen hood can affect pressure. A tight envelope needs ventilation. A radiant floor depends on finish materials. A network rack needs backup power. A leak sensor needs a water shutoff that actually closes. A labeled panel helps emergency response.

Commissioning should produce punch lists by system and by room.

**Code**

Whole-house acceptance may involve final building, plumbing, electrical, mechanical, energy, alarms, inspections, utility approvals, and occupancy approval.

Passing individual trade inspections does not prove the whole house is tuned.

## Post-Occupancy Monitoring

**Simple**

The best test is living in the house.

Track what works, what fails, what feels good, what wastes energy, and what needs redesign.

**Applied**

Monitor:

- Energy use.
- Water use.
- Indoor temperature.
- Indoor humidity.
- Ventilation behavior.
- Filter changes.
- Equipment runtime.
- Leak alerts.
- Radon or indoor air checks where appropriate.
- Comfort complaints.
- Maintenance work.
- Repair history.

Review the record each season.

**Professional**

Post-occupancy monitoring turns the first house into a learning system. Energy trends, comfort logs, water use, humidity, equipment runtime, air quality, maintenance events, and occupant observations show whether design assumptions survived real life.

The point is not to generate endless graphs. The point is to find drift, failure, wasted energy, comfort problems, moisture risk, and design lessons.

**Code**

Most post-occupancy monitoring is owner quality control, not code compliance. Some items, such as smoke alarms, CO alarms, water quality, septic, backflow, HVAC maintenance, or radon mitigation, may have legal, health, insurance, or manufacturer requirements.

Verify required recurring tests for the final systems actually installed.

## Knowledge Compound Effect

**Simple**

Every test makes the next decision better.

The first build teaches the second build. The record matters.

**Applied**

Keep a build dossier:

- Final approved plans.
- Test plan.
- Test results.
- Photos.
- Inspection records.
- Commissioning results.
- As-built notes.
- Product data.
- Maintenance records.
- Performance data.
- Lessons learned.

Write down what you would change next time.

**Professional**

A single test solves one question. A disciplined record across systems and builds becomes a private building science library.

That library should be searchable, dated, source-linked, and honest about failures.

**Code**

Private learning records do not replace approved plans, code compliance, required inspections, or professional design.

They do support future maintenance, troubleshooting, renovations, resale, and better future builds.
