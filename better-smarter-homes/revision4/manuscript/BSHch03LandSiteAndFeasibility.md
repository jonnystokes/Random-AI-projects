# Chapter 3: Land, Site, And Feasibility

Site conditions determine feasibility.

A house can look strong, efficient, and attractive on paper. The design must account for zoning, soil, septic, access, slope, snow, wind, water, title records, delivery trucks, and the actual driveway route.

Those facts matter. The right land reduces build complexity and supports daily life. The wrong land turns every system into a workaround.

This chapter puts property questions in order before the purchase becomes emotional.

## 3.1 Land Conditions Determine Feasibility

The right house on the wrong land is still the wrong project.

**Simple**

Land is more than scenery. Parcel conditions determine whether the house can be allowed, reached, drained, supplied with water, served by power, and built without wasting money.

An attractive parcel can still be a bad building site.

**Applied**

Before making an offer, or during the inspection period, sort the property into one of these piles:

| Result | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Dealbreaker | A core requirement is missing or blocked. | Walk away unless it is solved before closing. |
| Expensive risk | The parcel remains possible, but the fix is costly or uncertain. | Price it hard, add contingencies, and get written answers. |
| Design constraint | The parcel remains possible if the house adapts to it. | Carry the constraint into the site plan and budget. |
| Lifestyle tradeoff | The parcel remains possible, but daily life changes. | Decide if the tradeoff still fits the way you want to live. |
| Future-flexibility check | The parcel supports the initial plan, but could block later plans. | Check outbuildings, work areas, storage, solar, access, and future use. |
| Strong candidate | The major land, utility, access, septic, and code questions check out. | Move toward design with the site facts in hand. |

Do not commit to the view before checking septic, access, zoning, water, utilities, title, and drainage. Those are not details. They determine whether the project can exist.

## 3.2 Climate, Loads, And Site Conditions

Oregon is not one climate. A coastal lot, a valley lot, a mountain lot, and an eastern Oregon lot create different design loads and exposure conditions.

**Simple**

Climate sets the loads and exposures. Snow pushes down. Wind pushes sideways. Earthquakes shake the structure. Rain tests drainage and waterproofing. Frost changes how foundations are built. Sun and shade change comfort.

**Applied**

Before choosing the site, collect the design conditions for the exact parcel:

- winter design temperature
- summer design temperature
- ground snow load
- roof snow load method
- basic wind speed and exposure
- seismic design category
- frost depth or frost-protection requirement
- rainfall pattern
- wildfire exposure, if applicable
- floodplain status, if applicable
- elevation and exposure

Use planning ranges only for screening. They are not permit values.

| Condition | Early planning range for Oregon |
|---|---|
| winter design temperature | about -10 F to 25 F |
| summer design temperature | about 85 F to 105 F |
| ground snow load | about 15 psf to 300+ psf depending on elevation and location |
| common wind planning range | about 95 mph to 115 mph in much of Oregon, with higher exposure possible near the coast or gorge |
| frost depth | about 12 inches to 36 inches depending on location |
| annual rainfall | about 15 inches to 100+ inches depending on region |

Call the local building department and ask where they want design criteria pulled from for permit drawings. Save the answer in the project binder.

**Professional**

Site climate is not one number. The structural set requires design values for ground snow load, roof snow load, basic wind speed, exposure category, seismic design category, flood hazard, and frost protection. The envelope and mechanical design require outdoor design temperatures, humidity pattern, solar exposure, rain exposure, and wildfire smoke assumptions where they matter.

For a concrete home, wall mass does not make loose load paths acceptable. Gravity load, lateral load, uplift, sliding, overturning, and foundation demand must close into one permitted design.

**Code**

- ORSC R301: design criteria for residential structural loading.
- ORSC Table R301.2(1): climatic and geographic design criteria used by the jurisdiction.
- Oregon Design Criteria Hub: state BCD design-criteria tool for jurisdiction-level values where available.
- OSU/SEAO snow load lookup: site snow-load tool referenced in Oregon residential-code research notes.
- ASCE 7 hazard tools and referenced standards: wind, seismic, snow, and flood inputs where engineering uses them.
- ORSC R322: flood-resistant construction where flood hazard applies.

## 3.3 Zoning, Setbacks, And Allowed Use

Zoning is the legal label for the parcel. It defines the uses, dimensions, setbacks, and review paths that apply.

**Applied**

Before buying, call planning staff and verify the parcel. Do not ask in vague terms. Use the address, map number, or tax lot.

Ask these questions:

- Is a primary residence allowed?
- Is it allowed outright, or does it need conditional approval?
- Does the parcel meet minimum lot size?
- What are the front, side, rear, stream, road, and resource setbacks?
- Do the setbacks leave enough room for the house, driveway, septic, well, drainage, and staging?
- Is the intended height allowed?
- Are outbuildings allowed?
- Are accessory dwellings allowed?
- Are home occupations allowed?
- Are there limits on outdoor storage, testing equipment, noise, animals, farming, or exterior work areas?

Common rural categories to investigate:

| Zone type | Early read |
|---|---|
| Rural Residential | Often the cleanest target if lot size and use rules fit. |
| Mixed Use Agricultural | Can work, but depends heavily on the county. |
| Farm-Forest | Can allow a home under conditions, often with more review. |
| Non-Resource Rural Lands | Rare, but can be flexible when it exists. |
| Exclusive Farm Use | Treat carefully. Building a residence usually needs a specific qualifying path. |
| Urban residential | Usually buildable, but may not match the privacy, testing, and space goals. |

Get the answer before you commit money that cannot be recovered.

**Professional**

Land-use feasibility controls the legal envelope before the building code controls the construction. Zoning, overlay zones, resource-land rules, setbacks, road access, floodplain rules, wildfire overlays, and home-occupation limits can all change the shape of the project before the first footing is designed.

For this house, the unusual risks are not only the dwelling itself. They are the long driveway, staging area, equipment delivery, outbuildings, testing space, material storage, future business use, and anything that makes the property look less like ordinary residential use.

**Code**

- ORS Chapter 215: county land-use planning and resource-land authority.
- ORS Chapter 227: city planning and zoning authority.
- Local comprehensive plan and development code: zoning district, overlays, setbacks, conditional uses, home occupation rules, and local review path.
- County or city GIS and assessor records: parcel identity, zoning layer, tax lot, overlays, and mapped constraints.

## 3.4 Property Rights, Title, And Hidden Restrictions

Buying land does not always mean you control every right connected to it.

**Applied**

Before closing, review the preliminary title report and recorded property records for:

- access easements
- utility easements
- road maintenance agreements
- mineral rights
- timber rights
- water rights
- conservation easements
- covenants, conditions, and restrictions
- subdivision or homeowners association rules
- liens or recorded claims

If the parcel does not directly touch a public road, slow down. Confirm legal access, physical access, road maintenance responsibility, emergency access, and delivery access.

Walk the access route. A legal easement on paper is not the same as a road that can carry concrete trucks in February.

**Professional**

Title review and site feasibility belong together. A parcel can be physically buildable and still be constrained by easements, severed rights, private covenants, conservation restrictions, road obligations, water limitations, or a survey gap.

Treat the preliminary title report, assessor records, water-right records, seller disclosures, survey, road agreement, and county records as source documents. If a right affects the project, confirm it before closing.

**Code**

- ORS Chapter 93: conveyancing and recording framework for deeds and recorded property interests.
- County clerk or recorder records: deeds, easements, covenants, liens, plats, and recorded restrictions.
- Preliminary title report: practical working index of recorded matters that may affect ownership or use.
- Oregon Water Resources Department records: water rights and well-record research where private water supply is part of the plan.

## 3.5 Utilities, Water, Wastewater, And Access

Cheap land gets expensive when everything needed to live there is far away.

This is the first utility pass. Water and wastewater get a deeper check because they can stop the project before the house is even placed on the site.

**Applied**

Verify each system directly. Coverage maps and listing descriptions are not enough.

| System | Questions to answer |
|---|---|
| electrical power | Is service nearby? Where is the nearest transformer? What does extension cost? What does the utility require? |
| water | Is there municipal water, an existing well, or strong well evidence nearby? |
| wastewater | Is sewer available, or can the property pass septic evaluation? |
| internet | Which providers serve the exact address? What speed can actually be installed? |
| road access | Is it public or private? Who maintains it? Can trucks reach the building site year-round? |
| gas | Is it available? The design may not depend on it, but the answer affects options and resale. |

Call each provider with the address or tax lot. Ask for extension requirements and rough cost ranges where they will provide them. Save names, dates, and answers.

For access, think like a delivery driver:

- Can a concrete truck turn around?
- Can a pump truck set up?
- Can a lowboy deliver equipment?
- Can block, rebar, roofing, septic tanks, and windows be staged without blocking the road?
- Can emergency vehicles reach the house?

**Simple**

Power, water, wastewater, internet, and road access are not conveniences. They are what turn land into a place a house can function.

**Professional**

Utility feasibility sets early geometry. Electrical service affects service entrance and trenching. Water affects pump, pressure, storage, treatment, and backup power. Wastewater affects building location before floor plans are fixed. Access affects concrete delivery, pump setup, excavation, reinforcement, waterproofing, septic work, roofing, and emergency response.

Do not design a polished house plan for a site without verified water, wastewater, access, utilities, and service routes.

## 3.6 Septic And Water Feasibility

No sewer means septic. No water means no house.

**Applied**

If sewer is not available, make the purchase contingent on a satisfactory septic site evaluation.

The evaluation must answer:

- Does the soil drain properly?
- Is there enough usable area for the system?
- Is there a reserve area for replacement?
- What type of system is required?
- Are there setbacks from wells, water, slopes, property lines, or buildings?
- Does the system fit the house location, driveway, drainage plan, and future outbuildings?

If there is no municipal water, investigate the well path before closing:

- existing well log, if there is a well
- well yield and recovery
- static water level and drawdown where records exist
- water quality test results
- nearby well depths and yields
- legal water-use limits
- location conflicts with septic, driveway, utilities, and building footprint

Draw the house, driveway, septic area, reserve area, well, utilities, and drainage on the same site sketch. Do not place the house first and then try to squeeze water and wastewater around it.

**Simple**

Some land cannot handle wastewater. Some land cannot provide reliable water. Either problem can kill the project.

**Professional**

Septic feasibility ties soil texture, percolation behavior, limiting layer, seasonal high groundwater, slope, drainfield sizing, reserve area, and setbacks into one site layout. Water feasibility ties well yield, pump curve, static water level, drawdown, storage, treatment, pressure, freeze protection, and backup power into the same plan.

The septic area is protected infrastructure, not leftover land.

**Code**

- OAR Chapter 340 Division 71: Oregon onsite wastewater treatment and disposal rules.
- OAR 340-071-0600: sewage-disposal-service licensing rule, with owner-permittee installation context handled under the onsite program.
- ORS 537.747: water-supply well constructor licensing framework.
- OAR 690-205-0175: landowner well permit path for an owner who is not a licensed well constructor.
- OAR 690-200 through 690-240: water-supply-well construction standards referenced in the landowner well path.
- Local onsite program or county sanitarian: site evaluation, system approval, pre-cover inspection, and certificate of satisfactory completion where applicable.

## 3.7 Topography, Drainage, And Buildability

Slope and drainage affect water, truck access, and foundation choices.

**Applied**

Walk the site after rain if possible. Look for:

- where water flows
- where water stands
- soft ground
- erosion
- steep driveway routes
- low spots near the building area
- uphill water aimed at the future house
- evidence of old grading or fill
- room for trucks, concrete delivery, pump setup, and material staging

Use maps before the visit, but trust the visit more than the map. A contour line does not show mud on your boots.

**Professional**

Topography drives cut and fill, positive drainage, retaining needs, driveway grade, erosion control, staging area, waterproofing demand, flood exposure, and foundation complexity. A simple rectangle on a contour map can turn into stepped footings, tall retaining walls, import fill, difficult compaction, or a driveway that fails in winter.

For a concrete house, staging is part of feasibility. Concrete, block, rebar, equipment, and waterproofing work require physical space. A site that looks good from a drone photo can still be a poor construction site.

**Code**

- ORSC R401.3: surface drainage around foundations.
- ORSC R403.1.7: footings on or adjacent to slopes.
- ORSC R322: flood-resistant construction where flood hazard applies.
- Local grading, erosion-control, driveway, stormwater, and floodplain rules: permit triggers and site-specific conditions.

## 3.8 Distance, Logistics, And Daily Life

Distance changes the whole build. Farther land can be cheaper and quieter, but every mistake takes longer to fix.

If the hardware store is an hour away, one missing fitting can eat half a day.

**Applied**

Drive the route before buying. Check it for:

- hardware and material runs
- grocery trips
- medical access
- emergency response
- winter road conditions
- steep grades
- bridge limits
- delivery trucks
- concrete trucks
- internet and cell service

Drive it in bad weather if you can. A road that feels pleasant in July can become the project bottleneck in January.

**Professional**

Distance affects labor productivity, subcontractor willingness, delivery cost, inspection scheduling, emergency response, utility options, resale pool, and owner fatigue.

The more remote the site, the more important staging discipline becomes: extra fittings, backup tools, weather supplies, temporary power, temporary water, spare fuel, and clear delivery planning.

## 3.9 The Purchase Decision Review

This is the walk-away review.

**Applied**

Before the land purchase becomes final, sort each issue by severity.

| Severity | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Dealbreaker | Do not buy unless this is solved before closing. | No legal dwelling path, failed septic path, no legal access, unusable water source. |
| Expensive risk | Can work, but the price or uncertainty must be carried into the offer. | Long utility extension, major driveway work, difficult drainage, uncertain title issue. |
| Design constraint | The house must adapt to it. | Slope, snow load, wind exposure, solar orientation, staging limits. |
| Lifestyle tradeoff | The land works, but daily life changes. | Long drive, weak internet, winter access, distance from services. |
| Future-flexibility check | The land supports the initial plan, but could limit later plans. | Outbuildings, accessory dwelling, home occupation, testing space, equipment storage. |

Then confirm:

- zoning allows the intended primary residence
- parcel size meets the zoning minimum
- setbacks leave room for the house and site systems
- height limits work for the intended house
- septic feasibility is confirmed if sewer is not available
- water source is confirmed
- electrical service is available or extension cost is acceptable
- road access is legal, physical, and usable year-round
- internet service is acceptable for the project
- title report does not reveal a dealbreaker
- easements, conservation limits, and private restrictions are understood
- mineral, timber, and water rights are understood
- floodplain and drainage risks are acceptable
- climate design values can be obtained for the parcel
- topography works for excavation, drainage, staging, and delivery
- solar orientation is acceptable if solar is part of the plan
- distance to town, services, and emergency response is acceptable
- outbuilding, accessory dwelling, and home occupation rules fit the long-term plan

Do not force the wrong property to work. Land can be changed, but it cannot be changed for free.

If a risk cannot be priced, permitted, engineered, insured, accessed, or inspected, treat it as a possible dealbreaker.
