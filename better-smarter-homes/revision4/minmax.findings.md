# figurative Language Findings: Chapters 1-8

## Purpose

A previous retrofit pass removed personification (giving inanimate things intentions, feelings, opinions, demands). That pass succeeded at its narrow target but left a wider problem: prose that prioritizes poetic effect over literal truth. This document catalogs that wider problem.

## What Was Scanned For

- Literal-truth violations: Sentences whose surface meaning is impossible, untrue, or nonsensical when read literally.
- Figurative language standing in for plain language: Metaphor, simile, idiom, or imagery used where a direct statement would be clearer.
- Broken or strained conceits: Metaphors where the connection between vehicle and tenor is weak or forced.
- False poeticism: Writing that draws attention to itself through ornate phrasing or exaggerated framing.
- Decorative language hiding ordinary content: Phrases dressed up beyond what their content requires.
- Sensory flourish without function: Rhythmic phrasing that exists for sound rather than to convey information.
- Aphoristic phrasing: Short declarative sentences shaped to feel quotable.
- Idioms and clichés: Stock figurative expressions used in place of literal description.

## Patterns Across Chapters

1. **Personification of materials**: Concrete "gets attention," "conceals," "hides mistakes"; soil "carries the load"; the foundation "holds," "spreads," "ties into." This is the most common recurring habit — giving inanimate materials active agency.

2. **Personification of code/rules**: "Code is not a barrier... it is the approval path"; "the goal is to know which lane each decision belongs in." Code treated as an actor.

3. **Aphoristic phrasing**: Several moments where technical content is dressed in short, parallel, quotable-sounding sentences. Examples: "A house is not one big decision. It is thousands of small decisions stacked together." "A heavy house is not automatically a strong house."

4. **"carried"/"carries" imagery**: The opening line establishes a pattern where physical handling metaphors are used for conceptual work. Later chapters use "carries" in technical contexts ("soil carries the load"), which works literally, but the opening metaphor may have primed this pattern.

## Patterns Not Flagged Above

1. **Warmth in technical sections**: Occasional language that adds warmth but not information ("real satisfaction," "demanding work"). This is borderline between warmth (allowed) and decorative (questionable). The style guide appears to permit warmth as specificity and respect — these moments don't clearly violate that standard.

2. **"The X is not Y. The X is Z" structure**: Several sections use a rhetorical structure of negation-then-clarification that performs as insight but delivers basic technical definitions. Examples in Chapters 1, 4, and 6. These are repetitive patterns worth watching, but individually the meaning survives.
