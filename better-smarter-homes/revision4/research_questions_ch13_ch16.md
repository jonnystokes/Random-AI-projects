# Chapter 13: HVAC, Ventilation, Humidity, And Air Quality

## 1. Oregon Mechanical Specialty Code (OMSC) — current adoption

The **2025 OMSC**, based on the **2024 IMC and 2024 IFGC** with Oregon-specific amendments, became effective **October 1, 2025** with a six-month phase-in, and is **mandatory as of April 1, 2026**. Today is May 1, 2026, so 2025 OMSC is the current code. The phase-in has expired; the 2022 OMSC is no longer permitted.

For the OSSC parallel: **2025 OSSC** (based on 2024 IBC) is effective and mandatory on the same dates.

## 2. ORSC scope vs. OMSC scope for one- and two-family dwellings

This is the single most important framing point for the chapter, because it changes which code controls which detail.

The **2023 ORSC** (based on 2021 IRC, mandatory since April 1, 2024) governs construction of one- and two-family dwellings and townhouses, and includes a complete residential mechanical block in **Part V (Chapters 12–23)** plus the energy provisions in **Chapter 11**. ORSC R101.2 explicitly defers to OMSC only for items not addressed by ORSC, and for hydrogen-generating/refueling spaces. So for a single-family BSH home, the controlling code is the ORSC, not the OMSC, except in narrow gap-fill cases.

The **2026 ORSC** is in development with anticipated adoption October 1, 2026 — meaning a build permitted in late 2026 or 2027 may fall under different chapter numbering and amendments than today. This is a moving target Jon should plan around.

## 3. Whole-house mechanical ventilation — Oregon-specific requirements

Oregon **does not directly reference ASHRAE 62.2** for SFRs. ORSC has its own Section M1505 (Oregon-amended), which is parallel to but distinct from 62.2, and is more prescriptive on one key point: **Oregon requires balanced ventilation. Single-point exhaust-only ventilation is explicitly unacceptable** under ORSC, even though IRC base text would allow it. The June 2021 BCD Technical Bulletin makes this unambiguous and shows compliant configurations.

The continuous airflow rate is set by **Table M1505.4.3(1)**, or by formula:

**CFM = (0.01 × floor area sqft) + [7.5 × (bedrooms + 1)]**

That formula is identical to the ASHRAE 62.2-2010 calculation, but the Oregon table rounds into 15-cfm bins (30/45/60/75/90/105/120/135/150/165). Intermittent operation is allowed via **Table M1505.4.3(2)**, with multipliers — 25% runtime requires 4× rate, 50% requires 2×, 100% is 1×. Extrapolation beyond the table is prohibited.

Compliant configurations include: paired supply and exhaust fans; HRV/ERV; CFIS (central fan integrated supply) where the furnace is the intake fan, interlocked with exhaust and an override switch; or balanced bath/kitchen exhaust with paired ducted supply. The system must be balanced to within 10% supply-vs-exhaust and have user-accessible manual override controls. A 1.0-sone limit applies to continuously-operating fans.

## 4. ASHRAE 62.2 reconciliation

ASHRAE **62.2-2022** is the current published edition, with addendum e (2023). It uses the same basic formula for whole-building airflow but adds: a 50% infiltration credit when blower-door tested per Section 4.1.3 (ORSC does not give this credit); local exhaust requirements separately tabulated in Section 5; specific filtration requirements in 4.1.4; and explicit installation/maintenance requirements in 6.2. ORSC takes 62.2 concepts but cuts the infiltration credit and adds the balanced-ventilation mandate.

For BSH, treat 62.2-2022 as the **best-practice design standard** and ORSC M1505 as the **floor**. They will not conflict for a system designed to 62.2; they will conflict only if you try to take the 62.2 infiltration credit, which Oregon doesn't allow.

## 5. Local exhaust — kitchen, bath, laundry

ORSC M1505.5 sets minimum local exhaust capacities by Table M1505.5. For comparison, ASHRAE 62.2 baseline values are:

- Bathrooms: 50 CFM intermittent or 20 CFM continuous
- Kitchens: 100 CFM intermittent or 5 ACH continuous (referenced to kitchen volume, not whole house)
- Laundry: not separately required by 62.2; ORSC does not require dedicated laundry exhaust beyond the dryer duct

Bathroom and kitchen exhaust must terminate outdoors. ORSC bars recirculation, attic, soffit, ridge-vent, and crawl-space terminations.

## 6. Range hood capture and makeup air

ORSC M1503/M1505 makeup-air rule is triggered when any kitchen exhaust system **exceeds 400 CFM**. Below that threshold, no makeup air is required. At or above, makeup air must be approximately equal to exhaust airflow, must include at least one damper meeting M1503.6.2, and must discharge into the same room. There is an explicit exception for cooling-only exhaust systems intended to operate only with windows open.

ORSC does **not** mandate that makeup air be tempered. Practitioner consensus and ENERGY STAR programs do recommend tempering for occupant comfort and to avoid pressure imbalances driving combustion-appliance backdrafting, but it is not a code requirement for SFRs. Duct material and termination clearances are governed by ORSC M1503 — the standard prohibition on screens at termination, backdraft damper required, minimum 3-foot clearance from openings.

## 7. Dryer exhaust

Under **ORSC M1502** (based on 2021 IRC), the rules are:

- Material: smooth-interior metal duct, minimum 0.0157" (28 ga.), 4" nominal diameter
- Maximum length: **35 feet** equivalent length, dryer to terminal, excluding the transition duct (Table M1502.4.6.1 gives elbow deductions — typically 5 ft per 90°, 2.5 ft per 45°)
- Transition duct: maximum 8 ft, single length, listed/labeled to UL 2158A, not concealed
- Termination: outdoors only, minimum 3 ft from building openings, backdraft damper, no screens
- **Domestic booster fans are prohibited** under Oregon's M1502.4.5 amendment. UL 705 dryer-exhaust duct power ventilators (DEDPVs) are permitted; this distinction matters because the two are commonly confused.
- Where the duct equivalent length exceeds 35 ft, a permanent label or tag identifying the equivalent length must be placed within 6 ft of the dryer connection
- Heat pump (condensing) dryers are exempted from the duct-required rule when listed and labeled, but a condensate path to a drain is required per the appliance listing — ORSC does not tabulate the condensate plumbing, that's the OPSC and the manufacturer's instructions. If installed before occupancy, the dryer-duct space requirement is waived.

**Note on the 2026 ORSC trajectory:** the 2024 IRC reduced the base maximum equivalent length from 35 ft to 25 ft. If the 2026 ORSC adopts the 2024 IRC baseline without amendment, this will tighten — Jon should design to 25 ft now to be future-proof.

## 8. Wood stoves and pellet stoves — DEQ regime, not just BCD

This is its own regulatory layer, separate from the mechanical code.

Under **OAR Chapter 340, Division 262** (statutory authority **ORS 468A.460–468A.515**):

- Any new solid-fuel burning device sold, installed, or advertised for sale in Oregon must be DEQ- or EPA-certified for emissions (OAR 340-262-0500/0600)
- An uncertified used wood stove **cannot be sold or reinstalled** in Oregon
- A mechanical permit will not be issued for a new install of a non-certified used stove
- Antique stoves (pre-1940 with high market value), cookstoves, and central wood-fired furnaces are exempt categories
- At sale of a residence, any uncertified device must be removed and destroyed (OAR 340-262-0700); the seller is responsible unless the buyer accepts the obligation in writing (then 30-day post-close cure)
- Pellet stoves are categorized as solid-fuel burning devices and are subject to certification, but get more lenient treatment in the curtailment program (OAR 340-262-0800) except in PM nonattainment zones
- During Stage I/II air-stagnation advisories, burning may be curtailed; sole-source-heating residences and low-income households are exempt

For BSH, this means any wood or pellet stove specified in the design must be a current EPA-certified model at time of install. The mechanical permit will not issue otherwise.

Combustion-air, hearth, clearance, and chimney/vent requirements are in **ORSC Chapter 17 (Combustion Air)** and **Chapter 18 (Chimneys and Vents)**. For sealed-combustion appliances inside the building thermal envelope, ORSC N1103 (per the 2023 amendments) requires combustion air to be **ducted directly from outdoors** — this is also a sealed-combustion mandate triggered by the energy code, not just safety.

## 9. LP gas — separate licensing regime, no homeowner exemption

Already established in the prior chapter research and unchanged: LP gas piping, system installation, container setting, and connection to appliances are governed by **ORS 480.430–480.460** and **OAR 837-030**, administered by OSFM. There is **no homeowner exemption** — all LP gas work requires a licensed LPG installer/contractor. The owner-builder exemption from CCB licensing does not extend to LP gas.

Gas piping system design (sizing, materials, pressure testing) for natural gas is in IFGC chapters incorporated through OMSC, but residential gas piping is also addressed in **ORSC Chapter 24 and Appendix AA**. Pressure testing requirements, leak testing, and acceptance procedures are in those chapters.

## 10. CO alarms

Statutory framework: **ORS 455.360, ORS 105.836–842, ORS 476.725; OAR 837-047; ORSC R315**.

Required in all new Group R structures (and any reconstruction/alteration/repair requiring a permit). Location: outside each separate sleeping area in immediate vicinity of bedrooms; within 15 ft of every bedroom door; bedrooms on separate floors require separate alarms. Where a fuel-burning appliance is in a bedroom or its attached bathroom, an alarm is required inside that bedroom. Power: battery, hardwired with battery backup, or plug-in with battery backup. UL listing required per ORS 105.836 definition. Combination CO/smoke detectors permitted if listed to UL 2075 and UL 217.

## 11. Energy code interactions — blower door, duct location, HRV/ERV path

ORSC Chapter 11 (current 2023 edition, mandatory since April 1, 2024) sets the energy framework. Critical numbers for HVAC design:

- **Air leakage:** 3.25 ACH50 maximum for the prescriptive blower-door path (N1104.8.2). The prescriptive sealing alternative is in Table N1104.8.
- **Achievement-tier (Additional Measure 7 in Table N1101.1(2)):** 2.75 ACH50 with a heat-recovery ventilation system providing minimum 66% sensible HRV efficiency and 1.6 CFM/W combined fan efficiency.
- **Ducts inside the thermal envelope** is now the default. Ducts outside the envelope must use specified deeply-buried configurations with R-8 duct insulation, R-30 floor between crawl and dwelling, R-19 below buried duct, sealed crawl vapor barrier with 12" overlap.
- **Sealed combustion** is required for any appliance inside the thermal envelope. Combustion air must be ducted directly from outdoors.
- **Smart thermostat** required (ORSC N1105.4.1.2; carries through into the Reach Code).
- **Blower door test reporting** is documented on BCD's residential blower-door form, submitted to the building official before C of O.

The ORSC test/balance posture stops at the blower-door result. ORSC does not require **duct leakage** testing in the same way some IECC jurisdictions do, though duct leakage limits show up in some Additional Measures.

## 12. Oregon Residential Reach Code

The **2023 Oregon Residential Reach Code** is effective July 1, 2024, and is **voluntary** — adoption is at the applicant's election under HB 3409 (2023). Local jurisdictions must permit its use; they cannot require it. If elected, it replaces Chapter 11 of the 2023 ORSC. The compliance path is **ENERGY STAR Single-Family New Homes National Program Version 3.2 (Rev. 13)**, with on-site verification.

For BSH: since the Reach Code requires an ENERGY STAR rater and additional verification overhead, it's an option but not an obligation. If a BSH home will be sold and the marketing benefit of an ENERGY STAR or Reach Code label matters, this is the path. If not, the base ORSC Chapter 11 with the high-Achievement Additional Measure (the HRV path at 2.75 ACH50) is essentially equivalent in performance and simpler in inspection.

## 13. Heat pump standards — ACCA, AHRI, IGSHPA, ASHRAE

The standards-body landscape:

- **ACCA Manual J** (residential load calculation) is the ANSI standard referenced in IRC M1401.3 and carried into ORSC. Loads must be calculated per Manual J or an approved equivalent.
- **ACCA Manual S** governs equipment selection from manufacturer expanded performance data, capped at oversize limits relative to Manual J load.
- **ACCA Manual D** governs duct system design.
- **ACCA Manual T** governs register and grille selection.
- **AHRI 210/240** rates air-source heat pumps and air conditioners (SEER2/HSPF2 as of 2023 testing standards).
- **AHRI 13256** and **AHRI 870** rate water-source and ground-source heat pumps.
- **CSA/ANSI/IGSHPA C448:25 Series** (2025 edition) is the current ANSI standard for design and installation of GSHP systems for commercial and residential buildings. The legacy IGSHPA Accredited Installer (AI), CGD, CGI, and CRD certifications are being sunset and replaced by new C448-aligned curriculum, with full transition by end of 2025/early 2026.

For Jon's site selection between ground-source (horizontal/vertical/open-loop), air-to-water, and air-source: this should be a Manual J + life-cycle-cost decision keyed to the specific site after well log, soil thermal conductivity, and load calc. For western Oregon, air-source heat pumps (ASHP) at modern cold-climate ratings (HSPF2 ≥ 9.0) typically achieve favorable LCOE versus GSHP given mild winter design temperatures and high GSHP installation costs. Eastern Oregon's wider seasonal swing and colder design temperatures shift the balance toward GSHP. Open-loop GSHP triggers Oregon Water Resources Department water-use registration under ORS 537 and OAR 690 chapters — that's a separate research stream not covered here.

The 2023 ORSC Additional Measure pathway recognizes ductless heat pumps with HSPF 10 / HSPF2 9.0 in primary zone as a qualifying measure (Table N1101.1(2)).

## 14. Radiant floor — materials and standards

Tubing standards landscape:

- **PEX (PEX-a, PEX-b, PEX-c):** ASTM F876/F877; CSA B137.5; NSF 14
- **PEX-AL-PEX:** ASTM F1281; CSA B137.10
- **Oxygen barrier (EVOH coating):** DIN 4726 limit on oxygen permeation
- **Chlorine resistance:** ASTM F2023

For closed-loop hydronic radiant heating with any ferrous components (cast-iron circulators, steel boilers, manifolds with steel parts), **oxygen-barrier PEX or PEX-AL-PEX is mandatory** to prevent dissolved-oxygen corrosion. Non-barrier PEX is acceptable only if every wetted ferrous part is replaced with brass/bronze/stainless — typically more expensive overall.

Slab placement: tubing fastened to wire mesh or proprietary clips at design spacing (typically 6"–12" o.c.), positioned at mid-slab depth or per design, with continuous below-slab insulation (R-10 minimum is common practice; R-15 to R-20 is best practice in colder climates and produces noticeably better response time). A slip sheet or vapor retarder beneath the slab is standard; perimeter insulation of equal R-value at slab edges is required to avoid edge heat loss. Loop length should be capped per manufacturer (typically 250–300 ft per 1/2" loop) to keep pump head reasonable. Manifolds should have isolation valves, balancing valves, and air vents per loop.

Pressure testing: typical practice is hydrostatic at 1.5× working pressure (often 60–100 psi depending on system) held for 24 hours minimum before slab pour, and re-tested after pour. ORSC does not give a specific number for hydronic pressure testing — that comes from the OPSC and manufacturer instructions.

Hydronic water treatment: corrosion inhibitor, scale inhibitor, biocide as needed, and pH buffering. For oxygen-barrier closed loops, glycol may not be needed indoors but is advisable if any tubing routes through unconditioned space. For GSHP closed loops, propylene glycol concentration sized to 10°F below average loop temperature at design conditions is the IGSHPA convention.

Finish floor R-value: each additional R-1 of finish floor reduces output by roughly 10%. Hardwood and thick carpet are the practical limits; manufacturers typically restrict total finish-floor R-value to about 2.0–2.5.

## 15. Radiant cooling feasibility for western Oregon

This is genuinely promising for the BSH design philosophy — slow-response, no-fan, no-duct cooling — but it has hard physics constraints.

The defining limit is **dew point**. The radiant surface (floor, ceiling, or wall) cannot be below interior dew point, or it will sweat and you get condensation, mold, and slippery floors. Floor-radiant cooling is generally limited because comfort standards cap floor surface temperature at the **low end around 64°F (18°C)** per ASHRAE 55. In coastal western Oregon, summer interior dew points commonly run 55°F–60°F at design conditions (drier than southeast U.S.), so floor radiant cooling has 4°–9°F of headroom — workable but tight. Ceiling radiant has more capacity because the comfort-based surface temperature limit is lower (around 55°F), but ceiling temperatures hitting 55°F create high condensation risk if interior humidity isn't controlled.

ASHRAE Handbook—HVAC Systems and Equipment, Chapter 6 (Radiant Heating and Cooling), and Standard 55-2023 govern the design constraints. Key control elements:

- Supply water temperature regulated above dew point (typically EWT held 1–2°F above measured/predicted dew point)
- Dew point sensors at multiple representative locations
- Window contacts to interrupt cooling when windows open
- Dedicated dehumidification system (separate dehumidifier or DOAS with cooling coil) to keep dew point low
- Floor surface moisture sensor for backstop protection
- Backup conventional cooling (small ducted system or mini-split) for design days when latent load exceeds dehumidifier capacity

For BSH in western Oregon: radiant cooling is feasible but needs a paired dehumidification strategy and a small backup. Pure radiant cooling without backup is not a defensible default — humidity excursions from cooking, showers, or weather will exceed the system. In eastern Oregon (drier and warmer), radiant cooling has more thermal headroom but air conditioning sensible loads dominate, so the value proposition changes.

## 16. Humidity, dehumidification, filtration

ASHRAE 55 puts the indoor humidity comfort range broadly between roughly 30% and 60% RH at typical operative temperatures, with no upper limit specified in some climates and conditions. Practical residential design target is 30–50% RH year-round. Below 30% in winter is uncomfortable and dries out occupants and wood; above 60% in summer encourages mold and dust mites.

Dehumidifier sizing comes from the latent load portion of the Manual J, typically expressed in pints/day. Whole-house dehumidifiers sized 70–130 pints/day cover most well-built homes in western Oregon. Condensate is gravity-drained or pumped to an indirect waste; the OPSC governs the drain side.

Filtration: ORSC does not mandate a specific MERV. Best-practice for HRV/ERV systems is **MERV 13 minimum** at the system filter, which captures most pollen, smoke fines, and bacteria-sized particles. ASHRAE 62.2-2022 references MERV 13 for residential filtration in many recent addenda. For wildfire smoke specifically, MERV 13 cuts most PM2.5 with adequate runtime; for serious smoke events, an additional **HEPA bypass filter** (or a stand-alone HEPA unit per main living zone) is the practical answer because the static-pressure penalty of a fully-installed HEPA is too high for typical residential air handlers. Fan deltas, filter-bank static, and design CFM should all be sized to maintain MERV 13 without choking the equipment.

Source control matters as much as filtration: low-VOC finishes, hard-surface floors over carpet, sealed combustion appliances, range-hood interlock, dedicated mudroom/entry zoning, and outdoor air intake located away from driveways and dryer vents.

## 17. Duct design, mechanical room, controls

Duct design defaults to Manual D as discussed. Materials: rigid metal preferred in primary trunks; flex acceptable in branches with limited length (typically max 5 ft, fully extended, no kinks). Sealing per Section M1601.4 — listed mastics, foil tape per UL 181B-FX, or aerosol sealing. Insulation: R-8 outside thermal envelope (per ORSC N1103 amendments); R-6 within unconditioned but insulated assemblies.

Mechanical room: working clearance per the equipment listing (typically 30" front service clearance, 24" sides for boilers and air handlers); a floor drain or condensate sump is needed for condensing equipment, condensing dryer, water heaters, and dehumidifier; condensate pumps acceptable where gravity isn't available; vibration isolators or pads on pumps and air handlers; sound isolation (mass-loaded barrier or insulated wall assembly) where mechanical room adjoins sleeping areas; equipment labeling with model/serial, install date, gas pressure (where applicable), and water/refrigerant volumes.

Electrical working space coordination: the OESC/NEC requires 36" clearance and 30" wide working space in front of any equipment likely to be serviced energized — coordinate this against mechanical clearances early in plan layout.

Controls and smart-home integration are outside the scope of any specific Oregon code, but ORSC N1105.4.1.2 requires a smart thermostat. For BSH's "no-internet-required" rule, the relevant constraint is: any thermostat that requires cloud connectivity for its primary control function should be rejected. Local-first thermostats (e.g., those that retain full functionality on local network with no WAN) align with the rule. This is a market-availability constraint, not a code constraint — most "smart" thermostats are partially or fully cloud-dependent.

## 18. Commissioning and acceptance documentation

ORSC does not impose an HVAC commissioning regime as detailed as ASHRAE 90.1 commercial. The acceptance trail under current ORSC is:

- Manual J/S/D documentation submitted with permit
- Concealment inspection of dryer duct and combustion air paths
- Blower-door test results on BCD residential blower-door form before C of O
- Equipment installation per manufacturer instructions (verified at rough/final)
- Smart thermostat verification
- HRV/ERV efficiency documentation if claiming Additional Measure 7
- Duct leakage testing if required by Additional Measure path

For BSH best practice, layer on a private commissioning checklist covering: hydronic loop pressure-test logs, glycol concentration log, flow measurement at each manifold loop, air balance at each register, dehumidifier capacity verification, dew point sensor calibration, radiant control sequence verification, and seasonal tuning at 6 and 12 months. None of this is required by code; all of it is what makes a BSH system actually meet its performance design.

# Chapter 14: Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, And Livability

## 1. Habitable rooms — area, ceiling height, light, ventilation

Under **2023 ORSC R304** (current, mandatory since April 1, 2024):

- Habitable rooms other than kitchens must have a **floor area of not less than 70 sq. ft.**
- Habitable rooms other than kitchens must have a horizontal dimension of not less than **7 feet** in any direction
- Floor area in rooms with sloped ceilings less than 5 feet, or furred ceiling areas less than 7 feet, doesn't count toward the minimum
- IRC 2015 dropped the old requirement for at least one 120 sq. ft. habitable room; ORSC tracks the current IRC

**ORSC R305 — ceiling heights:**

- Habitable space, hallways, and portions of basements containing these spaces: not less than **7 feet**
- Bathrooms, toilet rooms, laundry rooms: not less than **6'-8"**
- Sloped ceilings: at least 50% of the required floor area must have ceiling height ≥ 7 feet, with no portion below 5 feet counted
- For bathrooms with sloped ceilings: up to 75% of the floor area may have ceiling below 6'-8"; clearance at toilets and lavatories per the section

**ORSC R303 — light and ventilation:**

- Habitable rooms must have natural light through glazing equal to **8% of floor area minimum**, with **4% openable** for natural ventilation
- Mechanical ventilation per Chapter 13 of the manuscript can substitute for natural ventilation under M1505
- Bathrooms and toilet rooms without bathing facilities: aggregate glazing area not less than 3 sq. ft. with ½ openable, OR mechanical ventilation per M1505.5

These are the floor-level geometric constraints. None of them limit BSH's design philosophy unless the manuscript proposes intentional sub-7' habitable rooms or deeply sloped roof spaces; in those cases the alternatives in R305 with sloped ceilings give enough flexibility for most loft configurations.

## 2. Emergency escape and rescue openings — R310 (with Oct 2024 amendment)

ORSC R310, with the Oregon-specific amendment effective **October 1, 2024**:

- **Required at:** every sleeping room; basements with one or more sleeping rooms (in each sleeping room); habitable attics
- **Net clear opening:** not less than **5.7 sq. ft.** (5.0 sq. ft. for grade-floor openings)
- **Net clear height:** ≥ **24 inches**
- **Net clear width:** ≥ **20 inches**
- **Sill height:** not greater than **44 inches** above the floor
- **Operation:** must be operable from inside without a key, tool, or special knowledge; net clear dimensions obtained by normal operation
- **Window wells** below grade: minimum **9 sq. ft.** horizontal area, ≥ 36" projection and width; ladder/steps required if the well depth exceeds 44"; ladder may encroach 6" into the well
- **Bars/grilles/screens/covers** are permitted only if they are releasable from inside without key/tool/special knowledge or force greater than normal operation
- **Sprinkler-protected basement exception:** under the 2023 ORSC amendment, a sleeping room in a sprinklered basement (NFPA 13D) may be exempt from EERO if the basement has two means of egress, or one egress route plus an EERO elsewhere
- **Door used as EERO:** must be a side-hinged door or slider; if below adjacent grade, an area well is required

The Oct 2024 amendment tightened certain replacement-window edge cases and clarified the area-well/ladder geometry — if the chapter discusses replacement scenarios, that text should be pulled directly from the 2024 amendment document.

## 3. Stairs, handrails, guards, landings — R311, R312

Standard ORSC residential geometry (based on 2021 IRC):

- **Stair width:** ≥ 36 inches above handrails (handrails permitted to project up to 4½ inches from each side)
- **Headroom:** ≥ 6'-8" measured vertically from a line tangent to the nosings
- **Riser height:** maximum 7¾"; minimum 4"
- **Tread depth:** minimum 10" measured horizontally between vertical planes of the foremost projection of adjacent treads
- **Variation between adjacent treads/risers:** maximum 3/8"; cumulative variation in a flight: maximum 3/8"
- **Landings:** required at top and bottom of each stairway; minimum 36" in the direction of travel; may not exceed 1½" below threshold for interior side of exterior doors; exterior landings ≤ 8" below threshold (with constraints if door swings out)
- **Handrails:** required on at least one side of stairs with ≥ 4 risers; height 34"–38" measured above the nosing; graspable cross-section per R311.7.8.5 (Type I or Type II)
- **Guards:** required where the walking surface is more than 30" above the floor or grade below; height ≥ 36" for residential; openings sized so a 4" sphere cannot pass through (4-3/8" sphere allowed at the triangular opening at riser/tread/guardrail); 6" sphere allowed at the open side of a stair guard between the tread, riser, and bottom rail

These are conservative defaults; jurisdictional variation is rare on these points but Portland and other PPP-delegated jurisdictions sometimes interpret the cumulative variation rule differently, especially on remodels.

## 4. Smoke alarms (R314) and CO alarms (R315) — interconnection

CO alarms were covered in Chapter 13 research (ORS 455.360, ORS 105.836, OAR 837-047, ORSC R315, with 15-foot bedroom rule and required-regardless-of-CO-source for new construction).

**Smoke alarms — ORSC R314:**

- Required in each sleeping room, outside each separate sleeping area in immediate vicinity of bedrooms, and on each story including basements
- **Interconnection:** new construction requires interconnection so activation of one alarm activates all alarms in the dwelling; interconnection may be hardwired or wireless if the wireless system is listed for the purpose
- **Power:** primary from building wiring with battery backup; or in some retrofit scenarios, 10-year sealed lithium battery alarms are permitted
- Combination CO/smoke alarms permitted where listed to UL 217 and UL 2075

**Smart-home monitoring interaction:** ORSC does not prohibit smoke or CO alarms from also reporting to a monitoring system, but the alarms themselves must meet the listed-product requirements (UL 217 / UL 2075 / NFPA 72). For BSH's local-first rule, the practical constraint is that the **listed alarm function must be local-and-immediate** — a network-dependent alarm that delays local annunciation while it queries a cloud service does not meet the listing intent. Most monitored alarm systems annunciate locally and report to the cloud separately, which is fine. Pure cloud-only "smart smoke detectors" without a listed local horn are not code-compliant.

## 5. Garage-to-dwelling separation — R302.5, R302.6

Critical detail: **the 2021 IRC (and therefore 2023 ORSC) added "self-latching AND self-closing or automatic-closing" requirements for garage doors.** This is a change from the 2018 IRC.

Current 2023 ORSC requirements:

- **Wall/ceiling separation per Table R302.6:** not less than ½" gypsum board on the garage side. **5/8" Type X** required where habitable rooms are above the garage; structure supporting the floor/ceiling assembly between dwelling above and garage below also requires ½" gypsum protection
- **Doors between garage and residence:** solid wood ≥ 1-3/8" thick, solid or honeycomb-core steel ≥ 1-3/8" thick, or 20-minute fire-rated doors. **Must be self-latching and equipped with a self-closing or automatic-closing device**
- **Sleeping-room prohibition:** openings from a private garage directly into a room used for sleeping purposes are not permitted (R302.5.1)
- **Duct penetrations (R302.5.2):** ducts in the garage and ducts penetrating walls/ceilings between garage and dwelling must be ≥ 26 gauge sheet steel or other approved material, with no openings into the garage; if a vibration isolator is used in the garage duct, install ≥ 18" from the penetration
- **Other penetrations (R302.5.3):** must be fireblocked per R302.11 Item 4 — annular space sealed with material that resists free passage of flame and combustion products
- **Garage finish:** the gypsum board itself is the required protection — it does not need to be a "fire-resistance-rated assembly" in the technical sense, just the prescribed thickness and type
- **Sprinkler exception:** where both the dwelling unit and garage are protected by an automatic fire sprinkler system per NFPA 13D or other approved system, the door rating and self-closing requirements are waived (but the wall separation is not)

For BSH if a future garage is planned but not yet built: code requires only what's installed at permit time. A "future garage" can be planned in the design without installing the door, sheathing, or self-closer, but the wall assembly must be appropriately sheathed once the connection is made and the garage is occupiable.

## 6. Interior wall/ceiling coverings — R702

Standard ORSC R702 covers gypsum board, plaster, and paneling installation. Key points:

- **Gypsum board** (per ASTM C1396): minimum thickness depends on framing spacing — Table R702.3.5 has the matrix. Single-layer ½" GWB is standard at 16" o.c. studs; 5/8" required for fire-resistant assemblies and on ceilings with framing > 16" o.c.
- **Glass mat gypsum** (per ASTM C1178): permitted for wet areas as backer for tile, but not by itself a waterproof barrier
- **Cement board** (per ASTM C1325): permitted as tile backer in wet areas; brand examples are Durock, HardieBacker, PermaBASE (the WP version meets ANSI A118.10 waterproofing without separate membrane)
- **Foam plastic thermal barrier:** per R316, foam plastic insulation must be separated from interior of building by an approved thermal barrier of minimum ½" gypsum or equivalent — this is the rule that prevents exposed-foam interior installations
- **Fasteners:** Table R702.3.5 gives spacing — generally 7" o.c. for screws, 8" o.c. for nails, on framing
- **Joint treatment:** taped and bedded with joint compound; alkali-resistant mesh tape required at cement board joints

**Fire-rated assemblies (R302.4):** for any required fire-resistance-rated wall (townhouse separation, two-family separation), the assembly must be tested per **ASTM E119** or UL 263. ORSC permits prescriptive assemblies from the IBC tables; alternatively, listed UL designs (e.g., U305, U419) work.

## 7. Wet-area waterproofing and tile installation — R702.4 + ANSI + TCNA

Current 2023 ORSC R702.4 framework:

- **Wall finish in showers:** must be of nonabsorbent surface to a height **not less than 6 feet** above floor, or 70" above shower drain inlet, whichever is greater (this carries over from OPSC 417.4.1 too)
- **Backer board (R702.4.2):** materials per Table R702.4.2 — cement board (ASTM C1325), glass mat gypsum (ASTM C1178), fiber-mat reinforced gypsum, or other approved materials, installed per manufacturer's instructions
- **Drywall (regular gypsum, ASTM C1396) is NOT permitted as tile backer in showers or tub/shower wet areas** unless the tile installation includes a listed waterproofing system (e.g., Schluter Kerdi over drywall is permitted by Kerdi's listing)
- **Shower lining (OPSC 417.5.2):** floors under shower compartments (except prefab receptors) must be lined waterproof; liner turns up ≥ 2" above finished threshold; cannot be nailed/perforated within the watertight portion; recessed/fastened to approved backing

**Industry standards layered on top of code:**

- **ANSI A118.10:** load-bearing bonded waterproofing membranes for ceramic tile installation. Schluter Kerdi, Laticrete Hydroban, Custom RedGard, Mapei AquaDefense, Wedi, GoBoard all certify to A118.10 in their respective listed configurations
- **ANSI A118.12:** crack-isolation membranes (separate certification from waterproofing; some products meet both)
- **ANSI A137.1:** ceramic tile specifications including DCOF (slip resistance)
- **TCNA Handbook for Ceramic, Glass, and Stone Tile Installation:** the de facto industry standard for assembly details — methods like B412 (curbless shower with linear drain), B415 (showers with site-formed mortar pan), F125 (curbed shower over wood subfloor), W245 (wet area walls with bonded waterproofing membrane). Manufacturer warranties typically reference TCNA methods
- **Movement joints:** TCNA EJ171 specifies that movement joints (soft joints with sealant) are required at tile changes of plane, at perimeters, at not greater than 8'–12' o.c. in interior dry areas, and 8' o.c. maximum in wet areas. Failure to provide movement joints is the most common tile-failure mode

**Curbless / linear-drain showers (TCNA B421 series):**

- Floor slope: 1/4" per foot to drain (1/2" per foot for some methods)
- Linear drains permit single-direction slope; point drains require double-pitch
- Threshold transition: bonded membrane must be continuous from inside the shower across the transition to dry-area floor; the dry-area side typically gets the membrane extended ≥ 6" beyond the curbless boundary
- Drain assembly must be listed for use with the chosen membrane (Schluter Kerdi-Line + Kerdi membrane is one matched system; others have similar matched pairs)

**For tile slip resistance (ANSI A137.1 / ANSI A326.3):**

- Level interior wet areas: **DCOF ≥ 0.42** (the floor of code/manufacturer claims)
- "Interior, Wet Plus" (showers, locker rooms, kitchens): **DCOF ≥ 0.50** strongly recommended
- Outdoor wet (clean, maintained): **DCOF ≥ 0.55** recommended
- Manufacturer should provide DCOF data per ANSI A326.3; record this on the tile product data sheet for the project file. Note that ANSI/TCNA explicitly state DCOF ≥ 0.42 is not a guarantee of safety — additional factors include drainage, surface structure, contaminants, and footwear

## 8. Tile over radiant floor — TCNA, manufacturer rules

For tile over heated slabs (TCNA F124, F125, F176, F189):

- **Crack isolation membrane required** between the slab and the tile installation (ANSI A118.12 certified). Schluter Ditra, Laticrete Strata-Mat, and similar uncoupling membranes serve this role
- **Slab moisture testing required** before installation: ASTM F2170 (in-situ relative humidity, ≤ 75% RH at 40% slab depth from drying side), or ASTM F1869 (calcium chloride MVER, ≤ 3 lb/1000 sq ft / 24 hr)
- **Surface temperature limits:** TCNA recommends maximum tile surface temperature of **85°F (29°C)** for normal occupancy comfort. For occupant comfort and grout/tile durability, ramp-up rates should not exceed about 5°F per hour
- **System start-up:** radiant systems should be commissioned at low temperature, gradually increased over several days to drive out residual slab moisture before tile setting
- **Thinset bonding:** modified or unmodified thinset selection depends on the membrane system — Schluter, for example, requires unmodified thinset over Ditra and Kerdi due to membrane impermeability

For other floor materials over radiant:

- **Engineered wood:** generally compatible at limited temperatures (typically ≤ 80°F surface), with manufacturer certification required. Solid hardwood is more problematic and usually limited to thin (≤ 5/16") engineered or laminated products
- **Polished concrete:** ideal for radiant; no surface temperature concerns at residential ranges. Sealers must be heat-resistant
- **Natural stone:** similar to tile (TCNA stone-specific methods); some stones (e.g., marble) require very low ramp rates due to thermal-shock sensitivity
- **Cork:** limited thermal mass; manufacturer typically caps at ~80°F surface; low conductivity makes cork sub-optimal for radiant
- **Carpet:** insulating; cuts radiant output ~50%; manufacturer rating required (carpet + pad combined R-value typically ≤ 1.0 for radiant compatibility)
- **Vinyl/LVP:** product-specific; many manufacturers cap surface at 85°F. PVC plasticizers can degrade at sustained higher temps

## 9. Concrete moisture testing for floor finishes

Standards landscape:

- **ASTM F2170:** in-situ probe relative humidity. Drill to 40% of slab depth (drying from one side) or 20% (drying from both sides). Sleeves/caps must extend the full hole depth. Equilibrate ≥ 24 hours before reading. Acceptance: typically **≤ 75% RH** for most resilient flooring; manufacturer-specific (some adhesives accept up to 80%)
- **ASTM F1869:** calcium chloride moisture vapor emission rate (MVER). 60–72 hour test under sealed dish. Acceptance: typically **≤ 3 lb/1000 sq ft / 24 hr** for resilient flooring (some products tighter)
- **ASTM F710:** standard practice for preparing concrete floors to receive resilient flooring — references both F2170 and F1869 testing
- **Conditioning:** slab and ambient air must be at service conditions for ≥ 48 hours before testing. For new construction, acceptance can take 30–90 days post-pour depending on slab thickness, mix design, and ambient conditions

ORSC does not adopt these tests directly — they are manufacturer/warranty requirements. But for any flooring installation over slab, the failure of the contractor to perform these tests is the single most common cause of premature floor failures (debonding, cupping, mold). Document the test results in the project file.

## 10. Bathroom fixture clearances, shower valves, anti-scald

**Fixture clearances (ORSC R307 and OPSC):**

- **Water closet:** ≥ 21" front clearance, ≥ 30" wide centered on the bowl, ≥ 15" from centerline of the WC to any sidewall or fixture (R307 / OPSC 402.5)
- **Lavatory:** 4" minimum clear at side of basin, ≥ 21" front clearance
- **Showers:** minimum interior dimensions 30" × 30" (1024 sq inches minimum interior); ≥ 24" clear in front of the entry; finished door opening to provide ≥ 22" clear
- **Tubs:** ≥ 21" clear at the open side

**Anti-scald (OPSC 424.3):**

- Shower and tub-shower combination valves must be **balanced-pressure, thermostatic, or combination balanced-pressure/thermostatic** valves conforming to **ASSE 1016 / ASME A112.18.1 / CSA B125** (Type P, Type T, or Type T/P)
- Field-set maximum temperature: **120°F (49°C)**
- The valve must be equipped with a means to limit the maximum setting; this is field-adjusted at installation
- **Bathtub valves (OPSC 424.5):** hot water to bathtubs and whirlpool baths must be limited to 120°F by an **ASSE 1070** temperature-limiting device, except where protection is provided by a combination tub/shower valve already meeting 424.3
- **Lavatory valves (some jurisdictions):** ASSE 1070 temperature-limiting devices on hot supply may be required; OPSC base does not require for residential
- **Master mixing at water heater (ASSE 1017):** not strictly required at residential scale, but best practice. Allows storage at 140°F for Legionella suppression while delivering 120°F to the distribution system

For BSH where any HRV/ERV-attached or DHW recirculation is in play: an ASSE 1017 master mixing valve at the water heater output, with point-of-use ASSE 1016 valves at showers and ASSE 1070 at lavatories/bathtubs, is the resilient configuration. Storage at 140°F + delivery at 120°F also addresses Legionella concerns in piping that has long stagnation periods.

**Bathroom ventilation:**

- Required per ORSC M1505.5 — minimum 50 CFM intermittent or 20 CFM continuous local exhaust, ducted to outdoors
- Wet-location electrical: GFCI required for all bathroom receptacles (NEC 210.8(A)(1)); receptacles must be within 36" of basin edge per OESC; lighting in tub/shower area must be marked "suitable for damp/wet location"

## 11. Kitchen — electrical, exhaust, lighting, appliance circuits

Under **2023 OESC (NEC 2023, mandatory October 1, 2023)**, with 2026 OESC anticipated October 1, 2026:

**Countertop receptacles (NEC 210.52(C)):**

- **Wall counter:** receptacle outlets required so no point along the counter is more than 24" from a receptacle (the 2-foot rule); applies to any counter ≥ 12" wide
- **Island/peninsula:** **major change in NEC 2023** — receptacle outlets are now **optional**, but if an island or peninsula does not have a receptacle, **provisions must be made for future installation** (junction box and conduit/cable accessible for retrofit). The interpretation of "provisions" varies by AHJ — confirm locally
- **If an island/peninsula receptacle is installed:** must be on or above the countertop (≤ 20" above), in the countertop using a listed countertop assembly, or in a work surface using a listed assembly. **Side-of-cabinet receptacles intended to serve the countertop are no longer permitted** under NEC 2023. This changed because of CPSC injury data on cord-pull burns (~9,700 ER visits 1991–2020)
- **GFCI:** all 125V through 250V receptacles in a dwelling-unit kitchen, regardless of whether they serve countertops, must be **GFCI-protected** (NEC 210.8(A)(6) revised in 2023). This expanded the prior rule which applied only to countertop-serving receptacles
- **Small-appliance branch circuits:** at least two 20-amp small-appliance branch circuits to serve countertop and refrigerator outlets (NEC 210.11(C)(1))
- **AFCI:** required on all 15- and 20-amp circuits in the kitchen (NEC 210.12)

**Closet lighting (NEC 410.16):**

- Surface-mounted incandescent or LED with completely enclosed light source: 12" minimum from edge of light source to nearest point of any storage area
- Recessed incandescent or LED with completely enclosed light source: 6" minimum
- Surface or recessed fluorescent: 6" minimum
- Surface-mounted fluorescent or LED with completely enclosed source identified for use within the storage area: 0" allowed
- Pendant fixtures and exposed-bulb fixtures: not permitted in clothes closets

**Range hood and makeup air:** covered in Chapter 13 (M1503/M1505 — makeup air required when exhaust > 400 CFM)

**Appliance circuits:**

- Dishwasher: dedicated 15- or 20-amp circuit; GFCI required (210.8(D)); AFCI required
- Disposer: dedicated circuit; switch typically wall-mounted
- Range/cooktop: typically 50-amp 240V; cord-and-plug or hardwired per appliance instructions
- Refrigerator: dedicated small-appliance circuit acceptable (or its own 15A circuit per some inspectors)
- Microwave: dedicated 20A circuit recommended for built-in/over-range units

**Lighting (NEC 210.70):**

- At least one wall-controlled lighting outlet in every habitable room and bathroom
- **Laundry rooms now added** (NEC 2023 change) — switched lighting outlet required, switched-receptacle alternative no longer permitted in laundry
- Hallways, stairways, attached garages, exterior egress doors: switched lighting outlet required
- Stair lighting: control at top and bottom of stairs with 6+ risers

## 12. Closet / storage / hardware items

Closet lighting clearances (NEC 410.16) summarized above. Other interior items:

- **Interior doors:** no general code minimum on interior door width except for accessibility rules (which don't apply to private SFR — see §15). Manufacturer rough-opening dimensions are 30"–32" common; 36" required for any planned-accessible bedroom/bath if visitability is targeted
- **Pocket doors:** acceptable; require structural framing for the pocket assembly; hardware must allow opening from both sides without keys for any room used as bedroom
- **Sliding/barn doors:** acceptable for non-egress applications; may not serve as the required egress door from a sleeping room
- **Privacy locks on bedroom/bathroom doors:** must be releasable from outside without a key, per ORSC R311.2 (egress operation rule). Standard privacy thumb-turns with a coin-slot release on the outside meet this
- **Lever vs. knob:** ORSC has no requirement for either; ANSI A117.1 requires lever or D-pull for accessibility (voluntary in SFR)

## 13. Exterior doors

ORSC R311.2 — egress door:

- At least one **side-hinged exterior egress door** required, providing minimum **32" clear width** when the door is open 90° (this is the prescriptive code requirement), and **78" clear height**
- Door cannot be more than 7'-7" tall in measured framing
- Threshold height: not greater than 1½" above interior floor; exterior side may be higher

**Safety glazing (ORSC R308):**

Required at hazardous locations:

- Glazing in doors and door assemblies
- Glazing within 24" of either side of a door, where the bottom edge of glass is < 60" above the walking surface
- Glazing in tub/shower enclosures (full pane safety glazing required)
- Glazing within 60" horizontally of a tub, hot tub, spa, or shower edge, where bottom edge of glass < 60" above adjacent walking surface
- Glazing adjacent to stairways, landings, or ramps within 36" horizontally of a walking surface where bottom edge is < 60" above the surface
- Glazing within 60" horizontal of bottom tread (in any direction) where exposed surface is < 60" above the nose of the tread

Safety glazing is tempered or laminated meeting ANSI Z97.1 or 16 CFR 1201.

**Air leakage (ORSC N1102.4 / NFRC):** exterior doors must meet U-factor and air leakage requirements of Chapter 11. Default door leakage maximum: 0.5 CFM per square foot when tested per AAMA/WDMA/CSA 101/I.S.2/A440.

**Weatherstripping, flashing, manufacturer install instructions** are required by R703 (exterior wall covering, including water-resistive barrier integration). Door flashing per ASTM E2112 is the industry-standard practice; integration with the WRB and any applied flashing tape per manufacturer instructions is required for warranty compliance.

## 14. Cabinet, countertop, and built-in attachment

ORSC does not give specific cabinet attachment criteria — these come from manufacturer instructions and trade practice:

- **Wall cabinets:** typically attached through the back rails into framing with #8 × 2½" or larger screws at each stud crossing. Two screws per stud at top rail and bottom rail. Solid blocking or cleat strips behind the back rail at stud locations is best practice
- **Stone or quartz counters (>10 lb/sq ft point load):** require continuous plywood substrate (typically 3/4") attached to base cabinets, plus mechanical anchorage of the cabinet to the floor. For unsupported overhangs > 6", steel brackets or cantilever supports per stone fabricator's specs
- **Heavy mirrors and grab bars:** require solid blocking in the wall framing. Standard practice: 2× blocking between studs at expected grab bar locations, even if grab bars aren't initially installed (this is the "future grab bar blocking" item that visitability programs require — see §15)
- **Standard recommended grab-bar blocking heights:** 33"–36" above floor at toilet and shower locations, full-width between studs, with positive backing ≥ 2× lumber so any common screw pattern can engage
- **Shelf load assumptions:** built-in shelving generally designed for 30 lb/sq ft; book shelves often rated 50 lb/sq ft. Cleats and supports sized accordingly
- **Plumbing fixtures with carrier requirements:** wall-hung sinks, toilets, and grab bars require steel chair carriers or in-wall blocking sized per manufacturer

## 15. Accessibility — what applies to private SFR (and what doesn't)

This is the framing question and the answer is unambiguous:

- **ADA does not apply to individually-owned private single-family homes.** ADA covers public accommodations and certain government and quasi-public facilities. The U.S. Access Board, DOJ, and HUD all explicitly state that private SFR housing is outside ADA scope (28 CFR Part 36)
- **Fair Housing Act (FHA) accessibility design requirements apply only to "covered multifamily dwellings."** A covered multifamily dwelling is a building with 4+ units. **Detached single-family houses are not covered.** Even attached townhomes and duplexes are generally not covered (HUD interpretation, 24 CFR 100.205)
- **Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act** applies only to federally-funded housing programs (HUD-funded, public housing, HOPE VI, etc.). Doesn't apply to a privately-built SFR for sale
- **Architectural Barriers Act** applies only to federal buildings
- **ANSI A117.1 (currently the 2017 edition; 2024 published)** is the technical accessibility standard referenced by FHA, ADA, and most state accessibility codes. **For private SFR, ANSI A117.1 is voluntary best practice — not required.**

**Visitability** is a voluntary framework promoted by some advocacy groups and adopted by some local jurisdictions for incentive purposes:

- One zero-step entrance into the home
- All ground-floor doors providing 32" clear opening
- A minimum half-bath on the ground floor with maneuvering clearance for a wheelchair

Oregon does not have a statewide mandatory visitability requirement for private SFR. Some Oregon jurisdictions (Portland is the main one) have referenced visitability as a goal in incentive programs but not as a building-code mandate. This is worth verifying for the specific BSH site jurisdiction.

**For BSH design discretion:**

- "Visitable" is achievable within ORSC compliance by simply providing a no-step entry, 36" doors at the ground floor, and a wider half-bath. None of this triggers any code requirement
- Full ANSI A117.1 Type A (fully accessible) compliance is voluntary for private SFR. Hallway widths, turning radii, threshold limits, ramp slopes, fixture-area clearances, and outlet/control reach ranges from A117.1 can all be designed in without ever invoking ADA or FHA
- **Future-proofing for elevator/lift:** stacked closets sized for a future LU/LA elevator (typical ~5' × 5' shaft) is a non-code-required design move. Block-and-stack the floor framing accordingly. Section 504 funded projects requiring accessible units are a separate regulatory regime

For the manuscript: this should be framed positively. BSH can adopt accessibility features as best practice without it triggering any regulatory regime — as long as no federal or state housing funding flows through the project, no rental/visitor-use criteria are imposed on the property, and the design is for a privately-owned SFR.

## 16. Composite wood, formaldehyde, low-VOC chemistry

**Formaldehyde — federal mandatory standards:**

- **TSCA Title VI (40 CFR Part 770)** is the binding federal standard. **All composite wood products** sold, supplied, manufactured, or imported in the U.S. after **March 22, 2019** must be certified and labeled as **TSCA Title VI compliant** by an EPA-recognized third-party certifier
- Coverage: hardwood plywood, MDF (medium-density fiberboard), particleboard, and finished goods containing them
- Emission limits are **identical to CARB ATCM Phase 2** — they were harmonized when the federal rule was finalized
- Labels must include: panel producer name, lot/date code, EPA-recognized TPC number, and TSCA Title VI compliance statement. Finished goods labels include fabricator name, production date, compliance statement
- **NAF (No-Added Formaldehyde)** and **ULEF (Ultra-Low Emitting Formaldehyde)** designations are sub-categories; NAF panels use non-formaldehyde resins (soy-based, MDI, PVAc); products with ULEF/NAF certification can be exempt from quarterly TPC verification testing under reduced-testing protocols
- **Solid wood is not regulated** — has trace natural formaldehyde but no added resins. Plywood with phenol-formaldehyde (PF) resin is regulated as composite wood but PF emits dramatically less than urea-formaldehyde (UF); virtually all PF plywood is TSCA Title VI compliant by default
- **Test methods:** ASTM E1333 (large chamber, primary method) and ASTM D6007 (small chamber, secondary method)

**Voluntary supplemental certifications:**

- **GREENGUARD Gold (UL):** stricter VOC limits than TSCA, includes total VOC and individual chemical limits beyond formaldehyde alone
- **FloorScore (SCS):** for hard-surface flooring VOC compliance (covers vinyl, laminate, engineered wood)
- **CRI Green Label Plus:** for carpet VOC emissions

**VOC chemistry in finishes:**

- **Paints:** SCAQMD Rule 1113 limits VOC content in architectural coatings. Most low-VOC paint products meet < 50 g/L for flat and < 100 g/L for non-flat. Zero-VOC products (≤ 5 g/L) are widely available
- **Sealants and adhesives:** SCAQMD Rule 1168 governs limits. Construction adhesives < 70 g/L are common; aerosol adhesives have separate limits
- **Stains and clear finishes:** SCAQMD Rule 1113 limits — varnishes typically capped at 350 g/L or lower; lacquers higher (550 g/L); waterborne acrylic and oil-modified urethane finishes can hit < 250 g/L
- **CDPH/EHLB Standard Method v1.2 (CA Section 01350):** the chamber-based emissions test that drives GreenGuard, FloorScore, LEED EQc4 credit. Tests for individual VOCs at chronic reference exposure limits
- **For BSH** with the durability/health priorities: spec products meeting CDPH 01350 (often labeled "California 01350 compliant" or GREENGUARD Gold) for paints, sealants, adhesives, flooring. Source-control through material selection is a much more effective IAQ strategy than ventilation alone

# Chapter 15: Smart Home, Network, Security, And Local Automation

## Anchor sentence for the manuscript

A BSH home that must function without internet still has to function under code: the wiring methods, listings, separations, licensing, and recording-law constraints that govern low-voltage, communications, security, and smart-home work in Oregon are independent of whether the system phones home, and Chapter 15 specifies the design accordingly — Cat6A structured cabling installed under NEC Articles 722/725/800, switches and devices wired to OESC §404.2(C) with neutrals at every habitable-room switch, cameras that record video without audio in accordance with ORS 165.540, alarm systems registered with the local jurisdiction (not the state), monitoring (if used) handled by a DPSST-licensed alarm-monitor entity, and a local-first controller stack (Home Assistant or equivalent) running on a UPS-backed, VLAN-segmented network behind a default-deny firewall.

## 1. The legal/regulatory layer first (this drives the design)

### Code in force in Oregon (May 2026)

The current Oregon Electrical Specialty Code (OESC) is the **2023 OESC**, which adopts the 2023 NFPA 70 (NEC) plus IEEE C2-2023 NESC, amended by Table 1-E. Effective date October 1, 2023; further amendments under BCD 13-2024 took effect January 1, 2025. The 2026 OESC adoption process has begun with anticipated effective date October 1, 2026 — Jon should re-verify that date if the build slips into late 2026. Oregon Residential Specialty Code in force is the **2023 ORSC** (mandatory April 1, 2024). Oregon Energy Efficiency Specialty Code in force is the **2025 OEESC** (effective Jan 1, 2025; mandatory July 1, 2025).

For Chapter 15 purposes the 2023 NEC is the controlling reference. Note the structural change: in the 2023 NEC, **Article 725 now covers only Class 2 and Class 3 power-limited circuits** (Class 1 was relocated to new Article 724), and **new Article 722 consolidates cable requirements** for power-limited, fault-managed, and optical fiber circuits. Article 800 remains the general requirements for communications systems.

### Owner-installed low-voltage in Oregon

Three independent rules govern owner-installed Class 2 / Class 3 / communications work:

**ORS 479.540(1)** — broad owner exemption from electrical licensing for work on residential property the owner occupies and does not intend to sell/lease/exchange. This is the same intent-based framework Chapter 4 documented. It applies to low-voltage just as it does to line-voltage. (Note: BCD's June 2016 clarification on ORS 479.540(1)(b) narrows what owners can do on **rental** property to maintenance/replacement only; this does not constrain Jon's primary residence build.)

**ORS 479.940(1)** — separately exempts certain limited-energy activity in 1- and 2-family dwellings from the LE technician licensing scheme: prewiring of cable TV and telephone systems owned by the owner, intercom/audio in 1- and 2-family dwellings, and similar — but ORS 479.940(2) restricts this to **CCB-licensed residential contractors**. So this is a contractor exemption, not an owner exemption.

**OAR 918-261-0039** — broader product-certification and permitting exemption for Class 2 wiring on the load side of a listed Class 2 transformer, and a permit exemption for Class 2 wiring on the load side of a listed garage-door controller in 1- and 2-family dwellings (when not part of original wiring). Excludes wet locations, plenums, OESC Chapter 5 areas, and protective signaling.

The professional licensing layer (separate question — who can legally pull money for low-voltage work) is the **Limited Energy Technician Class A (LEA)** for protective signaling/fire alarm/security/data/control, and **Class B (LEB)** for everything except protective signaling. Both run through OAR Chapter 918, Division 271. Jon doesn't need either for owner work on his own pre-sale residence; if BSH LLC ever does install low-voltage on a third party's home as a paid service, it needs a Limited Energy Contractor license with a signing supervisor holding LEA or LEB.

### Permit reality check

Even when an owner is licensing-exempt, ORS 479.540(1)(a) states the **work itself still has to comply with ORS Chapter 455 and the OESC**. Permits are required for new low-voltage installations except where specifically exempted. The Class 2 load-side exemption above is one such exemption; another is OAR 918-271-0030 (single-station smoke detectors in buildings ≤3 stories, CCTV in buildings ≤3 stories, MATV in buildings ≤3 stories, intercom/audio in 1- and 2-family dwellings — all installable under specific conditions without a permit). Jurisdictions differ on enforcement; Jon should confirm the Marion or Washington County (or whichever) low-voltage permit posture before final design.

## 2. NEC wiring methods, listings, separation, and penetrations

### Cable listings (Article 800 / Article 722)

For the residential structured cabling spine:

**CMP** (Plenum): Communications Multipurpose, Plenum-rated. Required only in plenum spaces. Highest fire performance (UL 910 / NFPA 262). Not required in a single-family residence absent a true plenum return.

**CMR** (Riser): Required for vertical runs through more than one floor. UL 1666.

**CM / CMG**: General-purpose. Permitted in 1- and 2-family dwellings for all applications per NEC 800.179. **This is the residential default.**

**CMX**: Limited-use, residential-only, dwellings ≤3 stories. Smaller cable; OK for in-wall single-story but Jon shouldn't standardize on CMX for a structured system because it limits future flexibility.

For BSH's "no internet required" methodology with structured cabling: Cat6A solid 23 AWG with a CMR jacket is the durable default (CMR substitutes for CM anywhere; using CMR consistently means no thinking about which cable goes where, including riser runs).

**Coax**: CATV/CATVR/CATVP under Article 820. RG-6 quad-shield with CMR/CATVR rating for in-wall.

**Speaker wire**: CL2 or CL3 rating for in-wall; CL2P/CL3P for plenum; CL3 if the run carries more voltage. Both fall under Article 725.

**Outdoor/direct burial**: CMX is rated for outdoor exposed but is light-duty; for buried conduit runs use direct-burial-rated outside-plant cables and pull through conduit with bonding per Article 800 Part II.

### Separation from power conductors

**NEC 800.133** and **725.136** govern separation of communications and Class 2/3 from power. Key rules: communications cables must be separated by at least 2 inches from light/power/Class 1/non-power-limited fire alarm conductors unless one circuit is in a raceway or in metal-sheathed cable, or the conductors are permanently separated by a continuous nonconductor at least equal to the insulation rating of the larger conductor. In the same enclosure, Class 2/3 conductors can share with power only when introduced solely to connect to the equipment served by the Class 2/3 circuit.

Practical translation for the BSH wiring methodology: use a separate raceway path (smurf tube or J-hooks) for the low-voltage trunk; never run Cat6A and Romex in the same drilled hole through a stud with no separation; keep low-voltage panel runs at least 2 inches off paralleling line-voltage feeders or use metal raceway for the line-voltage side.

### Penetrations and firestopping

**NEC 800.26** requires fire-stopping at any penetration of a fire-resistance-rated wall, partition, floor, or ceiling per 300.21 — i.e., the assembly's fire rating must be maintained. This applies to all the Article 800 cables. A through-penetration firestop system listed with the cable type (intumescent collars, mineral-wool batt, etc.) is the standard solution. Firestopping is universally required even where Oregon does not require fire-rated assemblies in the dwelling — penetrations of garage/dwelling fire separations under ORSC R302.6 and floor/ceiling assemblies that act as fire barriers must maintain the rating.

### Bonding / grounding (NEC 800.100)

The primary protector for incoming communications must be grounded with at least a 14 AWG bonding/grounding electrode conductor (not larger than 6 AWG required), as short and straight as practicable. **For 1- and 2-family dwellings the bonding conductor cannot exceed 20 ft**; if that's not practicable, drive a separate ground rod (≥8 ft) and bond it to the power grounding electrode system with at least 6 AWG copper. Practically: locate the demarcation/structured-media enclosure near the electrical service so the bond is short.

### Article 725 / 722 Power-limited bundling for PoE (this is the one most installers get wrong)

PoE bundle heating is governed by **NEC 725.144** and Table 725.144 (per-conductor ampacity by AWG, jacket temperature rating, and bundle size at 30°C ambient; correct for actual ambient using 310.15(B)).

For practical residential PoE design:
- Cat6A solid 23 AWG, 75°C rated cable supports IEEE 802.3bt Type 4 (Class 8, ~71 W at PD) in bundles up to 192 cables under code.
- Cat5e 24 AWG, 75°C supports 802.3bt Class 8 in bundles up to 91.
- The 802.3bt-compliant exception in 725.144 is that compliance with the table is not required where conductors are 24 AWG or larger and the rated current of the power source does not exceed 0.3 A (covers most 802.3af and most 802.3at applications).

For BSH cameras and APs (which often hit Type 3/Type 4 at 60–90 W to support IR illuminators, heaters, PTZ motors), specify Cat6A 23 AWG everywhere as the structured-cabling default; this both meets bundling rules for any plausible bundle Jon will ever pull in a residence and gives bandwidth headroom for Multi-Gig (2.5G/5G/10GBASE-T) over the install's expected lifetime.

## 3. Smart-switch wiring and lighting code interactions

### NEC 404.2(C) — neutral at switch

Since the 2011 NEC (in force in Oregon since 2014), a grounded (neutral) conductor must be present at switch boxes that control lighting in **bathrooms, hallways, stairways, and habitable rooms or occupiable spaces**. Five exceptions (raceway with capacity for the neutral; box accessible for retrofit cable without removing finish; integral-enclosure snap switches per 300.15(E); area lighting controlled by automatic means; switch controls a receptacle load). For a smart-home build, **assume every switch box gets a neutral** — there is no scenario in which Jon is building new and benefits from no-neutral devices.

Where multiple 3- and 4-way switches control the same lighting load and the entire floor area is visible from the combined locations, the neutral is only required at one location. For new construction, run it everywhere anyway — it costs nothing during rough-in and gives Jon the option to swap any device.

The exception's "5 devices per branch / 25 per feeder" cap on no-neutral electronic switches is irrelevant to a new-construction BSH design because every switch will have a neutral.

### Box fill (NEC 314.16)

Smart switches are physically deeper than dumb switches and most have an additional internal device count. Box-fill calculations under 314.16 still govern; don't downsize boxes. Default to deep boxes (e.g., 4" square with mud ring, or 22+ cubic inch single-gang). Ground wires count as one conductor of the largest size in the box; the device counts as two conductors of the largest size connected to it (314.16(B)(4)).

### Multi-way switching with smart devices

For multi-gang banks of smart switches, two clean approaches exist:
1. **Dedicated smart-switch traveler systems** (Lutron Caseta with Pico secondary, Inovelli Blue/Red with associations, Z-Wave central scene) — only the master switch is wired into the load circuit; secondary controllers are wireless and battery-powered or independently powered.
2. **Hardwired companion switches** (TP-Link Kasa, Leviton Decora Smart) — each device has a dedicated traveler and neutral; treat as conventional 3-way wiring with all conductors home-runned.

The second pattern is more reliable but consumes more conductors and more box volume. For BSH's reliability-first principle, the hardwired companion approach generally wins.

### Listing requirement

Every wired-in switch and dimmer must be listed (typically UL 20 for snap switches, UL 1472 for solid-state dimmers, UL 916 for energy-management equipment). No-name aliexpress smart switches without a U.S. listing mark fail OESC compliance even before the inspector cares about smart features. Specify devices that carry UL or ETL listing in the BSH bill of materials.

### LED dimming compatibility

This is a product-engineering issue rather than a code one, but worth flagging in the manuscript: LED dimming compatibility lists are maintained by every major dimmer manufacturer (Lutron's LED compatibility tool is the gold standard). The BSH spec should require any specified bulb to appear on the chosen dimmer's compatibility list at the supported control type (forward-phase, reverse-phase, ELV, MLV, 0-10 V, DALI). Sub-spec failure is the #1 cause of flicker, drop-out, and humming in residential LED systems.

### Lighting outlets and habitable rooms

ORSC Chapter 39 (the residential adoption of NEC requirements) requires at least one wall switch-controlled lighting outlet in every habitable room, kitchen, bathroom, hallway, stairway, attached garage, and detached garage with electric power. Switch-controlled receptacles satisfy the requirement in habitable rooms other than kitchens and bathrooms.

### Closet luminaires (NEC 410.16)

Permitted types in clothes closets are limited to surface-mounted or recessed incandescent or LED luminaires with completely enclosed light sources, surface-mounted or recessed fluorescent, or surface/recessed LED/fluorescent identified for installation within the closet storage space. Open or partially enclosed lamps and pendants are prohibited. Clearance from the storage space:
- 12 in for surface-mounted incandescent/LED with enclosed source
- 6 in for surface-mounted fluorescent or surface-mounted LED factory-wired with driver
- 6 in for recessed incandescent/LED with enclosed source
- 6 in for recessed fluorescent

Storage space is defined by 410.16(A) — extending vertically to 6 ft or the highest hanging rod, and horizontally 24 in from sides/back walls (and 12 in or shelf width from walls extending to ceiling). For a narrow (≤36 in wide) closet, often the only compliant option is a UL-listed closet-rated LED fixture (e.g., DMF S-Series, WAC closet-rated wafer) installed within the storage space.

### Wet/damp location fixtures (NEC 410.10)

Bathroom shower zone (3 ft horizontal × 8 ft vertical from threshold) requires luminaires listed for damp locations; if subject to direct spray, listed for wet locations. Outdoor under-soffit: damp location at minimum. Direct rain exposure: wet location. Smart bulbs/fixtures don't change these requirements.

### Energy code: lighting controls

The 2025 OEESC (commercial/multifamily) carries ASHRAE 90.1-2022 lighting control requirements. For a single-family residence under the 2023 ORSC, the lighting-control requirements come from ORSC Chapter 11 (energy efficiency). High-efficacy lighting requirements have largely become a non-issue with universal LED adoption. The 2023 ORRC (Oregon Residential Reach Code, optional adoption by jurisdictions) does not yet impose mandatory lighting-control schemes on single-family construction beyond ORSC base requirements.

## 4. Security cameras, microphones, and Oregon recording law

### ORS 165.540 — the operative statute

For BSH cameras in and around the home:

**Video-only recording in places without a reasonable expectation of privacy is not regulated by 165.540.** This means cameras pointed at the property's exterior, driveways, walkways, and even (per the Ninth Circuit's January 2025 en banc decision in Project Veritas v. Schmidt) public spaces do not require notice. Indoor cameras in private living areas of the owner's own home are also permitted under the homeowner exception in ORS 165.540(3).

**Audio recording is the trap.** ORS 165.540(1)(c) prohibits obtaining "any part of a conversation" by any device unless all participants are specifically informed. The Ninth Circuit upheld this in 2025. A doorbell camera or porch camera that captures conversational audio at the front door — including audio of approaching visitors talking to each other — is a felony-misdemeanor risk if those people are not informed. The clean BSH defaults:

1. **Disable audio by default** on all exterior and shared-space cameras.
2. Where audio is genuinely useful (intercom function at the front door), use a **device that announces it is recording** (most Reolink, Ubiquiti, and Amcrest doorbells have a configurable spoken announcement) **and post a sign** at the property line/door visible before the camera FOV that says "Audio and video recording in use."
3. For interior cameras in single-occupant private rooms, audio is generally fine under the 165.540(3) homeowner exception, but if Jon plans to host guests, treat it the same as exterior — disable audio or post notice.

### Camera placement and neighbors

Oregon does not have a specific statute prohibiting a camera from incidentally viewing a neighbor's property from your own property. The general rule in case law: there is no reasonable expectation of privacy in what is visible from public or your-own-property vantage. The harassment statute (ORS 166.065) and stalking civil-protection process can apply where a camera is **deliberately targeted** at a neighbor's window/yard with intent to surveil. Best practice: configure detection zones so that motion alerts fire only on your property; configure privacy masks on any portion of the FOV that shows a neighbor's window or fenced yard. Document the deliberate masking — this is the cleanest defense if a neighbor complains.

### Intimate-area recording (ORS 163.700–163.701)

Felony to record a person's intimate areas without consent in a place where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy. Bathroom and bedroom cameras are not categorically prohibited in your own home for your own use, but if a guest uses the space, a hidden camera can constitute the offense. BSH design default: **no cameras in bathrooms or bedrooms**, period. Exterior, garage, hallway, kitchen, living room, and exterior-of-home are the appropriate zones.

### Footage retention and sharing

No Oregon statute prescribes a retention period for residential security footage. Practical guidance:
- Local NVR storage of 14–30 days at full resolution + motion-only events for 90 days is a sensible default.
- Sharing footage with police: voluntary; no warrant required for footage you possess.
- Sharing publicly (e.g., posting a porch-pirate clip): video is generally OK; audio of identifiable third-party conversations is a 165.540(1)(e) "use or divulge" risk. Strip audio before publishing.

### Camera, NVR, and PoE design values

Defer to manufacturer guidance and to current product capability — these change every product cycle. As of May 2026 the durable defaults are:
- 4K (8 MP) main-stream + 1080p sub-stream as the standard for fixed cameras; 4 MP for cost-sensitive interior cameras.
- 15–20 fps main; 8–10 fps adequate for fixed-zone monitoring.
- H.265 / HEVC encoding; allocate ~6–8 Mbps VBR for 4K main at 15 fps.
- Storage: roughly 64–96 GB per camera per day at the above; size NVR for the longest retention you want plus 50% headroom. RAID 1 mirror or RAID 6 for 6+ camera systems.
- IR/color night vision per camera placement; weather rating IP66 or IP67 for exterior (IP67 for direct-rain exposure).
- Object detection (person/vehicle/package) is now table-stakes on Reolink, Ubiquiti, Hikvision, Dahua, Axis. Always enable object filtering to reduce false-positive notifications.
- PoE class: budget for 802.3at (PoE+) for most fixed bullets/turrets; 802.3bt Type 3 (60 W) for PTZ with heater, or for any camera that runs IR illuminators continuously in winter.
- Default to Cat6A for every camera run regardless of length; gives bandwidth and PoE headroom.

## 5. Access control, locks, and egress

### Smart locks vs. traditional locking

ORSC R311.2 requires the main egress door to be operable from inside without a key, special knowledge, or effort. A smart deadbolt with a thumbturn on the interior satisfies this. A smart lock that requires a phone or code to exit from the inside violates R311.2 — the interior egress mechanism must always be a single mechanical operation.

### Battery and power requirements

Defer to manufacturer specs; the durable design defaults are:
- Use locks with a **mechanical key override** as the third backup (battery dead AND keypad/Bluetooth dead).
- Specify locks with **external power contacts** (most Yale, Schlage, Kwikset, Level locks) that allow a 9 V battery touched to the bottom contacts to wake and unlock the device.
- Battery life expectations: 6–12 months on alkaline AA in mid-quality smart deadbolts; track battery state in the Home Assistant dashboard.

### Garage doors — UL 325 unattended operation

UL 325 (incorporated into federal law at 16 CFR Part 1211, latest revision 2018) governs residential garage door operators. Critical for Jon: any device that allows **closing the door without a person in line of sight** ("unattended operation") must comply with 16 CFR §1211.14 — pre-motion audible alarm ≥5 seconds, visual alarm (≥40 W incandescent equivalent or 360 lumens) flashing at ≥1 Hz for ≥5 seconds, automatic suspension of unattended operation after 2 attempts to close into an obstruction. **Unattended operation is prohibited on one-piece (swing-up) doors and swing doors** — sectional and horizontally sliding doors only.

This means: **do not relay-bridge a non-UL-325-compliant garage door opener to Home Assistant** for "close from anywhere" automation. Use a UL-325-compliant smart opener (Chamberlain MyQ, LiftMaster, Genie Aladdin Connect) and either (a) accept that the close-from-cloud function comes only from the manufacturer's app, or (b) integrate via the manufacturer's official local API where one exists. For a pure local-control BSH stack: a Konnected garage controller paired with a UL-325-compliant base opener that does its own audible/visual pre-motion alarm is the cleanest path, but verify that the specific device you choose maintains UL 325 compliance when operated via a third-party Class 2 input.

Gates: UL 325 also covers vehicular gate operators. Class I residential operators require monitored entrapment-protection sensors (photo eyes or edge sensors). UL 325 7th Edition (effective 2018) is current. ASTM F2200 governs the gate construction itself.

### Egress and access-control on doors generally

For an interior office or workshop door with an electronic lock, all the same R311.2/R311.5 egress rules apply if the room is a habitable space. Battery backup on any electronically-controlled door that is on an egress path is mandatory; failure mode should be **fail-safe** (unlocks on power loss) for life-safety doors, **fail-secure** only where life safety is not at stake (e.g., a server closet).

## 6. Alarm systems, monitoring, and police response

### What's regulated by Oregon (state)

- **Alarm-monitoring services and security professionals** are regulated by **DPSST under ORS 181A.840–181A.995 and OAR Chapter 259, Divisions 59 and 60**. An alarm monitor professional needs DPSST certification (12-hour basic course + assessment + 4-hour biennial renewal). A company providing private security or alarm monitoring in Oregon needs a DPSST **private security entity license** and an executive manager. A business does not need a physical office in Oregon to provide monitoring here.
- **Self-monitored** systems (Jon watches his own cameras, gets push notifications, decides whether to call 911) have **no DPSST involvement**.
- **DIY-installed but professionally monitored** systems (Frontpoint, SimpliSafe with pro-monitoring, Ring with pro-monitoring): the monitoring company needs DPSST entity licensing; the homeowner does not.
- **Fire alarm work** (protective signaling) requires **LEA** (Class A) at the contractor level, plus the system itself must comply with NFPA 72 and ORSC fire-alarm provisions.

### What's regulated locally (city/county)

False-alarm ordinances and alarm-permit registration are entirely local. Examples (verify current rates and rules at the time of build):

- **Portland**: Permit required within 30 days of installation; $25 residential, $125 commercial; free for seniors 62+. Penalty $100 for failure to register. PCC 14B.10.
- **Beaverton**: $30 residential, $60 commercial. First false alarm free per year; escalating fines from $50 (2nd) to $300 (12+).
- **Washington County (unincorporated, plus Banks, Cornelius, Gaston, Hillsboro)**: $20 first year; required even for unmonitored alarms with sirens; revocation for excessive false alarms.
- **Tigard, Oregon City, Redmond, Sweet Home**: All maintain alarm permit programs with similar structures.

Most ordinances also require Enhanced Call Verification (the monitoring company must call multiple contacts before requesting dispatch). If Jon's BSH design uses pro-monitoring, the contract should explicitly require ECV.

The BSH default: design every system to **avoid police dispatch on first alarm**. Use video verification (the camera shows a person before the alarm escalates), require multiple contact attempts, and program siren-only mode for the first 60 seconds with manual escalation to dispatch.

## 7. Audio/video low-voltage

In-wall speaker wire: **CL2 or CL3 listed**. CL2P/CL3P only required for plenum spaces. Conductor gauge per run length: 16 AWG for ≤80 ft runs at 8 Ω; 14 AWG for 80–200 ft; 12 AWG beyond. For 70 V distributed-audio systems used in larger BSH home configurations, gauge can be smaller (18 AWG sufficient for typical residential 70 V).

HDMI over Ethernet: HDBaseT-class extenders (Cat6/Cat6A, single cable, up to 100 m) are the durable choice for wall-mounted displays >25 ft from the source. Avoid IP-based HDMI distribution that depends on a specific multicast configuration unless Jon's network controller supports IGMP snooping properly. For a BSH "no internet required" design, point-to-point HDBaseT is preferred over networked AVoIP because it keeps the AV path completely independent of the network stack.

Outdoor speakers: weather-rated for the installation location; CL2/CL3-rated cable in conduit for buried runs; respect manufacturer's recommended setback from sprinklers and direct sun.

Central rack ventilation: 1 BTU/hr per watt of equipment dissipation as a planning rule. A typical residential rack with NVR (75 W) + switch (40 W) + UPS (10 W standby) + router (15 W) ≈ 140 W = ~480 BTU/hr. Passive ventilation with top vent panels is sufficient to ~20 °F above ambient; active 120mm fan kits drop that to ~5 °F. Don't put the rack in a sealed closet without conditioned air or active ventilation — heat is the #1 long-term killer of residential network gear.

## 8. Energy monitoring and water/utility integration

### Whole-panel energy monitors

Devices like Emporia Vue 3, IotaWatt, Sense, Span, and Curb fall into two installation categories:

1. **In-panel CT-clamp monitors with the head unit also inside the panel** (Emporia Vue, Sense): the manufacturer documents the head unit as field-installable per **NEC 312.8(B)** (power monitoring equipment in switchboards/switchgear/panelboards). The CTs are passive and listed as a system with the head unit. Most jurisdictions accept this without a separate permit; some require an electrical permit for the line-voltage tap that powers the monitor. Verify locally.

2. **External-enclosure monitors** (IotaWatt): the head unit lives in a separate enclosure outside the panel; CTs run through conduit. Cleaner from a code perspective because no third-party device is inside the panel; messier physically.

For owner-installed: ORS 479.540(1) covers Jon's own residence. He can install CTs himself. Anyone he pays to do it needs at least an LE Class B for the CT/data side and a general electrician for any line-voltage tap. The CTs themselves should be UL-listed (UL 2808 is the relevant CT listing standard).

### Water flow monitoring and shutoff valves

Flume, Phyn, Moen Flo, etc. The flow sensors are non-invasive (clamp around the meter or pipe) — no plumbing permit needed for those. Powered shutoff valves (Moen Flo, Phyn Plus, Flologic) tied into the main line **are** plumbing work; require a licensed plumber for the install in Oregon (no homeowner exemption for new plumbing under ORS 693 — same trade-license framework Chapter 4 documented for the owner-occupied build). Battery-backup is essential; a smart valve that fails closed during a power outage can trap people without water.

## 9. Smart appliances — remote start and unattended operation

**UL 858** governs household electric ranges, with **Section 47A** covering Smart-enabled appliances (added 2015 ed.). Remote start of an oven via a smartphone is permitted only if the appliance is listed for that mode under UL 858 — typically requires user-initiated local enable (set the temp at the panel, then permit remote start from app). Pure remote start of a cold oven from the cloud is generally not allowed for a UL-858-listed unit. Same family of restrictions applies under UL 749 (dishwashers), UL 2157 (washers/dryers), and UL 1083 (countertop cooking).

Practical BSH default: smart appliances are **monitor-and-notify**, not unattended-start. The dishwasher, washer, and dryer can be remote-started if the user has loaded them and pressed a "ready for remote" enable button at the appliance. The oven gets monitor-only — preheat at the panel locally.

UL 325 (above) covers garage doors and gates.

Pool/spa pumps, well pumps, sprinkler controllers: these are typically Class 2 control circuits and are fine to automate without restriction; the actuated equipment carries its own listing.

Water heaters: smart controls (Aquanta, Rheem EcoNet) tied to electric or gas water heaters are generally OK because the underlying water heater carries its own UL/CSA listing for thermal protection; smart controls are layered on top.

## 10. Local-first automation controllers (current state)

For a "must function without internet" BSH design, the controller question is the central architectural choice. As of May 2026:

**Home Assistant**: open source, runs on dedicated hardware (Home Assistant Yellow, Green, or self-hosted on x86/Pi/NUC). 2,700+ official integrations. Native Matter, Thread (via add-on border router), Z-Wave (via Z-Wave JS + USB stick), Zigbee (via Zigbee2MQTT or ZHA + USB stick). Strongest local control story; everything works without internet by default. Cloud features (remote access, voice) are opt-in via Nabu Casa subscription ($6.50/mo) or self-hosted via Tailscale/WireGuard. **Best fit for BSH.**

**Hubitat (C-8 Pro)**: closed-source commercial hub. Built-in Z-Wave 800, Zigbee 3.0; Matter and Thread added in C-8 Pro firmware. No subscription required. Smaller integration library than HA but covers major brands. Strong reliability/uptime characteristics for users who want plug-and-play with no maintenance. Backup option for BSH if HA's tinkering tax is a concern.

**openHAB**: open source, Java-based, smaller community than HA in 2026. Acceptable for a build but the integration ecosystem has fallen behind HA over the past few years. Recommend HA over openHAB for new builds.

**Lutron RadioRA 3 / HomeWorks**: commercial closed lighting-control system. Excellent reliability, fully local, integrates with HA via the LIP protocol. A good "lighting layer" complement to a HA-based whole-home stack if the budget supports it.

**Matter / Thread**: as of 2026, Matter 1.5 has added cameras, soil sensors, and energy-management classes; certified products in those categories are still rolling out slowly. Matter over Wi-Fi is reliable; Matter over Thread depends on having multiple Thread border routers (Apple HomePod, Aqara M3, HA Connect ZBT-2, Nest Hub Gen 2) and a router that handles IPv6 multicast cleanly. **Caveat for BSH's reliability principle**: Matter over Thread devices can fall out of mesh if the border router reboots or if Wi-Fi multicast breaks. For mission-critical devices (locks, alarms, smoke-detector tie-ins) prefer Z-Wave (still the most reliable mesh radio for residential) or hardwired over Matter/Thread.

**Z-Wave**: 800-series, S2 security, Long Range, SmartStart all mature in 2026. Single-vendor mesh — the most reliable wireless protocol for door locks, window/door sensors, and switches. Z-Wave JS in HA fully supports the modern stack.

**Zigbee 3.0**: cheaper devices (Hue, IKEA, Aqara, Sonoff, Third Reality). Zigbee2MQTT or ZHA. Reliable for sensors and bulbs; less reliable for security-critical devices because of historical interoperability issues.

**Wi-Fi smart devices**: avoid where possible for BSH — they congest the AP, depend on the cloud most often, and have the worst long-term firmware support track record.

The BSH controller spec: Home Assistant on dedicated hardware (HA Green or self-hosted x86 mini-PC), with Z-Wave 800 USB stick for security-critical devices (locks, leak sensors, smoke/CO bridges), Zigbee 3.0 USB stick for sensors and lighting, a Thread border router for Matter-over-Thread devices as the standard arrives, and Matter-over-Wi-Fi for compatible appliances.

## 11. Voice control, microphones, and privacy

For a "no internet required" BSH design, a cloud voice assistant (Alexa, Google Assistant, Siri) is structurally incompatible with the design philosophy. Local voice options as of May 2026:

- **Home Assistant Voice Preview Edition** (the dedicated voice satellite released by Nabu Casa in late 2024) — fully local STT/TTS via Whisper and Piper running on HA, intent matching via HA's conversation engine. Wake word is local (microWakeWord). Works without internet.
- **Rhasspy / Wyoming protocol**: the underlying open-source stack HA uses; Jon can roll his own satellite using ESP32 + INMP441 microphone array.
- **Willow** (open source from Tovera): ESP-BOX-based voice satellites with local STT.

For BSH purposes: deploy HA Voice Preview Edition or Willow as voice satellites in living spaces; reserve always-listening microphones to spaces where occupants reasonably expect that microphone is there (kitchen, living room) and disclose to overnight guests. Avoid mic placement in bedrooms and bathrooms by default.

**Recording-law tie-back**: ORS 165.540(1)(c) applies to any device that obtains a conversation. A local voice assistant that processes audio entirely on-device and does not retain a recording is a different posture than one that uploads/stores audio. Local-only is the safer position.

## 12. Network security architecture

The current authoritative sources (CISA CPG 2.0 released December 11, 2025; NIST SP 800-213 IoT Device Cybersecurity Guidance; NIST SP 800-41 Rev. 1 Firewalls; NIST SP 1800-15 IoT-MUD; CISA Microsegmentation Guidance July 2025) converge on a consistent set of practices for residential IoT-heavy networks:

**VLAN segmentation (minimum 4 VLANs):**
1. Management (router, switch, AP, NVR management interfaces)
2. Trusted (laptops, phones, workstations)
3. IoT (smart bulbs, switches, plugs, appliances, voice satellites — outbound internet only, no return path to Trusted)
4. Cameras/NVR (locked-down VLAN with no internet access for cameras; NVR has internet only for firmware updates)
5. (Optional) Guest — internet only, isolated from all other VLANs

**Firewall posture: default-deny.** Only explicit allow rules between VLANs. The pattern is asymmetric: Trusted can reach IoT; IoT cannot initiate to Trusted. mDNS reflection (Avahi reflector or equivalent) bridges discovery across VLANs without dropping the firewall.

**Remote access**: do not port-forward anything from the WAN to internal services. Use Tailscale, WireGuard, or Cloudflare Tunnel for remote access. For HA specifically: Nabu Casa Cloud or self-hosted Tailscale with HA Companion app.

**DNS filtering**: AdGuard Home or Pi-hole as the LAN DNS resolver; blocks ad/tracker/malware domains and gives visibility into IoT phone-home traffic.

**Camera isolation**: cameras live on their own VLAN with no internet egress except where explicitly required for NTP and firmware updates. The NVR is the only device that talks to cameras. The HA instance and the user's phones see camera streams via the NVR's RTSP, not by direct connection to the cameras.

**Passwords**: every device gets a unique password; rotate manufacturer defaults at install. Use a password manager (Bitwarden self-hosted is the BSH default).

**Updates**: enable automatic security updates on routers/switches/APs; manual but tracked updates on cameras/NVRs/HA (verify changelog before pushing).

**Backups**: HA configuration backup nightly to local NAS, weekly to off-site (Backblaze B2, Wasabi, or a friend's NAS over Tailscale). NVR storage is not "backed up" — the retention policy is the backup policy.

**Equipment recommendations** (durable as of mid-2026): UniFi (Ubiquiti) for the network spine — UDM SE or UDR7 + USW-Pro switches + U7 APs is the mid-build sweet spot. pfSense or OPNsense on a Protectli appliance is the maximum-control alternative. ISP gateway in bridge mode; never trust the ISP-provided router as the LAN router/firewall.

## 13. UPS, battery, and backup power for the smart-home stack

Residential UPS sizing rules of thumb for the network/smart-home rack:

- Sum the steady-state wattage of everything on the rack (router + switch + AP + NVR + HA hardware + ONT/modem + 1.25× safety factor for inrush).
- Pick a UPS with VA rating ≥ 1.6× the watt total (UPS rated VA assumes 0.6 PF for the cheap line-interactive units).
- Target 30 minutes minimum runtime at full rack load; 60 minutes is a more comfortable design point.

**Listed**: residential UPS units must be UL 1778 listed. APC, CyberPower, Eaton, Tripp Lite all carry it on their consumer/SMB lines.

**Battery type**: lead-acid (SLA) is the cheap default; lifecycle 3–5 years. LiFePO₄ UPS units (Bluetti, EcoFlow, some new APC SmartUPS Lithium) cost more upfront but last 8–10 years and tolerate heat better. For a BSH 1000-year-target design, the LiFePO₄ premium pays back inside the first replacement cycle.

**Surge protection**: whole-home Type 2 SPD (Eaton CHSPT2ULTRA, Square D HEPD80, Siemens FS140) at the main panel is the upstream defense; the UPS provides the second line. Don't rely on a $20 power strip.

**Graceful shutdown**: connect the UPS to the HA host (or the NVR) via USB; install Network UPS Tools (NUT) on HA. NUT broadcasts power state to dependent devices over the network so the NAS, HA, and NVR all shut down cleanly before the UPS dies.

**Ventilation**: UPS batteries off-gas hydrogen under deep discharge/charge. Don't enclose the UPS in a sealed cabinet. Mount in the rack with air gap above and below; if in a closet, leave the door slatted or vented.

**Replacement schedule**: every 3 years for SLA, every 7 years for LiFePO₄; document the install date on the UPS itself with a label. Battery monitoring via NUT generates alerts when capacity drops below replacement threshold.

For larger backup (whole-home or critical-loads panel during a multi-day outage), the architecture choice is between a generator (NG/propane standby) and a battery-with-PV system (Enphase IQ Battery, Tesla Powerwall 3, FranklinWH). Both are out of scope for Chapter 15 but the rack-level UPS sizing should be **independent** of any whole-home backup decision — the rack needs to ride through grid blips even if the rest of the house has whole-home backup.

## 14. Commissioning procedure

The minimum commissioning checklist that demonstrates BSH's "no internet required" guarantee actually works:

**Functional baseline (with internet up):**
- All scenes execute from voice and from app
- All cameras stream and record motion events
- All locks respond from inside and outside
- All sensors report state changes
- HA dashboard loads
- Backup completes successfully

**Internet-out test (pull the WAN cable for 4 hours):**
- All scenes still execute
- All sensors still report
- All locks still operate (locally and via Z-Wave/Matter)
- All cameras still record and HA still receives motion alerts
- HA dashboard still loads on local network
- Voice satellites still respond to local intents
- Document any feature that fails as a "cloud-dependent" feature in the design as-built

**Power-out test (cut main breaker for 30 minutes):**
- Rack stays up on UPS for the full duration
- HA continues to function
- NVR continues to record from PoE-powered cameras (PoE switch is on UPS)
- After the 30-minute mark, NUT-triggered graceful shutdown of HA and NVR completes before UPS dies
- Restore power; verify all devices come back cleanly without manual intervention

**Local-control test:**
- Disable HA's cloud add-ons; verify all automations still run
- Disable Wi-Fi; verify wired Ethernet devices still function
- Disable Z-Wave; verify all critical security automations have a Zigbee or wired fallback (or are explicitly accepted as Z-Wave-dependent)

**Scene/automation regression:**
- Run every defined scene/automation once through the app, once through voice (if voice is in scope), once through a physical switch
- Verify state changes propagate to all consuming integrations

**Alarm test:**
- Simulate a triggered alarm at off-peak hours; verify monitoring company response (if pro-monitored)
- Verify ECV procedure is followed
- Verify notifications reach all designated contacts before dispatch is requested
- Reset and document response time

**Camera review:**
- Verify motion zones for every camera don't extend onto neighbor property
- Verify privacy masks are in place where required
- Verify audio is disabled or notice is posted
- Verify IR illuminators or floodlights trigger correctly at night

**Lock behavior:**
- Verify mechanical override (key) works on every lock
- Verify external power contacts (jump-start) work
- Verify manual interior thumb-turn works on every lock
- Verify auto-lock timeout is set per door
- Document codes-issued and codes-revoked log

**Backup restore:**
- Take a fresh HA backup; restore it to a known-good test instance; verify everything comes up

**Documentation acceptance:**
- Network diagram (VLANs, switches, APs, devices) signed off
- Wiring diagram (every Cat6A run labeled at both ends, every CT identified)
- HA configuration committed to git (self-hosted Forgejo or local) and backed up
- Device inventory (model, MAC, IP, firmware version, install date, location) maintained
- Maintenance schedule documented (battery replacement dates, firmware update cadence, password rotation)

## Limits / what isn't in the law

- **There is no Oregon state-level smart-home regulatory regime.** The Smart Building Program at BCD focuses on commercial energy-management systems and does not impose requirements on single-family residential smart-home installations.
- **There is no Oregon equivalent to California's IoT Security Law (SB-327) or the federal Cyber Trust Mark voluntary scheme.** Device-level cybersecurity is unregulated at the state level for residential consumers; the FTC retains general unfair/deceptive-practices authority over IoT vendors.
- **The "5-device limit" on no-neutral switches in NEC 404.2(C) Exception is a retrofit-only constraint.** It does not apply to new construction where Jon installs neutrals at every switch.
- **UL 858 Section 47A on Smart-enabled cooking appliances is a product listing standard, not a building code.** The code path is "use listed equipment per its instructions" — non-compliance is a manufacturer/installer issue, not a permit issue.
- **Local jurisdictions vary substantially on alarm permit requirements, low-voltage permit thresholds, and camera-related ordinances.** Verify the specific Marion/Washington/whatever county AND the specific city at the build site before final design.
- **Oregon's audio-recording statute is currently stable** after the Ninth Circuit's January 7, 2025 en banc decision in Project Veritas v. Schmidt upholding ORS 165.540(1)(c). The U.S. Supreme Court denied cert in October 2025. The all-party-notice rule for audio is settled law and Jon should design accordingly.

## When to bring in paid help

- **Construction attorney**: review of any installation contract if BSH LLC ever installs low-voltage on a third-party home (the contractor-vs.-owner-builder line from Chapter 4 plus the LE Class A/B requirement).
- **Privacy attorney**: only if BSH's video/audio approach is novel (e.g., publishing footage as part of the YouTube methodology) and Jon wants a defensible position in writing.
- **Licensed electrician** (general supervising electrician or general journeyman): for any line-voltage tap that powers a smart panel or energy monitor, if the manufacturer's install guide isn't crystal clear that it's owner-installable.

# Chapter 16: Materials, Suppliers, Procurement, And Substitution

## Anchor sentence for the manuscript

Chapter 16 separates the verifiable from the volatile: material standards (ASTM, ACI, AWS, NSF/ANSI, IAPMO, AHRI, NEMA, AAMA, ICC-ES), product listing and labeling rules under ORSC §R102.4 and §R102.5, substitution authority under ORSC §R104.11, batch-ticket and documentation requirements under ASTM C94 and the relevant trade standards, OR-OSHA HazCom 2012 (29 CFR 1910.1200) labeling and SDS rules, and federal/state/utility incentive frameworks are stable enough to specify in writing today; prices, lead times, supplier availability, exact rebate dollar amounts, and current product approval listings change too quickly to lock in and must be re-verified at the estimating pass and again at order time.

## 1. The verification principle (this drives everything below)

Chapter 4 already established that the build documents have to be defensible. Chapter 16 has to extend that discipline to procurement: every material decision Jon writes into the manuscript needs an authority anchor that is durable across the design-to-build window, plus a verification step that says "confirm at order date." Three categories:

**Permanent references** (do not require revisiting per build): the consensus-standard families themselves — ASTM concrete and aggregate specs, ACI structural concrete codes, AWS welding codes, NSF/ANSI potable-water lead and health-effects standards, IAPMO plumbing product approvals, AHRI HVAC equipment ratings, NEMA enclosure ratings, AAMA/WDMA/CSA fenestration standards (NAFS-11/-17), UL listing categories, ICC-ES evaluation report framework. These reference systems do not move quarter-to-quarter.

**Slow-moving references** (revisit per code cycle): the Oregon code adoption (2023 ORSC, 2023 OESC, 2025 OEESC, 2022 OSSC, 2023 OPSC, 2022 OMSC) and their amendment tables. Revisit when the next adoption is announced (next OESC adoption is anticipated October 2026; ORSC/OPSC/OMSC track the same general cadence).

**Volatile data** (verify per build, not in the manuscript text): all unit prices, supplier minimums, current lead times, current rebate dollar amounts, current incentive funding availability, current product listing IDs (a manufacturer reformulates a product, the old listing gets superseded), current OR-OSHA rule version, current trade-ally lists. Treat any specific number Jon already has from earlier estimating as a historical note until replaced by a dated quote at the estimating pass.

The discipline that protects the manuscript: write the **standard** into the spec, not the brand. "Concrete shall conform to ASTM C94/C94M, designed per ACI 318 and ACI 301, with batch tickets per Section 14 of ASTM C94" is durable. "Knife River 4000 psi mix #X-12345" is one phone call away from being wrong.

## 2. Substitution authority — who has to approve what

This is the legally controlling framework for the whole chapter and is generally misunderstood on owner-builder jobs. Five distinct authorities, each with its own scope:

### Building official (ORSC §R104.11 / OSSC §104.11 / OAR 918-008-0095)

The building official is the only person who can approve an alternative material, design, or method of construction that departs from the prescriptive code. ORSC §R104.11 requires the alternative to be "satisfactory and complies with the intent of the provisions of this code" and to be "at least the equivalent of that prescribed in this code" in quality, strength, effectiveness, fire resistance, durability and safety. Test data per §R104.11.1 is the typical evidence, but the building official has explicit authority to consider "evidence of a history of successful use in lieu of testing" (Appendix AS for strawbale and Appendix AU for cob both rely on this provision).

If the building official denies an alternative, the petitioner can escalate to BCD for a **statewide alternate method ruling** under OAR 918-008-0095. Once issued, OAR 918-008-0110 makes that ruling enforceable in every Oregon jurisdiction; building officials who refuse to honor it risk certification revocation. This is a powerful but slow path — figure 60–180 days for a statewide ruling from petition to issuance. For the BSH methodology generally, plan to use prescriptive paths or AppU (cob) / AppS (strawbale) / AppW (existing alternative path) where available, and reserve statewide alternate-method petitions for items the design philosophy genuinely requires.

### Inspector

The inspector enforces what the building official approved on the construction documents. The inspector is **not** authorized to approve a substitution in the field. Field substitutions that change a stamped or approved detail must go back through the building official (often via the design professional, but legally through the building official). The inspector can sign off on equivalent products if the construction documents themselves allow equivalent approved equals — that requires the spec language to say so.

### Design professional in responsible charge (ORSC §R106.3.4 / OSSC §107.3.4)

If a registered design professional signed the construction documents, the design professional has contractual and professional-licensing authority over substitutions within the scope of the stamped work. For the BSH design-and-research positioning Chapter 4 established, this means: any portion of the work that has a PE stamp on it (the structural calculations, any specifically engineered detail) requires the PE's review of substitutions before the building official will accept them in the field.

### Manufacturer / warranty provider

A manufacturer's product warranty is a contract between the manufacturer and the buyer, and almost every product warranty has installation requirements as a precondition. ORSC §R102.4 (Exception) is unambiguous: "Where enforcement of a code provision would violate the conditions of the listing of the equipment or appliance, the conditions of the listing and manufacturer's instructions shall apply." The listing controls. Substituting a fastener, adhesive, sealant, or accessory not specified in the manufacturer's installation instructions can void the warranty even if the substitute is "equivalent" by spec. This is the sneaky one — the AHJ accepts the install but the manufacturer denies the warranty claim three years later.

For warranty-critical assemblies (waterproofing systems, window/door installations, roofing assemblies, structural sheathing assemblies, manufactured-stone veneer systems), specify the **manufacturer-listed accessory by name and part number** and document the substitution review in writing with both the design professional and the manufacturer's tech rep.

### Installer / contractor

A licensed contractor has independent professional liability for installations they pull money for — even when the customer or the design professional approves a specific product. If the installer has reasonable professional knowledge that a product is unsuitable for the application, accepting the substitution doesn't shield them from negligence. For the BSH owner-builder posture (Jon installing on his own pre-sale residence), this is internalized; for any future BSH-LLC consulting role on a third party's home, it's a contractual liability question that should be addressed in the design contract.

### The substitution review stack — write this into the spec template

Every material spec in the manuscript should identify which authority applies. A practical four-tier stack:

**Tier 1 — No substitution permitted.** Used for items where the listed system depends on the named accessory (e.g., a specific waterproofing membrane's manufacturer-listed primer, transition tape, and termination bar). Spec language: "Use only manufacturer-supplied accessories. No substitutions."

**Tier 2 — Substitution requires written approval by design professional and manufacturer.** Used for warranty-critical products where a different brand may meet the standard but the warranty demands the specific brand chain. Spec language: "Substitutions permitted only with prior written approval from [design professional] and confirmation from the originally specified product's manufacturer that warranty coverage is unaffected."

**Tier 3 — Equivalent product permitted with documentation.** Used for commodity products where any product meeting the cited standard is acceptable. Spec language: "Equivalent products permitted upon submission of [spec sheet / listing / test report] demonstrating compliance with [cited standard]. Documentation retained in project closeout records."

**Tier 4 — No approval required.** Used for ordinary materials with no engineered performance criteria (e.g., 2x dimensional lumber to a grade stamp). The grade stamp itself is the documentation.

## 3. Material standards by trade (the durable reference layer)

These are the standards the spec language should anchor to. Specific products and lot numbers belong in the bid-package and submittal log, not the manuscript.

### Concrete and reinforcement

- **Ready-mixed concrete**: ASTM C94/C94M (current edition C94/C94M-23). Mix design per ACI 318 (ACI 318-25 is current; verify Oregon adoption — OSSC structural provisions reference it indirectly via IBC).
- **Cement**: ASTM C150 (portland), C595 (blended), C1157 (performance hydraulic).
- **Aggregates**: ASTM C33/C33M (concrete aggregates), C330 (lightweight).
- **Admixtures**: ASTM C494 (chemical admixtures for concrete), C260 (air-entraining), C1017 (admixtures for flowing concrete).
- **Supplementary cementitious materials**: ASTM C618 (fly ash and natural pozzolan), C989 (slag cement), C1240 (silica fume).
- **Reinforcement**: ASTM A615 (deformed billet steel bars), A706 (low-alloy steel for ductility — required by ACI 318 for special seismic systems), A1064 (welded wire reinforcement). Epoxy-coated per A775 or A934. Stainless per A955.
- **Detailing and placement**: ACI 301 (Specifications for Structural Concrete), ACI 117 (Tolerances), ACI SP-66 (ACI Detailing Manual), ACI 305.1 (Hot Weather Concreting), ACI 306R (Cold Weather Concreting).
- **FRP reinforcement**: ACI 440.11 (FRP bars), ACI 440.6 (FRP bar specification). FRP is not yet a prescriptive ORSC item; if the BSH methodology specifies FRP bar, it's an alternative-material submission to the building official.
- **Fiber reinforcement**: ASTM C1116/C1116M (Fiber-Reinforced Concrete).

### Concrete batch tickets — what you legally have to receive

ASTM C94/C94M Section 14.1 requires the manufacturer to furnish a delivery ticket with each batch containing, at minimum: ready-mix company name and batch plant; serial or batch number; date; truck number; purchaser name; specific designation of the concrete (mix ID, class, strength); amount in cubic yards/meters; time loaded or first mixed; water added by receiver; and signatures. Section 6.1.10 (added in C94/C94M-21) requires the **time limit to complete discharge to be stated on the ticket** — if the purchaser doesn't specify, the producer establishes and communicates it. The traditional 90-minute / 1.5-hour default was removed in 2021; ACI 301 §4.3.2.2 still requires verification of slump, air content, and temperature at 1.5 hours regardless of stated discharge time. The BSH spec should explicitly state discharge time limits matched to weather conditions and placement method.

Retain every batch ticket as part of project closeout records — these are the substantive evidence of what was actually delivered if a structural question arises later. Photograph each ticket on-site (NCR copies fade) and store digitally with date/timestamp.

### Masonry

- **CMU**: ASTM C90 (load-bearing concrete masonry units). Specify weight class (lightweight ≤105 pcf, medium 105–125, normal weight ≥125) and strength (f'm typically 1500–2000 psi by unit testing).
- **Brick**: ASTM C216 (facing brick), C652 (hollow brick), C62 (building brick).
- **Mortar**: ASTM C270 (mortar for unit masonry); type S for most BSH structural applications, type N for non-structural and historical match, type M for high-strength below-grade.
- **Grout**: ASTM C476 (grout for masonry); fine vs. coarse grout per cell size.
- **Mortar/grout aggregate**: ASTM C144 (aggregate for masonry mortar), C404 (aggregates for masonry grout).
- **Reinforcement and ties**: ACI 530.1 / TMS 602 (Specification for Masonry Structures); joint reinforcement per ASTM A951; wall ties per A36 (steel) or A580 (stainless).
- **Detailing**: TMS 402 (Building Code Requirements for Masonry Structures) is the controlling code, referenced via OSSC.

### Structural steel

- **Wide flange / hollow structural sections**: ASTM A992 (W-shapes, Fy = 50 ksi), A500 Grade B or C (HSS), A36 (plates and bars where used), A572 Grade 50 (high-strength low-alloy plate where required).
- **Bolts**: ASTM F3125 (high-strength structural bolts; supersedes A325 and A490 designations, though those grades remain within F3125), with assemblies to A563 (nuts) and F436 (washers).
- **Anchor bolts**: ASTM F1554 (Grades 36, 55, 105 by yield).
- **Welding**: AWS D1.1 (Structural Welding Code — Steel), AWS D1.4 (Reinforcing Steel). Welder qualification per same.
- **Decking**: SDI standards (Steel Deck Institute) — RD (roof deck), C/CD (composite floor deck).
- **Connections**: AISC 360 (Specification for Structural Steel Buildings) and AISC Manual.
- **Cold-formed steel**: AISI S100 / S240 (cold-formed framing).
- **Galvanizing**: ASTM A123 (hot-dip galvanized coatings on iron and steel products), A153 (hot-dip galvanized hardware), A653 (galvanized sheet, coating designations like G60/G90).

### Anchors and fasteners

- **Post-installed concrete anchors**: ICC-ES AC193 (mechanical anchors), AC308 (adhesive anchors). For each anchor used, the spec should require a current ICC-ES Evaluation Service Report (ESR number) demonstrating compliance with ACI 318 Chapter 17 / ACI 355.2 / 355.4.
- **Wood-to-concrete connectors**: ICC-ES AC10 framework; Simpson and USP/Mitek publish ESR-listed catalogs.
- **Wood-to-wood connectors**: ANSI/AWC NDS-2024 (National Design Specification for Wood Construction) governs design values; Simpson Strong-Tie ESR reports cover proprietary products.
- **Fasteners (general)**: ASTM F1667 (driven fasteners — nails, spikes, staples), with specific products listed per manufacturer.
- **Stainless steel fastener grades**: ASTM F593 (bolts/screws/studs), F594 (nuts). Specify grade (304/316) by exposure environment.

### Waterproofing, drainage, flashing, sealants

- **Foundation waterproofing**: ASTM D6135 (self-adhering sheet membrane), C836 (hot-applied), D7832 (fluid-applied). The 2023 ORSC §R406.1 requires waterproofing under hydrostatic pressure conditions; §R406.2 dampproofing otherwise.
- **Drainage composites / drainage mat**: ASTM D7001 (plastic geocomposite drains).
- **Drainpipe**: ASTM F405 / F667 (corrugated PE), D2729 (PVC sewer and drain).
- **Geotextile**: AASHTO M288 (separation, filtration, drainage classes); ASTM D4751 (apparent opening size).
- **Drainage gravel**: ASTM D2487 / D2488 classification; the practical spec is "clean ¾-inch washed drain rock" with fines content limit per ORSC §R405.
- **Sheet flashing**: ASTM E2112 (Standard Practice for Installation of Exterior Windows, Doors and Skylights) is the procedural authority; AAMA 711 (self-adhering flashing) and AAMA 712 (fluid-applied flashing) cover product performance.
- **Sealants**: ASTM C920 (elastomeric joint sealants — Type, Grade, Class, Use designations matter); fire-rated through-penetration sealants per UL 1479 / ASTM E814.
- **WRB (weather-resistive barrier)**: ASTM E2178 (air permeance), E2357 (air barrier assembly), E331 (water penetration). The 2023 ORSC §R703 requires a WRB; performance is verified per the cited tests.

### Insulation

- **Mineral fiber batt**: ASTM C665 (mineral fiber blanket).
- **Rigid foam**: ASTM C578 (XPS, EPS), C1289 (polyiso).
- **Spray polyurethane foam**: ASTM C1029 (SPF), with thermal barrier per ORSC §R316.
- **Cellulose**: ASTM C739.
- **Mineral wool**: ASTM C612 (board), C764 (loose-fill).
- **Reflective**: ASTM C1224.
- **Code path**: ORSC Chapter 11 (Energy Efficiency) — current ORSC adopts the Oregon Residential Reach Code optionally, otherwise the prescriptive R-values in §N1102.1 / §N1102.2 apply.

### Windows, doors, skylights (fenestration)

- **Performance standard**: AAMA/WDMA/CSA 101/I.S.2/A440 (NAFS) — current edition NAFS-22, prior NAFS-17 still in widespread use. Each unit carries a Performance Class (R, LC, CW, AW) and a Performance Grade (PG, the design pressure rating).

- **Installation**: ASTM E2112 (Standard Practice — the procedural reference for any installation question).
- **Fire-rated assemblies**: NFPA 252 / UL 10C (door assemblies), NFPA 257 / UL 9 (window assemblies).
- **Garage doors**: ASTM E330 (structural performance), ANSI/DASMA 102 (specifications), DASMA 108 (air infiltration), DASMA 105 (wind load).

### Plumbing materials

- **Lead-free requirement**: Federal SDWA Section 1417 (since January 4, 2014) — all wetted surfaces of pipe, fittings, plumbing fittings, and fixtures used for human-consumption water must meet a weighted average ≤0.25% lead, certified per **NSF/ANSI/CAN 372**. As of January 1, 2024, NSF/ANSI/CAN 61 incorporates a more stringent leachate test marked **Q ≤ 1** for faucets and endpoint devices and **Q ≤ 0.5** for connector hoses, shut-off valves, and other last-liter components. Specify both NSF/ANSI/CAN 61 (health effects) and 372 (lead content) on the product listing for any potable-water material.
- **PEX**: ASTM F876 (tubing), F877 (system), F1807 (copper crimp), F1960 (cold expansion), F2080 (expansion + reinforcing rings), F2159 (plastic insert). UPC and IPC both accept PEX-A, PEX-B, and PEX-C with appropriate fittings.
- **Copper**: ASTM B88 (Type K, L, M tube). Solder must be lead-free per ASTM B32.
- **CPVC**: ASTM F441/F442 (pipe), F438/F439 (fittings).
- **PVC DWV**: ASTM D2665 (pipe and fittings); ABS DWV per ASTM D2661.
- **Cast iron DWV**: ASTM A74 (hub and spigot), A888 (hubless).
- **Backflow devices**: ASSE 1013 (RPZ), ASSE 1015 (DCV), ASSE 1024 (dual check), ASSE 1056 (spill-resistant vacuum breaker). Annual testing required; tester must be certified per OAR 333-061 for cross-connection control.
- **Water heaters**: tank-type per ANSI Z21.10.1 (gas), UL 174 (electric); tankless per ANSI Z21.10.3 (gas), UL 499 (electric); heat pump per UL 174 plus ENERGY STAR for incentive eligibility.
- **Tanks/cisterns**: NSF/ANSI 61 for materials; ARCSA/ASPE/ANSI 78 for rainwater catchment system performance; the 2023 OPSC has provisions for non-potable rainwater under Chapter 17, alternate water sources.
- **Pumps**: NSF/ANSI 61 wetted surfaces; UL listing for the motor/control side.
- **UV treatment**: NSF/ANSI 55 (UV systems, Class A for disinfection of microbiologically unsafe water, Class B for supplemental).
- **Joining**: per the pipe standard family; product approval through IAPMO (UPC) or ICC (IPC) listing — Oregon adopts UPC via OPSC.
- **Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code product approval**: Oregon BCD maintains a list of approved products under the OPSC; verify product is listed (or covered by an IAPMO/UPC listing the OPSC accepts) at submittal stage.

### Electrical materials

- **Listing requirement**: NEC 110.3 — equipment must be listed (or labeled) for the application; Oregon defines "listed/labeled" via ORS 479.530 ("certified electrical product"). Use NRTL listings (UL, ETL, CSA, MET, TÜV, etc. — accepted listing labs maintained by OSHA's NRTL program).
- **Conductors**: UL 83 (THWN/THHN), UL 854 (SE cable), UL 719 (NM cable / Romex).
- **Conduit**: UL 6 (RMC), UL 1242 (IMC), UL 797 (EMT), UL 651 (PVC), UL 360 (LFMC).
- **Boxes**: UL 514A (metal), UL 514C (nonmetallic).
- **Devices**: UL 498 (receptacles), UL 20 (snap switches), UL 1472 (solid-state dimmers), UL 67 (panelboards), UL 489 (molded-case circuit breakers), UL 943 (GFCI), UL 1699 (AFCI), UL 943C (combination GFCI/AFCI).
- **Surge protection**: UL 1449 (current edition 5; specify Type 1 service-entrance, Type 2 panel, Type 3 point-of-use as appropriate).
- **Grounding/bonding**: UL 467; clamps and connectors carry their own listing categories.
- **EV charging**: UL 2202 (EV charging system), UL 2231 (personnel protection), SAE J1772 (connector — being supplemented by NACS / SAE J3400 as of 2024).

- **Solar-ready**: 2023 ORSC §R325 / OEESC framework for solar-ready conduit and reserved breaker space; spec sheets for panels per UL 61730 (current safety standard, replacing UL 1703); inverters per UL 1741 / IEEE 1547.
- **Low-voltage cabling**: NEC Articles 722 (cables for power-limited circuits), 725 (Class 2/3), 760 (fire alarm), 770 (optical fiber), 800 (communications), 820 (CATV) — Chapter 15 has the deep dive.

The ESR cross-references the relevant code section, the test standards used, and the conditions of use. Specify by ESR number in the construction documents and retain a current copy in the project records — ESRs are renewed periodically and an old ESR version may not match the current code edition the AHJ enforces.

## 4. SDS, HazCom, and OR-OSHA

For any project where workers other than the homeowner are on-site (employees, paid helpers, subcontractors), **OR-OSHA's Hazard Communication standard applies** under OAR 437 Division 2/Z (general industry) and Division 3/D (construction). OR-OSHA adopted the GHS-aligned HCS in 2012 with full effective date of June 1, 2016. Federal OSHA published a HazCom 2024 final rule on May 20, 2024 (effective July 19, 2024 with phased compliance) updating to GHS Revision 7; OR-OSHA has signaled it will reevaluate after federal corrections settle. As of May 2026, plan to comply with both the 2012 HCS (still the OR-OSHA enforced baseline) and the 2024 federal rule for any product manufactured after the federal effective dates.

For owner-only work where Jon is doing his own labor on his own property, OR-OSHA HazCom does not apply as a regulatory matter — but the SDS framework is still useful for personal safety, and any time a paid worker steps on the site, the rules attach.

### What HazCom requires

- Maintain a written hazard communication program describing how the standard is implemented on the worksite.
- Maintain a chemical inventory list.
- Obtain and retain SDSs for every hazardous chemical used or stored — readily accessible to workers during all shifts. Digital SDSs are acceptable if the access method works without delay and without an internet connection (the construction site doesn't always have one).
- Ensure container labels meet GHS requirements (product identifier, signal word, hazard statements, precautionary statements, supplier identification, pictograms).
- Train workers on the standard at initial assignment and whenever a new hazard is introduced.

### SDS structure (16 sections per HCS Appendix D, OSHA-enforced for sections 1–11 and 16; sections 12–15 not OSHA-enforced)

1. Identification, 2. Hazard identification, 3. Composition/ingredients, 4. First-aid measures, 5. Fire-fighting measures, 6. Accidental release measures, 7. Handling and storage, 8. Exposure controls / PPE, 9. Physical and chemical properties, 10. Stability and reactivity, 11. Toxicological information, 12. Ecological (not OSHA-enforced), 13. Disposal (not enforced), 14. Transport (not enforced), 15. Regulatory (not enforced), 16. Other information including date of preparation/revision.

For the BSH project, the SDS-driven decisions Jon will hit include: storage temperature for foam adhesives and sealants (Sections 7 and 9), shelf-life from date of manufacture (Section 16), ventilation requirements during application (Section 8), PPE for application and cleanup (Section 8), and disposal of unused material (Section 13). Specifically:

- **Spray polyurethane foam (SPF)**: vapor and respiratory hazards during cure; manufacturer-specified rebreather or supplied-air respirator during application; reentry time per manufacturer (typically 24+ hours) before unprotected occupancy.
- **Concrete admixtures**: alkali burn risk, eye protection, glove specification per Section 8.
- **Asphaltic and solvent-based coatings**: VOC content, ventilation, ignition source removal (Section 5).
- **Two-part epoxies and adhesives**: skin sensitization risk, gloves rated for the chemistry, no contact with skin even with cured material if uncured residue is suspected.
- **Acids (muriatic for concrete prep, brick wash)**: eye protection, acid-resistant gloves, ventilation.
- **Mortar, grout, concrete (silica content)**: OSHA respirable crystalline silica standard 29 CFR 1926.1153 — Table 1 controls (water suppression or local exhaust ventilation) for any cutting, drilling, grinding of concrete or masonry; respiratory protection for unspecified tasks. OR-OSHA enforces this under OAR 437 Div 3.

## 5. Labels, listing marks, and the documentation chain

The document control flow that holds up to building inspection and to attorney review later:

**At specification time**: cite the standard and the listing (e.g., "Type X gypsum board, ASTM C1396 §5.4, UL listed for use in U419 wall assembly").

**At submittal**: contractor or owner-builder submits product cut sheets showing the standards, listing marks, and ICC-ES report numbers. Design professional or building official approves.

**At delivery**: verify the delivered product matches the submittal. Photograph batch numbers, listing labels, ICC-ES numbers visible on packaging or product. Retain delivery slip.

**At install**: keep the original packaging label until the inspector signs off the relevant inspection. Once buried, the label is gone — the photograph is the only remaining evidence.

**At inspection**: inspector verifies installed product against approved submittal. Note the inspection date and the products inspected on a project log.

**At closeout**: assemble project closeout binder containing
- Approved construction documents (final stamped set if applicable)
- All submittal approvals
- All ESRs / ICC-ES reports current at install date
- All batch tickets (ready-mix, concrete admixture, concrete additive)
- All product cut sheets and listing labels (photographs OK)
- All AHRI certificates for HVAC equipment
- All NFRC/AHRI/ENERGY STAR labels for fenestration and equipment
- HVAC startup reports per manufacturer's commissioning procedure
- Manufacturer warranty registrations and effective dates
- Building official approvals for any alternate methods
- All inspection records
- Any required test reports (concrete strength, soil bearing, blower door, duct leakage, energy code compliance forms — Oregon RES check or COMcheck output)

This binder is what Chapter 4 will need to reference as defensible evidence of how the home was built. Keep both a paper copy in a fireproof location and a digital copy in two independent locations (local NAS + cloud backup).

## 6. Energy rebates, tax credits, and utility incentives — current state (May 2026)

This is the most volatile section in the chapter. Document the framework, treat dollar amounts as verify-at-estimating.

### Federal tax credits — sunset

**Section 25C (Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit) and Section 25D (Residential Clean Energy Credit) both ended for property placed in service after December 31, 2025**, under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025). Any project completed in 2026 or later cannot claim these credits. Projects that completed by Dec 31, 2025 can still claim on a 2025 return using IRS Form 5695. The BSH manuscript should not rely on these credits for budget planning of any 2026+ build.

### Federal rebate programs — IRA-funded HOMES and HEAR

These programs are state-administered with federal funding from the 2022 IRA. Oregon's are administered by ODOE with implementation partners Earth Advantage and Energy Trust. Phased launch begins spring 2026:

- **HOMES (Home Efficiency Rebates Program)**: rebates for whole-home energy retrofits demonstrating ≥20% modeled energy savings. 50–100% of project costs up to $10,000 depending on income and savings tier. Requires energy assessment by program-enrolled auditor.
- **HEAR (Home Electrification and Appliance Rebates Program)**: rebates for low- and moderate-income households (≤150% AMI) for specific high-efficiency electric appliances (heat pumps, HPWHs, induction ranges, heat pump dryers, electrical panel upgrades, wiring). 50–100% of project costs up to $14,000 depending on income.

For BSH: HOMES and HEAR require an enrolled contractor; owner-installed work generally qualifies only for specific items with pre-approval. The owner-builder posture Jon is using is structurally awkward for HEAR specifically — verify with ODOE directly whether the BSH owner-builder install pattern qualifies. Do not budget HEAR/HOMES dollars into a build until ODOE confirms in writing.

### Oregon HP3 (Heat Pump Purchase Program)

State program funded by a separate $197 million EPA Climate Pollution Reduction Grant (independent of HOMES/HEAR). Up to $2,000 per qualifying heat pump for owner-occupied homes; up to $4,000 for certain rental and new-construction categories with stacked upgrades. Status as of January 2026: existing-owner-occupied Round 1 and Round 2 funding fully reserved; rental and new construction categories still had funding — verify before each build. Cannot stack with HOMES/HEAR; can stack with Energy Trust.

### Energy Trust of Oregon

Utility-funded (PGE, Pacific Power, NW Natural, Cascade Natural Gas, Avista) — not affected by federal sunset. Continues to offer incentives for ducted heat pumps ($2,000+), ductless heat pumps ($800–$1,800), heat pump water heaters (~$700), insulation ($1.25–$1.50/sf attic), windows, smart thermostats, and other measures. Trade-ally network required for some incentives; some allow owner-installed with pre-approval. Specific dollar amounts and equipment eligibility update at minimum annually — pull current incentive sheet at estimating.

### Procurement implication for the BSH spec

Two practical rules:

1. **Specify equipment that is AHRI-listed and ENERGY STAR-certified** (where ENERGY STAR exists for the category). This keeps every reasonable incentive door open without committing to one program. The cost premium for ENERGY STAR over baseline is usually small relative to the incentive value.

2. **Build the equipment-decision spreadsheet at estimating** with three columns: equipment AHRI/ENERGY STAR status, current incentive eligibility per program (pull current docs), net post-incentive cost. Decisions made before this spreadsheet exists will systematically miss money.

## 7. Suppliers — process, not specific vendors

Specific Oregon supplier names, lead times, delivery radii, minimum orders, and quote validity all change too quickly to commit to in a 1000-year-design manuscript. The framework that doesn't change:

### Concrete (ready-mix)

Knife River, Cemex, CalPortland, Kerr Contractors, Northwest Rock & Recycling, and several regional players cover most of Oregon. Verify at estimating: which plant serves the specific build site; what mix designs they hold ASTM C94 compliance for; cubic-yard minimum and load-charge schedule; pump truck availability and cost; admixture availability for any specialty mix (fiber-reinforced, integral waterproofing, low-shrinkage); concrete temperature controls for hot-weather pours.

### Aggregate

Gravel, sand, and crushed stone are typically sourced from the same mining operations that feed local ready-mix plants. Verify at estimating: ASTM C33 gradation availability; washed vs. unwashed; delivery vs. pickup; truck types (10-yard end-dump is the default residential delivery vehicle); access constraints to the build site.

### Masonry

Mutual Materials (Mt. Angel, OR plant covers most of the state), Oregon Brick, Oldcastle/CalPortland for CMU. Verify at estimating: in-stock vs. special-order CMU sizes/colors; mortar color options; anchor and tie inventory; pallet minimums; rebar fabrication relationship.

### Rebar fabrication

Cascade Steel Rolling Mills (McMinnville) for raw bar; fabricators include Harris Salem, Pacific Rebar, Knife River reinforcing services. Verify at estimating: fabrication lead time for shop drawings → cuts/bends → delivery; ASTM A615 vs. A706 availability; #3 (3/8") through #11 (1-3/8") residential range; accessory items (chairs, tie wire, dowels).

### Structural steel

PCB Steel Group, Knife River (large-format), Mid-Valley Steel (Albany), Greenberry Industrial. For residential-scale steel (lintels, beams, posts, anchors, plates), most contractors source through retail steel yards (Coastal Farm, big-box, or specialty steel suppliers). Verify at estimating: A992 wide-flange availability in residential sizes; HSS in stock vs. order; cut-to-length charges; galvanizing turnaround.

### Waterproofing, drainage, insulation, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, tile, cabinetry

Distribution channels are mostly national wholesalers with Oregon branches (Ferguson, Consolidated Supply, Pacific Plumbing Supply, Crescent Electric, CED, Platt, Carrier West, Daikin Pacific, Trane Supply, Daltile, MSI, Bedrosian, etc.). Verify at estimating: which distributor's account the contractor or BSH-LLC uses; standard delivery cadence (most wholesalers do daily delivery in metro areas); will-call vs. delivery pricing; return policy for unused stock.

### Rental equipment

Sunbelt, United Rentals, EquipmentShare, Star Rentals, plus regional rental yards (Buchholz, Reds Equipment in some areas). Verify at estimating: equipment types in stock at the closest yard; weekly vs. monthly rate breakpoints; operator-required equipment categories (skid steer, telehandler, bucket truck — most yards require operator certification or rent-with-operator); delivery and pickup fees; deposit and damage waiver structure; OSHA-required certification for operators.

For specific rental categories common to BSH builds: scaffolding (specifying frame vs. tube-and-clamp); shoring (post and beam, screw jacks); concrete equipment (mixers, vibrators, power trowels); compact track loaders; mini-excavators; man-lifts and scissor lifts; pressure washers; HEPA vacuums for silica-cleanup; impact tools.

### Tool acquisition vs. rental decision

The framework that doesn't depend on price:

- **Buy** tools used on more than one BSH build that have low storage cost: cordless drill/driver/impact, common power saws, levels, layout tools, basic hand tools.
- **Rent** equipment used once or twice with high storage and maintenance cost: large compressors, concrete saws, ride-on equipment, lifts.
- **Hire-with-equipment** for specialized operations: crane work, large excavator work, concrete pumping. The operator carries the certification and the insurance.

### Contractor licensing for any rented or hired help

Anyone Jon pays to be on-site doing CCB-licensed work needs a CCB license. Day labor through a temp agency is acceptable for unskilled work; the agency carries the workers' comp. Hiring a friend "to help out" without an agency is a workers' comp landmine documented in the prior chapters and remains so here. Any rental yard that delivers and operates equipment is providing a service; the operator's employer is responsible for workers' comp and OR-OSHA training.

## Limits / what isn't in the law

- **There is no statewide Oregon "approved product" list** Jon can pull from to pre-clear products. Oregon BCD does maintain alternate method rulings and code interpretations (OAR 918-008-0085, -0090, -0095), but most products clear via national listing (UL, NRTL, NSF, AHRI, ICC-ES) rather than a state list.
- **ICC-ES, IAPMO UES, Intertek CCRR, and DrJ TER are private evaluation services**, not government certifications. The building official has discretion to accept or reject them; in practice all four are widely accepted across Oregon AHJs.
- **Manufacturer's instructions trump code** under ORSC §R102.4 Exception only when enforcement of a code provision would violate the listing. The exception does not let the manufacturer's instructions waive a code requirement that's independent of the listing.
- **Energy rebate amounts and program availability change** without notice — sometimes mid-quarter when funding tranches close. Treat any specific dollar figure as a snapshot. The frameworks (HOMES/HEAR program structure, ETO incentive categories, HP3 eligibility tiers) are stable; the dollars and the funding-availability flags are not.
- **Federal 25C and 25D credits are gone** for property placed in service after Dec 31, 2025. Do not let any older blog post, contractor sales pitch, or pre-2025 manuscript draft suggest otherwise.
- **OR-OSHA HazCom alignment with federal HazCom 2024** is unsettled as of May 2026 — OR-OSHA has signaled it will adopt after federal corrections are finalized. The 2012 baseline still applies; the 2024 changes apply to manufacturers' SDSs but Oregon employer training requirements remain at the 2012 level until OR-OSHA acts.

## When to bring in paid help

- **Construction attorney** if any planned alternate-method submission is novel or commercially sensitive (a BSH-branded methodology that becomes a statewide alternate method ruling has IP implications worth a one-hour consult).
- **Tax attorney or CPA** for the specific tax treatment of incentive-stacked equipment purchases (some states treat utility rebates as taxable; Oregon's treatment for owner-builders deserves a look).
- **Industrial hygienist** if the build envelope uses chemistries Jon hasn't worked with (especially closed-cell SPF or any two-component coating with isocyanate), to confirm the OR-OSHA respiratory-protection program meets the standard.
- **Licensed PE** for any alternate-material submission to a building official where a stamp materially helps the case.
