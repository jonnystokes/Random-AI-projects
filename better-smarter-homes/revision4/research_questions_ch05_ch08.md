# Soil, Ground Prep, and Foundation Risk — Chapter 5 Research

## DCP interpretation: best professional source

The honest answer: there isn't a single canonical source for **residential foundation screening**, because every authoritative DCP source is written for pavement applications and explicitly says so. ASTM D6951/D6951M-18 is the binding standard, but its scope statement (§1.1) limits it to "undisturbed soil or compacted materials" [Oregon Legislature](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/lawsstatutes/1999orLaw0035.html) measured for "in situ CBR (California Bearing Ratio)" [Oregon Legislature](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/lawsstatutes/1999orLaw0035.html) and notes "The DCP described in this test method is typically used for pavement applications." [Oregon Legislature](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/lawsstatutes/1999orLaw0035.html) ASTM D6951 is the standard you cite, but you can't pretend it was written for footing screening.

The strongest secondary references stack like this:

1. **MnDOT User Guide to the Dynamic Cone Penetrometer** (Burnham & Johnson, Mn/DOT Report 1993-05) — the most-cited DCP operations and interpretation reference in the US, available free from MnDOT's digital library. Uses the USACE/Webster correlation framework and includes the layer-detection methodology.
2. **Webster, Brown, & Williams (1994), USACE Waterways Experiment Station** — the foundational paper establishing the modern DCP-CBR equations that ASTM D6951 reprints in its annex. The CL clay (<10 CBR) and CH clay (<10 CBR) special equations come from this source.
3. **Kessler DCP Manual** — the most accessible field manual, ASTM-compliant, with full correlation tables for shear strength and bearing values; commercially produced but technically credible.
4. **Mejías-Santiago et al. (USACE ERDC reports)** — modern revisions of the Webster equations with statistical recalibration.

For a manuscript citing DCP for residential screening, lead with ASTM D6951 (the standard), MnDOT user guide (the operations reference), and Webster et al. 1994 (the equation source). Note explicitly in the text that all three are pavement-rooted and that the application to shallow residential footings is by extension, not by direct standard.

## DCP → bearing pressure: not responsibly

Three reasons this conversion shouldn't be treated as authoritative for owner-builder screening:

**(1) The chain is doubled-correlated.** DCP → CBR is well-established (R² typically 0.85-0.95 for granular soils, lower for clays). CBR → bearing pressure is *not* well-established. The Portland Cement Association equation (qall ≈ 1.5·CBR + 5, in tsf) is sometimes quoted but is a rule-of-thumb extension of pavement-design CBR into shallow-foundation territory, not a real bearing-capacity calculation. Compounding two empirical correlations turns a screening number into a guess with confidence intervals nobody publishes.

**(2) DCP isn't a bearing-capacity test.** Bearing capacity in residential design is governed by Terzaghi/Meyerhof equations using c, φ, and γ, not by penetration rate. The IBC and IRC explicitly require "the allowable bearing capacity shall be determined by a soils investigation" [Oregon Public Law](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_90.100) when in-place soils are suspected to be below 1,500-2,000 psf. The code framework was deliberately written to keep amateurs out of the bearing-pressure business.

**(3) Reproducibility is poor outside trained operators.** Field DCP results vary with rod friction, hammer alignment, moisture content, and sublayer interference. Webster's 1992 USACE paper noted CV of ~40% even under controlled conditions.

What DCP **can** responsibly do for owner-builder screening:

- **Detect layered subsurface conditions.** A change of slope on a penetration vs. depth plot identifies a strata boundary, often within ±1 inch (Webster 1992).
- **Identify suspect zones.** A penetration rate >50 mm/blow in the 0-3 ft depth range on natural ground = "this site needs a real geotech investigation."
- **Verify compaction quality on a material-specific basis.** Once you know the target Proctor density and have driven a calibration set on known-good material, DCP becomes a reasonable QA/QC tool for compacted fill.
- **Provide a relative comparison across a footprint.** If five test locations on a building pad give wildly different penetration rates, the site has variability that prescriptive footings won't tolerate.

What DCP cannot responsibly do for SFR screening: produce a number you plug into footing sizing.

## Recommendation for Chapter 5: keep DCP values as soft screening

The values in Revision 3 should remain framed as **screening calibration**, not design guidance. Concrete recommendation: present three penetration-rate bands ("competent for prescriptive footings — investigate further — do not build without engineered foundation"), explicitly note the bands are screening only, and direct any reader who lands in the middle band to a licensed PE or geotechnical investigation. Don't publish DCP-to-bearing-pressure conversion tables. The IBC/IRC framework presumes you'll use Table R401.4.1 presumptive values + visual classification + lab confirmation when needed; DCP can pre-screen for "is the site even suitable" and post-verify "did the fill compact" but shouldn't produce a number that displaces those code paths.

## Pocket penetrometer to allowable bearing pressure (cohesive soils only)

This relationship *is* well-established and can be presented as hard guidance, with caveats. The pocket penetrometer reads **unconfined compressive strength (qu)** directly, typically in tsf or kg/cm². For saturated cohesive soils the chain is:

1. **qu** = unconfined compressive strength (PP reading)
2. **su** = undrained shear strength = qu/2 (definition for saturated clay)
3. **qult** = ultimate bearing capacity ≈ 5.14·su = 2.57·qu (Skempton's bearing factor for strip footings on saturated clay, φ=0)
4. **qall** = qult / FS, with FS = 3 (Bowles, Coduto, Das all converge here)

So: **qall ≈ 0.86·qu** for a strip footing on saturated clay with FS=3. Round to **qall ≈ qu** (in consistent units) as a conservative field rule.

A simpler and very widely used field rule cited in foundation engineering texts: **qall(psf) ≈ 1500 × PP reading (tsf)** — gives 1,500 psf at qu=1.0 tsf, 3,000 psf at qu=2.0 tsf, etc. This corresponds approximately to FS=3 with rounding for square vs. strip footings.

Stiff/firm/very-stiff calibration from PP readings (cohesive soil only):

- Soft clay: qu < 0.25 tsf (500 psf) → qall < 500 psf, do not place footings
- Medium clay: qu = 0.25-0.5 tsf → qall ≈ 500-750 psf
- Stiff clay: qu = 0.5-1.0 tsf → qall ≈ 750-1,500 psf
- Very stiff clay: qu = 1.0-2.0 tsf → qall ≈ 1,500-3,000 psf
- Hard clay: qu > 2.0 tsf → qall > 3,000 psf, but code requires engineering evaluation above 2,000 psf

**Critical caveat for the manuscript:** PP works ONLY on cohesive (saturated, fine-grained) soils. Granular soils, partially saturated soils, gravelly soils, and any soil where the cone reads against grain-size effects rather than cohesion — PP gives nonsense. Manuscript should explicitly say: PP is valid only when the soil ribbons, holds shape, and shows cohesion under hand pressure. For sands/gravels, use density and grain-size methods, not PP.

## DCP → Proctor compaction percentage

Direct correlation does not exist. MnDOT's research is explicit on this: "While MnDOT was unable to correlate DCP penetration index to in situ density, a procedure was developed in which a minimum penetration rate from DCP measurements was established for acceptance of compaction of individual materials. This procedure is based upon a material- specific relationship between in situ density and a desired level of compaction, such as 95 percent of modified proctor." [Oregon](https://www.oregon.gov/BCD/epermitting/Documents/aca/homeowner-acknowledgement.pdf)

Operational meaning for the manuscript:

- **No universal DCP → % Proctor table.** A penetration rate that means "95% Proctor" for a well-graded sandy gravel is a different rate than "95% Proctor" for a silty clay.
- **The Gabr et al. (NCSU) method** lets you build a soil-specific calibration: laboratory Proctor + Atterberg limits + DCP on lab specimens at known densities and moistures → a calibration curve for that specific material. This requires lab work owner-builders won't do.
- **Practical owner-builder approach:** if you're placing engineered fill, hire one day of geotech testing at the start to establish target density and a DCP penetration-rate target for *that specific material*. Then use the pocket DCP for daily verification. This is how DOTs use DCP in the field: not as a density meter, but as a calibrated material-specific QA tool.
- **Without that calibration**, DCP on fill is useful only for relative comparison ("this lift is denser than the previous lift") and gross identification ("this fill is loose enough to fail a presumptive bearing assumption").

## Ribbon test as PI predictor

The ribbon test predicts soil **texture** (sand/silt/clay proportions), not plasticity index directly. The USDA NRCS field guide assigns ribbon length to USDA texture class:

- Less than 1": sandy or silt loam (low clay)
- 1-2": loam or clay loam (moderate clay)
- Greater than 2": clay, silty clay, or sandy clay (high clay)

The relationship to PI is indirect:

- Ribbon length tracks clay content, which tracks PI but with significant variability based on clay mineralogy (kaolinite vs. illite vs. montmorillonite produces wildly different PIs at the same clay percentage)
- A 2"+ ribbon is *consistent with* PI > 20 in most natural soils, but the ribbon test cannot distinguish PI=25 (medium expansion) from PI=45 (high expansion) — both ribbon similarly
- For PI estimation, the **USCS toughness/thread test (ASTM D2488)** is closer: roll soil into a 3mm thread and assess toughness. Tough threads at PL → higher PI.

**Manuscript guidance:** Keep ribbon test as a **warning sign**, not a PI predictor. Specifically:

- Ribbon < 1": probably non-plastic to low-PI; prescriptive footings likely OK from a plasticity standpoint
- Ribbon 1-2": moderate clay; consider PI testing if foundation is critical or if other warning signs exist
- Ribbon > 2": **stop and get Atterberg limits done.** The lab cost is ~$50-150 per sample and replaces guesswork with a definitive number.

For BSH's 1000-year structural goal, any 2"+ ribbon should automatically trigger lab Atterberg testing. The asymmetric cost — cheap test now vs. catastrophic shrink-swell damage over decades — makes the decision trivial.

## PI thresholds for expansive-clay mitigation discussion (Oregon SFR)

Oregon doesn't publish state-specific thresholds; the framework defaults to the IBC/IRC, with Holtz & Gibbs (1956) being the most-cited classification system. Synthesizing:

**Holtz & Gibbs (1956) — the classical source:**
- PI 0-15: low swell potential
- PI 10-35: medium swell potential
- PI 20-55: high swell potential
- PI > 35: very high swell potential

**IBC §1803.5.3 expansive-soil trigger** (a soil is "expansive" if all of the following are met, or if EI alone > 20):
- PI ≥ 15
- ≥10% passing #200 sieve
- ≥10% finer than 5 µm
- Expansion Index > 20 (ASTM D4829)

**Practical Oregon SFR thresholds for the manuscript:**

- **PI < 15:** treat as non-expansive for design purposes; prescriptive foundations OK
- **PI 15-20:** screening-level concern; specify drainage, moisture barriers, no irrigation within 5 ft of foundation; consider thickened-edge slabs or perimeter footing with rebar continuity
- **PI 20-25:** mandatory mitigation discussion in the design; engineered foundation likely needed (deeper footings below active zone, drainage envelope, isolation joints, post-tension consideration)
- **PI 25-35:** engineered foundation required; consider over-excavate-and-replace, lime stabilization, or pier foundations bypassing the active zone
- **PI > 35:** active-mitigation territory; deep piers to non-expansive stratum, structural slab on void forms, or full engineered approach with site-specific design

For a 1000-year design goal in an Oregon Willamette Valley context where smectite-rich silty clays are common, **PI 15 should be the discussion trigger and PI 20 should be the engineered-foundation trigger** — more conservative than the IBC default because the design lifetime amplifies cyclic stress damage. The manuscript can defensibly state: "for BSH-spec homes, any PI ≥ 15 means the foundation type is no longer prescriptive."

## Oregon engineered fill documentation for owner-builder

Oregon's framework defaults the question to local building officials. The structural authority chain:

- **ORSC R401.4** triggers a soil test when expansive, compressible, or shifting soils are present, with documentation per **ORS 455.440** (recordation of foundation conditions)
- **ORSC R301.1.3** allows engineered design where prescriptive code is exceeded; in practice this means a PE-stamped fill specification when fill exceeds prescriptive thresholds
- **ORSC R401.4.2** addresses compressible/shifting soils: "when top or subsoils are compressible or shifting, they shall be removed to a depth and width sufficient to assure stable moisture content in each active zone and shall not be used as fill or stabilized within each active zone by chemical, dewatering or" [Oregon Public Law](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_18.395) other means

Practical observed practice across Oregon jurisdictions:

- **Small fills (<12" under footings):** typically accepted with visual observation, no formal documentation
- **Moderate fills (12-24"):** most jurisdictions require ASTM D698 (Standard Proctor) or D1557 (Modified Proctor) test results plus density verification at 1-foot lift intervals
- **Larger fills or critical fills:** PE-stamped fill specification, daily inspection logs by a qualified soils technician, density testing at specified frequencies (typically 1 test per 500-2,500 sf per lift), and final PE letter certifying compliance with the specification
- **Building-official discretion:** ORS 455.058 gives the building official authority to require additional documentation when soil conditions are suspect

For the owner-builder manuscript, the safest practical recommendation: **commission a one-day geotech consult before any structural fill is placed.** Cost is typically $800-2,500 for a residential site, deliverable is a fill spec with density targets, lift thicknesses, moisture conditioning protocol, and a testing frequency. The owner-builder then performs the construction and a separate testing agency (or the same geotech) does the in-place density verification. This gives the building official everything they need and protects the 1000-year goal.

The owner-builder cannot self-certify engineered fill. The building official will not accept "I read a manual and compacted it well" — the documentation has to come from a qualified third party, typically a registered design professional or AASHTO-certified soils technician.

## Oregon-specific residential geotech screening guidance

Direct answer: **none exists** that's targeted at owner-builder residential screening. The state's published residential framework (ORSC + BCD program guides) defaults to the IRC/IBC presumptive-value table and assumes any tests are done by professionals.

What does exist:

- **DOGAMI** publishes regional geological hazard maps (landslide susceptibility, liquefaction, seismic, tsunami) that are useful for site selection but not for foundation screening
- **OSU/SEAO snow load lookup** at snowload.seao.org/lookup.html (referenced in 2021 ORSC) — closest example of state-published technical tools but for snow not soils
- **Oregon DEQ** publishes percolation and septic-testing guidance (OAR 340-071) that incidentally documents soil texture and drainage — useful as a free secondary data point about your site's soil class
- **NRCS Web Soil Survey** (websoilsurvey.nrcs.usda.gov) — free, gives you the USDA soil series, expected texture, drainage class, and engineering interpretations for your specific parcel; the most useful free resource for Oregon owner-builders
- **County GIS portals** (Lane, Marion, Washington, Multnomah, etc.) often have soil overlays integrated into property mapping

This is a legitimate gap that BSH content can fill educationally — explaining how to use NRCS Web Soil Survey + county GIS + visual classification + ribbon test + PP + DCP as a layered screening approach. Stay on the educational side of the line; don't publish prescriptive numbers that owner-builders will use as design values.

## Chapter 5 vs Chapter 7: where does the matrix go

Recommendation: **two matrices, both anchored to topic, cross-referenced**.

**Chapter 5 holds the soil screening matrix.** Test → finding → next-action decision. This is the "what do I do with the result?" matrix. Sample structure:

| Field test | Result band | Interpretation | Next action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ribbon | <1" | Low clay | Continue with prescriptive; verify drainage |
| Ribbon | 1-2" | Moderate clay | PP test + consider Atterberg |
| Ribbon | >2" | High clay | Atterberg required; engineered foundation likely |
| PP (cohesive) | <0.5 tsf | Soft clay | Stop; redesign or excavate |
| PP (cohesive) | 0.5-1.0 tsf | Stiff | Acceptable for ~1,000-1,500 psf |
| PP (cohesive) | 1.0-2.0 tsf | Very stiff | Acceptable for prescriptive; verify with PE if approaching 2,000 psf |
| DCP | >50 mm/blow | Loose | Investigate further |
| DCP | 10-50 mm/blow | Variable | Material-dependent; layer detection only |
| DCP | <10 mm/blow | Dense | Confirms competent layer for footing |

**Chapter 7 holds the foundation type matrix.** Soil class + site condition → foundation options + trade-offs + when to escalate to engineered. This is the "given my soil, what foundation do I build?" matrix. It references Chapter 5 for the soil-class inputs but doesn't reproduce the screening logic.

Why split them: Chapter 5 readers need to know what to do with their tests. Chapter 7 readers need to know what foundation to build. A reader who's already past site selection shouldn't have to re-read screening logic. A reader doing site evaluation shouldn't have to read foundation design before they know whether they should buy the lot. Cross-references handle the seam.

The single anchor sentence for the chapter pair: **soil screening ends with a soil class; foundation design begins with that soil class — they are sequential decisions, not one combined chart, and they belong in their own chapters with explicit links between them.**

# Chapter 6: Structural System And Concrete Design

## Oregon's residential path for reinforced concrete homes

ORSC presents three layered paths for a concrete or concrete-and-masonry home, and only one of them stays prescriptive for most Oregon sites:

**(1) ORSC R608 prescriptive above-grade concrete walls.** Available only for "Detached one- and two-family dwellings in Seismic Design Category C with exterior above-grade concrete walls" [Portland](https://www.portland.gov/bds/residential-permitting/residential-electrical-permits/residential-electrical-permits-lights) . SDC C in Oregon is essentially the eastern half of the state plus narrow strips around inland valleys; the entire Willamette Valley, the Coast, and the I-5 corridor are SDC D0/D1/D2 — meaning **the R608 prescriptive path is unavailable for most BSH projects**.

**(2) Referenced-standard prescriptive paths for everything else.** For SDC D0/D1/D2 buildings with above-grade concrete walls, ORSC R301.2.2.5 (via R301.2.2.5 reference to PCA 100 / ACI 318 / ACI 332) requires: "Buildings with exterior above-grade concrete walls shall comply with PCA 100 or shall be designed in accordance with ACI 318." [Oregon Realtors](https://oregonrealtors.org/sites/default/files/Handbook%20(6-19-15).pdf) ACI 332-20 covers the same scope as a residential-specific code and is referenced by IRC for one- and two-family dwellings: "design and construction of cast-in-place concrete for one- and two-family dwellings and multiple single-family dwellings (townhouses), and their accessory structures." [UpCodes](https://up.codes/viewer/oregon/imc-2018/chapter/3/general-regulations)

**(3) Engineered design under R301.1.3.** This is where everything that exceeds prescriptive limits goes: "Where a building of otherwise conventional construction contains structural elements exceeding the limits of Section R301 or otherwise not conforming to this code, these elements shall be designed in accordance with accepted engineering practice." [Justia](https://regulations.justia.com/states/oregon/chapter-812/division-12/section-812-012-0140/) The 2023 ORSC reframes this slightly as "nonprescriptive design" but the function is identical.

**For a BSH-spec concrete-and-CMU home in the Willamette Valley:** the path is almost certainly engineered (R301.1.3) for the lateral system and PCA 100 / ACI 318 / ACI 332 for the gravity concrete. Treat the prescriptive path as a sanity check for member sizes, not a design path you can permit a real concrete house under.

## Concrete design standards to cite in Chapter 6 Code blocks

For reader-facing Code blocks the manuscript should standardize on this hierarchy and cite all four where applicable:

- **ACI 318-19 or 318-25** (current as of plan submission): Building Code Requirements for Structural Concrete — the primary structural concrete design standard. Cite for: cast-in-place walls, slabs, beams, columns, footings, retaining walls, foundation walls, anchorage, development length, lap splice, cover, exposure classes.
- **ACI 332-20**: Code Requirements for Residential Concrete — the residential-specific code with prescriptive tables. Cite for: residential footings, foundation walls, above-grade walls in scope, slabs-on-ground, expansive-soil residential design (Chapter 12).
- **PCA 100-12**: Prescriptive Design of Exterior Concrete Walls for One- and Two-Family Dwellings — explicitly referenced by IRC for SDC D0/D1/D2 above-grade walls. Cite for: above-grade exterior concrete walls when using the prescriptive PCA option.
- **ORSC R403, R404, R608, R506**: residential code sections for footings, foundation walls, above-grade walls, slabs-on-ground.
- **ORSC R301.1.3**: the engineered-design fallback path.
- **ASTM A615/A615M**: deformed reinforcing bars (Grade 60 typical).
- **ASTM A706**: low-alloy reinforcing bars (better weldability, ductility — preferred for seismic).
- **ASTM C150**: Portland cement.
- **ASTM C94**: ready-mixed concrete.
- **ASTM C260, C494**: air-entraining and chemical admixtures.

For retaining walls and basement walls specifically: ACI 318 Chapter 11 (walls) and Chapter 13 (foundations). ORSC notes "Retaining walls that are not laterally supported at the top and that retain in excess of 48 inches of unbalanced fill or retaining walls exceeding 24 inches in height that resist lateral loads in addition to soil shall be designed in accordance with accepted engineering practice" [UpCodes](https://up.codes/viewer/oregon/ifc-2012/chapter/9/fire-protection-systems) with a 1.5 minimum factor of safety against sliding and overturning.

The Code block format that works: state the controlling section number, the standard, and the deliverable. Example: *"Foundation wall design (Type V residential): ORSC R404 prescriptive tables OR ACI 332-20 Chapter 9, with reinforcement per ASTM A615 Grade 60 and concrete per ACI 318 Exposure Category F1/F2 minimum, f'c ≥ 3000 psi."*

## CMU double-wythe cavity wall classification

This is the architecturally interesting question. Under TMS 402-22, the answer depends on how the wythes interact:

**Composite wall (TMS 402 §5.1.4):** wythes bonded by a filled (mortared or grouted) collar joint and either masonry headers or metal ties. Wythes act as a single element. Allowable shear stress at collar joint: "5 psi (34.5 kPa) for mortared collar joints, b) 10 psi (69.0 kPa) for grouted collar joints" [Klamath County](https://www.klamathcounty.org/1019/Liquefied-Petroleum-Gas-LPG) . Collar joint must be filled. No insulation in the collar joint.

**Noncomposite wall / cavity wall (TMS 402 §5.1.4):** "imposed vertical loads are carried by the wythe closest to the center of span of the supported member; bending moments are distributed to each wythe in proportion to its relative stiffness; and loads acting parallel to the plane of the wall (shear loads) are carried only by the wythe affected. In addition, the cavity width is limited to 4 ½ in. (114 mm) unless a detailed wall tie analysis is performed." [Oregon Public Law](https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_812-002-0275) The 2016 TMS 402 expanded the prescriptive cavity to 6-5/8" with a 2"-or-less air space and 3/16" wire ties.

**Veneer (TMS 402 Chapter 12):** outer wythe is non-loadbearing, attached to a structural backup wythe. Backup carries all loads.

**For the manuscript's proposed double-wythe assembly:** the question is whether both wythes are loadbearing. If the inner CMU wythe is the structure and the outer wythe is non-loadbearing facing — it's veneer (Chapter 12 governs). If both wythes share gravity and lateral loads — it's composite or noncomposite per Chapter 5. **There is no IRC/IBC prescriptive path for two-wythe loadbearing CMU; this is engineered design territory by default**, and the engineer-of-record must classify it for purposes of permit submittal.

The cleanest and most defensible classification for BSH:

- **Hybrid engineered wall assembly** = inner reinforced CMU wythe carries gravity and lateral loads; outer wythe is loadbearing for its own self-weight only and provides thermal mass / weather protection / aesthetics. Designed under TMS 402 as a noncomposite (cavity) wall, with cavity used for continuous insulation. Plan submittal categorizes it as "reinforced masonry, noncomposite cavity wall, engineered design per ORSC R301.1.3 and TMS 402."

This is the classification a structural engineer will recognize, the building official will accept, and the insurance underwriter won't reject. Avoid calling it a "cavity wall" alone (ambiguous), "reinforced masonry" alone (suggests composite), or "veneer" (legally narrows what the outer wythe can do).

## Wall-tie requirements for the double-wythe

The TMS 402-22 provisions for noncomposite wall ties:

**Spacing (prescriptive cavity ≤ 4-1/2", or up to 6-5/8" with 2" airspace):**
- Adjustable ties: "Maximum horizontal tie spacing: 16 in. (406 mm) for adjustable ties 36 in. (914 mm) for all other ties" [Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/oregon/OAR-812-002-0660) with vertical max 24" for adjustable, 36" for non-adjustable
- Frequency: minimum 1 tie per 4.5 ft² of wall (W2.8 / 3/16" wire) or 1 per 2.67 ft² for W1.7
- One adjustable tie per 1.77 ft² of wall when adjustable type used

**Wire size:**
- Minimum W1.7 (9 gauge) for cavities ≤ 4-5/8"
- Minimum W2.8 (3/16" / MW18) for cavities > 4-5/8" up to 6-5/8"
- For BSH 1000-year + thermal mass: spec W2.8 minimum, 3/16" minimum diameter

**Material:**
- ASTM A82 cold-drawn wire (carbon steel)
- ASTM A580 or A240 stainless (Type 304 or 316)
- ASTM A951 joint reinforcement
- For 1000-year design: stainless steel Type 316 is the durability play. Type 304 is acceptable in non-coastal Oregon; Type 316 is the upgrade for coastal salt or any concern about long-term chloride exposure.

**Drip:** wire ties should NOT have a drip bend except where used as moisture-management devices, because "Such a bend in the tie reduces the capacity of the tie to transfer lateral load." [Woodburn-or](https://www.woodburn-or.gov/faqs?keys=&field_tags_tid=621) Use separate flashing/weep system rather than tie-integrated drips.

**Galvanic isolation:** "if in contact with carbon steel, a galvanic cell can result and actually increase the potential for corrosion. For this reason, combining stainless steel ties or screws with carbon steel or galvanized steel components is not recommended." [Woodburn-or](https://www.woodburn-or.gov/faqs?keys=&field_tags_tid=621) Spec all-stainless if any stainless is used.

**Thermal bridging:** stainless ties at the spec frequency (one per 2.7 ft² to one per 4.5 ft²) create roughly 0.5-1% point thermal bridge, dropping a 4" rigid foam cavity from R-26 to about R-23 effective. Acceptable for most envelopes. If pursuing aggressive R-values, specify thermally-broken ties (e.g., Halfen FM, Bekaert Murfor THERM, Wagner Wall-Tie ThermaTIE) which incorporate stainless or carbon-steel ends with a polymer or basalt thermal break in the cavity.

**Manuscript language for the Code block:** "Wall ties: ASTM A580 Type 304 or 316 stainless steel, minimum W2.8 (3/16" diameter), spaced at maximum 16" horizontal × 24" vertical for adjustable ties or 36" × 36" for non-adjustable, providing minimum one tie per 4.5 ft² of wall area. Cavity width: 2" airspace minimum. Per TMS 402-22 Chapter 5."

## FRP wall ties: not the default

The state of FRP for masonry ties:

- **AC125** (Acceptance Criteria 125) covers externally bonded FRP for *strengthening* concrete and masonry. Not applicable to wall ties for new construction.
- **AC454** covers FRP rebar in concrete. Limited to SDC A or B (not Oregon's SDC D territory), and applies to internal flexural reinforcement, not wythe-to-wythe ties.
- **TMS 402-22 Appendix D** introduces composite reinforcement (GFRP) for masonry partition walls — explicitly partition, not loadbearing exterior cavity walls.
- **Commercial FRP ties exist** (Wagner ThermalGrip, Halfen-Moltec FA, Pinnacle KOOL-PIN, Crossfield Products PIN-X) but typically gain code acceptance through ICC ESR (Evaluation Service Report) under AC04 or AC11, which is project-specific approval — the engineer must submit the ESR with the plans and the building official decides.

**Recommendation for the manuscript:** specify stainless steel ties (304 or 316, W2.8 minimum) as the default for BSH projects. Treat FRP ties as an upgrade option requiring ICC ESR submittal and engineer-of-record approval. Document in Chapter 6 that FRP is a viable thermal-bridge solution but is **not** prescriptive code and requires per-project approval. Don't put FRP ties in a "Code block" — put them in a "design alternatives with engineering review" sidebar.

## Reinforced masonry values: bar size, spacing, lap, grout, bond beam

For reinforced CMU under ORSC R606 + TMS 402:

**Bar size:**
- Maximum #11 (M#36) under TMS 402 allowable-stress and strength design
- IRC prescriptive R606 limits typically use #4 or #5 bars
- **#5 bars (5/8" diameter) are the practical sweet spot for residential reinforced CMU walls**: large enough to develop meaningful capacity in 8" CMU, small enough to fit in standard cells with grout, code-prescribed clearances satisfiable

**Vertical reinforcement spacing:**
- Prescriptive R404.1.4 SDC D0/D1/D2 foundation: minimum #4 at ≤ 4'-0" o.c.
- Engineered residential walls: typically #5 at 24"-48" o.c. depending on wall height, loads, and SDC
- Walls in SDC D2 typically need #5 at 16"-32" o.c. with full grouting

**Lap length:**
- TMS 402 §6.1.6: lap length depends on f'm, bar size, cover, spacing
- For #5 bar at f'm = 2,000 psi typical residential CMU: lap ≈ 40db = 25" minimum, often 48db = 30" for confidence
- **Manuscript recommendation: specify 48db (30" for #5) lap length as a single number** — covers conservative cases and avoids designer-by-designer variability
- Bars must be tied to maintain position during grouting

**Grout lifts:**
- ORSC R606.3.5.1 / TMS 602: "Grout shall be poured in lifts with a maximum height of 8 feet (2438 mm). Where a total grout pour exceeds 8 feet (2438 mm) in height, the grout shall be placed in lifts not exceeding 64 inches (1626 mm) and special inspection during grouting shall be required." [Building Codes Division](https://www.oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/Documents/interp-01-01-resfiresprinklers.pdf)
- Cleanouts required at base of walls when grout pour exceeds 5'-0"
- Grout spec: ASTM C476, fine or coarse, with 8" min slump (10-11" preferred for self-consolidating fine grout)
- Min grout strength: 2,000 psi at 28 days
- **Manuscript recommendation: specify max 5'-0" lifts** for owner-builder workmanship — falls below the cleanout threshold and the special-inspection threshold, eliminates two compliance traps

**Bond beam strategy:**
- Top of every wall: required (anchor for floor/roof diaphragm)
- At each floor level: typical and good practice
- Vertical spacing for crack control: "Bond beams were provided every 8 vertical feet" [Alternative Fuels Data Center](https://afdc.energy.gov/laws/12051) is a defensible interval; tighter spacing (4'-0") is the conservative choice for 1000-year design
- Reinforcement: minimum 2-#5 horizontal continuous through bond beam course
- Bond beam units required (knock-out web type or standard with cut webs)
- **Manuscript recommendation:** bond beam at top of wall, bottom of wall (or footing turn), each floor level, and one intermediate course in walls > 12 ft tall. 2-#5 continuous in each.

**Cover:**
- 2" exposed to weather or earth
- 1.5" not exposed
- 3" cast against earth
- Joint reinforcement: 5/8" cover when exposed to earth/weather, 1/2" otherwise

**Mortar:**
- Type M or S Portland cement-lime per ORSC R606.2.8.3 for lateral-force-resisting walls in SDC D0/D1/D2
- Type N is not permitted for the lateral system in SDC D0+

## Floor system path

Five viable options for a concrete-house BSH design, ranked by alignment with the 1000-year + concrete philosophy:

**(1) Cast-in-place reinforced concrete one-way slab on bond beams or steel beams.** 6"-10" thick depending on span, #4 or #5 reinforcement in two directions per ACI 318 minimums, full continuity into walls. Most consistent with concrete philosophy. Highest mass (~70-100 psf), best fire/sound, longest design life. Highest installation cost, requires significant formwork. PE-designed.

**(2) Hollow-core precast concrete planks with 2"-3" CIP topping.** 6"-12" plank depth depending on span (typically 8" plank for 25-30' spans). Industry standard for concrete homes; manufacturers include Spancrete, Kerkstra, Oldcastle, Coreslab. CIP topping provides diaphragm continuity and integration with bond beams. Faster than CIP, comparable longevity. Recommended primary path for BSH given balance of constructibility and durability.

**(3) Composite concrete on metal deck on steel beams or bar joists.** 3.5"-6" topping over 1.5"-3" composite deck, on W-section beams or LH/SLH bar joists. Structural steel introduces a non-concrete element but works well for long spans and for accommodating mechanical chases. Acceptable hybrid; less philosophically pure but very practical for BSH-class spans. 50-75 year structural design life as drawn; longer with maintenance.

**(4) Concrete on precast joists (e.g., Filigree, double-tee).** Less common in residential, more common in mid-rise. Mention but don't recommend as primary.

**(5) Wood-framed I-joist or LVL with concrete topping.** Inconsistent with the BSH concrete philosophy. Mention only as a "if you must" option.

**Recommendation for the manuscript:** lead with hollow-core precast as the BSH default for spans 16'-32', with CIP one-way slab as the alternative for shorter spans (≤16') where formwork is justified by the simplicity of a single material. Both are PE-designed and require shop drawings.

Span-to-depth ratios for sizing: CIP one-way slab ~ L/24 for typical residential loads; hollow core ~ L/40 for typical residential loads. So 24' span → 12" CIP slab or 8" hollow core.

## Fiber reinforcement: how to describe without overpromising

The boundary is sharp. ACI 544.4R-18 states the rule: "Fibers can be used, in general, to supplement and reduce the reinforcing bars in various structural members. In applications where the presence of continuous reinforcement is not essential to the safety and integrity of the structure such as slabs-on-ground, pavements, overlays, shotcrete linings, slabs-on-piles (ACI 544.6R), and some precast units, fibers may be used as the sole means of reinforcement." [Malheurco](https://www.malheurco.org/environmental-health/onsite-wastewater-treatment-program-oregon-deq/) ACI 318 has admitted steel fibers as shear reinforcement in specific contexts, but **fibers do not replace primary flexural reinforcement in residential structural members** under code.

Defensible manuscript language:

- ✅ "Fiber-reinforced concrete used to control plastic shrinkage cracking, improve impact and abrasion resistance, and supplement temperature-and-shrinkage reinforcement."
- ✅ "Macro-synthetic or steel fibers added to slabs-on-ground at 3-7 lb/yd³ for crack control; primary structural reinforcement (#4 at 16" o.c. each way for typical residential slab) per the engineer of record."
- ✅ "Steel fiber dosage of 25-50 lb/yd³ may be used as designed shear reinforcement per ACI 318 §11.4.6.1 in elements where designed by a registered engineer."
- ❌ "Fibers replace rebar."
- ❌ "No reinforcing required when fibers are used."
- ❌ "FRC eliminates need for primary reinforcement."

The exception worth flagging: industrial slab-on-ground design routinely uses FRC as primary reinforcement under TR 34 (UK) and ACI 360R guidance. For an *interior* slab-on-ground in a residential garage or service space, FRC at 4-7 lb/yd³ macro-synthetic with no rebar can be defended IF the engineer signs off and the slab is not part of the structural system.

## Service-layer story-height and habitable-space classification

ORSC defines a "story" (R202) as the portion of a building between the upper surface of a floor and the upper surface of the floor or roof above. The legal effects of the service layer depend entirely on what's inside that height.

**Three working classifications:**

**(a) Habitable space (R202).** Used for living, sleeping, eating, cooking. Triggers full ceiling-height (R305: 7'-0" min in habitable rooms, 7'-0" min in bathrooms/halls/kitchens, 6'-8" in basements without habitable space), light/ventilation (R303), egress (R310, R311), heating (R303.10) requirements. **Do not classify a service layer as habitable.**

**(b) Crawl space (R408).** Below the lowest occupied floor, height typically < 4'-0" with limited access. Requires ventilation OR conditioned-crawl provisions (R408.3), 18" × 24" min access opening, vapor retarder over earth, no heating required. **Service layer below the main floor is unambiguously a crawl space if accessible only for utilities.**

**(c) Concealed space / plenum / interstitial space (R302.11 fireblocking).** Not occupied, not regularly accessed except for inspection or maintenance. Fireblocking required at floor/wall intersections; if used as a return-air plenum, IMC restrictions on combustible materials apply. **A service layer between two habitable floors that's not large enough to access is a concealed space, NOT a story.**

**The Oregon plan-review issue:** if the service layer is large enough that a person could enter it (typically > 4'-0" height OR > 30" × 36" cross-section access), some building officials will classify it as either a mezzanine, an attic, or a story — depending on geometry. Most conservative: treat any space > 30" tall and accessible as a "mechanical space" requiring a permit-shown access door, and either:

1. Keep service layer at < 30" height and access only through removable panels (concealed space, no story implications)
2. Make it a full conditioned crawl space-style mechanical level with ventilation, lighting, and a permanent ladder or stair (counts as a story)
3. Build a true mezzanine ≤ 1/3 floor area of the room below per R325 (not a story, but limited area)

**Manuscript recommendation:** keep the service layer ≤ 30" clear height for a "concealed plenum" classification, OR design it as a full conditioned mechanical level (and accept the story-count implications). The middle ground — 30"-72" — is the plan-review trap that gets argued case-by-case and slows permits.

A second consideration: ORSC R302.11 fireblocking and R302.12 draftstopping requirements apply to concealed spaces in floor/ceiling assemblies. Service layer floors and walls bordering the service layer typically need 1-hour fire separation if the layer is open to multiple "stories" of habitable space.

## Roof family selection for the BSH default

For the 1000-year + concrete + reliability + simplicity goals, the roof is the single highest-risk durability item if specified wrong. Three viable families:

**(1) Concrete deck + protected membrane roof (PMR).** Concrete (8" CIP slab or hollow-core with 2-3" topping) at minimum 1/4"/ft slope, topped with 2-component fully adhered membrane (modified bitumen, EPDM, TPO, or PVC), then 4"-6" XPS or polyiso insulation, then ballast (river rock or concrete pavers). Membrane life 30-50 years; replaceable without disturbing structure. Concrete deck life 100+. **Best alignment with BSH philosophy. Best long-term economics.** Heaviest, requires structural design for ponding and ballast loads.

**(2) Concrete deck + sloped wood-framed false roof.** Concrete deck as above, topped with treated 2x sleeper system supporting a sloped framing (3:12-6:12) with metal or slate roofing. Decouples weather barrier from structure. Higher cost, more maintenance, but matches some aesthetic preferences. Metal panel (24-gauge minimum standing seam, 50-70 year design life) or natural slate (75-150 year life) are the durability picks.

**(3) Sloped wood-framed truss roof on top of CMU/concrete walls.** Standard in Oregon residential. 2x truss assembly at 24" o.c., engineered, on bond beam top of wall. Roofing typically asphalt shingle (15-30 year life) or metal (50-70 year). Most familiar to local trades and inspectors. Least matched to 1000-year goal, but the most economical and easiest to permit.

**Recommendation for BSH default:** concrete deck + PMR for the primary roof family, with concrete deck + standing-seam metal as the alternate where slope is required for visual or snow-shed reasons. Reject asphalt shingles entirely (15-30 year design life is incompatible with 1000-year framing). Permit the concrete-deck + truss/wood-framed sloped option as the "transition" choice for projects where local construction culture or budget can't accept a flat concrete deck.

Drainage spec for the manuscript: minimum 1/4"/ft slope, two roof drains per primary drainage area minimum (per IPC 1106), overflow/scupper sized for 100-year storm + drain failure, ASCE 7 ponding instability check on concrete deck, snow load per SEAO snow lookup tool with 25 psf minimum.

Solar integration: concrete deck makes future PV addition trivial — embed cast-in anchors or post-tensioned attachment plates at design time. Avoid through-roof penetrations if possible; ballasted PV racks on PMR-protected concrete deck is the cleanest 1000-year-friendly approach.

## Stair-opening reinforcement and diaphragm continuity

The structural rule: an opening interrupts the continuous flow of in-plane diaphragm forces, and the trim reinforcement around the opening must restore that flow.

**For CIP concrete slab diaphragms (ACI 318 §7.7 and §11.7):**

- Trim bars: replace the bars interrupted by the opening, distributed half above and half below the opening (at slab edges)
- Add diagonal bars at corners: 2-#5 at each corner of opening, 4'-0" lap into uninterrupted slab
- Boundary reinforcement: continuous chord steel along edge of opening; #5 minimum, lap 40db
- Minimum reinforcement ratio: per ACI 318 §11.6 (0.0012 for typical slab thickness)

**For reinforced CMU diaphragm walls and bond-beam continuity (TMS 402 §5.1.1):**
- Bond beams interrupted by openings need supplementary reinforcement with development length on each side of the opening
- Minimum 2-#5 in bond beam, lapped 48db on each side of opening
- Continuous bars are required at top and bottom of all openings; bars must extend 24" minimum past the opening edge

**For the manuscript Code block:**
"Stair openings and other floor diaphragm penetrations: trim with #5 bars equal in number to bars interrupted, half above/half below opening edge; provide 2-#5 diagonal bars at each opening corner with 40db lap into continuous diaphragm; chord reinforcement of 2-#5 continuous along opening edge with 48db lap into continuous diaphragm. Maximum opening dimension: 50% of diaphragm width without engineered analysis (R301.2.2.6 irregularity threshold)."

**Diaphragm-continuity gotcha to flag:** ORSC R301.2.2.6 lists openings in floor diaphragms exceeding certain dimensions as "irregularity" conditions that trigger engineered design for the entire story, not just the opening. For BSH-spec homes, any stair opening with the long dimension > 12'-0" or > 50% of diaphragm width should be designed by the structural engineer with full diaphragm collector and chord reinforcement — not by reference to a prescriptive trim detail.

- Any structural element using FRP reinforcement (per ICC ESR submittal)
- Structural concrete floor diaphragms (CIP or hollow-core with topping)
- Concrete or masonry roofs, regardless of slope
- Buildings classified as irregular under ORSC R301.2.2.6

**Almost certainly requires PE:**
- Sites with PI > 15 expansive soil (engineered foundation per ACI 332 Chapter 12 or equivalent)
- Sites in mapped landslide hazard, liquefaction, or unstable slope
- Story height > 11'-7"
- Ground snow load > 70 psf
- Wall heights > 10'-0" for any prescriptive type
- Any post-tensioned or prestressed element
- Any element where the manuscript's specification deviates from ORSC R608/R606 prescriptive

**May or may not require PE depending on jurisdiction and reviewer:**
- Steel beams supporting concrete floors (some jurisdictions accept manufacturer-stamped designs; others require PE)
- Stair openings > 50% of floor width (irregularity threshold)
- Mezzanines > 1/3 floor area of room below

**Does not require PE for a strict-prescriptive build:**
- Wood-framed walls under ORSC R602
- ORSC R608 above-grade concrete walls in SDC C only
- ORSC R606 masonry within prescriptive bar size and spacing limits in SDC ≤ C
- ORSC R403 footings using Table R403.1 presumptive bearing values

**For a BSH-spec concrete-and-CMU home in any part of Oregon west of Range 10 west of the Willamette Meridian:** assume PE involvement is required for the structural system as a whole. The manuscript should state this directly: a 1000-year concrete house in Oregon is engineered design, not prescriptive design. The engineer will sign:

1. A geotechnical report or site review for foundations
2. The structural drawings (plans, sections, details, schedules)
3. Calculations supporting the structural drawings
4. Special inspection program if any (typically required for grouted CMU, structural concrete with f'c > 4,000 psi, or post-tensioned elements per OSSC Chapter 17 — even though OSSC doesn't normally apply to residential, special inspections are sometimes triggered)

# Chapter 6 Amendment: Building Within The Prescriptive Envelope (No PE Required)

## The reframe

The previous research was diagnostic — it told you where engineering is required. This amendment is prescriptive — it tells you exactly what to build to stay inside the code envelope where a homeowner-applicant can permit the work without a stamped engineering submittal. The goal: design the BSH-spec home to fit entirely within ORSC prescriptive paths, eliminating the PE line item.

The core constraint: **everything you build must be specifically described and permitted by an ORSC prescriptive section, with no element that triggers R301.1.3 engineered design**.

## Threshold question: which SDC are you building in?

This is the gate. Look up your specific site at [snowload.seao.org/lookup.html](https://snowload.seao.org/lookup.html) and the ATC Hazards by Location tool ([asce7hazardtool.online](https://asce7hazardtool.online)) to get your seismic design category. The map roughly:

- **SDC C** (eastern Oregon, parts of southern Oregon, far inland): the friendliest territory. ORSC R608 prescriptive above-grade concrete walls are available. PCA 100 path also available. Most prescriptive options open.
- **SDC D0** (Willamette Valley margins, central Oregon): R608 is gone. PCA 100 still available. Reinforced masonry (R606) prescriptive available with seismic supplements.
- **SDC D1** (most of Willamette Valley, I-5 corridor): same as D0 for prescriptive limits, with stricter reinforcement.
- **SDC D2** (coastal Oregon, parts of Lane/Douglas/Coos counties): tightest prescriptive limits. Many BSH features unavailable.

**If your site is SDC D2, the prescriptive envelope is so narrow that the BSH design will almost certainly need engineering.** Read the rest of this amendment as conditional on SDC ≤ D1.

## The prescriptive-only design rules

Build to all of these and you have a no-PE permit path:

### Foundation (ORSC R403, R404)

- **Footings:** continuous strip per Table R403.1, sized for soil bearing value (use 1,500 psf assumed unless soil testing supports higher; do not exceed 2,000 psf without engineering)
- **Footing depth:** 12" minimum below finished grade on undisturbed soil
- **Footing reinforcement (SDC D0/D1):** one #4 bar within 12" of top of stem wall, one #4 bar 3-4" from bottom of footing, one #4 vertical at max 4'-0" o.c. with standard hook to bottom of footing extending 14" into stem wall
- **UFER ground:** uncoated #4 bar minimum 20'-0" long, 3" from bottom of footing, encased in min 2" concrete (R403.1.8)
- **Anchor bolts:** ½" diameter min, embedded 7" into concrete, 6'-0" o.c. max (4'-0" o.c. for 2-story in SDC D2), within 12" of plate ends, 3"×3"×0.229" plate washers (R602.11)
- **Foundation walls:** per R404.1 prescriptive tables. Stay at or below 8'-0" wall height with ≤4'-0" unbalanced backfill to keep simplest tables. Plain masonry foundation walls limited to 8'-0" height in D0/D1/D2

### Above-grade walls — picking the prescriptive path

You have three paths, and they're mutually exclusive on a single project:

**Path A: Wood-framed walls (R602).** This is the simplest no-PE path. Doesn't match BSH concrete philosophy but is the easiest permit. Skip if BSH concrete is non-negotiable.

**Path B: Reinforced masonry (R606) — recommended for SDC D0/D1 BSH.** Stay within these envelopes:
- 8" nominal CMU minimum thickness for bearing walls
- Wall height ≤ 10'-0" (R301.3 height limit; use ≤ 9'-0" for safety margin)
- Single-wythe construction (do NOT do double-wythe loadbearing CMU under prescriptive — there's no path)
- Vertical reinforcement: minimum #4 at 48" o.c. for D0/D1 prescriptive; in practice spec #5 at 32" or 48" o.c. for stiffness
- Horizontal reinforcement: bond beam at top of wall, at each floor level, and at top of foundation. Joint reinforcement (9-gauge ladder type) every 16" vertically (every other course) for crack control
- Mortar: Type S Portland cement-lime (R606.2.8.3 requires Type M or S in D0/D1/D2)
- Grout: ASTM C476, lifts ≤ 5'-0" to avoid cleanout requirement
- Lap length: 48db for #5 = 30"
- Grout fill: solid grout all reinforced cells; for D0/D1 stem walls, fully grouted
- 3 stories above grade plane maximum (R301.2.2.7)
- Roof eave-to-ridge ≤ 15'-0"
- ½" gypsum board on interior side fastened per Table R702.3.5

**Path C: PCA 100 above-grade concrete walls.** Available in all SDC categories, but PCA 100 is itself a prescriptive engineering manual — the homeowner-applicant has to demonstrate compliance with its tables, which is doable but requires more detailed plan submittal. Stay within PCA 100 wall thickness, reinforcement, and opening tables exactly as published. R608 (the simpler IRC prescriptive concrete path) is available in SDC C only.

### What you MUST NOT do (each triggers PE requirement)

- Double-wythe loadbearing CMU or concrete walls — no prescriptive path exists
- Cavity walls beyond TMS 402 prescriptive limits (cavity > 4-5/8", or > 6-5/8" with 2" airspace and 3/16" wire)
- Wall heights > 10'-0" in any prescriptive path
- Story heights > 11'-7"
- More than 3 stories above grade plane
- Roof eave-to-ridge > 15'-0"
- Cantilevered floors > 24"
- Retaining walls > 4'-0" or with surcharge
- Any concrete or masonry roof (no prescriptive path exists)
- Any "irregular" building geometry per R301.2.2.6 (8 conditions listed including offsets, openings, mass irregularities)
- Mixing wood framing above masonry/concrete in stories above grade plane (this is irregularity #6)
- Bearing pressures > 2,000 psf
- Engineered fill (loose fill below footings)
- FRP reinforcement in any structural element
- Stair openings > 50% of floor diaphragm width
- Mezzanines > 1/3 floor area of room below
- Ground snow load > 70 psf
- Special wind regions (parts of Oregon coast, Columbia Gorge) at >130 mph design wind
- Sites in mapped landslide hazard, liquefaction zone, or unstable slope
- Any soil with PI > 15 unless mitigated by full removal-and-replacement to non-expansive material

### Roof: the practical no-PE option

Concrete roof has no prescriptive path. **For no-PE construction, the roof must be wood-framed.**

Acceptable prescriptive wood roof options:
- Engineered roof trusses (manufacturer-designed, manufacturer-stamped — does NOT count as your engineering, the truss company carries the design responsibility)
- Conventional rafter-and-ceiling-joist framing per R802 span tables
- Roof sheathing per Table R803.1
- Eave-to-ridge ≤ 15'-0"
- Roofing material: metal panel (50-70 yr design life, prescriptive R905.10), standing seam preferred for BSH durability goals
- Insulation per R806 ventilation requirements OR unvented assembly per R806.5

**The 1000-year-friendly compromise:** specify a wood-truss roof designed by the truss manufacturer (their stamp, not yours), with 24-gauge standing seam metal roofing on synthetic underlayment over plywood sheathing. The roof is replaceable on a 60-80 year cycle, and the truss design carries no engineering line item for the homeowner. This is the practical "1000-year structure with replaceable shell" pattern.

### Floor system: the practical no-PE option

Cast-in-place concrete floors and hollow-core precast both require engineering. **For no-PE construction, floors must be wood-framed.**

Acceptable prescriptive wood floor options:
- Wood I-joists (manufacturer-designed and manufacturer-stamped — same logic as trusses)
- Solid sawn lumber per R502 span tables (Table R502.3.1)
- Plywood or OSB subfloor per Table R503.2.1
- Engineered LVL or PSL beams (manufacturer-stamped)

**The hybrid that makes BSH work without PE:** masonry walls (Path B above) + manufacturer-engineered floor trusses or I-joists with manufacturer's stamp. Manufacturer-stamped products are accepted under ORSC R301.1.3 reference to "accepted engineering practice" — but the manufacturer carries the engineering, not you. Your permit submittal includes the manufacturer's product evaluation report (PER) and span tables, not a custom calculation.

### What the manuscript design probably needs to drop or modify

Reviewing the original BSH design scope against the no-PE envelope:

- **Double-wythe CMU cavity wall:** drop. No prescriptive path. Replace with single-wythe 8" reinforced CMU + exterior continuous insulation + cladding (rainscreen) of the manuscript's choice
- **Concrete floor or hollow-core floor:** drop. Replace with wood I-joist or open-web wood truss floor on the masonry walls (manufacturer-stamped)
- **Concrete roof:** drop. Replace with manufacturer-engineered wood truss roof + standing seam metal
- **FRP reinforcement anywhere:** drop entirely
- **Service layer between floors:** keep < 30" or design as fully accessible mechanical level (the latter triggers PE — the former doesn't)
- **Stair openings:** keep < 50% of diaphragm width to avoid irregularity threshold
- **Wall heights:** cap at 9'-0" for safety margin (10'-0" is the hard limit)

## The amended structural recommendation

A no-PE BSH-spec home in SDC D0/D1 Oregon, building within prescriptive limits:

- 8" nominal reinforced CMU exterior walls, single wythe, #5 vertical at 32" o.c., #4 horizontal in bond beams at top of wall + each floor + foundation, joint reinforcement at every other course, full grout at reinforced cells, Type S mortar
- Continuous concrete footing per Table R403.1 with R403.1.3 SDC D0/D1 reinforcement
- Wood I-joist or open-web wood truss floor (manufacturer-stamped) on bond beams with cast-in anchors
- Wood truss roof (manufacturer-stamped) on bond beams, 24-gauge standing seam metal roofing
- Continuous exterior insulation outside the CMU (rigid mineral wool or polyiso) with rainscreen cladding
- Wall height 9'-0", 2 stories above grade plane maximum
- Roof eave-to-ridge ≤ 15'-0"
- No basement or crawl space-only foundation (full basement requires engineered foundation walls in most BSH-spec scenarios)
- All openings sized within R703 prescriptive header tables
- All structural anchorage per R602.11 anchor bolt schedule + Simpson Strong-Tie or USP equivalents per their published catalogs (manufacturer-engineered hardware)

This builds. This permits as a homeowner-applicant. This costs you no engineering line item. It also still aligns with the BSH design philosophy at 70-80% — you keep the reinforced masonry walls, the bond beam continuity, the long-life envelope, the metal roof. You give up the concrete floors and the concrete roof, which are the two highest-engineering-cost elements of the original design anyway.

## What you trade away by avoiding PE

The trade-off is real and worth stating:

- **Lower envelope mass** — you lose the concrete floor's thermal mass benefit; partially compensated by the reinforced CMU walls
- **Shorter design life on floors and roof** — wood I-joist + truss assemblies are 50-100 year systems vs. concrete's 100-200+ year systems. Replaceable, but they're a maintenance item the original concrete design avoided
- **More vulnerability to fire, water, and pests** — the wood elements need detailing protection (sealed envelope, no condensation, treated where required) that concrete didn't need
- **Less architectural flexibility** — prescriptive opening sizes, span limits, and irregularity rules constrain the floor plan more than an engineered design would
- **Probably no cantilevered features, no large open spans, no irregular geometry** — all of which would push you back into engineering

## When the PE line item is actually worth it

Just so the manuscript doesn't oversell the no-PE path: an Oregon residential structural engineer for a custom 2-story 2,500-3,500 sf home runs roughly $4,000-$10,000 for a complete structural design including foundation, walls, floors, roof, and lateral system, plus maybe $1,500-$3,500 for a geotechnical investigation. **Total $5,500-$13,500 for full engineering.**

Against the cost of a BSH-spec home (likely $400,000-$700,000+ depending on size and finishes), the engineering line item is 1-3% of project cost. If the original design had 1000-year aspirations, the engineering is the cheapest component of getting them right.

The honest framing for the manuscript: there's a real "build-to-prescriptive" path that delivers 70-80% of the BSH vision without engineering, and there's a "build-to-engineered" path that delivers 100% of the vision for an engineering line item that's small relative to total project cost. Both are legitimate. The reader should know which one they're choosing and why.

The single anchor sentence for this amendment: **building entirely within ORSC prescriptive paths is possible for a BSH-spec home in SDC C/D0/D1 if you accept single-wythe reinforced CMU walls, manufacturer-stamped wood-framed floors and roof, and the geometric constraints of the prescriptive code; the no-PE envelope is real but it costs you the concrete floors, the concrete roof, and the double-wythe wall — not negotiable, those are the elements that require engineering.**







# Chapter 7: Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement

## Oregon-specific foundation inspection timing and readiness guidance

Oregon doesn't publish a single statewide foundation inspection guide, but most jurisdictions publish near-identical checklists that are citable in the manuscript. The closest thing to an authoritative state document is BCD's **"Cite It, Write It" checklist** (oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/Documents/cite-it-write-it-checklist.pdf), which lists the most common code violations found at residential inspections, organized by inspection stage. For foundation-specific readiness, the Marion County and West Linn checklists are the most-cited models, both of which directly mirror ORSC R403 and R404 language.

**Foundation inspection timing — Oregon practice:**

- **Footing inspection (pre-pour):** required by ORSC R109.1.1. Forms set, reinforcement placed, anchor bolt locations marked, UFER ground bonded to vertical rebar before concrete. Inspector verifies footing depth (12" min below finished grade), width (per Table R403.1), reinforcement size/grade/spacing/cover/laps, soil bearing on undisturbed material, no debris/water/ice in forms, and slope (top level, bottom not exceeding 1:10).
- **Stem wall / foundation wall inspection (pre-pour or post-pour, varies):** reinforcement, anchor bolt placement, dampproofing/waterproofing readiness, drain tile installation.
- **Underfloor / pre-slab inspection:** required before slab is poured. Inspector verifies vapor retarder, insulation, sub-slab plumbing, electrical conduit, radon piping (per Appendix F where applicable), and aggregate base.
- **Foundation drainage inspection (R405):** typically combined with stem-wall inspection. Required around concrete or masonry foundations that retain earth and enclose habitable or usable space below grade.

**Citable readiness checklist items (synthesized from Marion County, West Linn, Lane County, and BCD Cite-It Write-It):**

- Footing on undisturbed soil or engineered fill verified (R403.1)
- Footing 12" minimum below finished grade or below frost line (R403.1.4) — Oregon's 12" frost depth applies to most of the state
- Loose soil, mud, water, debris, and ice removed from spaces to be occupied by concrete (R403.1, ACI 318 §26.5.2)
- Reinforcement size, grade, spacing, splicing, and cover per approved plans (R403.1.3.5)
- Bottom horizontal #4 bar placed 3-4" from bottom of footing, top horizontal #4 within 12" of top of stem wall (R403.1.3, SDC D0/D1/D2)
- Vertical #4 at max 4'-0" o.c. with standard hook to bottom of footing extending 14" into stem wall (D0/D1/D2)
- Anchor bolts: ½" diameter min, 7" embedment, 6'-0" o.c. max (4'-0" for D2 2-story), 3"×3"×0.229" plate washers, located 3.5"-12" from plate ends (R403.1.6, R602.11)
- Hold-downs and rebar for ABW or portal frame tied in place — *not wet-set* (R602.10.6)
- UFER ground: uncoated #4, 20'-0" min, 3" from bottom of footing, 2" concrete cover (R403.1.8)
- Setbacks from slope per Figure R403.1.9.1 (R403.1.9 — minimum 1H:1V slope setback measured on imaginary 45° plane from toe)
- Top of foundation 6" minimum above finished grade (R317 protection; some jurisdictions stricter)
- Top of foundation 12" plus 2% above street gutter discharge or drainage device inlet (R401.3)
- Surface drainage 6" minimum fall within 10'-0" of foundation (R401.3)
- Drain tile around concrete or masonry foundations enclosing habitable space below grade (R405.1)

**For the manuscript:** cite the Marion County Residential Footing and Foundation Checklist as the model; note that all Oregon jurisdictions follow the same R403/R404 framework but vary in inspection scheduling (some combine footing and pre-slab into one inspection, others split). Direct readers to call their local building department's IVR or scheduling line before scheduling — Marion County uses (503) 588-5147, Portland uses (503) 823-7388, etc.

## Frost-protected shallow foundations under 2023 ORSC

The path is unchanged from prior IRC editions. ORSC R403.3 governs for heated buildings; ASCE 32-01 governs for unheated buildings or buildings outside R403.3 prescriptive limits.

**ORSC R403.3 prescriptive FPSF — applicability:**
- Heated building maintained at 64°F (18°C) minimum monthly mean
- Cannot be used for unheated spaces (porches, garages, utility rooms, carports) — these need engineering or full frost-depth footings
- Air freezing index from IRC Table R403.3(2) (Oregon AFI typically < 1,500 °F-days for Willamette Valley sites; higher in the Cascades and east of the mountains)
- Vertical wall insulation along stem wall (typically 4'-0" deep) plus horizontal wing insulation extending outward (varies by AFI: 12"-48" depending on climate severity, increased at corners)

**ASCE 32-01 path:**
The standard is "Design and Construction of Frost-Protected Shallow Foundations" (American Society of Civil Engineers, 2001). Referenced by IRC R403.1.4.1 as one of the four frost-protection options: deeper than frost line, R403.3, ASCE 32, or solid rock. ASCE 32 covers unheated buildings, semi-heated, and provides more rigorous design methods than R403.3 prescriptive. Soil must be Non-Frost Susceptible (NFS) — granular with < 6% by weight passing #200 sieve — for the prescriptive simplifications.

**Oregon amendments:** ORSC adopts R403.3 essentially unmodified from the model IRC. The 2023 ORSC includes the same Figure R403.3(1)/(2)/(3)/(4) and Table R403.3(1) values. No Oregon-specific air-freezing index map; use IRC Table R403.3(2) or NOAA climate data for the project location.

**For the BSH manuscript:** specify the FPSF assembly with these elements:
- Vertical insulation: rigid foam (XPS preferred for ground contact, ASTM C578 Type VI or VII at ≥40 psi compressive resistance) extending from top of footing to grade-level horizontal insulation, full perimeter
- Horizontal wing insulation: XPS, ASTM C578 Type VII (40 psi) or higher, extending outward per Table R403.3(1) (typically 24" for AFI 1,500-2,500 in most of Oregon)
- Wing insulation slope: minimum 1/4"/ft sloping away from foundation
- Gravel cover: minimum 6"-12" of gravel/soil over horizontal insulation to protect from impact and UV
- Insulation must be protected from UV at all exposed locations (parge coat, metal flashing, or pressure-treated wood per R403.3.2)
- For unheated attached spaces (garage, porch): treat per R403.3.1.1 with vertical insulation between heated FPSF and unheated slab, OR use full frost-depth footing for the unheated portion

**Don't write exact insulation geometry without referencing Figure R403.3(2)** — the table is climate-specific and the manuscript can't generalize beyond "consult Table R403.3(1) for Dimension A and insulation R-values for your AFI." For Willamette Valley AFI ≈ 500 °F-days, Dimension A is approximately 12" with R-4.5 horizontal insulation; for Bend area AFI ≈ 1,500 °F-days, Dimension A is approximately 24" with R-5.6.

## Slab stack layer order

The order has been the subject of code and industry debate for two decades. The current best-practice order, accounting for Oregon's R506 vapor retarder requirement, ORSC Appendix F radon requirements where applicable, and ACI 302.2R's revised guidance, is:

**Standard slab-on-grade stack (top to bottom) for BSH-spec home in Oregon:**

1. **Concrete slab** (typically 4"-6" depending on use; 4" minimum in conditioned habitable space per R506.1)
2. **Vapor retarder** — 10-mil ASTM E1745 Class A, joints lapped 6", sealed at penetrations (R506.2.3)
3. **Rigid insulation** — XPS, ASTM C578 Type IV (25 psi) minimum for residential floor loads; Type VI (40 psi) for spaces with point loads, garages, or industrial use
4. **Soil-gas-retarder** (where ORSC Appendix F applies — see radon section below) — 6-mil poly, separate from the vapor retarder
5. **Aggregate / capillary break** — minimum 4" of clean ¾" or #57 crushed stone (per R506.2.2), gas-permeable for radon mitigation
6. **Compacted subgrade** — natural soil or engineered fill, free of organic material, 95% Standard Proctor minimum

**The vapor retarder placement debate — resolved:**
ACI 302.1R-15 and ACI 302.2R-06 reversed earlier guidance and now recommend the vapor retarder directly below the slab, with no sand layer between vapor retarder and concrete. The reason: bleed water rising from the concrete is preferable to bleed water trapped in a sand layer that re-introduces moisture later. **Place the vapor retarder directly below the slab, on top of the insulation. Do not place sand between vapor retarder and slab.**

If using rigid foam insulation under slab, the order becomes: subgrade → aggregate → soil-gas retarder (if required) → insulation → vapor retarder → slab. The insulation goes BELOW the vapor retarder, not above.

**Variant for hydronic radiant floor:**

Same order, but specify:
- Insulation R-value increased to R-10 minimum (R-15+ recommended for BSH 1000-year energy goals)
- Slab thickness 4"-6" with PEX tubing tied to top of welded wire reinforcement or spaced bar mat
- Vapor retarder must be intact below tubing — radiant heat will drive moisture upward if vapor retarder is compromised
- No insulation between tubing and slab top (heat goes UP not DOWN)
- Edge insulation: vertical rigid foam at perimeter, R-10 minimum, full slab thickness
- Concrete mix: 5-bag minimum, 0.50 max w/c, 4,000 psi at 28 days, with consideration for thermal lag and crack control

**Variant for radon counties (Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, Yamhill):**

ORSC Appendix F mandates passive subslab depressurization in these counties. The stack adds:
- 4" minimum aggregate base (gas-permeable layer for radon collection)
- 3" diameter ABS, PVC, or equivalent gas-tight vent pipe embedded vertically in subslab aggregate before slab is cast (AF103.6.1), with T-fitting to ensure pipe opening remains in permeable layer
- 6-mil polyethylene soil-gas-retarder above aggregate (separate function from the 10-mil vapor retarder)
- All slab joints, control joints, isolation joints sealed with polyurethane caulk or elastomeric sealant (AF103.4.2)
- Sumps covered with gasketed lids (AF103.4.4)
- Hollow block masonry foundation walls have continuous solid course or grouted course at or above grade (AF103.4.5)
- Vent pipe accessible for future fan installation through attic (AF103.8)
- Electrical circuit terminated in attic for future fan power (AF103.12)

The 6-mil soil-gas retarder and the 10-mil vapor retarder are separate products serving separate code requirements. In practice, a single 15-mil ASTM E1745 Class A vapor retarder will satisfy both requirements simultaneously and is the common practical solution — but specify it explicitly in plans as serving both R506.2.3 and AF103.3 functions.

**Final slab stack order for BSH-spec home in a radon county with radiant floor:**

```
[interior]
4"-6" concrete slab with PEX radiant tubing
─────────────────────────────────────
15-mil ASTM E1745 Class A vapor retarder (also satisfies AF103.3 soil-gas retarder)
─────────────────────────────────────
R-15 XPS rigid insulation (ASTM C578 Type IV minimum)
─────────────────────────────────────
3" passive radon vent pipe penetrating up through slab to attic
─────────────────────────────────────
4" minimum #57 clean crushed stone (gas-permeable)
─────────────────────────────────────
Compacted subgrade (95% Standard Proctor, no organics)
[earth]
```

## Vapor retarder requirements — Oregon specifics

ORSC R506.2.3 was updated in the 2021 ORSC cycle to match the 2021 IRC. The current requirement: "A minimum 10-mil (0.010 inch; 0.254 mm) vapor retarder conforming to ASTM E1745 Class A requirements with joints lapped not less than 6 inches (152 mm) shall be placed between the concrete floor slab and the base course or the prepared subgrade where a base course does not exist." [UpCodes](https://up.codes/viewer/oregon/irc-2015/chapter/1/scope-and-administration)

**Requirements summary:**

- **Material:** ASTM E1745 Class A (the strongest of the three classes — A, B, C — based on physical durability)
- **Performance:** maximum permeance 0.1 perms after conditioning per ASTM E1745 Section 7.1 testing
- **Tensile strength:** 45 lb/in minimum (Class A)
- **Puncture resistance:** 2,200 g minimum (Class A)
- **Thickness:** 10-mil minimum (15-mil and 20-mil products available — Stego, Perminator, Vapor Block, etc.)
- **Joint laps:** 6" minimum
- **Sealing:** all joints, seams, penetrations sealed with manufacturer's tape and/or mastic to maintain continuity
- **Edge treatment:** turned up at footings/walls, sealed to vertical termination

**Exceptions (R506.2.3):**
- Garages
- Utility buildings
- Other unheated accessory structures
- Unheated storage rooms < 70 sf
- Carports
- Driveways, walks, patios, flatwork not likely to be heated
- Where approved by building official based on local site conditions

For BSH conditioned habitable space: vapor retarder is mandatory.

**Specification language for the Code block:**
"Under-slab vapor retarder: 15-mil ASTM E1745 Class A, lapped 6" minimum at all joints, sealed at all seams and penetrations with manufacturer's tape and mastic compatible with the membrane, turned up 6" minimum at all foundation walls and footings. Penetrations sealed with manufacturer's pipe boots or hand-formed caulked seal. Permeance ≤0.03 perms after conditioning per ASTM E1745 §7.1. Examples of compliant products: Stego Wrap 15, W.R. Meadows Perminator 15, Insulation Solutions Viper VaporCheck II 15."

The 15-mil (rather than 10-mil minimum) recommendation: 1000-year design framing makes the durability upgrade trivial; cost difference is minor; puncture resistance benefit is significant during construction.

## Under-slab insulation specifics

Two codes apply: ASTM C578 (Standard Specification for Rigid, Cellular Polystyrene Thermal Insulation) governs both XPS and EPS materials with type designations indicating compressive resistance; the manufacturer's ICC ESR (Evaluation Service Report) verifies code compliance for specific products.

**Material options for under-slab residential application:**

| Material | ASTM C578 type | Compressive resistance | Suitable for |
|---|---|---|---|
| EPS Type II | II | 15 psi | Light residential, no point loads |
| EPS Type IX | IX | 25 psi | Standard residential floor |
| XPS Type IV | IV | 25 psi | Standard residential floor |
| XPS Type VI | VI | 40 psi | Heavy residential, garage |
| XPS Type VII | VII | 60 psi | Industrial, high point load |
| EPS Type X | X | 100 psi | Heavy industrial |

**For BSH residential application:** XPS Type IV (25 psi) is the prescriptive minimum for habitable space; XPS Type VI (40 psi) for garage slabs and any area expecting point loads; XPS Type VII for any commercial-grade BSH variant.

**Long-term creep (deformation under sustained load):**
ASTM C578 specifies short-term compressive strength but doesn't directly address long-term creep. For residential floor loads (live load 40 psf + dead load 10 psf = 50 psf = 0.35 psi), even Type II EPS at 15 psi has a 40× safety margin against immediate compression failure. **Long-term creep is not a residential floor concern** at typical loads. It becomes a concern under continuous loads above ~30% of the rated compressive strength — relevant for structural slab edges, isolated footings on insulation, or heavy industrial floors.

**Moisture exposure:**
- XPS: closed-cell, ~0.1% water absorption by volume in long-term tests; preferred for direct-ground contact
- EPS: open-cell to semi-closed, 0.5-3% water absorption depending on type; acceptable but more conservative when in ground contact
- Both materials lose R-value when wet; protect with vapor retarder above and gravel layer below

**Point loads and edge loading:**
- Point loads (column footings, wall plates, equipment pads): use Type VI minimum (40 psi); consider local thickening of slab over insulation to distribute load
- Edge loading (wall plate bearing on insulation): typically not allowed in prescriptive design — extend footing to bear on subgrade, with insulation only under non-bearing slab area
- Garage door frames, jambs, and adjacent slabs: thicken slab and use Type VII; consider 6" or 8" structural slab edge

**Accepted product standards:**
- ASTM C578 (the master standard)
- ASTM C518 (R-value testing)
- ICC ESR for specific products (DOW, Owens Corning Foamular, Atlas EPS, etc.)
- ASTM E1745 if used as combined insulation/vapor retarder system (rare; typically separate products)

**Specification language for the Code block:**
"Under-slab insulation: XPS rigid foam complying with ASTM C578 Type IV (25 psi minimum compressive resistance) for habitable space; ASTM C578 Type VI (40 psi minimum) for garages and equipment pads. Continuous installation with minimum 1" overlap or shiplapped edges, no gaps. Total R-value: R-10 minimum for residential floor; R-15 minimum for radiant slab. ICC ESR-required documentation submitted with permit. Examples: Owens Corning Foamular 250 (Type IV, R-10 at 2"), DOW Styrofoam HighLoad 40 (Type VI), Atlas Foamular ThermoPlus."

## Basement waterproofing vs. dampproofing

**ORSC R406.1 dampproofing — the default for below-grade walls without high water table:**

Required for: foundation walls that retain earth and enclose interior spaces and floors below grade.

Acceptable materials (R406.1):
- Bituminous coating (asphalt-based)
- 3 lb/yd² acrylic-modified cement
- 1/8" surface-bonding cement complying with ASTM C887
- Any material permitted for waterproofing in R406.2

**Masonry foundation walls** require additional 3/8" Portland cement parging applied to the exterior before dampproofing material.

Coverage area: from finished grade to the higher of (top of footing) or (6" below top of basement floor).

**ORSC R406.2 waterproofing — required where high water table or severe soil conditions:**

Triggered by: "In areas where a high water table or other severe soil-water conditions are known to exist" [Oregon Legislature](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors672.html) — this is a building-official judgment call based on local site conditions. The manuscript should treat any of the following as triggers:

- Bottom of footing within 12" of seasonal high groundwater
- Site mapped as wet or hydric soil per NRCS Web Soil Survey
- Basement floor below seasonal high groundwater
- Documented neighbor groundwater issues
- Clay soil with poor drainage and basement floor below grade
- Any site where standing water has been observed in excavations
- Most coastal Oregon and lower-elevation Willamette Valley sites with full basements

Acceptable waterproofing materials (R406.2):
- Two-ply hot-mopped felts
- 55-lb roll roofing
- 40-mil polymer-modified asphalt
- 60-mil flexible polymer cement
- 1/8" cement-based, fiber-reinforced waterproof coating
- Or other approved methods

Modern best practice for BSH-spec waterproofing (1000-year framing): combine multiple components — a 60-mil peel-and-stick polymer-modified asphalt membrane (Henry Blueskin, Grace Bituthene, Tremco Tuff-N-Dri, or equivalent) + dimple drainage board + protection board, with all penetrations and corners detailed per manufacturer's instructions.

**Foundation drains (R405.1):**

Required around concrete or masonry foundations enclosing habitable or usable spaces below grade. Basic specification:
- Drains shall be installed at or below the area to be protected
- Discharge by gravity or mechanical means to approved drainage system
- Granular aggregate around drains, minimum 6" deep, ¾"-1.5" stone
- Drain pipe: 4" minimum perforated PVC or equivalent
- Filter fabric over aggregate to prevent fines migration
- Dimple drainage board behind waterproofing membrane to channel water down to drain

**Sump pump:**
Required where gravity drainage to daylight isn't feasible. Sump pit per R405.2:
- 24" minimum depth
- Tight-fitting cover (Appendix F sump cover required if in radon county)
- Discharge above grade, 5'-0" minimum from foundation
- Backup power consideration for 1000-year design (battery backup or generator)

**Habitable basement requirements:**
A "habitable basement" triggers all of the habitable-space requirements (R305 ceiling height 7'-0" minimum, R303 light/ventilation, R310 emergency egress with 5.7 sf clear opening minimum) plus all the drainage and waterproofing items above. **For BSH no-PE prescriptive path, a habitable basement multiplies the inspection burden and tightens the structural envelope.** The manuscript should flag this: a daylight basement (with at least one wall above grade) is dramatically easier to permit than a fully-buried basement with habitable space.

**Crawl space (R408):**
Different rule set. R408.1 ventilation OR R408.2 unvented conditioned crawl OR R408.3 unvented unconditioned crawl with ground vapor retarder. Foundation walls dampproofed per R406.1; waterproofing not typically required for crawl spaces except in severe sites.

**Inspection triggers:**
- Foundation wall pre-backfill (after waterproofing/dampproofing applied, drains installed, before backfill)
- Pre-cover inspection if drain tile is field-installed
- Final inspection to verify backfill protection of waterproofing

**Specification language for the Code block:**
"Below-grade waterproofing per ORSC R406.2 where seasonal high groundwater within 24" of footing or where designated by site soils evaluation. System: 60-mil peel-and-stick polymer-modified asphalt membrane (or equivalent listed in R406.2), continuous from top of footing to finished grade, all penetrations sealed per manufacturer's instructions. Dimple drainage board over membrane to drain at base of wall. Foundation drain per R405.1: 4" perforated pipe, ¾" clean stone surround, filter fabric, gravity discharge to daylight where possible or to sump pump with above-grade discharge. Where R406.2 doesn't apply, dampproofing per R406.1 with bituminous coating or 1/8" surface-bonding cement on parged masonry wall."

## Concrete testing for owner-builder residential

This is where the "no-PE prescriptive path" question intersects with practical reality. The codes set test frequencies that are designed for engineered commercial work and disproportionate for owner-builder residential.

**ACI 318-19 §26.12 testing frequency (engineered work, baseline):**
- 1 strength test per 150 yd³ of each concrete class placed each day, OR
- 1 test per 5,000 sf of slab/wall surface area placed each day, OR
- 1 test per day per class of concrete (whichever produces the most tests)
- Each "test" = average of two 6×12" cylinders or three 4×8" cylinders, tested at 28 days
- Minimum 5 strength tests per project per class of concrete

**ACI 332-20 (residential code) testing — is it required?**
ACI 332 §3.3 references back to ACI 318 for testing when testing is specified. **Critically, ACI 332 doesn't mandate testing for residential prescriptive applications — it permits the building official to require testing for cause, but the default residential approach uses concrete batched to a specified mix without project-specific cylinder testing.**

**Practical reality for owner-builder residential in Oregon:**

For a BSH home built within prescriptive ORSC limits using ready-mix concrete from a reputable supplier:

1. **Routine testing is NOT required** by ORSC or by ACI 332 for a residential prescriptive build. Building officials accept the ready-mix supplier's batch ticket as evidence of compliance with the specified mix design.
2. **Batch ticket requirements** are real and inspectable: mix design number, water/cement ratio, time of batching, time of placement, delivery quantity, slump on delivery (often noted by driver), specified strength (f'c).
3. **Field testing CAN be required** by the building official for cause — typically when there's reason to suspect the concrete isn't as ordered, or for elements at the edge of prescriptive (e.g., approaching code limits on retaining wall heights, foundation walls > 8'-0" tall, or slab thicknesses below normal range).
4. **For BSH 1000-year design,** the manuscript should recommend voluntary testing on critical elements (foundation walls, slab pours > 50 yd³, structural elements) even when not required, as part of a quality-assurance ethos.

**Recommended owner-builder testing protocol for BSH:**

For each major pour (typically: footing, foundation walls, slab):
- **Slump test** (ASTM C143): 1 per delivery truck, tested at point of discharge by the driver or owner, recorded
- **Air content** (ASTM C231 pressure or C173 volumetric): 1 per first truck and any time slump or appearance changes, only required when air-entrained mix specified (which it should be for any concrete with freeze-thaw exposure — most Oregon site classes F1 or F2)
- **Concrete temperature** (ASTM C1064): 1 per first truck and any time conditions change, especially in hot weather (>80°F) or cold weather (<50°F)
- **Compression cylinders** (ASTM C31 making, ASTM C39 testing): 1 set of 4 cylinders per major pour. Test 1 at 7 days, 2 at 28 days (averaged for the strength test), 1 hold cylinder at 56 days if the 28-day result is suspect

**For owner-builder, who can perform the tests?**

The catch: ACI 318 §26.12.1.1(c) requires "Qualified field testing technicians performed the test on the fresh concrete" [Building Codes Division](https://www.oregon.gov/bcd/Formslibrary/4823pen.pdf) , with ACI Grade I Field Testing Technician certification or equivalent. The owner can:

- Hire a testing lab to come for the major pours (typical cost $400-800 per visit including lab cylinder breaks; for 3 major pours = $1,200-2,400 total)
- Take ACI Grade I Field Testing certification themselves (~$400-600 course + exam, valid 5 years) and self-perform field tests, then send cylinders to a CCRL-inspected lab for compression testing ($25-50/cylinder)
- Skip formal testing and rely on supplier's batch tickets — the most common owner-builder approach in Oregon, accepted unless building official requires testing

**For the manuscript:** recommend the middle path. Owner gets ACI Grade I certification (it's a one-day course, useful for life as a builder), self-performs slump/air/temperature in the field, takes cylinders for major pours (footing, walls, slab), sends cylinders to a local CCRL lab for compression testing. Total cost ~$200-400 per major pour vs. $400-800 for full third-party testing. The certification investment pays for itself on a single BSH project and produces defensible documentation.

**Acceptance criteria (ACI 318 §26.12.3.1):**
1. Average of any 3 consecutive strength tests ≥ f'c
2. No individual strength test < (f'c - 500) psi for f'c ≤ 5,000 psi, or < 0.90·f'c for f'c > 5,000 psi

A failure of criterion 2 triggers a low-strength investigation; a failure of criterion 1 requires action to increase strength but doesn't trigger structural review.

**Specification language for the Code block:**
"Concrete testing per ACI 318-19 §26.12 standard practice, scaled for residential prescriptive work: slump (ASTM C143), air content where specified (ASTM C231), temperature (ASTM C1064) on first truck of each major pour and as conditions vary; compression cylinders (ASTM C31 making, ASTM C39 testing) at 1 set per major pour, with 1 cylinder tested at 7 days, 2 cylinders averaged at 28 days, 1 hold cylinder available for 56-day re-test. Specified strength: f'c = 3,000 psi minimum for footings, 3,500 psi for foundation walls, 4,000 psi for slab-on-grade and structural elements. Testing performed by ACI Grade I Field Testing Technician or owner-applicant with ACI Grade I certification; cylinder compression testing performed at CCRL-inspected laboratory. Records retained per ORSC R104 for the life of the building."

## When to reference ACI 318, ACI 332, ASTM, project specs, or local inspection

Hierarchy for the manuscript Code blocks:

1. **ASTM standards for test methods** (always cite when describing a test): C31 cylinder making, C39 cylinder compression, C143 slump, C172 sampling, C231 air pressure method, C173 air volumetric method, C1064 temperature, C42 cores, E1745 vapor retarder, C578 rigid foam, A615/A706 reinforcing steel, C476 grout
2. **ACI 332** for residential prescriptive concrete design and construction (footings, foundation walls, above-grade walls, slabs-on-ground)
3. **ACI 318** for engineered concrete and as the fallback when ACI 332 or ORSC is silent (covers everything ACI 332 doesn't)
4. **ORSC R403, R404, R405, R406, R506** for Oregon residential prescriptive (cite first when prescriptive path is being followed)
5. **ORSC Appendix F** for radon-county requirements (Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, Yamhill)
6. **PCA 100** for above-grade exterior concrete walls in SDC D0/D1/D2 prescriptive (referenced by R301.2.2.5)
7. **ACI 301** as the master specification for structural concrete (cite when project specifications are needed beyond ACI 318)
8. **ACI 302.1R / 302.2R** for slab-on-ground construction guidance (cite for vapor retarder placement, joint spacing, finishing)
9. **Local inspection requirements** governing in cases of conflict — building official has final authority

**Example Code block format combining the references:**

> *"Concrete footings: ORSC R403.1 prescriptive sizing per Table R403.1; reinforcement per R403.1.3 and Figure R403.1.3 for SDC D0/D1/D2. Materials: concrete f'c ≥ 3,000 psi at 28 days per ACI 332-20 §3.3; reinforcement ASTM A615 Grade 60. Placement per ACI 318-19 §26.5. Testing per ACI 318-19 §26.12 with ASTM C143 (slump), C231 (air), C1064 (temperature), C31 (cylinders), C39 (compression). Final inspection per ORSC R109.1.1 prior to backfill."*

This format gives the reader a single pointer for each compliance question and is defensible in plan review.

## What to include in Chapter 7 vs. later chapters

Recommendation: **Chapter 7 includes the operational records that are inseparable from placement; later chapters hold the formal QA/QC documentation framework.**

**Chapter 7 should include:**

- Concrete batch ticket minimum content (mix design ID, w/c ratio, batch time, placement window, slump as delivered, total water added on site, supplier name, truck number) — this is the essential "did we get what we ordered" record
- Pre-pour checklist (forms set, rebar placed, embedments located, vapor retarder inspected, weather conditions, temperature)
- Placement conditions log (ambient temperature at pour start and end, concrete temperature, weather, wind, time of pour, rate of placement, vibrator use, finishing techniques)
- Post-pour curing log (curing method, duration, ambient temperature, moisture maintenance, form removal time)
- Field test record (slump, air, temperature with date/time/truck number)

**Later testing-and-documentation chapters should include:**

- Test cylinder identification, storage, and transport protocols (the formal "who did what when" trail)
- Laboratory reports format and retention
- Acceptance/rejection decision tree based on test results
- Low-strength investigation procedures (cores, in-place testing, structural evaluation)
- Project quality assurance plan (testing frequency, hold points, sign-offs)
- Long-term documentation retention strategy for 1000-year framing
- Photographic and video documentation protocols
- Permit closeout documentation

**The split:** Chapter 7 answers "what records do you keep at the moment of placement?" The later chapter answers "how do you organize, archive, and reference those records over decades?"

For practical use: every concrete pour generates a one-page operational log (batch ticket + field test record + placement notes + curing observations) that lives in the construction binder. Those one-page logs become the raw inputs that feed the formal documentation system in the later chapter. Don't bury the operational log requirement in the testing chapter — it has to be available at the truck on the day of the pour, which means it belongs in Chapter 7 alongside the placement guidance.

## Foam Plastic Protection — Section R316

The primary rule is simple: foam plastic must be separated from the interior of a building by a thermal barrier unless an exception applies. The default thermal barrier is ½-inch gypsum wallboard or material tested to NFPA 275.

**The concrete/masonry exception — directly relevant to BSH:** A thermal barrier is not required for foam plastic installed in a masonry or concrete wall, floor or roof system where the foam plastic insulation is covered on each face by not less than 1-inch (25 mm) thickness of masonry or concrete. This is the key exception for a double-wythe or concrete sandwich wall. If the foam is fully embedded between concrete wythes with at least 1 inch of concrete on each side, no separate thermal barrier (gypsum) is required.

**Attic/crawl space ignition barrier exception:** The thermal barrier is not required where the space is entered only for purposes of repairs or maintenance and the foam plastic insulation has been tested per R316.6 or is protected by an ignition barrier, which may be 1½-inch mineral fiber, ¼-inch wood structural panel, ⅜-inch particleboard, ¼-inch hardboard, ⅜-inch gypsum board, corrosion-resistant steel, 1½-inch cellulose insulation, or ¼-inch fiber-cement. This matters for any foam applied under a concrete roof deck where the space is not occupied.

**Below-grade foam:** For below-grade exterior foam, ORSC N1104.2.3 (referenced in the slab technical bulletin) requires insulation protection — a covering protecting the above-grade portion of the exterior insulation from UV and mechanical damage. Below grade, the foam is covered by soil, but the transition zone from grade upward requires protection. The ORSC technical bulletin illustrates this with a 45° bevel cut and insulation protection notation.

**Service layer and exposed foam:** Any foam exposed to occupied interior space needs the full thermal barrier unless the concrete-face exception applies. In practice, a BSH concrete wall with foam in the cavity and no exposed interior foam faces achieves code compliance without gypsum — the interior concrete wythe substitutes as the thermal barrier.

**Best primary source for R316:** Section R316 of the 2023 ORSC as published by ICC Digital Codes (codes.iccsafe.org/content/ORRC2023P1/) — this is the Oregon-specific adopted code. UpCodes has the same content but the ICC source is authoritative. ## Insulation Strategy for Double-Wythe Masonry or Concrete Wall

This is a building-science and code-acceptance question, not a pure code question. Here is the analysis organized by the criteria you listed.

**Moisture dynamics and drying potential:** Both concrete and CMU are highly vapor-permeable when uncoated (concrete vapor permeance varies roughly 3–10 perms depending on mix and thickness). In Oregon's 4C climate (west side), the predominant vapor drive is inward in winter; in 5B (east side), drying to either side is possible. For a double-wythe wall with insulation in the cavity:

- **Polyiso (foil-faced):** High R-value per inch (R-6 to R-6.5 aged), but foil facing is a vapor barrier. If placed in the cavity between two concrete wythes, you're trapping any moisture that condenses in the cavity. Acceptable in above-grade applications where the exterior face can dry to outside, problematic if used at/below grade. Foil-faced polyiso in a fully sealed cavity is a moisture trap — only use if the cavity is designed to drain.
- **EPS (expanded polystyrene):** R-3.8/inch, vapor-open (2–5 perms depending on density), widely accepted below grade and at grade transitions. Good moisture tolerance, cheap, easy to cut. Code-accepted for below-grade applications. No fire issue when covered on both faces by concrete per R316 exception. This is the most defensible general-purpose choice for a concrete sandwich or double-wythe with insulated cavity.
- **XPS (extruded polystyrene):** R-5/inch, very low permeance (0.3–1 perm) — essentially a vapor retarder. Below grade it's more moisture-resistant than EPS. Above grade in a cavity, it impedes drying. Long-term thermal drift is a concern (XPS R-value degrades about 10-15% over 20 years as blowing agents off-gas). Not the best choice for a cavity that cannot dry to outside.
- **Mineral wool:** R-4.2/inch, vapor-open, non-combustible, fire Class A, no blowing agents, no thermal drift, excellent dimensional stability, completely inorganic. Can absorb and release moisture without degrading. The challenge is structural: mineral wool boards used in a masonry cavity need lateral restraint to stay in plane; they cannot take compressive load from wall ties. Requires stiff boards (minimum 16 psi compressive strength for masonry applications, per NAIMA guidance). High-density mineral wool board (e.g., Rockwool COMFORTBOARD 80 or similar) is appropriate. No foam plastic fire provisions apply. Best choice for above-grade portions of the cavity where tie penetration and drying matter.
- **Cellular glass (Foamglas):** Vapor-impermeable, zero water absorption, structurally rigid, non-combustible. Used in bridge abutments and cold-storage. Very expensive. In a masonry cavity, the rigidity means ties must penetrate it cleanly; it doesn't crush around penetrations the way EPS does. Not a typical residential choice. The moisture impermeability means any water that gets in has nowhere to go — drainage design is critical. Overkill for residential above-grade use; may have merit at below-grade/below-slab applications where complete waterproofing is already provided.

**Code acceptance:** All of the above are accepted materials under ORSC R316 when covered on each concrete face by ≥1 inch of concrete. The thermal barrier exception applies regardless of foam type. Mineral wool is not foam plastic and has no R316 restrictions at all.

**Fire and tie compatibility:** For wall ties that penetrate the insulation layer, EPS and XPS can be pierced cleanly; mineral wool deforms around penetrations (requires tie plates); cellular glass requires drilling. No foam plastic in a fully covered concrete sandwich assembly triggers R316 ignition barrier concerns.

**Verdict for a concrete or double-wythe CMU wall:**
- **At/below grade:** EPS (vapor-open, moisture-tolerant, code-accepted for below-grade, economical, compatible with drainage mat systems).
- **Above grade, cavity position:** High-density mineral wool board if budget and tie design support it (non-combustible, vapor-open, no thermal drift, no R316 fire provisions); EPS as the code-safe economical alternative.
- **Above grade on exterior of outer wythe (continuous insulation):** Mineral wool or polyiso, depending on whether the exterior will be clad or exposed. Polyiso above grade on exterior is fine when protected (rainscreen or cladding) and drainage is provided.

Do not finalize a specific insulation product in the manuscript until the assembly detail is drawn — insulation choice interacts with tie spacing, WRB location, drainage plane, and local building official discretion on below-grade applications.

## FRP Wall Ties — What Is Known and What Isn't

This is the item that requires the most explicit limit-setting in the manuscript.

**What FRP wall ties are:** For concrete sandwich panels, fiber-composite connectors (typically GFRP, carbon, or basalt fiber with epoxy or vinyl ester matrix) span the insulation layer to connect the two concrete wythes. The leading product in this space is Thermomass, whose MC/MS Series connectors are tested in accordance with ICC-ES Acceptance Criteria 320 and listed with ICC-ES (ESR-1746), verifying their compliance with the building code.

**Thermal performance:** FRP ties have thermal conductivity roughly 0.3–1.0 W/m·K depending on fiber type and matrix — dramatically lower than steel (50 W/m·K) or stainless steel (14–16 W/m·K). This is the main argument for FRP over steel ties in a thermally broken wall. Each steel tie through an insulation layer creates a linear thermal bridge that significantly degrades the effective R-value of the assembly; FRP ties reduce this by an order of magnitude. The actual penalty depends on tie density, size, and insulation thickness — a PE with thermal bridging modeling experience (THERM software) can calculate this.

**Structural acceptance:** FRP bars have been successfully used as structural reinforcement in concrete members in building and bridge projects for the past three decades, and ICC-ES acceptance criteria AC454 and AC521 were developed under Section 104.11 of the IBC and R104.11 of the IRC. For wall ties specifically, the product approval path is through ICC-ES Evaluation Reports — each product requires its own ESR. Thermomass ESR-1746 is the precedent, but other FRP tie products require their own reports.

**For masonry wythe ties (not concrete sandwich):** The ORSC governs masonry wall ties in Section R606 (masonry) and references TMS 402 (Masonry Society standard). Standard masonry ties are specified in terms of steel wire gauge and embedment. Using FRP ties in a masonry cavity wall instead of standard steel ties is an alternate method under ORSC R104.11 — it requires the building official's approval and should be supported by manufacturer data and, ideally, an ICC-ES ESR. There is no code section that explicitly prohibits FRP masonry ties, but there is also no prescriptive path that explicitly permits them. This is a prior-approval situation.

**Fire exposure:** Structural FRP degrades when exposed to fire — resin matrix begins to soften around 200°C for most epoxy systems. For ties embedded in concrete, the concrete provides passive fire protection. For exposed tie ends in an accessible cavity, this is a design concern that varies by tie system.

**Corrosion behavior:** No galvanic corrosion; no moisture-induced corrosion. FRP ties are selected specifically for coastal and chloride-exposure environments for this reason.

**What the manuscript should and shouldn't say:** Chapter 8 can note that FRP wall ties are available with dramatically reduced thermal bridging compared to stainless steel, that Thermomass ESR-1746 is the established precedent for concrete sandwich walls, and that any FRP tie used in a masonry cavity wall application requires prior building official approval and manufacturer documentation. Do not state that FRP ties are code-permitted by right for masonry cavity walls in residential construction — that isn't currently true without an ESR.

## Radon — County Applicability, Statutory Authority, Code Layer

**Statutory authority:** ORS 455.365, enacted by SB 1025 (Chapter 83, 2010 Oregon Laws). This statute directed the Building Codes Structures Board and the Residential and Manufactured Structures Board to adopt radon construction standards.

**Mandatory counties:** The following Oregon counties require radon-resistant new construction (RRNC): Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, and Yamhill counties. These seven counties are codified in ORSC Appendix AF (the 2023 code renumbered it from Appendix F to Appendix AF). The county list is hard-coded in the appendix — adding a new county requires consultation with the Oregon Health Authority by the boards, not a local adoption vote.

**Local adoption mechanism:** Additional counties may be added as specified in Chapter 83, 2010 Oregon Laws (SB 1025), Section 2, after the boards consult with the Oregon Health Authority regarding local radon levels. A county cannot opt itself in unilaterally — expansion requires board action at the state level.

**Code layer — what Appendix AF requires:** For the seven mandatory counties, new construction must include:
- A minimum 4-inch gas-permeable layer (clean aggregate or geotextile drainage matting) under all concrete slabs within living spaces
- A 6-mil polyethylene soil-gas retarder membrane over the permeable layer
- Sealant at all concrete joints, penetrations, and gaps (polyurethane or elastomeric)
- A passive subslab depressurization vent pipe routed through the building and terminating above the roof, with the vent pipe accessible for future fan installation

**Does radon requirement vary by jurisdiction or risk map?** No. Within Oregon, applicability is binary by county as listed above. There is no map-triggered threshold mechanism in the ORSC. The EPA radon zone map (zone 1/2/3) does not control ORSC applicability — the county list in ORS 455.365/Appendix AF controls. If a site is in one of the seven counties, Appendix AF applies regardless of site-specific geology. If outside those counties, Appendix AF does not apply by code — though BSH should voluntarily build radon-ready regardless, as it's low cost and the sub-slab permeable layer doubles as drainage for moisture management.

## ANSI/AARST Standards — RRNC vs. CCAH

The two standards cover different scopes:

ANSI/AARST RRNC-2020 (Rev. 10/22) provides minimum requirements for the rough-in of radon control system components in new dwelling units under construction. RRNC is harmonized with ANSI/AARST CCAH, which additionally addresses activation of radon control systems, if required.

ANSI/AARST CCAH-2020 (Rev. 5/23) provides minimum requirements for the rough-in of radon control system components in new dwelling units under construction. CCAH also includes minimum requirements for verifying if radon concentrations are below the national action level and, if required, activation of radon control systems.

**For the manuscript:** RRNC is the appropriate citation for the code compliance layer — what you must build into the rough construction. CCAH is the appropriate citation for the verification/testing layer — the protocol that confirms whether the passive system is working or whether activation (adding a fan) is required. Oregon's ORSC Appendix AF does not explicitly reference either ANSI/AARST standard — it specifies construction techniques directly in the appendix text. However, both standards are recognized by the EPA and are compatible with Appendix AF requirements.

**Practical recommendation for BSH:** Build to RRNC (required or not, depending on county), and explicitly reference CCAH as the post-construction verification protocol. If the home is in one of the seven counties, Appendix AF controls the construction requirements. If outside those counties, stating compliance with ANSI/AARST CCAH is the strongest voluntary position. Do not conflate these — ORSC Appendix AF is the binding code layer in covered counties; ANSI/AARST standards are industry practice standards that exceed or complement the code layer.

## Below-Grade Waterproofing — Chapter 7 vs. Chapter 8 Split

The ORSC (Chapter 4, Sections R405–R406) controls below-grade waterproofing at the foundation level. Here is the framework:

**Dampproofing (R406.1) — default:** Except where required by Section R406.2 to be waterproofed, foundation walls that retain earth and enclose interior spaces and floors below grade shall be dampproofed from the higher of the top of the footing or 6 inches below the top of the basement floor, to the finished grade. Masonry walls shall have not less than 3/8 inch portland cement parging applied to the exterior of the wall. Acceptable dampproofing materials include bituminous coating, acrylic modified cement, surface-bonding cement (ASTM C887), or any waterproofing material from R406.2.

**Waterproofing (R406.2) — triggered by site conditions:** In areas where a high water table or other severe soil-water conditions are known to exist, exterior foundation walls that retain earth and enclose interior spaces and floors below grade shall be waterproofed from the finished grade to the higher of the top of the footing or 6 inches below the top of the basement floor. Compliant waterproofing systems include two-ply hot-mopped felts, 55-lb roll roofing, 40-mil polymer-modified asphalt, 60-mil flexible polymer cement, 1/8-inch cement-based fiber-reinforced waterproof coating, or 60-mil solvent-free liquid-applied synthetic rubber.

**Subsurface drainage (R405):** Required in tandem with waterproofing when waterproofing is triggered. Drain tile or perforated pipe at the footing, draining to daylight or sump.

**Chapter split for BSH manuscript:**
- **Chapter 7 (Foundation/Structural):** Full treatment of R405 drainage + R406 dampproofing/waterproofing — what the code requires, how the foundation wall is prepared, how insulation interfaces with the waterproofing layer at the foundation/wall transition.
- **Chapter 8 (Envelope):** Reference only — the envelope chapter should note that below-grade exterior insulation must be a material approved for below-grade wet-environment applications (per ORSC N1104.2.4 and N1104.2.3) and must be protected from mechanical damage, but the full waterproofing specification lives in Chapter 7. Do not duplicate the R406 treatment in Chapter 8 or you'll create a contradiction risk if you update one and not the other.

## Windows and Doors — Full Envelope Requirements

Pulling the complete picture from Chapter 11 and the general code:

**U-factor:** Windows U-0.27 (no SHGC requirement); skylights U-0.50; exterior doors U-0.20; doors with > 2.5 ft² glazing U-0.40. Rating source must be NFRC label or certified product list.

**Air leakage (N1104.4.3):** Windows, skylights and sliding glass doors shall have an air infiltration rate of not greater than 0.3 cfm per square foot, and for swinging doors not greater than 0.5 cfm per square foot when tested in accordance with NFRC 400 or AAMA/WDMA/CSA 101/I.S.2/A440 by an accredited, independent laboratory and listed and labeled by the manufacturer. Site-built windows, skylights and doors are excepted.

**Water rating:** The ORSC references AAMA/WDMA/CSA 101/I.S.2/A440 (commonly known as NAFS — North American Fenestration Standard) for testing. The relevant performance grade for a residential SFR depends on wind exposure and opening size. For most residential applications, Performance Class R (residential) at a Structural (S) test pressure consistent with local wind load is the baseline. No specific ORSC section mandates a water rating performance grade by number — that determination comes from NAFS and the design pressure calculation.

**Emergency escape and rescue openings (EERO):** ORSC R310 requires emergency escape openings in sleeping rooms. Minimum net clear opening area: 5.7 sf (with exceptions for grade-level openings at 5.0 sf); minimum net clear opening height: 24 inches; minimum net clear opening width: 20 inches; maximum sill height above floor: 44 inches. For masonry walls, the lintel over an EERO window opening must be designed for the dead load above plus any applicable live load — there is no ORSC-prescribed lintel table for masonry openings. Masonry lintels are designed per TMS 402/MSJC standard or as precast concrete per ACI 318, and the loading is verified by the building official. For a single-story concrete wall with a nominal 3-foot window opening, a standard precast lintel is easily engineered, but this must be documented on the structural drawings.

**Condensation resistance:** Not an ORSC mandatory requirement for residential. NFRC 500 provides a condensation resistance factor (CRF) rating. Not code-required in Oregon; relevant for BSH design guidance in cold-climate zones but should not be stated as a code requirement.

**Masonry flashing:** ORSC R703.8 (masonry veneer) and R703.1 (general weather resistance) require flashing at wall-to-window transitions. Flashing must be corrosion-resistant, extend to the exterior face of the wall or masonry, and provide drainage. For concrete/masonry walls with large window openings, the flashing at the sill, jamb, and head (or lintel soffit) is a critical detail — the ORSC does not specify materials but common practice is through-wall copper or stainless steel flashing at masonry lintel soffits.

## Roof Assembly Options for Concrete Slab Roof

For a concrete roof deck, the prescriptive Table N1101.1(1) flat ceiling path of U-0.021/R-49 does not translate directly. A 6-inch concrete slab has an R-value of approximately R-0.5 — you cannot achieve R-49 equivalent with concrete mass alone. The UA trade-off path is required.

**Insulation above deck (inverted or warm-roof):** Rigid insulation placed above the structural slab, below the membrane. EPS or XPS are common. Advantage: slab stays in the conditioned temperature range (protected from thermal cycling). For a flat or low-slope roof, this means the roofing membrane is the primary waterproofing layer, and insulation is between the membrane and any overburden. Drainage slope must be built into the insulation or the slab itself (minimum ¼ inch per foot per ORSC R905.10 for low-slope membranes).

**Insulation below deck (cold-roof):** Interior spray foam or rigid board on the underside of the slab. This creates a thermal bridge at any concrete projections (ribs, beams) and requires vapor control analysis — the slab will be cold in winter, so the interior face of the insulation must be the vapor control layer (closed-cell spray foam works; open-cell would need a separate vapor retarder).

**Vapor control for concrete roof:** ORSC R318 governs vapor retarders. In climate zones 4C and 5B, a Class II vapor retarder is required on the warm side of insulation in wall assemblies. For roofs, the requirement defaults to the drainage plane/membrane, which must be a Class I or II vapor control layer. For insulation-above-deck assemblies, the membrane is typically vapor-impermeable (TPO, EPDM, modified bitumen) — this provides the vapor control at the roof surface.

**Overflow and drainage:** ORSC R903.4 requires secondary (overflow) drainage for low-slope roofs where the primary drain can become clogged. Secondary drains or overflow scuppers must be sized to handle the full design rainfall. Parapet walls at low-slope roofs require through-wall scupper openings or secondary overflow drains at a height that prevents ponding above 2 inches.

**Parapet flashing:** At concrete parapet walls, the membrane must be terminated above the water line with a metal cap flashing or embedded termination bar. For a concrete BSH build, the parapet cap flashing detail is a significant design point — condensation within the parapet cavity, freeze-thaw cycling at cap mortar joints, and UV exposure at membrane terminations are all failure modes that need explicit detailing.

**The manuscript anchor:** A concrete roof assembly in Oregon requires the UA trade-off path, minimum ¼"/ft drainage slope, secondary overflow drainage per R903.4, and a vapor-impermeable membrane that doubles as vapor control for the assembly.

## Envelope Testing Standards — Chapter 8 vs. Testing Chapter

**Reader-facing in Chapter 8:**
- Blower door (ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380) — directly code-required, connects to CO timing, belongs in the envelope compliance discussion
- AAMA/WDMA/CSA 101/I.S.2/A440 (NAFS) — the window/door testing standard referenced by ORSC N1104.4.3; reader needs to know what label to look for when selecting windows

**Keep in the testing chapter:**
- ASTM E1557 / ASTM C1363 (hot box thermal testing) — characterizes actual assembly thermal performance, not a code requirement, appropriate for the testing chapter
- ASTM E1186 or ASTM E1105 (water infiltration testing) — not code-required at the residential scale, testing chapter
- Thermography / infrared scanning — not a code deliverable, belongs in testing chapter as a quality-verification tool
- Heat-flux meter measurements — research-grade, testing chapter
- Moisture content monitoring (ASTM E2170, continuous loggers) — not a code deliverable

**The line:** If the test result is a code deliverable that the building official sees before CO, it belongs in Chapter 8. If it's a quality-assurance or performance-verification tool that Jon performs independently to confirm the build quality, it belongs in the testing chapter. Blower door is the only test with a hard code hook (CO gate). Everything else is voluntary best practice.

## Annual IAQ and Moisture Review — Chapter Placement

This belongs in the **maintenance chapter**, not Chapter 8, the HVAC chapter, or the testing chapter.

The reasoning: IAQ and moisture review is an ongoing operational protocol — it happens after the building is complete and occupied, and it recurs annually. The HVAC chapter covers system selection and specification. The envelope chapter covers construction and code compliance at the time of permit. The testing chapter covers commissioning-phase testing (blower door, duct leakage, system balancing). None of those chapters are the right home for "here's what you do each year to verify the envelope is still performing."

The maintenance chapter is where BSH's 1000-year framework has the most value to add, since conventional construction documentation never addresses post-occupancy monitoring. Annual moisture review (checking for elevated readings at envelope transitions, slab perimeter, and roof penetrations with a calibrated moisture meter) and annual IAQ check (CO₂ baselines, VOC spot-check, radon test every 2 years per EPA guidance) are the right level of operational discipline for a building designed to last a century.

One caveat: if Chapter 8 includes a BSH-specific design performance target (e.g., "this assembly is designed to maintain interior RH below 60% without mechanical dehumidification"), that target creates the basis for the annual maintenance check. Reference it, don't duplicate it.
