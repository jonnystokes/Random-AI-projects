## Chapter 17: Build Sequence And Site Coordination

## Anchor sentence for the manuscript

Chapter 17 sequences the build around three independent timing systems that have to align: Oregon's land-use → trade → final inspection chain (ORS 215/197 → ORS 455/479/693/447/480/690/340 → ORSC §R109 / OSSC §110), the trade-licensing constraints on what Jon-personally can install at each stage (the three-layer framework Chapter 4 established — CCB, trade licenses, structural code), and the manufacturer/material curing/conditioning windows (ASTM/ACI for concrete, AAMA/ASTM E2112 for fenestration, manufacturer instructions controlling under ORSC §R102.4) — and on owner-builder rural Oregon builds, the binding-path order is land-use clearance and septic/well feasibility before the building permit is issued, not the other way around.

## 1. Permit sequence — the controlling chain (rural and urban)

The right mental model is not "go get a building permit," it's a dependency graph where downstream permits cannot issue until upstream ones close. For an Oregon owner-builder primary residence, the ordering is:

### Stage 1 — Land use and feasibility (months, not weeks)

**Land-use clearance** comes first. For rural builds in EFU, forest, or rural-residential zones, this means a county planning sign-off under ORS 215 (county zoning) and ORS 197 (statewide planning). For urban-growth-boundary builds, city zoning under the local code. The 2024 update to HB 2001 (residential infill), the wildfire hazard map under SB 762/HB 4044 (re-released 2024), and any applicable Goal 5 / Goal 7 / Goal 14 constraints can all materially change what the planning department signs off on.

**Septic feasibility / site evaluation** under OAR 340-071 (DEQ Onsite Wastewater Management) — a soil scientist or DEQ-licensed evaluator does a site evaluation; a Site Evaluation Report determines whether and what type of system is feasible (standard gravity, pressure, ATT, sand filter). The site evaluation has to be approved before a building permit can issue on a parcel without sewer.

**Well feasibility** under OAR 690-200 through 690-240 — the Oregon Water Resources Department issues water-use permits and requires a licensed well constructor for any new well. Existing well? Verify well log on file with OWRD and water rights status before relying on it.

**Driveway access permit / approach permit** — county for rural, city for urban, ODOT if access is to a state highway. ORS 374 governs ODOT approach permits. Driveway must meet fire-access standards (Oregon Fire Code §503; Appendix D for one- and two-family dwellings has specific dimensions for length, turnaround, vertical clearance).

**Wildfire hazard zone designation** — if the parcel is in the high or extreme hazard zone under the current map, ORSC Section R327 (the Oregon WUI provisions, formerly SB 762 implementation) imposes Class A roofing, ignition-resistant siding, ember-resistant venting, and defensible space requirements that materially change the design. Verify the current map status at the time of the design pass — the map has been re-issued and challenged repeatedly since 2022.

### Stage 2 — Building permit application

Building permit application under ORSC §R105 / OSSC §105 requires the construction documents (R106), the prior approvals from Stage 1, and any required PE stamps for items outside the prescriptive code. The plan review fee is paid at submittal; permit fee at issuance. Plan review timelines vary substantially — coastal counties and growth-pressured urban jurisdictions run longer than the rural inland counties.

**Erosion control / 1200-C permit** (DEQ NPDES construction stormwater) is required for any disturbance of 1 acre or more cumulatively. Under 1 acre but in a regulated area (within a UGB with local stormwater rules, or in a sensitive watershed) may still require local erosion-control approval. ORS 468B and OAR 340-045 are the statutory anchors.

### Stage 3 — Trade permits

Electrical (ORS 479 / 2023 OESC), plumbing (ORS 693 / 2023 OPSC), and mechanical (ORS 446-457 / 2022 OMSC) permits typically issue separately from the building permit. Some jurisdictions issue them all under a single combination permit; some require separate applications. The owner-builder exemption Chapter 4 anchored applies to these permits as well — Jon-personally pulling the permit on a residence he occupies and does not intend to sell at the time of permitting is the controlling test.

### Stage 4 — Inspections and final

ORSC §R109 lays out the required inspection points (foundation, plumbing/electrical/mechanical rough, framing, insulation, air sealing/blower door, gypsum, final). OSSC §110 covers commercial. The Certificate of Occupancy under §R110 / §111 is the gate to legal occupancy; partial or temporary CO under §R110.4 / §111.3 is available at the building official's discretion when life-safety items are complete.

### Practical scheduling note

Stage 1 (land use + septic + well) routinely takes 3–9 months in rural Oregon counties — sometimes longer if the wildfire hazard map status is contested or if water rights need to be perfected. Building permits through plan review typically run 4–12 weeks in unincorporated counties and longer in metro jurisdictions. Build the schedule backward from a target occupancy date with these realities baked in; do not assume any permit can be obtained on demand.

## 2. Permit expiration, extension, and inspection scheduling

**Permit expiration**: ORSC §R105.5 / OSSC §105.5 — building permits expire if work is not commenced within 180 days of issuance, or if work is suspended for 180 days. Some local AHJs have stricter rules; verify locally. Trade permits typically follow the same 180-day rule.

**Extensions**: §R105.5 allows the building official to grant one or more extensions of time for periods not more than 180 days each, on written request showing justifiable cause. Get extensions in writing before the permit expires; reviving an expired permit usually means a new permit application and a new fee.

**Inspection scheduling**: most jurisdictions accept inspection requests via online portal or phone with same-day or next-day inspection during business days. Same-day requests typically need to be in by 7–8 AM. Document every inspection request and result — screenshots of the portal status, email confirmations, dated photos of the inspector's tag or signed inspection record.

**Reinspection**: §R109 / §110 framework permits the building official to charge for reinspection when work is not ready, when work has not been corrected, or when the requested inspection cannot proceed because of access issues. Local fees vary — typically $50–$200 per reinspection. Avoid them by not calling for inspection until the work is complete and accessible.

**Partial approval**: building officials can approve specific portions of work and allow construction to proceed on those portions. The classic example is a foundation-only permit allowing the pour and cure to proceed while plan review continues on the superstructure. Coordinate with the plans examiner before assuming partial approval is available.

**Temporary occupancy (TCO)**: §R110.4 / §111.3 — building official may issue a temporary CO for a portion of the building safe to occupy before full completion. Typical conditions: life-safety systems complete (smoke alarms, CO alarms, egress, guards/handrails, working water and waste, working heat or working temporary heat, no exposed conductors), plus a clear written list of remaining work and a deadline. TCO is discretionary; don't assume it's available, and don't use it as a planning crutch.

**Certificate of Occupancy (CO)**: §R110 / §111 is the legal gate to occupancy. Required when all inspections are signed off, all energy code documentation is filed, all required testing (blower door, duct leakage, well water test where applicable, septic startup) is documented, and any conditions of approval are satisfied.

## 3. Owner-builder limits at each stage

The Chapter 4 framework controls and is recapped here in build-sequence form:

**CCB licensing (ORS 701)**: not required for owner-builder work on Jon's primary residence under the intent test. Stays valid through the entire build as long as the residence is not intended for sale at the time of permitting.

**Electrical (ORS 479.540)**: owner can perform new and substantial electrical work on owned, occupied, non-rental residence. Permits still required. New low-voltage installations within Class 2 product-load-side scope are exempt from permitting under OAR 918-261-0039.

**Plumbing (ORS 693.020)**: owner exemption for residential property the owner occupies; does not extend to mobile-home, multi-family, or rental work. Permits still required.

**Mechanical (ORS 447.115; 2022 OMSC)**: owner exemption parallels the plumbing/electrical structure for owner-occupied residential.

**Refrigerant work**: separate federal layer — Section 608 of the Clean Air Act requires EPA-certified technician for any work that handles HFCs, HFOs, or HCFCs (R-410A, R-454B, R-32, etc.). Brazing the refrigerant lines on a split-system heat pump, evacuation/charging, leak repair — all require EPA 608 certification. The four-tier framework is Type I (small appliances ≤5 lb), Type II (high-pressure), Type III (low-pressure), and Universal. For a typical residential heat pump the installer needs Type II at minimum, Universal in practice. Owner-builder exemption from Oregon trade licensing does not waive federal EPA 608. Practical implication: Jon can mount the indoor and outdoor units and run line set conduit, but a certified HVAC technician must do the brazing, evacuation, charging, and startup. Plan the schedule to bring that tech in for one-day service rather than spreading it across the build.

**Gas piping**: ORS 480.420–480.460 and OAR 837-030 (LP gas) plus OPSC Chapter 12 (fuel gas). LP gas has no homeowner exemption — must be installed by a licensed LP gas installer. Natural gas piping inside the building under OPSC Chapter 12 follows the plumbing exemption framework — owner-installed permitted in own occupied residence with permit and inspection.

**Structural**: owner can build to prescriptive ORSC paths without engineering. Anything outside prescriptive (irregular geometry, large openings without prescriptive bracing, alternative foundation systems, alternative wall systems other than those in ORSC Appendix S/AU/AW) requires a PE stamp. PE involvement is purchasing a service, not licensing Jon — Jon-personally still pulls the permit.

**Septic install**: OAR 340-071-0150 — installation by an Oregon-licensed installer. There is no homeowner exemption for septic system installation. Owner can do site work (excavation, backfill) under the licensed installer's supervision; pipe and tank install is the licensee's work.

**Well drilling**: OAR 690-210 — only an OWRD-bonded well constructor can construct a well. No owner exemption.

**Specialty work**: solar PV requires a licensed electrician for the line-side connection (under the ORS 479 framework even for owner-occupied with the homeowner exemption, the AHJ may scrutinize PV interconnection more closely; verify locally); fire sprinklers require a NICET-certified or licensed fire-protection contractor in most jurisdictions; elevators require BCD-licensed elevator mechanics under OAR 918-400.

**The sequence implication**: the owner-builder cannot just "schedule the trades" — Jon-personally is the trades for the bulk of the work, and the trade specialists (HVAC tech for refrigerant, well driller, septic installer, LP gas installer if applicable) are scheduled around Jon's owner-installed work, not the other way around.

## 4. Utility coordination

### Temporary power

Most Oregon serving utilities (PGE, Pacific Power, the rural co-ops — Consumers Power, Lane Electric, Central Lincoln PUD, Salem Electric, EWEB, etc.) require:

1. Electrical permit for the temporary service mast/pole (200 A pedestal is the standard residential rough-in).
2. Inspection sign-off on the temporary service from the BCD electrical inspector or the local jurisdiction's inspector.
3. Service application and connection fee paid to the utility.
4. Utility scheduling for the meter set and energization.

Lead time from utility application to energization runs 2–8 weeks for routine residential service drops, longer for any service that requires a transformer install or pole work. Get the temporary power application in early; it is one of the longest-lead utility items.

Per OESC and ORS 479, the temporary service must be a permitted, inspected installation — not a "we'll do it later" item. Use a manufactured construction service per OAR 918-261-0038 (100 A, 240 V single-phase or less) for the temporary, which is exempt from licensing requirements but still requires permit and inspection.

### Permanent electrical service

Application to the utility, easement documentation if the service crosses adjacent property, transformer pad installation (utility-supplied transformer, owner-supplied or contractor-supplied pad and conduit per utility's spec), service entrance conductors and meter base per the utility's standard, then meter set and energization after inspection sign-off on the service equipment.

Conduit from the transformer or service drop to the meter is the customer's responsibility on most Oregon utilities; size and burial depth per the utility spec sheet (typically 4-inch Schedule 40 PVC for residential 200 A, 24+ inches deep, with sweep elbows and tracer wire). Conduit runs longer than ~150 ft may need upsizing for voltage drop — verify with utility engineering.

### Internet and communications conduit

Per BSH's "no internet required" methodology this is still worth installing during the trench. Specify a separate 1-inch PVC conduit from the demarc to the street, with pull string. Even if the home is initially served by satellite or fixed-wireless, fiber-to-the-premises will eventually arrive in most Oregon service areas (the federal BEAD program funded via the IIJA is rolling out broadband through 2026–2030); having conduit in place means a one-day pull instead of a directional bore through finished landscaping.

### Utility trenching coordination

Best practice: dig the utility trench once, drop power, communications, and any low-voltage conduits in the same trench with required separations. NEC 300.5 controls minimum burial depths for power; communications follows separate minimums per Article 800. Typical separations: 12 inches between power and communications horizontally, or a barrier/raceway equivalent. Document the trench with photos before backfill — this is your future record of what's in the ground and where.

Locates: call 811 (Oregon Utility Notification Center, www.callbeforeyoudig.org) at least 2 business days before any digging. Required by ORS 757.541–757.571. This is nonwaivable — even for owner-builders on raw land, locates are required if any utility line could conceivably be present.

### Meter and disconnect placement

Per OESC and the serving utility's standards: meter base and main disconnect must be accessible, readable from outside the structure, weatherproof per the local environment. The 2023 NEC 230.85 requires an emergency disconnect on the exterior of one- and two-family dwellings — verify the local interpretation (some jurisdictions accept a service-rated disconnect at the meter; others require a separate labeled emergency disconnect). The Oregon BCD Statewide Code Interpretations file has rulings on 230.85 implementation; verify current interpretations at design pass.

### Service energizing

Request the meter set after the electrical inspector signs off on the service equipment. Most utilities will not energize without an inspection green tag or equivalent. Coordinate the meter set with the rough-in trade schedule so the electrical contractor (or Jon-personally) can complete the panel work and start interior trade work as soon as the meter is energized.

## 5. Site preparation

### Survey staking

Boundary survey by a registered Professional Land Surveyor (PLS) under ORS 672.025 — required before any site work if the lot lines are not clearly marked or if any setback is tight. Setback violations are expensive to fix after construction.

Building corners and grade stakes set by the surveyor or by the builder from the surveyor's reference points. Document staking with photos, GPS coordinates, and offsets to fixed references.

### Erosion control

Required at the start of site disturbance, not retrofitted later. Standard package: silt fence around the lower-elevation perimeter of disturbed area; inlet protection on any storm drain inlet within reach of sediment runoff; gravel construction entrance to capture mud from tires; stockpile covers (poly tarps weighted down) for any soil pile sitting more than ~14 days during the wet season (October–May in most of Oregon).

DEQ 1200-C NPDES permit applies to disturbances ≥1 acre (or smaller in regulated locations). The permit requires an Erosion and Sediment Control Plan (ESCP), weekly inspection records, and a Stormwater Pollution Prevention Plan (SWPPP). For sub-1-acre BSH builds, local jurisdiction requirements often parallel a simplified version of the same — verify locally.

### Stormwater management

Permanent stormwater management for the finished site is typically required by the local jurisdiction's stormwater code. Common residential approaches: roof drains to splash blocks then to dispersal across vegetated area; roof drains to bioswale or rain garden; roof drains to drywell or infiltration trench. Larger sites may require detention or treatment. Verify the specific stormwater design requirement at the planning/permitting stage; some jurisdictions require a stormwater plan stamped by a civil engineer.

### Driveway approach

Approach permit per Sec 1; install per the permit's typical-section requirement (compacted base, surface material, sight-distance verification, culvert if a roadside ditch is crossed). Fire access requirements under Oregon Fire Code §503 / Appendix D control width (12 ft minimum unobstructed, 13'-6" vertical clearance), turn radii (minimum 28 ft inside, 48 ft outside for typical apparatus; verify locally), grade (≤10% generally; up to 15% with AHJ approval), and turnaround for any driveway >150 ft.

### Culverts

Sized to handle the design storm at the crossing point — typically 25-year storm for residential driveway crossings, sometimes 50-year. CMP (corrugated metal pipe) or HDPE per local preference. Headwall and rip-rap energy dissipation at the outlet. ODFW review if the crossing is in fish-bearing waters (ORS 509.605–509.910 — fish passage requirements administered by Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife).

### Temporary sanitation

OR-OSHA Division 3/D (29 CFR 1926.51 by reference): one toilet for up to 20 workers; handwashing facility required. For owner-builder solo work, OR-OSHA does not technically apply — but practical sanitation matters and a porta-potty rental for the duration of the build is typical.

### Material staging

Plan staging areas before the build starts: where the framing lumber lands (close to the structure but not blocking access for concrete trucks), where the drywall lifts go (interior protection from weather), where the cabinetry lands (finish-grade protected staging). A single location that gets repeatedly walked over by foot traffic is usually a mistake — stage by phase.

### Jobsite address/signage

Required by Oregon Fire Code §505.1 — address numbers ≥4 inches tall, contrasting background, visible from the street. For long driveways, a separate address sign at the driveway entrance is required (and is often the only way emergency responders find the site). Required during construction as well as after occupancy — temporary signage acceptable during the build, permanent signage at occupancy.

## 6. Excavation safety and trenching

OR-OSHA Division 3/P (29 CFR 1926 Subpart P) controls excavation safety. The thresholds:

**Trench depths ≥5 ft** require a protective system (sloping, benching, shoring, or trench shield) unless excavated entirely in stable rock. **≥20 ft** requires a registered PE design.

**Soil classification** by a competent person per Appendix A of Subpart P: Type A (stable cohesive), Type B (intermediate), Type C (granular or wet). Maximum allowable slope angles: A = 53° (¾:1), B = 45° (1:1), C = 34° (1½:1). Most Oregon residential soils are Type B or C in practice — assume C unless tested.

**Spoil piles** at least 2 ft from the trench edge. Larger setback for Type C or wet soil.

**Access/egress**: ladders, stairs, or ramps within 25 ft of any worker in trenches ≥4 ft deep.

**Water accumulation**: workers cannot enter trenches with accumulated water unless adequate precautions are in place (continuous pumping, water removal equipment monitored, harnesses).

**Daily inspection** by a competent person before each shift and after any condition change (rain, vibration, surcharge load, etc.).

For owner-builder solo work, OR-OSHA does not apply to Jon-personally; for any paid worker on-site (subcontractor, day laborer), it does. Practical guidance: the standard exists because trenches kill more people per worker-hour than almost any other construction activity. Even solo, follow the rules.

### Practical residential framework

- Foundation excavations to ~4 ft (footing depths in most of Oregon's frost-zone areas — verify with local frost depth table; common values 12–18 inches in valley locations, deeper in eastern Oregon high desert): no protective system required, but maintain spoil pile setback.
- Foundation walls with full-height excavation to ~8–10 ft: protective system required; either cut back the slope per soil type or use shoring.
- Utility trenches: typically narrow and deep; protective system required at ≥5 ft.
- Septic drain field excavation: shallow, generally no protective system needed; coordinate with the septic installer who carries the OR-OSHA responsibility for their crew.

## 7. Concrete placement sequencing

Three independent timing factors govern: structural cure, weather window, and waterproofing/backfill readiness.

### Hot-weather and cold-weather placement

ACI 305.1 (Hot Weather Concreting): concrete temperature at delivery ≤95°F; ambient temperature plus relative humidity plus wind speed determine evaporation rate (use ACI 305 nomograph). When evaporation rate exceeds ~0.2 lb/sf/hr, surface checking and plastic shrinkage cracking risk rises sharply. Mitigations: schedule for early morning placement, fog spray, evaporation retarder, sun shade, wind break.

ACI 306R (Cold Weather Concreting): concrete temperature at placement minimum per ACI 306 table — 55°F for thin sections (<12 inches), 50°F for medium (12–36 inches), 45°F for large mass (>72 inches); maintain protected curing temperature for 3–7 days depending on mix design and placement size. Cold-weather placement under 40°F ambient requires heated enclosure or insulated blankets.

In the Willamette Valley, the practical pour window is March–November for foundation work without extensive protection; eastern Oregon high desert narrows further. Don't schedule a foundation pour for January in Bend without budgeting for heating.

### Curing and loading

ACI 318 / ACI 301: concrete must reach specified strength before removal of formwork supporting structural load. For typical 4000 psi residential mix:
- Vertical wall forms (no load): may strip in 24–48 hours; surface cure benefits from longer.
- Suspended structural members: per design, typically 7 days minimum with cylinder break verification, often 14 or 28 days for full design strength.
- Foundation footings supporting only their own weight and stem walls: stem wall pour can proceed 24–48 hours after footing pour.

Backfill against foundation walls per ACI 332 / engineering: typically not until the wall has cured to design strength AND first-floor framing or temporary bracing is in place to resist lateral earth pressure. For unbraced 8-ft tall walls, that's typically 7–14 days plus framed-and-decked first floor. Backfilling too early is a leading cause of foundation wall cracking.

### Waterproofing timing

Manufacturer-specific. For self-adhered membranes (Grace Bituthene, Henry Blueskin, Carlisle CCW): concrete substrate cured a minimum of 7 days (some manufacturers require 14 days or 28 days for full strength), surface dry, primer applied per manufacturer instructions, membrane installed within stated temperature and humidity windows. Hot-applied and fluid-applied systems have similar but distinct sequencing. The manufacturer instructions control under ORSC §R102.4 — read them before scheduling.

### Pump truck coordination

For any pour where the truck cannot back to the placement point: schedule the pump truck (line pump for pours <50 yards, boom pump for larger or hard-to-reach), confirm reach from staging area, verify access for outriggers, confirm return-line and washout location. Pump truck minimum hire is typically 4 hours with pour minimums; coordinate with ready-mix delivery to keep continuous flow without holding the pump truck idle.

### Inspection sequencing

ORSC §R109.1.1 lists foundation inspection as the first required structural inspection: after footings are excavated, forms are set, reinforcement placed, all required hardware (anchor bolts, holdowns, structural connectors, plumbing rough-ins through the footing) installed — but **before concrete placement**. Schedule the inspection with enough lead time that a failed inspection doesn't blow the concrete delivery slot. Some jurisdictions allow same-day inspection if requested early; many do not.

## 8. Masonry sequencing

Most BSH builds will be wood-framed, but masonry-bearing or masonry-veneer walls are within the BSH design space. For reinforced CMU:

**Mortar mixing and use window**: ASTM C270 — mortar must be used within 90 minutes of initial mixing (90-minute board time); retempering with water is permitted only to replace water lost to evaporation, not to revive partially-set mortar.

**Lift heights for grouted CMU**: TMS 602 / ACI 530.1 — fine grout lifts up to 5 ft without intermediate consolidation; coarse grout lifts up to 4 ft. High-lift grouting (>5 ft) requires cleanouts at the bottom course of every cell to be grouted, and verification of cleanliness before sealing the cleanouts.

**Cleanout requirement**: cleanouts ≥3 inches in least dimension at the bottom of each grout pour for any pour height >5 ft 4 inches (or as required by the controlling code/spec).

**Inspection points**: TMS 402 §3.1.6 / OSSC framework: typically reinforcement inspection after rebar placement and before grouting; grout inspection observing the pour; final masonry inspection. For Risk Category I (most residential), inspections may be reduced; verify with the local AHJ.

**Wall bracing**: temporary bracing required during construction per OSHA 1926 Subpart Q and Mason Contractors Association of America (MCAA) Standard Practice for Bracing Masonry Walls Under Construction. Walls cannot stand unbraced more than the limits in the standard, which depend on wall height, wind exposure, and soil type. Practical rule: brace any masonry wall >8 ft tall with diagonal bracing on each face at intervals not greater than 15 ft.

**Lintel handling**: precast lintels handled by lift equipment with rigging rated per the lintel weight; CMU lintels grouted in place after the bond beam reinforcement and bearing supports are in place; steel lintels per the structural drawings. Verify bearing length per the manufacturer or per the structural design (typically 4 inches minimum each end for residential lintels).

**Temporary shoring** of openings during construction until the bond beam or header is in place and grouted/cured.

## 9. Roof sequencing

### Fall protection

OR-OSHA Division 3/M (29 CFR 1926 Subpart M): fall protection required for any work surface ≥6 ft above a lower level. Owner-builder working solo on own residence is exempt from OR-OSHA but the falls hurt the same. Practical approach for the BSH build: rope-grab personal fall arrest system anchored to a temporary anchor through the ridge, used for any roof work. A few hundred dollars in equipment versus a permanent disability.

For employees / paid workers on-site: full OR-OSHA compliance — anchor points rated 5,000 lb per worker, full-body harness, shock-absorbing lanyard or self-retracting lifeline, written fall-protection plan if conventional methods are not feasible.

### Crane / lift requirements

For trusses or large structural members lifted to a roof level: typically a crane or boom truck with rigging rated for the load. Roof trusses for residential typically run 100–400 lb each; 30-ft trusses with engineered lift points are routinely set with a 12-ton boom truck. Verify reach and capacity at the boom angle required, not at the boom-down rating. The crane or boom-truck operator typically carries a license and the rigging insurance — Jon-personally typically does not need to certify to lift trusses, but anyone riding the load or working under a suspended load has OSHA training requirements.

### Roof inspection

ORSC §R109.1.4 includes the roof in the framing inspection (sheathing, structural members). Roofing inspection (the actual roof covering) is sometimes a separate inspection in some jurisdictions; verify locally. Inspection for ice barrier, underlayment, flashings, kick-out flashings (the corner where a roof slope meets a vertical wall — kick-out flashings prevent water intrusion at this critical detail and are explicitly required by ORSC §R903.2.1).

### Roofing manufacturer sequencing

Asphalt shingle manufacturers (GAF, CertainTeed, Owens Corning, Malarkey) require specific underlayment, starter strips, ridge ventilation, and nailing patterns per their warranty. Departing from the listed system voids the manufacturer's warranty even if the AHJ accepts the install. Specify by manufacturer's installation instructions; document the install with photos of nailing pattern, underlayment overlap, flashing details.

Standing seam metal roofing has manufacturer-specific clip spacing, panel engagement, and flashing details; closed-cell SPF foam roofing has applicator-certification requirements and curing/recoat windows; TPO/EPDM single-ply membrane has seam welding/adhering requirements that need controlled conditions.

### Waterproofing inspection

ORSC §R905 specifies underlayment and flashing requirements per roof covering type. Critical inspection points: ice barrier (where required by climate — the western Oregon valley typically does not require ice barriers, eastern Oregon and high-elevation builds do; ORSC §R905.1.2), underlayment overlap and attachment, valley waterproofing, penetration flashings, drip edge.

### Roof drainage

ORSC §R903 — gutters and downspouts required where roof drainage discharges within 5 ft of the building or causes erosion. Sized per local rainfall intensity (typically 1 sq inch of downspout per 100 sq ft of roof drainage area in coastal/wet Oregon, less in dry climates). Discharge per local stormwater code — splash blocks, dispersal pipes, drywells, or rain gardens.

### Weather-window assumptions

Western Oregon roof installation works year-round but reliably dry-in is best between mid-June and mid-October. Eastern Oregon high desert: similar window with snow load risk in shoulder seasons. Schedule roof tear-off and dry-in to fit a single weather window — never tear off shingles with rain in the 48-hour forecast unless the dry-in is on-deck.

## 10. Windows and exterior doors

### Ordering and lead time

Custom windows and exterior doors typically run 6–16 week lead times in 2026; production lines for major manufacturers (Andersen, Marvin, Pella, Milgard, Sierra Pacific, Loewen) have shifted with supply-chain pressure since 2020 and timing varies. Treat lead time as verify-at-order, not assume-from-history. Order at the framing schedule decision point so windows arrive before sheathing/WRB is closed in.

### Rough-opening tolerances

Manufacturer-specified, typically ½ inch wider and ½ inch taller than the unit dimension for residential vinyl/aluminum-clad windows (some manufacturers require ¼ inch, some allow ¾ inch). The construction documents should specify the rough-opening size based on the chosen unit; deviation from the manufacturer's tolerance can invalidate the installation warranty and can prevent correct shimming and squaring.

### Flashing standards

ASTM E2112 (Standard Practice for Installation of Exterior Windows, Doors and Skylights) is the procedural authority for sequence. The four-step "shingle-style" approach: (1) sill flashing first, draped down over the WRB below; (2) install window with manufacturer-approved fastening; (3) jamb flashing over the nailing flange and lapped over the sill flashing; (4) head flashing over the top of the unit, lapped under the WRB above. AAMA 711 (self-adhering flashing) and AAMA 712 (fluid-applied flashing) cover product performance. Manufacturer's specific instructions control under ORSC §R102.4.

### Safety glazing

ORSC §R308 — required in hazardous locations: in or adjacent to doors (within 24 inches of door edge AND ≤60 inches above floor), glazing in tubs/showers (within 60 inches of any walking surface adjacent to wet area), glazing in windows where the glass is large (>9 sq ft), bottom edge <18 inches above floor, top edge >36 inches above floor, AND a walking surface within 36 inches of the glazing. Specify tempered or laminated per the supplier's product line.

### Egress requirements

ORSC §R310 — every sleeping room and every basement (regardless of sleeping use) must have at least one emergency escape and rescue opening. Minimum net clear opening 5.7 sq ft (5.0 sq ft for grade floor or below grade), minimum height 24 inches, minimum width 20 inches, sill height ≤44 inches above floor. Window manufacturers publish egress tables for each model; verify the specific unit specified meets the egress requirement BEFORE ordering — a window that meets the rough-opening dimensions but not the operable clear-opening dimensions is a costly mistake.

### Water testing

For high-end builds and any unit installed in a critical location, ASTM E1105 field water testing can verify the install. Not typically required by ORSC, but worth specifying for the BSH durability target.

### Manufacturer installation requirements

Read them before ordering. Each manufacturer has subtly different fastening patterns, sealant requirements, shim requirements, and integration details with various WRB types. Train the installer (or yourself) on the specific product before the first unit goes in.

## 11. Rough-in sequencing

The classical order, after framing is complete and the building is dried-in:

1. **Plumbing top-out / DWV first** — heaviest pipes, most clearance demand, drains have to slope to specific points. Plumbing rough includes all DWV venting through the roof (penetrations to be flashed by the roofer), all supply lines stubbed to fixture locations with rough-in valves, gas lines for any gas appliance.

2. **Mechanical rough second** — ductwork sized at 6–14 inches takes the next-largest paths; HRV/ERV ducting; refrigerant line set runs (sleeves through framing for insulated copper or LineSet conduit, with the brazing left for the EPA-608 tech later); condensate drains; bath and laundry exhaust ductwork.

3. **Electrical rough third** — flexible Romex or conduit fits around the larger fixed plumbing and mechanical. Box locations per plan, switch and outlet boxes set to finished-wall depth, panel rough-in with circuits home-run to box and labeled.

4. **Low-voltage rough last** — Cat6A, coax, speaker wire, alarm, security cabling, smart-home satellites. The 2-inch separation rule from NEC 800.133 / 725.136 (Chapter 15) means low-voltage is best run after high-voltage is in place so you can route around it.

### Firestopping

ORSC §R302 / OSSC §714 — firestopping required at penetrations of fire-resistance-rated assemblies and at draftstops in concealed spaces. For typical single-family wood-framed BSH builds, the principal firestop locations are: top and bottom plates (caulk or fireblocking around penetrations), garage/dwelling separation (the wall and ceiling assembly between attached garage and dwelling), and any required draftstop in attics/floor cavities exceeding 1,000 sq ft. Schedule firestop before insulation.

### Energy code rough-in items

ORSC Chapter 11 / 2025 OEESC for any commercial portion: blower-door test target (ORSC currently requires ≤3.0 ACH50 for new construction in most jurisdictions, with stricter values for Reach Code), duct leakage test (ducts in unconditioned spaces ≤4 cfm/100 sf at 25 Pa), insulation R-value verification, fenestration U-factor and SHGC verification. Most of this is verified at insulation/drywall inspection or at final, but the rough-in is what enables passing those tests later — air sealing every penetration, gasketing top and bottom plates, sealing recessed light cans (use IC-AT rated only).

### Inspection sequence

ORSC §R109 typical sequence: foundation → underfloor framing/plumbing if applicable → rough plumbing → rough electrical → rough mechanical → framing (after all rough-ins complete and all firestopping/fire-blocking installed) → insulation/air-sealing → drywall (some jurisdictions skip) → final. Some jurisdictions combine rough trade inspections with framing inspection; verify locally.

The framing inspection cannot proceed until all rough trade inspections are signed off — a missed plumbing or electrical inspection blocks framing inspection, which blocks insulation, which blocks drywall. The schedule cascades.

## 12. Air sealing, insulation, radiant, slab readiness

### Air sealing

Test target as above. Practical air-sealing checklist for the BSH build:
- All penetrations through top/bottom plates sealed (mastic or fire-rated caulk depending on assembly)
- All penetrations through subfloor sealed
- All electrical box flanges sealed to drywall (use box gaskets or caulk after drywall)
- All recessed lights IC-AT rated and sealed at ceiling
- All bath/kitchen exhaust ducts sealed at house wrap penetration
- All HVAC ducts in unconditioned spaces sealed (mastic, not tape — UL 181B-M / B-FX rated)
- Rim joist insulated and air-sealed (closed-cell SPF or rigid foam with caulked perimeter)
- Attic hatches insulated and weatherstripped

### Insulation

Per ORSC §N1102.1 / Reach Code where adopted. For Climate Zone 4 (most western Oregon valley): R-49 ceiling, R-21 wall (with some exceptions), R-30 floor, R-15 basement wall, R-10 slab edge. Climate Zone 5 (higher elevations, eastern Oregon): higher values. The 2025 OEESC and any Reach Code adoption push these up.

Order matters: ceiling and wall insulation after rough-ins and framing inspection; floor insulation and rim joist insulation can go in earlier since they don't compete with other trades.

### Radiant tubing

Hydronic radiant typically tied to manifolds with PEX-A or PEX-B (oxygen barrier per DIN 4726 mandatory). Tubing layouts and spacing per the manifold/system design (typically 9–12 inches on-center for slab applications, 8 inches in high-loss zones). Pressure-tested before slab pour at 1.5× operating pressure (typically 80–100 psi for residential) with verification by the inspector and documented pressure hold.

### Hydronic pressure testing

Hold pressure under test for the duration required by the AHJ (typically 1–2 hours for the inspector; longer holds are best practice). Document the gauge reading at start and end, photograph with date stamp, retain in closeout records.

### Topping slab

Lightweight gypsum-based or sand-based topping slab over radiant tubing per the system designer. Curing: gypsum-based 7+ days, sand-based per ACI standards. Manufacturer-specific moisture and pH targets before any flooring goes down.

### Slab moisture

For any flooring sensitive to moisture (engineered wood, vinyl, rubber, epoxy): ASTM F2170 in-situ relative humidity probes are the current default test method (calcium chloride per ASTM F1869 is older and less reliable). Most flooring manufacturers require ≤75% RH at the slab; some require ≤80% RH; specialty epoxies require ≤70% RH. Test before flooring install; document results.

### Finish-floor readiness

In addition to moisture testing: surface flatness (typically F_F 25–35 for residential per ACI 117), surface cleanliness, completion of all overhead trades that could damage the floor, environmental conditioning (temperature and humidity per the flooring manufacturer's spec for at least 72 hours before, during, and after install).

## 13. Cover, drywall, fire-rated assemblies

Drywall goes up after insulation inspection and any required Reach Code documentation is filed. Critical sequencing items:

**Wet-area backing**: cement board (ASTM C1325) or glass-mat gypsum (ASTM C1658) at tub/shower walls. ANSI A118.10 waterproofing membrane over the substrate before tile. Schluter Kerdi, USG Durock Fiboard, Laticrete Hydro Ban — manufacturer-specific systems with manufacturer-specific accessory requirements. Tier-1 substitution restriction (Sec 2 of Chapter 16): use only the manufacturer-supplied accessories.

**Fire-rated assemblies**: garage/dwelling separation per ORSC §R302.6 — at minimum ½-inch gypsum on the garage side, 5/8-inch Type X on any wall supporting habitable space above the garage. Fire-rated door per §R302.5.1 (1-3/8 inch solid wood, solid steel, or 20-minute fire-rated). Penetrations of the fire-rated wall sealed per the listed assembly. Verify the listed assembly UL number on the installation; document with photos.

**Foam thermal barrier**: ORSC §R316.4 — foam plastic insulation (SPF, rigid polystyrene, polyiso) requires a thermal barrier on the interior side of the foam. Generally ½-inch gypsum board or equivalent. Thermal barrier required even on closed-cell SPF in attics and crawlspaces unless specific exception applies (§R316.5 — attic and crawlspace exceptions for ignition barriers in some configurations).

**Smoke alarms / CO alarms**: ORSC §R314 (smoke) and §R315 (CO). Smoke alarms in every sleeping room, immediately outside each sleeping area, on every story including basements. CO alarms outside each separate sleeping area in dwellings with fuel-burning appliances or attached garages. New construction requires hardwired with battery backup, interconnected so all alarms sound when one triggers. UL 217 (smoke) / UL 2034 (CO) listing required. Document with photos at final inspection.

**Final inspection**: ORSC §R110 — life-safety items first (alarms, egress, guards/handrails, GFCI/AFCI verification, all utilities energized, all fixtures functional, water heater commissioned, HVAC commissioned), then cosmetic completion.

## 14. TCO and CO

The minimum bar for a Temporary Certificate of Occupancy varies by jurisdiction but generally requires:

- All life-safety items signed off: smoke and CO alarms tested, guards and handrails per §R311–R312, egress windows operable and sized correctly, GFCI and AFCI protection per OESC, all electrical panel labeling complete, no exposed conductors.
- Minimum habitable spaces complete: at least one bathroom (functional toilet, lavatory, tub or shower), kitchen with sink and counter (full appliances may not be required for TCO at AHJ discretion), at least one bedroom complete with egress.
- Permanent power energized; permanent water and waste functional; permanent heat functional (or temporary heat acceptable per AHJ).
- Stairs, guards, handrails, exit doors per code.
- Address numbers visible.
- Septic system commissioned (if applicable).
- Well water tested for potability (if applicable) — typically coliform/bacterial test required by some jurisdictions; arsenic/nitrate testing required in some areas.

The TCO will list remaining work and a deadline (typically 90–180 days). Failure to complete by the deadline can trigger occupancy revocation and code-enforcement action.

The CO requires everything in the TCO list complete plus all remaining permitted work signed off. After CO, any subsequent work that requires a permit (additions, alterations, system replacements) starts a new permit cycle.

For BSH-LLC sale of the home: the CO is the threshold for marketing the home as a finished residence. Selling a home before CO is risky — Oregon Seller Property Disclosure requirements (ORS 105.464–105.490) require disclosure of any non-permitted or non-final-inspected work, and a buyer who discovers post-close that work was not signed off has a basis for claims.

## 15. Phase duration, labor-hours, and cost

Per the briefing's framing, all of these are verify-at-final-scope items. The framework that doesn't depend on specific numbers:

### Phase duration drivers

- **Site-specific factors**: lot access, soil type, slope, distance from utility connection points, distance from supplier centers
- **Season**: October–April in western Oregon adds time to every exterior phase (foundation, framing, roofing, siding, exterior paint)
- **Hired-help strategy**: solo owner-builder takes ~2–4× longer than contractor-built for the same scope, but cost-per-task is much lower for owner-installed work
- **Trade dependencies**: the EPA-608 tech, well driller, septic installer, surveyor, and inspector are external dependencies whose schedules constrain the build calendar
- **Permit cycle**: every plan-revision cycle adds 2–8 weeks
- **Material lead times**: trusses, custom windows/doors, structural steel, manufactured stair components — verify at order

### Labor-hour estimating frameworks

Industry references for owner-builder estimating: NAHB (National Association of Home Builders) labor-hour databases, RSMeans (commercial-skewed), Craftsman National Construction Estimator (residential-skewed). All publish annual updates with regional factors. Treat the published hours as a starting point and adjust for owner-builder learning curve (typically 1.5× for first occurrence of a task).

### Contractor-day vs. owner-builder-day

A contractor crew of 3–4 framers will dry in a typical 2,000 sq ft single-story house in 1–2 weeks. A solo owner-builder doing the same work runs 6–10 weeks for the same task, with weather interruptions. Build the BSH schedule honestly with the actual labor pool.

### Cost assumptions

Treat all cost figures as dated quotes. Build a spreadsheet at estimating with three columns per line item: estimated cost (current quote), date of quote, source of quote. Refresh quotes within 30 days of order for any item over a meaningful threshold (typically $1,000 for materials, $5,000 for subcontracted labor).

### Documentation of actual

Track the actual hours spent and actual cost paid for each phase as the build progresses. This becomes the BSH methodology's empirical labor-hour database — the YouTube/methodology side of the business depends on this data being collected, not estimated.

## Limits / what isn't in the law

- **There is no Oregon statewide standard inspection sequence** beyond the ORSC §R109 framework. Local AHJs vary in how they combine or split inspections, what they require photographic documentation of, and how they handle re-inspections. Verify with the specific permitting jurisdiction at design pass.
- **TCO is discretionary**, not a right. A building official can deny TCO even when the minimum life-safety items are complete, if there are conditions of approval that haven't been met or if the building official determines that occupancy would create a hazard. Do not assume TCO availability in the schedule.
- **The EPA-608 refrigerant requirement is federal** and does not yield to Oregon's owner-builder exemption framework. Plan around it.
- **Permits cascade**: a missed inspection or failed inspection blocks downstream inspections, which blocks downstream work. Build the schedule with buffer for inspection failures (typically assume 1 in 4 inspections will require re-work or re-inspection on a complex owner-builder build).
- **The wildfire hazard map status** materially affects rural builds and is unsettled as of May 2026 — re-released maps have been challenged repeatedly. If the build site is in or near a high/extreme zone on the current map, get the current designation in writing from the county before designing the envelope (Class A roof, ignition-resistant siding, ember-resistant venting, defensible space requirements all flow from this designation).
- **Utility timelines do not bend for the build schedule**. Temporary power, transformer install, meter set are utility-controlled. Apply early.
- **The OR-OSHA framework applies to paid workers, not to Jon-personally on his own residence**. The moment a paid helper steps on the site, the rules attach.

## When to bring in paid help

- **Civil engineer** for any erosion control plan or stormwater design beyond the prescriptive jurisdiction template, and for any culvert/crossing in fish-bearing waters.
- **PLS (Professional Land Surveyor)** for boundary verification and corner staking — required if any setback is tight or if the property has any survey ambiguity. ORS 672.025 framework.
- **Structural PE** for any non-prescriptive structural element. The PE-stamped portion is what the building official requires; the PE relationship is on a service-purchase basis, not a licensing one.
- **Construction attorney** for any unusual permitting situation (e.g., wildfire map appeal, septic feasibility appeal, land-use appeal under ORS 197.825 / LUBA).
- **Geotechnical engineer** if the soil is not clearly Type B/C standard residential conditions — slopes >15%, expansive clay (some Willamette Valley locations), saturated soils, or any history of slope movement on the parcel.
- **Mechanical engineer** for any non-standard HVAC configuration (geothermal, large central air handler, multi-zone hydronic with heat pump, ERV with complex zoning).
- **Energy consultant / HERS rater** for Reach Code compliance verification or for incentive program eligibility verification.

# Chapter 18: Inspection And Approval Sequence

## Anchor sentence for the manuscript

The Oregon inspection system has one statutory backbone (ORSC §R109 / OSSC §110, ORS 455.148/.150 governing which jurisdiction administers the program, and ORS 455.690 governing appeals) but multiple parallel inspection authorities (BCD, the local building department, the local plumbing inspector, the local electrical inspector — sometimes BCD, sometimes county, sometimes city — county environmental health for septic, OWRD for wells, the road authority for the driveway approach, the serving utility for the meter set, the fire authority for fire access and any sprinkler work, and an approved third-party tester for blower door and duct leakage); the BSH build sequence has to script around all of them, the cover-and-conceal prohibition in §R109.1 governs the build flow, and the closeout package has to assemble inspection records into a single audit-ready binder before the CO can issue.

## 1. The statutory framework

**ORSC §R109 / OSSC §110** lays out the inspection requirement. Verbatim from §R109.1: "Construction or work for which a permit is required shall be subject to inspection by the building official and such construction or work shall remain accessible and exposed for inspection purposes until approved." This is the legal hook for everything in this chapter — work that has been covered, backfilled, energized, pressurized, insulated, drywalled, or occupied before approval is presumptively non-compliant and the building official may require uncovering at the permit holder's expense.

**ORSC §R109.3 / OSSC §110.3** puts the burden of providing access on the permit holder: "It shall be the duty of the permit holder to provide access to and means for proper inspection."

**ORSC §R109.2 / OSSC §110.2** allows the building official to accept reports from approved agencies (third-party testing labs, ICC-ES reports, special inspectors per OSSC Chapter 17) — this is how blower door, duct leakage, and engineering-intensive items get accepted into the inspection record without the building official personally witnessing the test.

**ORS 455.148 (comprehensive municipal building inspection programs) and ORS 455.150 (selective programs)** govern which jurisdictions administer their own inspection programs vs. delegating to BCD. Most populated Oregon counties and cities run their own programs; BCD provides direct services in Curry, Grant, Morrow, and Umatilla counties as of mid-2026, plus the statewide programs for elevators, manufactured dwellings, prefabricated structures, boilers, and amusement rides.

**ORS 455.160** specifically prohibits jurisdictions from failing to provide timely inspections and creates a mandamus remedy if they do — practically irrelevant for a routine residential build, but worth knowing if a jurisdiction ever stalls on a politically charged BSH project.

## 2. Inspection list — typical Oregon residential build

The exact list depends on the jurisdiction, the project complexity, and any conditions of approval. The minimum statutory list under ORSC §R109.1 is short (foundation, plumbing/electrical/mechanical rough, frame and masonry, insulation/vapor retarder, final). The practical list every BSH build will face is much longer:

### Site and pre-construction (before the building permit can issue)

- Land-use sign-off (planning department; ORS 215/197)
- Septic site evaluation approval (county env health, delegated by Oregon DEQ under OAR 340-071)
- Well construction report on file (OWRD; not technically a building inspection but a documentation gate)
- Driveway/access approach permit (county roads; ODOT for state highways under ORS 374)
- Erosion and sediment control plan approval (DEQ 1200-C if ≥1 acre, or local stormwater authority)
- Fire-access verification (local fire authority or AHJ enforcing OFC §503)
- Address posting confirmation (local building dept)

### Footing, foundation, and below-grade work

- Setback / property staking verification
- Footing excavation inspection (depth to undisturbed soil, frost protection, bearing material)
- Foundation reinforcement inspection (rebar size, spacing, cover, splices, anchor bolts, holdowns, CEE/UFER ground per NEC 250.52(A)(3))
- Underfloor / under-slab inspection — vapor retarder, insulation, in-slab piping, conduit, sleeves, radon barrier where required, radiant tubing pressure test
- Foundation drainage / footing drains
- Foundation waterproofing (some jurisdictions do separate inspection; many roll into pre-backfill)
- Pre-backfill inspection — many jurisdictions require this distinct from the foundation inspection; verify locally

### Masonry and structural

- Masonry rebar placement (before grouting)
- Grout placement / cleanout verification (for high-lift)
- Bond beam / lintel inspection
- Floor framing and floor sheathing
- Wall framing
- Roof framing and roof sheathing
- Shear wall nailing (separate inspection in many jurisdictions for shear-critical assemblies)
- Engineered-item documentation (truss layout per truss manufacturer drawings; LVL/glulam per stamped engineering)

### Rough trades

- Rough plumbing — DWV pressure test (water or air), supply pressure test, gas piping pressure test if applicable, fixture rough locations
- Rough mechanical — duct rough, exhaust runs, refrigerant line rough (line set in place but not brazed), condensate routing, makeup-air paths
- Rough electrical — service rough, panel rough, branch circuit rough-in, box locations, GFCI/AFCI circuit identification, smoke/CO alarm rough, low-voltage/communications rough
- Firestopping — at penetrations of fire-rated assemblies; sometimes inspected as part of framing, sometimes separately
- Frame and masonry combined inspection (after all rough trades sign off; per §R109.1.4)
- Moisture content verification (§R109.1.4.1) — written notification by the GC (or owner-builder) that moisture-sensitive framing members are ≤19% moisture content before interior finishes are installed

### Pre-cover

- Insulation inspection (after install, before drywall; §R109.1.5.2)
- Air-sealing visual inspection (or blower door report submitted; depends on energy-code path)
- Vapor retarder verification (where required by §R702.7 / climate zone)
- Drywall inspection — many Oregon jurisdictions do not require a separate drywall (gypsum) inspection for residential; some do, particularly for fire-rated assemblies and shear walls

### Energy code compliance

- Blower door test result (third-party tester; §N1104.8 / §N1105 framework; required ACH50 target depends on path chosen — 3.0 ACH50 for the Reach Code, 4.0 ACH50 base ORSC for some paths, with variation)
- Duct leakage test (third-party tester; ducts in unconditioned spaces ≤4 cfm/100 sf at 25 Pa)
- Insulation R-value / U-factor verification (signed certification or manufacturer label retention)
- Fenestration NFRC label verification (the inspector verifies the label is still attached at the rough)
- Lighting compliance — high-efficacy lighting verification at final
- HVAC equipment efficiency verification (AHRI certificate)
- ERI compliance documentation if the ERI path is chosen (NA1109 framework — third-party HERS rater certification)

### Final trade and final building

- Plumbing final — fixtures connected, pressure under load, water heater commissioned, backflow tested if applicable
- Electrical final — devices in place, panel labeled, all circuits energized and tested, GFCI/AFCI tested, smoke/CO alarms tested, service energized
- Mechanical final — equipment installed, startup completed, controls operational, commissioning documentation submitted
- Building final (§R109.1.6 / §R110) — life safety items, egress, guards, handrails, address numbers, well water test result if applicable, septic startup if applicable

### Occupancy

- Temporary Certificate of Occupancy (§R110.4) — discretionary
- Certificate of Occupancy (§R110) — final approval to occupy

## 3. Who handles which inspection (the jurisdictional matrix)

This is one of the most-misunderstood aspects of the Oregon system. The answer depends on the specific jurisdiction. The general patterns:

**Building department** (city or county, or BCD where it administers) — handles structural, framing, foundation, energy, fire-rated assemblies, drywall (where inspected separately), final building, TCO, CO. The building department employs Oregon-certified building inspectors per OAR 918-098.

**Plumbing inspector** — separate plumbing inspector employed by the same building department or a separate program; some jurisdictions cross-staff (one inspector certified in multiple disciplines), some keep them separate. Handles all OPSC inspections.

**Electrical inspector** — also separate from the building inspector. In some jurisdictions the city/county runs the program; in many parts of Oregon, BCD runs the electrical program directly (BCD's regional offices in Salem, Pendleton, Coos Bay, and Bend cover counties without their own electrical programs). The Coos County example: county inspectors handle plumbing and electrical, BCD covers the cities of Lakeside, Coos Bay, and North Bend for structural — variations like this are common.

**Mechanical inspector** — typically rolled into the same staff as the building inspector for residential; HVAC-specific inspectors more common in commercial.

**County environmental health** — septic site evaluation, septic install inspection, septic operation/maintenance verification; delegated authority from Oregon DEQ under OAR 340-071. Some counties have their own program; others run the DEQ program directly. Verify locally.

**Oregon DEQ** — directly handles 1200-C NPDES construction stormwater permits (>1 acre disturbance); also retains authority over septic in counties without delegated programs.

**Oregon Water Resources Department (OWRD)** — well construction reports, water rights perfection. Not on-site for inspection but documentation gate.

**Roads department** (county) — driveway approach permit and inspection; final approach grade and drainage.

**ODOT** — driveway approach to a state highway; ORS 374 framework.

**Serving utility** (PGE, Pacific Power, NW Natural, rural co-op, etc.) — meter set, service energization. The utility energizes after the BCD or local electrical inspector has signed off on the service equipment with a "green tag" or equivalent.

**Fire authority** — fire access verification (driveway dimensions, turnaround, clearance), any fire sprinkler inspection (if required for the project type — most single-family residences in Oregon do not require sprinklers, but check local amendments and the wildfire hazard map provisions). Some jurisdictions have a separate fire marshal review at building permit application.

**Third-party tester** — blower door tester (BPI IDL or RESNET HERS Rater certification typical), duct leakage tester (same certifications), HERS rater for ERI path compliance, special inspector for any structural item subject to OSSC Chapter 17 special inspection requirements (most BSH residential is not subject to special inspections, but verify), water testing lab for well coliform/nitrate testing.

For the BSH manuscript: build a project-specific matrix at design pass that names the agency for each inspection, with contact info and scheduling method. Different jurisdictions group these differently; the matrix is verify-at-design-pass.

## 4. Inspection scheduling logistics

### Method

Most Oregon jurisdictions accept inspections through one of three channels:

1. **Oregon ePermitting (BuildingPermits.Oregon.gov)** — the BCD-administered statewide system, accepted by 100+ Oregon jurisdictions including BCD-direct counties. Online scheduling, status tracking, inspector notes available post-inspection. The inspection request goes to the assigned inspector for the jurisdiction.

2. **Local jurisdiction portal** — Portland's PortlandMaps Permits, Multnomah County, Washington County, Lane County, Deschutes County, and a few others run their own systems.

3. **Phone request** — most jurisdictions still accept phone scheduling; some require it for specific inspection types.

### Timing rules (typical, verify locally)

- **Same-day requests**: typically accepted if submitted before 7:00 AM the day of inspection (some by 8:00 AM); not all jurisdictions offer this.
- **Next-day requests**: standard; submit by 4:30 PM the prior business day.
- **Weekend/holiday inspections**: generally not available; some jurisdictions offer paid emergency inspections.
- **Inspection windows**: most jurisdictions give a half-day window (AM or PM); some give a four-hour window; very few commit to a specific time.
- **Rural inspection limitations**: in less-populous counties, the inspector may cover a large geographic area on specific routes — Tuesdays in the north end of the county, Thursdays in the south end. This compresses the schedule. Verify the route schedule for the build's specific area before assuming any-day availability.
- **Cancellation**: typically free if cancelled before midnight prior; some jurisdictions charge if cancelled the morning of.
- **Reinspection fees**: typically $50–$200 per reinspection. Charged when the work is not ready, not accessible, or when a previously failed item has not been corrected.

### Practical scheduling discipline

- Schedule inspections at least one day in advance unless the jurisdiction reliably offers same-day.
- Confirm in writing (portal note, email) what the inspection is for and what's ready.
- Be on-site for the inspection or have someone competent to answer questions — inspectors will fail or defer if no one can speak to the work.
- Photograph the inspector's tag/signed card after pass.
- Read inspector notes after the inspection in the portal — failed items often have specific correction instructions in the notes.

## 5. Owner-builder responsibilities at inspection

Per ORSC §R105.7 / OSSC §107.3 and §R106.3.1 / OSSC §107.3 the following must be on-site and available to the inspector at every inspection:

- **Approved construction documents** (the stamped/approved set returned by the building department) — the inspector verifies installed work against this set.
- **Permit placard** — visible from the street, weatherproofed.
- **Inspection card** (where the jurisdiction issues one) — signed/stamped at each inspection pass.
- **Any required test setups** — gauges in place for pressure tests, blower door equipment for blower door inspection, ladder access to elevated work.
- **Access** — clear physical access to the work, including keys to locked spaces, removed obstructions, and safe access (no ladders missing rungs, no excavations without ladder access).
- **Documentation as required** — engineer's letter for any field-modified item, ICC-ES report for any non-prescriptive product, manufacturer install instructions for items where the listing controls (per §R102.4 exception).
- **Owner-builder presence** — typical practice. Some jurisdictions will conduct an inspection without the permit holder present if access is provided and the work is clearly ready; most prefer the permit holder be available for questions.
- **Correction notices** — when work fails inspection, the inspector issues a correction notice (paper or portal). The notice must be addressed before reinspection. Document the correction and request reinspection through the same channel.

## 6. Cover-and-conceal — the binding flow rule

ORSC §R109.1 prohibits covering work before approval. The build sequence has to respect this:

- **Do not pour concrete** over reinforcing steel, anchor bolts, holdowns, vapor retarder, in-slab plumbing/electrical conduit, radiant tubing until foundation/under-slab inspection passes.
- **Do not backfill** against foundation walls until foundation, drain, and waterproofing inspections all pass and the walls are braced (typically by first-floor framing being in place).
- **Do not insulate** until rough trades and framing inspections pass.
- **Do not drywall** until insulation and any required pre-cover inspections pass.
- **Do not energize** electrical service until the electrical inspector has signed off on the service equipment and (if temporary power) the temporary disconnect.
- **Do not pressurize** plumbing supply or hydronic systems for service until the trade inspection passes the pressure test.
- **Do not occupy** any portion of the building until at least a TCO is granted.
- **Do not use temporary services** as permanent without the trade inspector's specific sign-off — temporary power per ORSC §R107.3 is limited to 180 days under §R107.1 (extensible).

When a covering is necessary before inspection (e.g., overnight closure of a trench against weather), document the work with date-stamped photographs from multiple angles before covering, and notify the inspector when scheduling. Most inspectors will accept clear photographic evidence in lieu of uncovering for routine items — but this is at the inspector's discretion, not a right. For anything structurally critical (rebar placement, anchor bolt placement), do not cover under any circumstances without inspector approval.

## 7. Site and preconstruction approvals

For a rural BSH build, this is the longest-lead phase Chapter 17 documented:

**Land-use clearance**: planning department sign-off under ORS 215/197. Verify the parcel is approved for the intended dwelling type, that any required HB 2001 / SB 762 wildfire hazard / Goal 5 / EFU template-dwelling provisions have been satisfied, and that no concurrent land-use process is open against the parcel.

**Septic**: site evaluation under OAR 340-071-0220 — soil profile, perc data, slope, setbacks, water table, sized for the planned dwelling. Site Evaluation Report approved before building permit can issue. Construction installation permit issued separately when a licensed installer is engaged.

**Well**: well log for an existing well must be on file with OWRD. New well construction requires an OWRD-bonded well constructor (OAR 690-210). Water test (coliform/E. coli at minimum; some jurisdictions require nitrate, arsenic, lead) before CO; results submitted as part of closeout.

**Driveway access**: per Sec 1 of Chapter 17 — county roads or ODOT. Often a separate inspection at completion verifying compliance with the approach permit.

**Erosion control**: 1200-C if applicable; otherwise local plan approval. Inspections may be triggered by complaint or by routine site visits during the build.

**Address posting**: per OFC §505.1, ≥4-inch numbers visible from the street, contrasting background. For long driveways, separate sign at driveway entrance. Required during construction; verified at site address inspection (often the first visit by the building inspector).

**Property staking**: PLS-set boundary markers and building corners as needed. Setback violations are catastrophic to fix after concrete is poured — the building inspector verifies setbacks at the foundation inspection (and sometimes at a separate site inspection before excavation).

**Environmental conditions**: any wetlands or fish-bearing waters require Oregon DSL / ODFW review separate from the building permit. Wildfire hazard zone designation per the current map controls envelope requirements (ORSC §R327).

## 8. Foundation inspection — what's verified

Per ORSC §R109.1.1: inspection is made after excavation, after forms are erected, after reinforcing steel is in place, **before** concrete is placed. The inspector verifies:

- **Excavation depth** — to undisturbed bearing soil; minimum 12 inches below grade or below frost line, whichever is greater (frost depth varies — verify local table).
- **Excavation dimensions** — footing width and depth per ORSC Table R403.1 or per stamped engineering.
- **Bearing material** — undisturbed native soil or properly compacted engineered fill; soils inspector or geotechnical sign-off if engineered fill is used.
- **Forms** — set to required dimensions, square, level, braced, oiled or release-agent treated as needed.
- **Reinforcement** — bar size, spacing, cover (3 inches at earth-formed surfaces, 1.5 inches at formed surfaces typical), splice lengths, ties, chairs supporting bars at correct elevation.
- **Anchor bolts** — size, spacing, embedment depth, projection, washer size per ORSC §R403.1.6 / §R602.11.
- **Holdowns** — Simpson HDU/PHD/etc. as specified, properly tied and supported per the structural plan; ICC-ES ESR number documented.
- **CEE / UFER ground** — concrete-encased electrode per NEC 250.52(A)(3): 20 ft minimum of bare copper #4 AWG or larger (or #4 rebar), encased ≥2 inches in concrete near the bottom of the footing, bonded to the grounding electrode system at the panel. This is the first electrical inspection that overlaps the foundation inspection — coordinate.
- **Vapor barrier** (under-slab, for habitable space) — typically 6-mil minimum polyethylene over base course; some jurisdictions require Class A vapor barrier per ASTM E1745.
- **Insulation** (slab edge / under-slab where required by energy code) — R-15 slab edge for Climate Zone 4 per most current ORSC paths.
- **Radon barrier** (Class 1 areas — most of Oregon is Class 2 or 3 but some areas like parts of central Oregon are Class 1; verify EPA radon zone map).
- **Sleeves** — for any plumbing, electrical, mechanical, or low-voltage penetration through the foundation.
- **Waterproofing** (separate inspection in some jurisdictions) — applied to required wall surfaces per ORSC §R406.1 / §R406.2.
- **Drainage** — footing drains, drainage gravel, geotextile, drainage composite if used (per ORSC §R405).
- **Pre-backfill** — verifies the wall is braced, drainage is in place, waterproofing/dampproofing is complete, and protection board (where used) is installed. Some jurisdictions combine with the waterproofing inspection.

## 9. Masonry and structural inspection

For reinforced CMU walls:

- **Reinforcement placement** — bar size, spacing, cell layout per the engineering. Inspected before grouting.
- **Cleanouts** — at the bottom of every cell to be grouted on high-lift pours (>5 ft 4 inches per TMS 602).
- **Cleanout closure** — visual verification of cleanliness before sealing the cleanouts and grouting.
- **Grout pours** — observation of pour, consolidation; verified that grout reaches all cells and that no voids are left.
- **Bond beam reinforcement** — typically 2-#4 continuous in bond beams at floor and roof bearing levels, with corner bars per the engineering.
- **Lintel reinforcement and bearing** — bar size, bearing length per design.
- **Wall ties** (for masonry veneer) — per ORSC §R703.8: corrosion-resistant metal ties spaced not more than 24 inches OC vertically and 32 inches OC horizontally; one tie per 2.67 sq ft of wall area; wire ties of corrugated 22-gauge minimum or wire ties of 9-gauge minimum.
- **Wall bracing** during construction per OSHA / MCAA standards.

For wood-framed structural:

- **Floor framing** — joist size, spacing, hangers, blocking, doubled members at openings, beam pockets, bearing length.
- **Floor sheathing** — thickness, fastening pattern (size and spacing of nails or screws), edge support, T&G or blocked edges as required.
- **Wall framing** — stud size and spacing, top plate doubled and lapped, header sizing, wall bracing per ORSC §R602.10 (the prescriptive bracing tables — methods CS-WSP, ABW, PFG, etc.).
- **Wall sheathing** — fastening pattern verified (the most common framing-inspection failure: nails too far apart or not driven flush).
- **Roof framing** — rafter or truss size, spacing, bearing, ties (collar ties, ridge straps, hurricane ties at exposed locations).
- **Roof sheathing** — thickness, fastening, edge clips per manufacturer.
- **Anchorage** — anchor bolts and holdowns inspected at foundation; sill plate to anchor bolt connection inspected at framing; engineered straps and connectors per the plan.

For engineered items (LVL, glulam, manufactured trusses, prefabricated panels): the inspector verifies the installed product matches the engineer's drawings and the manufacturer's installation instructions. ICC-ES ESR or manufacturer's installation manual retained on-site.

## 10. Rough plumbing inspection and tests

Per ORSC §R109.1.2 and OPSC §103, rough plumbing inspection occurs after pipes are installed, before they are concealed. Standard tests:

- **DWV test** (OPSC §712): water test (10 ft head minimum, hold for 15 minutes minimum) or air test (5 psi for 15 minutes minimum). Water test is the default; air test acceptable where the building official allows.
- **Water supply pressure test** (OPSC §609): static water pressure at 1.5× working pressure but not less than 50 psi, hold for 15 minutes minimum. Most inspectors want to see the gauge holding when they arrive.
- **Gas piping pressure test** (OPSC §1213): for natural gas at 3 psi minimum (or 1.5× working pressure, whichever is greater) for 15 minutes minimum; LP gas at 10 psi minimum. Hold time longer for larger systems.
- **Cleanouts** — at code-required intervals (every 100 ft for horizontal sewers, at base of stack, at change of direction >45°, etc.).
- **Water heater** — TPR valve discharged to safe location, expansion tank if required, drain pan and drain to safe location, seismic strapping (Oregon is seismic zone — required per OPSC §507.3).
- **Backflow preventer** — installed at proper orientation, accessible, tested and tagged (RPZ and DCV require annual testing by a certified tester).
- **Pumps** — power supply per OESC, control wiring per the equipment, accessible disconnect.
- **Manifolds** (PEX home-run) — labeled at the manifold for each circuit; pressure test as part of supply test.
- **Rainwater / non-potable systems** (OPSC Chapter 17) — color-coded purple piping, "NON-POTABLE WATER — DO NOT DRINK" labeling on all components, separate inspection for the alternate water source piping.
- **Fixture rough-ins** — supply stub-out heights and centerlines verified against fixture cuts; drain rough-in heights and centerlines verified.

## 11. Rough and final electrical inspection

OESC §90.4 (NEC 90.4) is the inspection authority. Specific items the inspector verifies at rough:

- **Service** — meter base, service entrance conductors, main disconnect, sized per load calculation, weatherproof, accessible per NEC 230.
- **Panel** — panel board listed and labeled, mounted level, accessible (working space per NEC 110.26: 30-inch width, 36-inch depth, 6 ft 6 inch headroom typical for residential 120/240V).
- **Feeders and branch circuits** — conductor size matches the breaker, conductors properly supported and protected from damage, proper bundling per NEC 310.15(B)(3) ampacity adjustment, NM cable stapling within 8 inches of boxes and 4.5 ft on runs.
- **Boxes** — proper depth set to finished surface, fill calculated per NEC 314.16, proper supports.
- **Grounding** — equipment grounding conductor in every cable, EGC properly terminated to boxes and devices, grounding electrode system per NEC 250.50 (CEE/UFER, ground rod where required).
- **Bonding** — service neutral bonded to ground at the service per NEC 250.24, bonding jumpers around water meters and dielectric unions per NEC 250.104.
- **GFCI** (NEC 210.8) — protection on bathroom receptacles, kitchen counter receptacles, garage receptacles, outdoor receptacles, basement receptacles, laundry receptacles, dishwasher, and others per current 2023 NEC list.
- **AFCI** (NEC 210.12) — protection on most 15A and 20A 120V branch circuits in dwellings; the 2023 NEC continued to require AFCI on bedrooms, living rooms, family rooms, kitchens, dining rooms, halls, closets, and similar areas.
- **Smoke alarms** (NEC 210.19 for power; ORSC §R314 for placement) — hardwired with battery backup, interconnected so one trips all, in every sleeping room, immediately outside each sleeping area, on every story.
- **CO alarms** (ORSC §R315) — outside each separate sleeping area in dwellings with fuel-burning appliances or attached garage.
- **Low-voltage separation** — Class 2/3 cabling separated from line-voltage per NEC 800.133 and 725.136 (Chapter 15 has the depth on this).
- **Smart-home wiring** — Cat6A, coax, speaker wire properly listed (CMR/CL2/CL3) and supported.
- **Solar/EV provisions** — solar-ready conduit and reserved breaker space per ORSC §R325 (where adopted); EV-ready receptacle and circuit per ORSC §N1103.6 / local AHJ interpretation.
- **Labeling** — panel directory complete, branch circuits identified, service disconnect labeled, emergency disconnect per NEC 230.85 (for 1- and 2-family dwellings) labeled.

At final electrical:
- All devices in place; all faceplates installed
- All circuits energized and tested
- GFCI tested with the test button (every protected receptacle)
- AFCI tested with the test button (every protected breaker)
- Smoke and CO alarms tested (audible, interconnected operation verified)
- Service energized, meter set, all utility coordination complete
- Panel directory final
- Listed grounding electrodes documented

## 12. Mechanical inspection

Per OMSC and ORSC Chapters 13–24:

- **Ventilation** — whole-house ventilation per ORSC §R303 / §M1507 / current OEESC adoption (typically balanced ventilation with HRV/ERV for current Oregon code paths). Sized per ASHRAE 62.2 or ORSC table.
- **Local exhaust** — kitchen range hood (per ORSC §M1503), bath fans, laundry exhaust. CFM per source verified.
- **Duct sealing** — Class B mastic on all duct seams (no fabric tape per Oregon amendment; UL 181B-M / B-FX rated tape acceptable in some locations). Inspected before insulation/cover.
- **Duct insulation** — R-8 minimum on ducts in unconditioned spaces; R-6 conditioned attics; verify per current ORSC §N1103.
- **Dryer exhaust** (ORSC §M1502) — minimum 4-inch smooth metal duct; maximum length per fitting count (38 ft equivalent length less 5 ft per 90° elbow typical); termination outdoors with backdraft damper, no screen on residential dryer terminations.
- **Kitchen hood** — sized per the cooking equipment (typically 100 cfm minimum for residential, more for high-output ranges); ducted to outdoors; makeup air required at exhaust ≥400 cfm per ORSC §M1503.6.
- **Makeup air** — passive or motorized; if motorized, interlocked with the exhaust source; sized per the exhaust airflow.
- **Combustion air** (for any combustion appliance) — direct vent (sealed combustion) preferred; if atmospherically vented, combustion air supplied per ORSC §G2407 / §M1701.
- **Hydronic / radiant systems** — pressure tested at 1.5× operating pressure, hold for inspector verification. Manifolds labeled, controls operational. Glycol concentration verified for any system that includes antifreeze.
- **Heat pumps** — set, mounted level, refrigerant lines insulated, condensate drained to approved location, electrical connection per OESC. Refrigerant brazing and charging by EPA-608 certified tech (Chapter 17 Sec 3) — startup report retained.
- **Condensate** — primary drain to floor drain or other approved location; secondary (overflow) drain or float switch on equipment in attic or above habitable space (ORSC §M1411 / §G2427).
- **Controls** — thermostat installed, programmed; outdoor temperature lockouts on any heat pump with auxiliary electric heat verified.
- **Startup** — manufacturer's startup procedure performed and documented (refrigerant charge verified by superheat/subcool method, airflow verified, electrical draw verified). Required by AHRI for warranty.
- **Commissioning** — for the BSH "no internet required" methodology, additional verification: local controls function without internet, scenes work without cloud, emergency overrides accessible. Document each verification.

## 13. Insulation, energy, blower door, air-sealing, vapor control, firestopping, drywall, pre-cover

### Insulation inspection (§R109.1.5.2)

After all insulation and any required vapor retarders are installed; before lath, gypsum, or other interior finishes. The inspector verifies:
- Insulation type, thickness, and R-value match the energy compliance documentation
- Insulation installation quality (Grade I per RESNET if going for ENERGY STAR or Reach Code) — fully filling cavities, no gaps/voids/compression beyond manufacturer tolerances
- Vapor retarder where required (per ORSC §R702.7 / §R702.7.1) — typically Class II vapor retarder (kraft facing or vapor-retarder paint) on the warm side of walls in Climate Zones 5–7; not required in Climate Zone 4 walls, required on ceilings in some assemblies
- Eave baffles to maintain attic ventilation airflow above the insulation
- Knee wall insulation continuous and supported

### Air-sealing

May be inspected separately or as part of insulation. ORSC §N1104.8 requires either compliance with the air-barrier criteria of Table N1104.8 OR a blower door test result not greater than the path-required ACH50. For any path that includes the blower door requirement, the test must be done before final inspection (typically after insulation but before drywall, so any failed sealing can be remediated; some jurisdictions allow post-drywall testing).

### Blower door test

Performed by a third-party tester (BPI IDL or RESNET HERS Rater certification typical). Test per ASTM E779 or ASTM E1827. Equipment calibrated. Multi-point depressurization test (single-point acceptable for some multifamily but not single-family). Result reported on Oregon BCD's standard form (orsc-blower-door.pdf) and submitted to building official before CO. Target depends on path:
- Base ORSC: 4.0 ACH50 (some paths)
- 2023 ORSC §N1104.8.1 alternative path: 3.25 ACH50 (some paths)
- Reach Code: 3.0 ACH50
- ERI path: per the proposed ERI score

### Duct leakage test

Per ORSC §N1105.3, for any ducts in unconditioned spaces. Test at 25 Pa (the rough-in test) or post-construction (the total leakage test). Maximum 4 cfm/100 sf of conditioned floor area total leakage, or 3 cfm/100 sf to outdoors. Same third-party tester typically does both.

### Vapor control

Crawl space ground cover per ORSC §R408.1: 6-mil polyethylene minimum, lapped 6 inches at seams, extending up the foundation wall ≥6 inches. Verified at insulation inspection.

### Firestopping

Per ORSC §R302.11 / §R302.12: fire blocking at concealed spaces (top and bottom of walls, between stories of stairs at 10 ft horizontal intervals, around penetrations of top and bottom plates, where ceiling and wall meet, in soffits and dropped ceilings). Draftstopping where floor cavities exceed 1,000 sq ft. Firestopping at penetrations of fire-rated assemblies per UL listing (the listed system controls the materials and installation).

Inspected as part of framing inspection. Failed firestopping is one of the most common framing-inspection failures (per the BCD "Cite It, Write It Checklist" — fireblocking missing at R602.8 is consistently in the top 10 missed items).

### Drywall inspection (where required)

Most Oregon residential AHJs do not require a separate drywall inspection. Where required, the inspector verifies:
- Drywall type matches the assembly (Type X for fire-rated, moisture-resistant for wet areas)
- Fastener pattern per the assembly listing (typically 8 inches OC at edges, 16 inches OC in field for ceiling; 12/16 for walls)
- Joint treatment per the listed assembly (some fire-rated assemblies require taped and finished joints; some require backing)
- Penetrations sealed/firestopped

For shear-rated drywall (where used as a bracing element under the prescriptive bracing tables), nailing pattern is typically inspected at framing.

### Pre-cover

Generic "pre-cover" inspection in some jurisdictions verifies all rough trade inspections have been signed off, all firestopping is in, all required documentation is on-site, before insulation can go in.

## 14. Final inspections, TCO, and CO

### Final inspection per trade

Each trade has its own final:
- **Plumbing final** — fixtures connected, water heater commissioned, all stops accessible, all pressure under load
- **Electrical final** — all devices live, GFCI/AFCI tested, alarms tested, panel labeled
- **Mechanical final** — equipment installed, startup documented, controls operational

### Building final (§R109.1.6)

After all work required by the building permit is completed. The building official verifies:
- All required trade finals are signed off
- Energy code compliance documentation is filed (blower door, duct leakage, AHRI certificates, NFRC labels, lighting compliance form)
- All life-safety items operational (alarms, egress, guards, handrails)
- Required testing complete (well water if applicable, septic startup if applicable, radon mitigation if applicable)
- Address numbers in place
- All conditions of approval satisfied

### Temporary Certificate of Occupancy (TCO; §R110.4)

Issued at building official's discretion when the building or portion is safe to occupy but full completion remains. Typical TCO requirements:
- All life-safety items signed off
- Minimum habitable spaces complete (one bath, kitchen with sink and counter, one bedroom)
- Permanent power, water, waste, and heat (or temporary heat acceptable)
- Stairs, guards, handrails per code
- Septic and well commissioned (where applicable)
- Address numbers visible
- Written list of remaining work and completion deadline (typically 90–180 days)

TCO is not a right. The building official can deny TCO even when minimum items are complete if conditions of approval are unmet or if occupancy would create a hazard. Plan the schedule with CO as the target, treat TCO as an option not a guarantee.

### Certificate of Occupancy (CO; §R110)

Final approval to occupy. All inspections signed off, all conditions satisfied, all required documentation filed. After CO, any subsequent permitted work starts a new permit cycle.

### Unfinished-area separation

Where TCO is granted with unfinished portions, the unfinished portions must be physically separated from the occupied portion (locked door, barrier) and labeled "Not for Occupancy — Construction Area." For BSH builds where finish work continues after move-in, design for this — a clean lockable separation between the occupied wing and the in-progress wing.

## 15. Correction notice, reinspection, partial approval, stop-work, appeal, field revision, engineer letter, plan revision

### Correction notice

When work fails inspection, the inspector issues a correction notice (paper card, portal note, or both). The notice identifies the failed item(s) and the code section. The permit holder addresses each item before requesting reinspection. Document the correction with photos and a written response.

### Reinspection

Same scheduling channel as the original inspection. Reinspection fee charged per the local schedule (typically $50–$200). Most jurisdictions limit the number of reinspections before the building official may require a meeting with the design professional or a stop-work order.

### Partial approval

The building official may approve specific portions of work and allow construction to proceed (per ORSC §R104.10 modifications, or by direct decision). Examples: foundation-only permit while plan review continues on superstructure; framing approved in one area while another area is corrected. Document partial approvals in writing.

### Stop-work order

ORSC §R114 / OSSC §115 — building official authority to issue a stop-work order when work is being done in violation of the code, without permit, or in a manner that creates a hazard. Stop-work order must be in writing, posted at the site. Continuing work after a stop-work order is a separate violation, typically with daily civil penalties under ORS 455.770.

### Appeal

Two paths:

1. **Local Board of Appeals** — most jurisdictions maintain a local appeals board per ORSC §R112 / OSSC §113 to hear disputes over code interpretation or building official decisions. File within the time frame stated in the local ordinance (typically 30 days of the decision).

2. **State appeal** — ORS 455.690 and OAR 918-008-0120 — state-level appeal of a building official decision. Petition filed with BCD; the appropriate advisory board reviews. Statewide rulings under OAR 918-008-0095 are an alternate path: instead of contesting one building official's decision, petition for a statewide alternate method ruling that binds every Oregon jurisdiction.

For the BSH manuscript: most disputes resolve at the local level with a phone call or a meeting with the building official. State-level appeals are rare and slow (60–180 days). Reserve state escalation for items that genuinely matter to the BSH methodology.

### Field revision

Minor changes during construction that do not affect life safety or substantially alter the approved design can typically be approved in the field by the inspector. Examples: a shifted door opening, a relocated outlet, a substituted equivalent product. Document in writing — annotation on the field set, photo, inspector initials. If the inspector is uncertain, defer to the building official.

### Engineer letter

When a structural element deviates from the stamped engineering, a letter from the structural engineer (PE-stamped) confirming that the as-built condition is acceptable is the standard remedy. Required when:
- Field substitutions of structural members or connectors
- Changed loading conditions
- Damaged members repaired in place
- Variations in foundation configuration

The letter is filed with the building official, retained in closeout records.

### Plan revision

Substantive changes that affect life safety, structure, or compliance require a formal plan revision: revised sheets submitted to the building department, plan reviewer re-reviews, revised approval issued. Plan revision fee applies. Common triggers:
- Added or removed structural elements
- Changed window/door schedule that affects egress or energy compliance
- Changed mechanical system that affects ventilation or energy compliance
- Owner-driven design changes during construction

Plan revision can take weeks; do not assume same-day approval. Schedule any anticipated revisions early.

## 16. Inspection records — legally required vs. recommended

### Legally required (must be retained or available)

- **Approved construction documents** kept on site until completion (ORSC §R106.3.1; OSSC §107.3.1)
- **Permit placard** posted (ORSC §R105.7; OSSC §107.4)
- **Inspection card** signed off at each inspection where the jurisdiction issues one (ORSC §R109.4 / R117.1)
- **Special inspection reports** under OSSC Chapter 17 (commercial; rarely applies to residential but if any portion is OSSC-regulated, the special inspector's reports are a legal record)
- **Floodplain elevation certificates** under §R109.1.3 / §R109.1.6.1 (if in a flood hazard area)
- **Energy compliance forms** — blower door results (BCD form), duct leakage results, AHRI certificates, NFRC labels, lighting compliance form — required before CO
- **Manufacturer's installation instructions** for any product where the listing controls (per §R102.4 exception) — required to be available on site at inspection
- **CCB contractor information form** (BCD Form 4823) at building permit for any contractor work

### Recommended project documentation (best practice for BSH)

Beyond the legal minimum, the BSH closeout binder Chapter 16 outlined should include:

- All inspection sign-offs (paper cards photographed; portal screenshots)
- All inspector notes and any correction notices with the corrective action documented
- All batch tickets (ASTM C94 for ready-mix concrete)
- All engineer letters for any field deviation
- All product cut sheets and listing labels (photographed before install, retained as PDFs)
- All ICC-ES ESRs current at time of install
- All AHRI certificates and NFRC labels
- All HVAC startup reports
- All warranty registrations and effective dates
- All commissioning documentation (per Chapter 15 for smart-home; per equipment manufacturer for HVAC)
- All test results (well water test, septic startup, radon if applicable, hydronic pressure test)
- Photographic record of every concealed condition before cover (foundation rebar, in-slab plumbing, in-wall framing, in-wall plumbing/electrical/mechanical, in-ceiling cable runs)
- All plan revisions
- All permits and the final CO

This binder is the BSH home's "as-built" record. Keep one paper copy in a fireproof safe at the home, one digital copy on the home's NAS, one digital copy off-site (Backblaze, Wasabi, or trusted off-site backup). When the home eventually sells (per the Chapter 4 framework — Jon-personally sells as a personal residence), this binder is the substantive evidence behind the property disclosure form and is what an attorney will ask for if any defect claim arises post-sale.

## Limits / what isn't in the law

- **No statewide standardized inspection list.** ORSC §R109 is the floor, not the ceiling. Local AHJs add inspections, combine inspections, or split inspections per local practice. Verify the project-specific inspection list at design pass with the controlling jurisdiction.
- **The building official's discretion is broad.** TCO can be denied when minimum items are met. Reinspection fees can be waived or charged at discretion. Field revisions can be accepted or pushed to formal plan revision. Build the relationship with the building official rather than relying on the rules.
- **Third-party tester credentials are not specifically named in ORSC.** §R109.2 allows the building official to accept reports from "approved agencies" — practically that means BPI IDL or RESNET HERS Rater for blower door/duct leakage, ICC-ES for product evaluation, registered PE for structural, and other agencies the building official accepts. Verify the building official will accept the specific tester before scheduling.
- **The statewide ePermitting system covers most but not all Oregon jurisdictions.** Portland, Multnomah County, Washington County, and a few others run their own systems. Verify the channel for the build's specific jurisdiction.
- **Federal EPA-608 refrigerant certification is independent of OESC inspection.** The mechanical inspector verifies the install, not the technician's certification. The certification requirement is a federal CAA §608 obligation enforceable by EPA; non-certified work is illegal even if it passes the OMSC inspection.
- **OR-OSHA inspections of construction sites are independent of the building inspection process.** OR-OSHA may visit a site for a complaint or as part of routine inspection programs. Compliance with the 1926 standards is a separate regulatory layer from compliance with ORSC/OSSC.

## When to bring in paid help

- **Construction attorney** for any stop-work order, any building official decision being escalated to BCD, any code-enforcement action.
- **Structural PE** for any field deviation from stamped engineering — the engineer letter is the only authoritative way to clear the deviation.
- **HERS rater / energy consultant** for the blower door, duct leakage, and ERI-path documentation. For the BSH "no internet required" methodology, consider engaging a HERS rater familiar with above-code performance — the same person can support both code compliance and any incentive program eligibility.
- **Special inspector** if any portion of the build triggers OSSC Chapter 17 (rare for residential — typically only if a portion is non-prescriptive structural or commercial).
- **Geotechnical engineer** for foundation inspection on any soil that's not clearly Type B/C standard residential — the geotech's letter satisfying the building official is often the only path to an approved foundation on marginal soils.
