## The Voice Target

The book gives instructions and states facts. Every sentence does one of five jobs: states a requirement, states a fact, gives an instruction, gives a value, or names a source. A sentence doing any other job (sounding wise, sounding warm, painting a scene, balancing a phrase) gets rewritten into one of the five or deleted.

The enforcement test, applied to every sentence: **if it sounds quotable, rewrite it until it is usable.** Usable means the reader can extract an action to take, a value to check, or a constraint to obey. Wisdom-shaped sentences fail the test even when they are true.

Two modes are permitted. Requirement mode uses must, shall, may, and imperatives ("Order the site evaluation before closing"). Fact mode uses plain declaratives about verifiable things ("The evaluation costs $500-$1,200 (2026)"). Timeless-wisdom claims ("A house is the sum of a thousand decisions") are neither mode and do not appear. Explanations of why belong in `Simple` blocks and design-rationale notes, and even there they state mechanisms, not proverbs.

Claims about the book or the house state the property and the mechanism, never a scene that implies them. Not "you can hand a chapter to a contractor and have a useful conversation" but "each chapter is self-contained: it includes its code citations and context." Second person is welcome exactly when it carries the same informational density as third person.

Readability comes from four sources only: short sentences, defined terms, real numbers, and real consequences. When a passage feels flat, the fix is a number, a product name, a code section, or a consequence. The fix is never phrasing.

## The Root Metaphors (Owner Directive, 2026-07-10)

Line-editing the visible rhetoric is not enough; the metaphor system underneath regenerates it. The second external review traced the surviving performance to four false root metaphors. The replacements below are generation rules, not preferences.

**1. The book is a structured reference, not a person and not a conversation.** The book, a chapter, a section, a block, a layer, a table, and an entry never act. They do not carry, answer, say, tell, give, touch, travel, hold, want, need, cover, or have jobs. Approved verbs when a document element is the subject: contains, lists, states, specifies, defines, describes, includes, provides, records, identifies, documents. "This book has four jobs" becomes "This book serves four functions." "The chapter carries the code sections" becomes "The chapter lists the code sections."

**2. Information is present or absent. It does not move.** No traveling, no being handed, no going into binders. "The citations travel with the chapter" becomes "The chapter includes the citations." "What goes into the binder" becomes "the binder contents."

**3. The reader is an agent executing tasks, not a guest in a scene.** Imperatives addressed to the reader are the normal instruction form and are always permitted. Staged scenes of the reader are not: no "open one chapter while working on that system," no "years from now," no imagined moments. Describe the document property; the use follows. "Open one chapter while working on that system" becomes "Each chapter functions as a standalone reference for its system."

**4. Specifications are stated, not performed.** Three sub-rules:
- No contrastive negation against an unstated default ("There is no wood framing" as identity display). State the materials positively. An exclusion is stated once, plainly, only when it has regulatory or structural consequence.
- No permission-hedges. "A system may be only as complicated as the work requires" becomes "Add complexity only when a simpler system cannot meet the requirement."
- Requirements name the actor and the action. "Service systems carry the opposite requirement: routed for access" becomes "Route service systems for access. Label components for identification." Requirements are never attributed to abstract entities.

Fossils of the old metaphor system, all banned when the subject is text or information: carry, answer, say, tell, give, touch, travel, hold, run on, wear out sooner (unanchored comparative), by design (unattributed intention), jobs. The unanchored comparative rule extends the size-word rule: sooner, later, better, and worse appear only with their referent in the same sentence.

## Prohibited Rhetorical Devices (Owner Directive, Fourth Review, 2026-07-10)

The fourth external review (on the finished chapter 8) closed the last allowances for pedagogical devices. The five rules below are hard rules, in force in every layer including `Simple`. A violation is an error of the same severity as a wrong code citation: it gets fixed, not weighed against readability.

1. **No explanatory analogies.** No comparison to any object outside the assembly under discussion, in any layer. The old `Simple`-layer allowance ("a concrete everyday comparison to real objects") is withdrawn. Not "a thermos bottle keeps coffee hot because an insulating wall wraps the liner; this wall repeats that arrangement" but "placing insulation outside the structural wall keeps the concrete at conditioned temperature; placing it inside exposes the concrete to outdoor temperature cycles and loses the thermal-mass benefit." State the mechanism of the actual assembly in ordinary words.

2. **No personification of materials, assemblies, or documents.** Nothing inanimate acts with intent or receives an agent verb: nothing compensates, defends, refuses, rewards, or wants. Physical processes are stated as processes: not "no thickness of material elsewhere compensates for the gap" but "adding thickness elsewhere does not close a gap in a control layer." Plain statements of physical behavior ("mass without a defined load path adds cost without adding capacity," "concrete resists fire") are facts, not personification, and pass. Biological and psychological framings are personification too: a device does not "die of abandonment" (vendor support ends), a house has no "future self" (the record serves future occupants), and a statute contains presumptions, not "tripwires."

3. **No second-person imperatives outside `Applied` blocks.** Imperatives are the working register of `Applied` blocks and explicitly procedural lists, and nowhere else. In `Simple`, `Professional`, `Code`, and orientation prose, instructions are recast as declaratives with the actor named: not "the list grows, so re-ask at permit time" but "the adopting-jurisdiction list changes; the applicant confirms the parcel's status with the building department at permit application."

4. **No admonitory declaratives shaped like proverbs.** A physics or code fact delivered in warning cadence is a wisdom line even when true. Restate it as definition plus consequence: "a gap in a control layer is a discontinuity; material thickness at other locations does not restore continuity."

5. **No rhetorical questions and no self-check instructions aimed at the reader's thought process.** Not "for each load, ask: where does it go next?" but "trace each load from its point of application to the foundation; if the path is not shown on the drawings, the design is incomplete." The one framing question per section permitted by the targets is a question the very next sentence answers with facts; a question left hanging, implied, or used for cadence is banned.

## Sentence Mechanics (Fifth Review, 2026-07-11)

The fifth external review (chapters 9 through 12) found no register failures; what remained was syntax and density. Three rules close the gap. They are polish rules with the same force as the rest of this guide.

1. **No comma splices.** Independent clauses take periods, semicolons, or a formatted list. Three clauses joined by commas is a list compressed into a sentence: not "well water is raw, its quality belongs to the site, and it needs testing" but "well water is raw. Its quality belongs to the site, and it is tested before any treatment is chosen."

2. **Density splits by topic.** A sentence stacking roughly five or more coordinated elements, or a paragraph mixing more than two regulatory topics, is divided even when every sentence passes the length bands. The six-item-list rule extends upward: a 200-word Professional paragraph spanning an approval sequence, a setback table, and pump rules becomes separate paragraphs or a table.

3. **Project decisions are stated as decisions, not worthiness judgments.** "Worth," "deserves," and "merits" frame a specification as an opinion. State what the project does and the reason: not "the shock is still worth controlling" but "this project controls the shock at the valves that cause it." A genuine cost-benefit statement with the cost stated ("whether a parcel justifies the further work") passes.

## Verb-Object Agreement (Third Review, 2026-07-10)

When one subject governs several verbs, every verb must accept that subject as its object type. Information-objects (a decision, a record, a plan, a specification) take information verbs: recorded, reviewed, revised, communicated, understood. Physical objects (a wall, a system, the work) take work verbs: built, inspected, repaired, replaced. Attaching work verbs to information nouns produces sentences that read as fluent and mean nothing.

The case that set this rule: "Recorded decisions can be built, inspected, and repaired by people other than the person who made them." A decision cannot be built or repaired; the sentence skips the work product and attaches the work's verbs to the record. The repair introduces the missing object: "With the record, a contractor can build what the decision specifies, an inspector can check the built work against it, and a future owner can repair the result without guessing at the original intent."

## The Reader's Context

The writer holds the whole project in mind; the reader holds only the current page. Three rules follow:

1. Every sentence names its referents. No placeholder nouns ("the thing"), no comparatives that resolve in a previous paragraph.
2. Causal chains are written out, one link per clause: the record enables understanding, understanding enables correct work. Compressing a six-step chain into one clause forces the reader to reconstruct it.
3. Where a claim depends on material stated elsewhere, the sentence includes enough of it to stand alone, or names the chapter that holds the rest by topic.
4. Where two chapters treat the same subject from different angles (the device and its test, the rule and its procedure, the value and its record), each treatment names the other chapter by topic at the point of overlap (seventh review, 2026-07-12).

## History: Why This Guide Exists

The project has been through three style failures, and this guide exists to block all of them. Revision 4 performed loudly: aphorisms, antithesis, persuasive headings. Revision 5 hid in committee prose: 26-word sentences, noun piles, no reader. The first Revision 6 calibration batch (2026-07-10) performed quietly: balanced sentence pairs, gnomic wisdom lines, hedges, and scene-painting vignettes that passed the surface-pattern lint while still performing. The common root of all three is any sentence written for the reader's admiration instead of the reader's use. The numbers below come from pattern scans across every manuscript file; the full analysis is in `WRITING_STYLE_ANALYSIS.md`, the Revision-5 audit in `IMPROVEMENT_PLAN.md` (Issue 10 covers the quiet-performance failure).

|---|---|---|---|
| Total words | 200,056 | 89,338 | 106,028 |
| Inline parenthetical definitions | 2,782 | 0 | 366 |
| Dollar figures | 744 | 0 | 102 |
| Antithesis constructions ("X is not A. It is B.") | 1 | 34 | 16 |

**Revision 2 and 3 are the voice benchmark.** Reader feedback confirms it: both the owner and a family reader prefer the pre-Revision 4 text. That is exactly the audience the `Simple` layer exists for, so treat this preference as data, not nostalgia. Revision 2's voice was plain, question-driven, full of real dollar figures and inline definitions. Its weaknesses were structural, not vocal: thin code citations, duplicated content across its two layers, heavy em-dash habit, and no inspection or verification depth.

**Revision 4 is the first failure: the poet.** It cut the book by 55 percent and filled the space with rhetoric. Memoir openers ("I have carried this house around for about ten years"), persuasive headings ("Code Is A Map, Not A Wall"), antithesis ("Inspections are not interruptions. They are checkpoints."), and aphorisms ("Never bury a code question in concrete"). Every inline definition and every dollar figure died in this revision. The poetry and the deletion were the same event: when information is removed, style fills the space.

**Revision 5 is the second failure in progress: the committee.** Its structure is the best the book has ever had, and Revision 6 keeps that structure completely. But the Revision 5 style guide is a list of prohibitions aimed only at the poet, so writers steering by it drift the other way: the newest chapters have the longest sentences in the book's history, a specification-document register, a quarter of Revision 2's cost density, and almost no direct address. Clean, correct, and dry.

Revision 6 aims at the open middle: Revision 2's voice inside Revision 5's architecture.

## Part 1: The Contract

These fit on one page. If context is tight, this section plus one exemplar from Part 2 is the minimum load.

Absolutes (zero tolerance, checkable by script):

1. No metaphor and no analogy, in any layer including `Simple`. Describe the literal thing; a comparison to any object outside the assembly under discussion is banned (owner directive, 2026-07-10; see Prohibited Rhetorical Devices).
2. No personification, including of the book itself and of materials and assemblies. Inanimate things do not want, tell, ask, demand, resist, care, compensate, defend, or refuse. Document elements do not act: see The Root Metaphors. The only verbs with a document subject are contains, lists, states, specifies, defines, describes, includes, provides, records, identifies, documents. Plain physical-behavior statements ("concrete resists fire") pass.
3. No performance of any kind, loud or quiet. Banned regardless of surface pattern: aphorisms and gnomic wisdom lines (timeless truths about houses, decisions, or life); antithesis and definition by negation in every form, not just "X is not A. It is B."; chiasmus ("a cheap choice ... is not cheap"); balanced clause pairs and parallel sentences built for rhythm ("build the shell so it can stay, and route the systems so they can change"); anaphora ("You can... You can... You can..."); scene-painting vignettes that imply a claim instead of stating it; persuasive headings; blockquote pronouncements; closers and wrap-up lines. Enforcement is the quotable test from the Voice Target: quotable gets rewritten into usable. Parallel structure is permitted only where the content is genuinely parallel (a checklist, a comparison of enumerated options).
4. No em dashes. Use periods, commas, colons, or parentheses.
5. No invented citations, code values, or engineering numbers. Unverifiable claims get marked for research, not written around.
6. No reader-facing revision history and no old project acronyms.
7. Reference other chapters by topic name, never by number.
8. No second-person imperatives outside `Applied` blocks and procedural lists, and no rhetorical questions or reader self-check instructions anywhere. The one framing question per section (Targets, item 4) must be answered by the next sentence. (Owner directive, 2026-07-10; see Prohibited Rhetorical Devices.)

Targets (measurable, checked per section):

1. First use of a trade term in `Simple` or `Applied` gets a short parenthetical definition: "a title report (a legal document from a title company listing what the property includes)."
2. Any discussion of purchased work, materials, or services names a dollar range with the estimate year: "roughly $500-$1,200 (2026)."
3. Sentence length: `Simple` averages under 14 words with no sentence over 25. `Applied` uses imperative steps averaging under 15. `Professional` averages under 22. If a sentence lists six or more items, convert it to a list or cut items.
4. At most one direct question per section, used to frame the reader's real question. "You" is allowed in `Simple` and `Applied`; imperatives are preferred in `Applied`.
5. Every risk passage ends with an action: a check, a call, a record, or a decision.
6. Every `Code` entry has three parts: the requirement in plain words, the exact limit or value, and the source (book name and code number). No source, no entry.
7. No layer echo. A layer that restates another layer's content in different words gets deleted, not polished.
8. Property plus mechanism, never scene plus implication. A claim about the book or the house states what is true and what makes it true ("each chapter is self-contained; cross-references use topic names"), not a hypothetical moment that lets the reader infer it.
9. No unanchored size words. "Cheap," "expensive," "little," "a lot," "small," "large" appear only with a number, a ratio, or a named standard attached, taken from a real source. Where no real number exists, name the mechanism or cut the claim. Never invent a value to satisfy this rule; the no-invented-values absolute outranks it.
10. No conversational hedging. "Informally," "literally," "essentially," "in a sense," and standalone "may be" soften claims without adding conditions. Real uncertainty (jurisdiction, edition, site, product) is stated as a condition with the controlling authority named; everything else is asserted or cut.
11. Metadiscourse is confined to orientation. A chapter overview may state the chapter's scope in one to three sentences. Body text never narrates itself: no "the point is," "what this means is," "in short."
12. Preserve content. Never compress, merge, or drop material without user approval. This is the Revision 4 lesson and it outranks every style preference in this file.
13. A claim that the design exceeds a code minimum states the design's actual value next to the minimum ("9-foot ceilings against the 7-foot floor"), never an unquantified "exceeds it everywhere it matters" (seventh review, 2026-07-12).

## Part 2: Gold Standards

Imitate these before consulting any rule. They outrank the tables below when they seem to conflict. Exemplars A (its Applied block), B, and C are Applied-register passages; their imperatives are correct there and only there.

### Exemplar A: a technical section (Simple, Applied, Code)

> **Simple**
>
> Before buying rural land, find out whether it can support a septic system (the buried system that treats wastewater when there is no city sewer). Some soils drain too slowly, some drain too fast, and some parcels cannot support any system at all. A failed evaluation means no dwelling can legally be built on the parcel.
>
> **Applied**
>
> Order a site evaluation before closing, and make the purchase offer contingent on the result.
>
> 1. Hire a licensed evaluator through the county or DEQ list. Cost: roughly $500-$1,200 (2026).
> 2. The evaluator digs test pits and classifies the soil.
> 3. Read the result for system type, not just pass or fail. A standard system costs about $8,000-$15,000. An alternative system for poor soil costs $20,000-$50,000. Price that difference into the offer.
> 4. Save the evaluation report in the project binder. The septic permit will reference it.
>
> **Code**
>
> ```
> OAR 340-071: Oregon onsite wastewater rules, DEQ administered, often delegated to county health.
>   - Site evaluation procedure, system permit, installation inspection, certificate of satisfactory completion.
> ```

### Exemplar B: a risk passage

> One issue is poor form bracing. Forms can spread during placement, which bows the wall and moves anchor bolts out of tolerance. Before the truck arrives, walk the forms, check corners and openings, and tighten any weak bracing. Fixing a weak brace before the pour takes about ten minutes. A form that spreads during the pour can waste the entire load.

### Exemplar C: a decision passage

> Closer to town (15-30 minutes) means easier material runs, faster emergency response, and better internet, at higher land cost and with tighter zoning. Farther out (30-60 minutes) means cheaper acreage, more privacy, and room to run noisy equipment (a block press, an excavator) without complaints, but every hardware run becomes a half-day and the buyer pool shrinks at resale. Drive the route at different times of day before deciding, and check whether trucks can reach the site in January.

What the exemplars have in common: every sentence carries information, the stakes are stated in dollars and consequences, terms are defined in passing, the reader is addressed where it is natural, and nothing performs.

## Part 3: The Two Ways To Fail

The Revision 5 guide showed only poet-side failures, so writers overcorrected. This guide shows both directions. The middle column is the one this project has actually been producing lately; treat it as seriously as the left one.

| Too poetic (Revision 4's failure) | Too dry (the overcorrection) | Target |
|---|---|---|
| The land gets a vote. | Site conditions constitute a controlling input to feasibility determination across multiple project dimensions. | The site sets the foundation cost, the septic location, and the truck access. Verify all three before making an offer. |
| Inspections are not interruptions. They are checkpoints. | Inspection events gate progression between construction phases per the adopted administrative provisions. | Inspections are scheduled checkpoints. Concrete cannot be poured, walls cannot be closed, and the house cannot be occupied until the matching inspection passes. |
| Never bury a code question in concrete. | Unresolved compliance items should be remediated prior to concrete placement to avoid rework costs. | Resolve code questions before the pour. After concrete cures, exposing a misplaced sleeve or missed inspection means demolition, not paperwork. |
| I have carried this house around for about ten years. | The genesis of this project spans approximately one decade. | I have wanted to build this house for about ten years. This book is the plan. |

Diagnostics for the dry side, since the poet side is already well policed:

- A `Simple` sentence over 25 words is failing its reader.
- A sentence chaining six or more noun phrases ("cut and fill, positive drainage strategy, retaining-wall demand, driveway grade, erosion-control planning...") is a list wearing a sentence's clothes. Break it or trim it.
- Abstract-noun stacks ("erosion-control obligations," "feasibility determination," "compliance posture") signal committee drift. Name the physical thing.
- A section about buying, hiring, or building that contains no dollar figure is incomplete, not neutral.
- A `Simple` block with no "you" and no question in an entire chapter has drifted formal. One of either, well placed, is the fix. The dosage limit is one framing question per section.

## Part 4: Positive Rules

1. **Open with the reader's question or a plain topic statement.** "Does the property already have power, water, and road access, or will extending them cost more than the land?" is a working section opener: the question carries a real comparison. One question maximum per section.
2. **Define trade terms in parentheses at first use.** Short, plain, on the same line. The glossary is the formal home; the parenthetical is what keeps a non-builder reading.
3. **Give money as ranges with the year.** A dollar range from a real source does more for the reader's attention than any phrasing.
4. **Write short sentences with one idea each.** When a sentence needs a semicolon, try a period first.
5. **Address the reader where it is natural.** Imperatives in `Applied` ("Walk the site after rain"). Occasional "you" in `Simple`. Impersonal voice in `Professional` and `Code`.
6. **Use the micro-scaffold when a section covers a purchased service or a gated step:** why this matters, when to do it, what it costs, how to verify. These four answers made Revision 2 easy to navigate. They can be prose or bold lead-ins; they do not need to be literal headers every time.
7. **Reach for specifics before style.** A number, a product name, a code section, or a measurement fixes thin prose. Nothing else does.
8. **Risk pattern: name the issue, state the next step.** Every risk passage ends with something the reader can do.
9. **End when the information ends.** No summaries, no callbacks, no final lines.
10. **Preserve and grow content.** If a passage seems redundant, flag it for the user instead of deleting it.

## Part 5: The Layer System

The four-layer structure carries forward from Revision 5. The definitions below are the owner's expanded audience descriptions (2026-07-09) and they replace all earlier versions. Each layer is a different kind of content for a different reader, never the same paragraph in four registers.

### Simple

For anyone, regardless of background. A reader who never went past high school finishes the block understanding what the thing is and why it matters in this house. The work of this layer is finding the best plain way to state the actual mechanism: ordinary words, short sentences, real quantities, and the stake stated in facts (what it costs, what breaks, what it protects). Analogies and comparisons to objects outside the assembly are banned here as everywhere (owner directive, 2026-07-10); the plain explanation describes the thing itself. Any unavoidable technical word gets a plain parenthetical definition on the same line. "You" in declaratives and one framing question, answered by the next sentence, are allowed. Imperatives are not; instructions belong in `Applied`. Sentences average under 14 words.

The Simple block is the layer the manuscript keeps losing. It is not optional garnish. Any section a family member might reasonably want to understand gets one. Even a pure reference section (a standards table, a wire-size table) or an all-Professional section gets a two-or-three-sentence Simple block stating what the material is and how it is used; a reader never faces a dense table or a wall of Professional prose cold (seventh review, 2026-07-12).

### Applied

For the owner-builder doing the work: a reader who understands science and physics but does not know the trade jargon. This is the action layer. It says what actions will be taken, in what order, with what checks, tools, and records. Verbs are physical and performable: dig, form, measure, mark, call, schedule, photograph, test, label, save. When a trade term is needed, it is used and translated in passing, so the reader learns the vocabulary while getting the instructions. Physics-level reasoning is welcome ("the regulator drops street pressure to 60 psi because fixtures and hoses are rated for 80"); trade-insider shorthand is not. A reader who finishes an Applied block can do the work tomorrow morning or knows exactly what to learn first.

### Professional

Full correct jargon, exactly as the trade says it. Written for someone who went through school for building buildings: complete enough that they can check the design, catch an error, or hold up their end of a substantive conversation. It is never purposefully hard to read; it is one idea per sentence with every required parameter, mechanism, tolerance, and coordination rule present. If the Professional block would only restate the Applied block with longer words, it is skipped.

### Code

The rule layer. It states everything regulated in the section, each rule tied to its source so the reader sees the information and then exactly where it comes from. Every entry uses three parts:

1. **The requirement**: what must happen, in one plain sentence.
2. **The point**: the actual limit, value, geometry, test, or trigger, short and exact.
3. **The source**: the book name and code number, with edition where it matters.

Format each entry like this:

```
Anchor bolts must tie the sill plate to the foundation.
  1/2-inch bolts minimum, embedded 7 inches, spaced 6 feet or less (4 feet for two-story in SDC D2), one bolt within 12 inches of each plate end, 3x3x0.229-inch plate washers.
  Source: ORSC R403.1.6 (2023 edition).

Surface water must drain away from the foundation.
  Grade falls 6 inches in the first 10 feet; where 10 feet is not available, use swales or drains.
  Source: ORSC R401.3 (2023 edition).
```

Rules for the layer:

- Every entry ends with a source line. A line with no source is not a `Code` line; it moves to another layer or gets researched.
- A `Code` block requires at least one entry sourced to a statute, rule, or consensus standard. Where a section's only citable material is guidance (NIST publications, agency practice) or project practice, the section carries no `Code` block: the guidance is cited inside `Professional`, and where the absence of any governing code is itself the finding, `Professional` states it (seventh review, 2026-07-12).
- A `Code` block never summarizes the section's own advice. If the block would only restate what `Applied` already said, the block is skipped.
- No invented citations, ever. Unverifiable entries are marked for research. Verify against the research files and `revision6_code_library/README.md` routes.
- Name the edition when the value depends on it.

### Register by layer

Each layer has a fixed register, and the register is what keeps specification separate from teaching:

- `Simple` is the only layer where explanation lives. It may use "you" in declaratives and one framing question that the next sentence answers. No aphorisms, no vignettes, no wisdom lines, no analogies, no imperatives: Simple states the actual mechanism in ordinary words, it does not philosophize and it does not compare the work to unrelated objects.
- `Applied` is instructions: imperatives, requirements, checks, records. Zero framing, zero motivation, zero commentary. If a sentence in Applied explains why, move it to Simple or a design-rationale note.
- `Professional` is facts: declaratives with parameters, tolerances, and mechanisms. Same rule: no framing, no philosophy.
- `Code` is the three-part rule list and nothing else.

### Choosing layers

Layers are not a quota. The Revision 5 completion audit found chapters where every section mechanically carried Applied, Professional, and Code blocks with matching counts while Simple nearly vanished; both halves of that pattern are wrong. Procedure: write `Applied` first. Add `Simple` unless the section is purely internal reference. Add `Professional` only if it contributes a mechanism, parameter, or tolerance not already stated. Add `Code` only if there are real sources to cite. Delete any layer that repeats another layer's content in different words.

Judgment checks (a writer or reviewer answers these five):

1. Would a curious non-builder keep reading the `Simple` blocks, and could they retell the point afterward?
2. Could the owner-builder perform each `Applied` block tomorrow morning?
3. Does every risk passage end with an action?
4. The quotable test, sentence by sentence on anything that stands out: does it hand the reader an action, a value, or a constraint? If it hands over a feeling, rewrite it.
5. Does any sentence state a timeless truth about houses, decisions, or life instead of a requirement or a checkable fact?
6. Does any passage imply a claim through a scene ("you can hand this to...") instead of stating the property and its mechanism?
7. Does any evaluative word (cheap, large, fast, hard) lack a number, ratio, or named standard, where a real one exists in the sources?
8. Is any document element (book, chapter, section, block, layer, table, entry) the subject of an action verb outside the approved list (contains, lists, states, specifies, defines, describes, includes, provides, records, identifies, documents)?
9. Does any requirement lack an actor, or sit attributed to an abstract entity ("carries the requirement") instead of stated as actor plus action?
10. Do all verbs sharing one subject accept that subject as their object type? Information nouns take information verbs; physical nouns take work verbs; a mismatch means a missing referent.
11. Can each sentence be understood from the current page alone: referents named, comparatives resolved in-sentence, causal chains written out?
12. Does the text preserve every line on the Owner's Voice protected list unchanged?
13. Is there any passage a reader would call boring where a number, cost, or consequence is available and missing? Add it.
14. Does any sentence, in any layer, explain by comparison to an object outside the assembly (analogy), or deliver a fact in proverb or warning cadence? Recast it as the actual mechanism, or as definition plus consequence.
15. Does any imperative or question appear outside an `Applied` block or procedural list? Move it into `Applied`, or recast it as a declarative with the actor named. A framing question passes only if the next sentence answers it.
16. Does any sentence join independent clauses with commas alone, or compress a list into clause form? Split it or format the list.
17. Does any sentence stack five or more coordinated elements, or any paragraph mix more than two regulatory topics? Divide by topic, even where the length bands pass.
18. Does any claim that the design exceeds a minimum omit the design's actual value? State the value beside the minimum.
19. Where a subject is treated in two chapters, does each treatment name the other by topic? Does every dense reference table and all-Professional section have its Simple orientation block?

When a check passes cleanly, it passes cleanly. Do not stretch to find problems.

## Part 7: Compact Working Prompt

For small-context sessions, load this plus Exemplar A instead of the whole guide.

Write a technical reference manual for an Oregon owner-builder building a concrete home. Every sentence states a requirement, states a fact, gives an instruction, gives a value, or names a source. Nothing else. Plain literal language, short sentences, one idea each. Define trade terms in parentheses at first use. Give costs as dollar ranges with the year, from real sources only. Imperatives in instructions; "you" is welcome when the sentence carries full information; at most one framing question per section.

Never: metaphor, analogy (no comparisons to objects outside the assembly, even in Simple), personification (of materials and assemblies as well as documents), aphorisms or wisdom lines, facts in proverb or warning cadence, antithesis or chiasmus or balanced clause pairs in any form, anaphora, vignettes that imply a claim instead of stating it, imperatives outside Applied blocks, rhetorical questions or ask-yourself instructions, hedges (informally, literally, may be), unanchored size words (cheap, a lot) without numbers, self-narration (the point is, in short), persuasive headings, wrap-up closers, em dashes, invented citations or values, chapter-number cross-references.

The test for every sentence: quotable gets rewritten until usable, meaning the reader can extract an action, a value, or a constraint. Claims about the book or house state property plus mechanism, never a scene.

Root metaphors: the book is a structured reference, not a person or conversation; document elements only contain, list, state, specify, define, describe, include, provide, record, identify, or document. Information is present or absent, never moving or carried. The reader is an agent executing tasks: imperatives yes, staged scenes no. Requirements name the actor and the action; no contrastive negation against unstated defaults; no permission-hedges ("may be only as complicated as").

Use the four layers only where each adds new information. `Simple` is the only layer that explains; it uses ordinary words and still bans aphorisms. `Applied` is instructions with zero framing. `Professional` is facts with parameters. `Code` entries are three parts: requirement, exact value, Source line with book name and section number; no source, no entry. Risk passages: name the issue, then the next step. Preserve existing content; never compress silently. When prose feels thin, add a number, a product name, a code section, or a measurement. When prose feels long, cut words, not information.
