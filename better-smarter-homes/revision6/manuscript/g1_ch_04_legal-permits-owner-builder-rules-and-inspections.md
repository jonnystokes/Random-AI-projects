# Chapter 4: Legal, Permits, Owner-Builder Rules, And Inspections

Legal requirements accompany the physical build from permit application until the certificate of occupancy is issued and the records are closed. This chapter identifies who may perform which work in Oregon and what the owner-builder path requires: the homeowner statement, the resale-timing rules that exist (and the ones that do not), the ownership structures that preserve the path, and the inspection sequence at the legal level.

The detailed inspection-readiness criteria for each phase are in the Inspection And Approval Sequence chapter. The Oregon Code And Compliance Map chapter contains the overview of all regulatory layers. This chapter contains the legal detail.

This chapter is general information, drawn from public Oregon statutes, administrative rules, agency forms, and case law as of the dates in the source paths. It is not legal advice for any specific project. The right structure for a particular owner depends on facts that require an Oregon construction attorney, a CPA familiar with multi-entity small business, and, where stamped work exists, a licensed Oregon professional engineer.

## 4.1 Legal Requirements Are Construction Tasks

Permits, inspections, occupancy approvals, and the compliance records are construction tasks, scheduled with the physical work.

**Simple**

Every regulated piece of work has a legal side: the framed wall, the concrete pour, the wire, the pipe, the installed appliance. Someone has to pull a permit, sign for the work, schedule an inspection, save the result, and prove later that it was done right. A skipped legal step causes problems at four points: cover inspection, the certificate of occupancy, insurance, and sale.

**Applied**

For each phase of work, know:

- which permit covers the work
- who is legally allowed to do the work
- what documents must be submitted at application
- what the inspector must see before the work is covered
- which work cannot be covered until inspection is approved
- what correction process applies if the work fails inspection
- what record enters the project binder
- when the permit expires if work pauses, and how to extend it

The worst time to discover a permit problem is after concrete is poured, a trench is backfilled, insulation is installed, drywall is up, or a finish material is applied. Resolution becomes destructive once work is covered.

A permit is not open-ended. Under ORSC R105.5, a permit expires if the work does not start within 180 days of issuance, or if the work stops for 180 days. Written extension requests of up to 180 days each are available for justifiable cause, with no stated limit on the number of extensions. The code contains no reinstatement procedure for an expired permit; what happens after expiration is local policy, so request the extension before the deadline instead of testing the local policy after it.

**Code**

```
State law authorizes the building codes and the permit system.
  ORS chapter 455 contains the authority for the specialty codes, the uniform permit, inspection, and certificate-of-occupancy requirements (ORS 455.055), the owner-built structural exemption (ORS 455.320), and the rural-county exemption framework (ORS 455.330-455.350).
  Source: ORS chapter 455.

Contractor licensing and the owner-occupier exemption come from a separate statute.
  ORS chapter 701 contains the CCB licensing framework, the owner-occupier exemption with its intent-to-sell prima facie test (ORS 701.010(5)), and the homeowner-applicant statement requirement (ORS 701.325).
  Source: ORS chapter 701.

State rules administer the residential code and the certificate of occupancy.
  OAR chapter 918 division 480 adopts the ORSC (918-480-0005) and contains the certificate-of-occupancy rule (918-480-0140).
  Source: OAR chapter 918 division 480.

The residential code's first chapter contains the process rules.
  Permits, required inspections, certificate of occupancy (R110), temporary certificate of occupancy (R110.4), and the correction-notice framework.
  Source: ORSC Chapter 1 (2023 edition).

A permit expires after 180 days of inactivity.
  Two triggers: work not commenced within 180 days of issuance, or work suspended or abandoned for 180 days. Written extensions of up to 180 days each are available for justifiable cause; the code contains no reinstatement procedure, so post-expiration recourse is local policy.
  Source: ORSC R105.5; ORSC R105.3.2 (the same clock for pending applications).

Local procedure controls the workflow.
  Each city or county administers the state codes through its own permit, inspection, and approval workflow; local amendments, fee schedules, and scheduling vary.
  Source: the local building department's adopted procedures.
```

## 4.2 Owner-Builder Responsibility And The Homeowner Acknowledgement

The owner-builder path is a defined legal role with attached duties. The owner-builder becomes the permit holder, the document keeper, the inspection scheduler, the correction manager, and the first person responsible for proving the work stayed inside the approved path.

**Simple**

Oregon lets a property owner build their own home without a contractor license, under specific rules. The owner has to own the property, intend to live in the finished home, and sign a state form acknowledging responsibility. All the code, permit, and inspection requirements that bind a licensed builder still apply. The exemption removes the licensing requirement only.

**Applied**

If using the homeowner path:

- apply as the property owner where allowed by the local jurisdiction
- read the homeowner statement before signing it
- sign the required Property Owner Statement before regulated work begins
- keep the permit and approved plans on the project site
- schedule each inspection before the work is covered
- resolve correction notices before moving the next phase forward
- hire CCB-licensed contractors when required by the work or beyond the owner's competence
- if hiring subcontractors, the form requires that they be CCB-licensed (and for plumbing or electrical permits, also BCD-licensed)
- keep subcontractor licensing, insurance, permit, and inspection records in the binder

### What The Property Owner Statement Says

The statewide BCD Property Owner Statement (Information Notice to Property Owners About Construction Responsibilities, ORS 701.325(3), most recently revised January 2, 2025) is a single form covering residential building, electrical, mechanical, and plumbing permits.

The form requires the applicant to check that they will be performing work on property they own and reside in or will reside in. It requires acknowledgement that the permit is non-transferable. Two optional checkboxes apply when the applicant intends to perform their own electrical work (under ORS 479.540) or their own plumbing work (under ORS 693.020).

The form does not contain a "minimum holding period before sale" attestation. The sale and rental restrictions come from the substantive exemption statutes, not from the form.

The page-one disclosures the applicant is acknowledging include:

- that homeowners using unlicensed paid labor may be considered employers subject to Oregon withholding tax, unemployment insurance, BIN registration, workers' compensation insurance, federal Social Security, and federal income-tax withholding
- that the homeowner is responsible for code compliance and inspection scheduling
- that the homeowner should obtain liability and property-damage insurance covering accidents and injuries to non-employees on the site

The closing certification requires the applicant to certify that they are the homeowner applying for the permit and that the information on the homeowner statement is true and accurate.

**Professional**

The 2008 amendment to ORS 701.010 changed the rules for owners hiring subcontractors directly. An owner who directly hires subcontractors for permit-required work loses the homeowner-GC exemption unless the work is performed under the direction of a licensed general contractor. The full map of which licensing regime controls which trade is in the who-can-do-which-work section of this chapter.

**Code**

```
The homeowner statement is a statutory requirement.
  The applicant must acknowledge construction responsibilities before the permit is issued.
  Source: ORS 701.325(3).

One statewide form satisfies the statement requirement.
  The BCD Property Owner Statement (last revised January 2, 2025) applies to residential building, electrical, mechanical, and plumbing permits; named in this book, not displayed.
  Source: oregon.gov/bcd/epermitting.

The structural exemption defines who counts as an owner-builder.
  An owner-built dwelling has structural components constructed entirely by the owner who intends to occupy, or by the owner with friends and relatives on an unpaid basis; an owner who used the exemption within the prior five years does not qualify.
  Source: ORS 455.320.

The electrical exemption is limited to non-sale property.
  Owner or immediate-family work on residential or farm property "not intended for sale, exchange, lease or rent"; permit, inspection, and code compliance still required.
  Source: ORS 479.540.

The plumbing exemption applies to the owner's own building.
  No sale-or-lease restriction in the statute itself, and no immediate-family extension; permit, inspection, and code compliance still required.
  Source: ORS 693.020.

The CCB defines family for the contracting-side exemption.
  Parents, spouses, sisters, brothers, daughters, sons, daughters-in-law, sons-in-law, and grandchildren.
  Source: OAR 812-002-0275.

The CCB defines speculative construction.
  A structure constructed in anticipation of, or with the intent of, selling to another entity during or after construction; the basis for implied-warranty exposure.
  Source: OAR 812-002-0680.

Coordinating construction is itself contractor work.
  Selecting contractors, obtaining permits, scheduling, and purchasing materials for a project constitutes construction management requiring CCB licensure.
  Source: OAR 812-002-0160.
```

## 4.3 The Oregon Resale-Timing Question

There is no statewide fixed waiting period before an owner-built home may be sold. Claims of one conflate four separate regimes, or repeat a city's local interpretation as if it were state law. Oregon uses an intent test under contractor-licensing law plus several separate timing rules, each controlling a different thing.

**Simple**

Oregon law contains no fixed waiting period before an owner-built home may be sold. What the law contains is a test of whether the owner intended to occupy the home or intended to sell. Living in the home is the strongest evidence of intent to occupy. There are also a few separate rules about how often certain exemptions can be reused and a 75-day notice requirement when a recently completed home is sold. And the federal government has its own rule about how long a person has to live in a home to avoid capital-gains tax on a sale. None of these add up to a state-mandated waiting period.

**Professional**

The four overlapping regimes that actually exist:

| Regime | Authority | What it controls | What it does not control |
|---|---|---|---|
| CCB contractor-licensing intent test | ORS 701.010(5) | Whether construction is treated as owner-occupier work or as contracting for sale | No fixed time period; non-occupancy after completion is prima facie evidence of intent to sell |
| Structural-exemption five-year cooldown | ORS 455.320 | How often an owner can use the structural-code exemption (room sizes, ceiling heights, temperature) on a new permit | Not a resale waiting period |
| Rural-county exemption five-year limit | ORS 455.335 | How often a qualifying exempt owner-built-dwelling permit can be issued in participating rural counties | Not a resale waiting period |
| Homebuyer-protection 75-day notice | ORS 87.007 / ORS 87.093 | A lien-disclosure obligation when a residential structure is sold within roughly 75 days of substantial completion | Not a prohibition on sale |

Federal capital-gains treatment under IRC §121 (the personal-residence exclusion) requires ownership and use of the home as a principal residence for two of the five years before sale. The exclusion is up to $250,000 for an individual and $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. The §121 timeline often becomes the practical floor for resale, but it is a federal tax rule, separate from Oregon construction law.

A common local point of confusion: Portland's residential permitting page states that a homeowner may pull their own electrical permit only if they own and occupy the home and are not planning to sell or rent within six months. That six-month threshold is Portland's working interpretation of the statutory phrase "not intended for sale, exchange, lease or rent" in ORS 479.540. Other Oregon jurisdictions use different thresholds or none at all.

The Tandem Properties LLC v. Construction Contractors Board decision (184 Or App 28, 2002) is the controlling Oregon Court of Appeals case on the intent test. Owners who arrange residential construction with the intent of later offering the structure for sale are excluded from the owner-occupier exemption. Intent to sell without occupancy makes the owner a contractor by operation of law.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: clean owner-builder posture.**
Owner buys land in their personal name. Pulls permits as the homeowner. Builds the home over two years while living elsewhere. Receives certificate of occupancy. Moves into the home as their primary residence. Maintains utility accounts, mail, voter registration, tax filings, and insurance at the address. After three years of actual residence, sells and moves to a new property.

Result: the intent-to-sell prima facie evidence (non-occupancy after completion) is rebutted by actual occupancy. The federal §121 exclusion applies (more than two of the last five years occupied as principal residence). No CCB exposure.

**Example 2: borderline posture, fact-dependent.**
Owner buys land in their personal name. Pulls permits as the homeowner. Builds the home and receives certificate of occupancy. Moves in for nine months, then accepts an out-of-state job offer and lists the home for sale.

Result: a genuine post-completion change of circumstances is a defense to the intent-to-sell test, not a violation. The federal §121 exclusion may not yet be available (less than two of five years), so capital-gains tax may apply. Lien-notice obligation under ORS 87.007 applies if sale falls within roughly 75 days of substantial completion. CCB exposure is fact-dependent and depends on whether the original intent was occupancy or sale.

**Example 3: speculative posture, hard exposure.**
Owner buys land in an LLC name. LLC pulls permits as a developer. Construction begins. The owner never moves in; the property is listed for sale before the certificate of occupancy is issued.

Result: the LLC is a contractor by operation of law (Tandem Properties pattern). The owner-occupier exemption is unavailable because the LLC is not a natural person. CCB enforcement risk under ORS 701.098, civil penalties under ORS 701.992, loss of standing to bring claims before the CCB, and potential implied-warranty exposure as a speculative builder under OAR 812-002-0680.

**Applied: defensible owner-builder posture in Oregon**

1. Hold title in the correct personal ownership before relying on the homeowner path.
2. Pull permits as the homeowner where allowed.
3. Build because of an intent to occupy.
4. Do not list, market, or solicit offers while owner-builder work is in progress.
5. Move in only after the building official issues the certificate of occupancy or temporary certificate of occupancy.
6. Maintain ordinary occupancy records: utility bills, mail forwarding, voter registration, tax-residency address, insurance address, and daily-life records.
7. If circumstances change after move-in, document the actual reason (job change, family change, health change).
8. Before any sale, lease, trust transfer, LLC involvement, or tax-planning move, obtain professional advice.

The strongest position is genuine occupancy with records that match the facts. The federal §121 timeline (two of the last five years) is usually the binding resale-timing constraint, not a state-construction rule.

**Code**

```
The licensing exemption turns on intent, with no fixed time period.
  The exemption does not apply to a person who builds or arranges construction with intent to offer the structure for sale before, upon, or after completion; non-occupancy after completion is prima facie evidence of intent to sell.
  Source: ORS 701.010(5).

The structural exemption has a five-year reuse limit.
  An owner who used the exemption may not use it again for five years; use is recorded with the county clerk. A limit on exemption frequency, and separate from resale timing.
  Source: ORS 455.320.

Rural counties may adopt a broader exemption, with its own five-year limit.
  Participating counties issue exempt owner-built-dwelling permits to a person at most once every five years, with purchaser-notice obligations and buyer remedies.
  Source: ORS 455.330; ORS 455.335; ORS 455.345-455.350.

A sale within about 75 days of substantial completion requires a lien-protection notice.
  The seller serves the Homebuyer Protection Act notice; the trigger is substantial completion as defined in the lien statutes. An obligation on the sale, and separate from any prohibition.
  Source: ORS 87.007; ORS 87.093; ORS 87.045.

Federal tax sets its own occupancy clock.
  The personal-residence capital-gains exclusion requires ownership and use as a principal residence for two of the five years before sale; $250,000 excluded for an individual, $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. A federal tax rule, and separate from Oregon construction law.
  Source: IRC section 121.

The certificate of occupancy gates occupancy, and only occupancy.
  The CO and TCO control when the home may be lived in; neither starts a resale clock.
  Source: ORSC R110; OAR 918-480-0140.

The controlling case on intent is Tandem Properties.
  An entity that owns property and arranges construction with intent to sell is a contractor by operation of law; the owner-occupier exemption requires a natural person with occupancy intent.
  Source: Tandem Properties LLC v. Construction Contractors Board, 184 Or App 28 (2002).
```

## 4.4 Ownership Structure And The Owner-Builder Posture

The owner-builder path depends on who owns the property and who is doing the work.

**Applied**

Confirm the ownership structure before relying on the homeowner path:

- individual ownership
- spouse or co-owner on title
- revocable trust
- irrevocable trust
- LLC ownership
- future transfer plan
- financing or insurance requirements

Fixing title after a project starts is harder than choosing the clean structure at purchase.

**Professional**

The safest owner-builder posture is a natural person who owns the property, signs the homeowner statement, intends to occupy, performs or properly contracts the work, and keeps the records.

| Structure | Effect on owner-builder posture |
|---|---|
| Individual title | Cleanest. Direct fit with ORS 455.320 and ORS 701.010 owner-occupier framing. |
| Spouses on title (tenancy by the entirety, ORS 93.180) | Generally accepted. CCB family-member definition under OAR 812-002-0275 is broad (parents, spouses, sisters, brothers, daughters, sons, daughters-in-law, sons-in-law, grandchildren). For trade-permit purposes (electrical), Portland's enumeration is narrower (mother, father, sister, brother, daughter, son) and notably omits spouses; verify the local jurisdiction's working list. |
| Revocable living trust where the trustee is also the beneficiary-occupant | Typically accepted by jurisdictions when the occupant clearly controls the trust. Verify locally before relying on this. |
| Irrevocable trust with the occupant as beneficiary only | Fact-specific and shakier. No statewide bright-line rule; local building departments make the call. |
| LLC ownership of construction-intent property | Presumptively a developer structure under ORS 701.005. The LLC owner-builder posture is treated as contracting, prima facie. This is the Tandem Properties pattern. |
| Married couple titling the project in a jointly held LLC | Worst posture: looks speculative and loses the natural-person owner-occupant defense. |

If title will be held by a trust, an LLC, or any arrangement other than direct individual ownership or tenancy by the entirety: obtain confirmation before relying on the homeowner path. Ask local planning, the building department, and an Oregon construction attorney.

**Code**

```
The statute defines who is a developer.
  A developer is a contractor that owns property with the intent to sell; LLC ownership with construction intent fits the definition.
  Source: ORS 701.005.

The owner exemption requires a natural person with occupancy intent.
  The exemptions from contractor licensure center on personal ownership and intent to occupy.
  Source: ORS 701.010.

Spousal title defaults to tenancy by the entirety.
  Transfers to spouses take this form unless stated otherwise.
  Source: ORS 93.180.

The CCB family-member list controls the contracting-side exemption.
  Parents, spouses, sisters, brothers, daughters, sons, daughters-in-law, sons-in-law, grandchildren.
  Source: OAR 812-002-0275.

Speculative construction is defined by intent to sell.
  A structure constructed in anticipation of, or with intent of, selling to another entity during or after construction.
  Source: OAR 812-002-0680.

The controlling case is Tandem Properties.
  LLC ownership combined with intent to sell makes the entity a contractor by operation of law.
  Source: Tandem Properties LLC v. CCB, 184 Or App 28 (2002).
```

### Brand And LLC Separation Pattern

If a separate LLC carries education, content, plan licensing, or research activity (for example, "Better Smarter Homes LLC"), the LLC must be kept out of the construction-and-sale stream. The risk-control structure has two layers.

**Activity separation:**

- the LLC owns intellectual property (plans, content, branding, trademarks, domain names, video, photographs, written material)
- the LLC sells educational material, runs advertising, accepts sponsorships, and provides purely advisory consulting
- the LLC does not buy, build, or sell homes
- the LLC does not supervise or coordinate construction on the owner-builder's homes
- the natural-person owner holds title to the land, pulls the permit, signs the homeowner statement, and occupies
- a licensed Oregon professional engineer is hired per project where stamped engineering is required

**Money separation:**

- LLC revenue stays in the LLC bank account
- personal home-build expenses come from personal funds
- the LLC does not pay for materials that go into the personal home
- LLC accounting and personal accounting do not commingle

Marketing language matters. Oregon's professional engineering statute (ORS 672.007) treats any verbal claim, sign, advertisement, letterhead, or business card that implies the speaker is a professional engineer as the practice of engineering. ORS 672.020 makes that practice without registration unlawful. The Oregon State Board of Examiners for Engineering and Land Surveying (OSBEELS) has imposed civil penalties on individuals who held themselves out as engineers without registration.

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| "Engineered by Better Smarter Homes" | "Designed by Better Smarter Homes" |
| "Better Smarter Homes-engineered design" | "Built using Better Smarter Homes design principles" |
| "Engineering services by Better Smarter Homes" | "Based on research by Better Smarter Homes" |
| "Stamped by Better Smarter Homes" | (do not use any visual that resembles a PE seal) |
| "Better Smarter Homes-certified" | "Built to Better Smarter Homes' published methodology" |
| "Approved by Better Smarter Homes engineering" | (only if a licensed PE is actually involved: "Structural engineering by [PE name and Oregon license number], in collaboration with Better Smarter Homes") |

The architecture statute is gentler. ORS 671.030(1) exempts plans and specifications for single-family residential dwellings, farm buildings, and certain small structures from the practice of architecture. A design-and-research LLC can produce single-family home plans without an architect license. The words "architect," "architecture," and "architectural" still cannot appear in marketing without registration.

**Code**

```
Claiming engineer status is itself the practice of engineering.
  A verbal claim, sign, advertisement, letterhead, card, or any title or representation implying engineer status constitutes practice.
  Source: ORS 672.007.

Practicing engineering without registration is unlawful.
  An Oregon PE license is required to practice, with seal requirements for stamped documents; aiding or abetting unlicensed practice and false representation are separately prohibited.
  Source: ORS 672.020; ORS 672.045.

The engineering board publishes its penalty record.
  Civil penalties for unlicensed engineering practice typically run $1,000-$7,000 per violation.
  Source: OSBEELS disciplinary actions, oregon.gov/osbeels; OAR chapter 820.

Architecture has a registration bar and a residential exemption.
  Practicing architecture or using the title without registration is prohibited; plans and specifications for single-family dwellings, farm buildings, and structures up to 4,000 square feet and 20 feet tall are exempt from the practice definition. "Home designer" is permitted wording; "residential architect" is not.
  Source: ORS 671.020; ORS 671.030(1).
```

## 4.5 Who Can Do Which Work

The contracting-side exemption (CCB licensing) is independent from the trade-side exemptions (electrical and plumbing) and from the licensing regimes for special systems (LP gas, septic, well, refrigerant, boiler).

**Simple**

Oregon lets an owner do most of the work on their own house: the building, the plumbing, the wiring, the heating equipment. Special owner permits even allow installing your own septic system and drilling your own well. A few jobs are off limits at any price. LP gas work requires a state-licensed installer. Anything involving refrigerant (connecting a heat pump's lines, charging it) requires a federally certified technician. Every job, owner-done or hired, still gets a permit and an inspection.

**Applied**

Use this table before deciding to self-perform any regulated work.

| Work area | Owner-builder posture | Permit and inspection still required |
|---|---|---|
| General residential building | Often allowed under the homeowner path | Yes |
| Plumbing | Owner work allowed on owner's own building (ORS 693.020). No sale or lease restriction in the statute itself. No immediate-family extension. | Yes |
| Electrical | Narrower owner-or-immediate-family exemption (ORS 479.540), tied to property "not intended for sale, exchange, lease or rent." | Yes |
| Mechanical (HVAC) | No separate trade license; covered through the BCD homeowner-applicant framework alongside the CCB exemption. | Yes |
| Refrigerant work | EPA Section 608 certification required regardless of homeowner status. | Yes (mechanical permit; heat pumps also require an electrical permit) |
| Natural gas piping | BCD allows homeowners or licensed contractors to install natural gas piping. | Yes |
| LP gas (any work) | Hard stop. ORS 480.432 prohibits any person, employer or individual, from doing LP gas installation, fitting, or piping work without a State Fire Marshal license. No homeowner exemption. | Yes (SFM-licensed) |
| Septic / onsite wastewater | Property owner may install with their own septic permit. DEQ-certified installer is otherwise required. | Yes (DEQ / county) |
| Water well | Landowner Well Permit available under OAR 690-205-0175. Otherwise an OWRD-licensed Water Supply Well Constructor is required. | Yes (OWRD) |
| Fire sprinklers | Oregon's ORSC did not adopt mandatory residential automatic sprinklers for one- and two-family dwellings. Where required by another path, plumbing-licensing rules govern installer qualifications. | Yes where required |
| Boiler / pressure vessels | Separate license (ORS 480.630) required; some high-BTU water heaters may cross into this jurisdiction. | Yes |

For each phase of regulated work, verify four things before starting:

1. who pulls the permit
2. who is allowed to perform the work
3. what inspection or test is required
4. what record proves approval

### Permit-Required Work By Trade

**Electrical:** Permit-required electrical work includes any permanent wiring, new circuits, panel upgrades, fuse-to-breaker conversions, garage-door opener receptacles, and low-voltage systems (alarms, audio-video, network). Permit-exempt electrical work is narrow: like-for-like replacement of outlets, switches, and fixtures with GFCI installed; lamp replacement; resetting fuses or breakers; replacing 30-amp-or-less garbage disposals or dishwashers; and low-voltage garage-door wiring.

**Plumbing:** Permit-required plumbing work includes new water-supply piping, drain-waste-vent piping, fixtures requiring concealed work, water heaters, sewer lines, water service, and backflow devices. Permit-exempt plumbing replacements are limited to like-for-like replacement of fixtures without altering concealed piping (faucets, toilets, sinks, hose bibbs, water filters, garbage disposals).

**Mechanical:** Permit-required mechanical work includes installing, altering, or repairing vented heating or cooling systems, woodstoves and fireplace inserts and pellet stoves, ductwork, gas piping, bath fans, dryer exhausts, kitchen range exhausts, heat pumps, mini-splits, and AC condensers. There is no blanket exemption for residential equipment replacement; a like-for-like furnace swap still requires a mechanical permit and final inspection.

**Professional**

The CCB licensing exemption (ORS 701.010) governs the contracting side. The plumbing exemption (ORS 693.020) and the electrical exemption (ORS 479.540) are independent statutes. LP gas (ORS 480.432), septic (DEQ rules under ORS 454 and OAR 340-071), water wells (ORS 537.747 and OAR 690-205), refrigerant handling (EPA Section 608), and boilers and pressure vessels (ORS 480.630) are separate licensing regimes that do not flow from the residential-permit framework.

Tenants do not have an owner-occupier exemption. ORS 90.145 prohibits a tenant from doing electrical or plumbing installation, maintenance, or repair without proper licensure even with a rent-reduction agreement. Property managers, landlords' employees, and business entities cannot use the owner-occupier exemption for someone else's residence.

**Code**

```
An owner may do the plumbing on their own building.
  Exempt from journeyman-plumber licensure when complying with the plumbing law and building code; permit and inspection still required (ORS 693.020(4)); no sale-or-lease restriction in the statute; the companion "not for hire" exemption sits in the plumbing law itself.
  Source: ORS 693.020; ORS 447.060(1)(a).

An owner or immediate family may do the electrical work, on non-sale property.
  Available on residential or farm property owned by the person or immediate family, "not intended for sale, exchange, lease or rent"; permit, inspection, and OESC compliance still required.
  Source: ORS 479.540.

LP gas work requires a State Fire Marshal license, with no homeowner exemption.
  No person, as employer or individual, may do LP gas installation, fitting, or piping work unlicensed; the only statutory exemptions are journeyman plumbers for piping (not tanks or connections), boiler-licensed pressure-piping installers (same restriction), and manufactured-dwelling and RV work. License types: Master Fitter, IC Fitter, RV Fitter.
  Source: ORS 480.432; OAR chapter 837 division 30.

An owner may install their own septic system under their own permit.
  The sewage-disposal licensing rule contains a property-owner exception for installation on the owner's property under a properly issued construction permit; a DEQ-certified installer is otherwise required.
  Source: OAR 340-071-0600; OAR chapter 340 division 71.

An owner may drill their own well under a Landowner Well Permit.
  Available to a property owner who is not a licensed Water Supply Well Constructor; construction must meet the same standards as licensed drillers; $550 permit fee, surety bond, day-of-drilling notification, four-hour-advance seal-placement notification, permit valid 6 months.
  Source: ORS 537.747; OAR 690-205-0175; OAR 690-200 through 690-240.

Refrigerant work requires federal certification regardless of ownership.
  EPA Section 608 technician certification applies whenever refrigerant is handled, including connecting mini-split line sets and charging.
  Source: 40 CFR part 82 subpart F.

Boilers and pressure vessels have their own license.
  Some high-BTU water heaters cross into this jurisdiction.
  Source: ORS 480.630.

Tenants have no owner exemption.
  A tenant may not perform electrical or plumbing installation, maintenance, or repair without licensure, even under a rent-reduction agreement.
  Source: ORS 90.145.

Directly hiring subcontractors changes the owner's position.
  An owner who directly hires subcontractors for permit-required work loses the homeowner-GC exemption unless the work proceeds under the direction of a licensed general contractor.
  Source: ORS 701.010 (2008 amendment).
```

## 4.6 Paid Help, Family, And Subcontractors

ORS 455.320(1)(b) defines an owner-built dwelling as one whose structural components are constructed entirely by the owner who intends to occupy, or by the owner and friends and relatives of the owner assisting on an unpaid basis. The operative words are *entirely* and *unpaid*.

**Simple**

Friends and family can help build the house for free with no legal complications. The moment anyone gets paid, the rules change. A paid helper who is not a licensed contractor can make the homeowner an employer, with tax withholding, workers' compensation, and federal obligations attached. The two clean options are unpaid help and properly licensed contractors.

**Applied**

Before anyone touches the project, write down the labor plan:

- who will work
- whether they are paid
- what they will do
- what license they hold, if any
- which permit covers the work
- who supervises the work
- what insurance covers the jobsite

If anyone is paid and is not a properly licensed contractor, stop and resolve the employer obligations in the table below before work continues. Paid labor can make the homeowner an employer.

If a licensed subcontractor is hired:

- verify the CCB license before work starts (ccb.state.or.us license search)
- verify the trade license (BCD-licensed for plumbing or electrical) where applicable
- collect the certificate of insurance with the homeowner as additional insured if requested
- confirm workers' compensation coverage for the sub's employees
- file the contract, license record, insurance certificate, permit, and inspection result in the binder

**Professional**

The duties created when paid help is not properly licensed:

| Duty | Authority | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Workers' compensation coverage | ORS 656.027 | All workers are subject workers unless an exception applies |
| Householder/repair exception | ORS 656.027(2) | Applies to maintenance, repair, remodeling, or similar work in or about a private home; *Caddy v. SAIF*, 110 Or App 353 (1991) limited this to work on an existing private home, leaving new construction from scratch on raw land likely outside the exemption |
| Casual-labor exception | ORS 656.027(3) | Applies when total labor cost in any 30-day period is less than the indexed threshold (originally $500, $1,000 in the 2023 ORS edition, indexed annually) and the work is not in the employer's trade or business |
| Income-tax withholding and unemployment insurance | ORS 316 | If workers are not licensed contractors, the homeowner is treated as their employer; BIN registration may be required |
| Liability insurance | BCD form recommendation | Sufficient coverage for non-employee injuries on site |
| Federal employer obligations | IRS rules | Social Security and federal income-tax withholding |

The defensible posture: unpaid family or friends (no wages and no in-kind compensation) helping with structural work fits ORS 455.320; everyone else triggers employer obligations under ORS 656, ORS 316, and federal employment law. The only safe sub-case for paid help is using actual licensed CCB-endorsed subcontractors, with the licensed-GC-supervision rule from the 2008 amendment to ORS 701.010 applied where it applies.

**Code**

```

  Source: ORS 455.320(1)(b).

  All workers are subject workers under the workers' compensation law by default.
```

## 4.7 Legal Risk Triage

Use the same severity-sorting habit that applies to land feasibility. Not every legal issue has the same weight.

| Risk level | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Hard stop | Do not proceed until solved | LP gas work without SFM licensing; occupying without CO or TCO; constructing through an entity (LLC) intending to sell |
| Pause and verify | Path may be acceptable; an authority needs to confirm | Trust ownership, LLC involvement, paid helpers, future resale or lease plan, rural-county exemption use |
| Permit gate | Work proceeds inside the permit path | Structural, plumbing, electrical, mechanical, septic, well, grading, driveway access |
| Documentation gate | Work proceeds with a record kept | Inspection passes, batch tickets, product listings, manufacturer instructions, photographs |
| Continuing duty | Ongoing requirement after issuance | Permit non-transferability, CO conditions, deferred submittal closures, surety releases |

## 4.8 Certificate Of Occupancy And Temporary Certificate Of Occupancy

Legal occupancy of a new dwelling requires either a certificate of occupancy or a temporary certificate of occupancy. Under ORSC R110.1, a building or structure may not be used or occupied until the building official has issued a certificate of occupancy.

**Simple**

Nobody may live in the house, even the owner who built it, until the building official issues the certificate of occupancy (the CO). If the house is safe to live in but small items remain unfinished, the official can issue a temporary certificate (a TCO) with a deadline for finishing. Moving in without either one is illegal occupancy, and it typically voids or suspends homeowner insurance coverage; the exact effect depends on the policy language.

**Applied**

Before the certificate of occupancy is issued, three requirements must all be met.

1. **All inspection disciplines passed.** Building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, and site-development inspections are all approved. Site development includes permanent erosion control, on-site stormwater, sanitary facilities, and any landscape requirements imposed by zoning or land-use review.
2. **Contractor information disclosure submitted.** OAR 918-480-0140(3) requires the general contractor or owner who was issued the structural permit to provide the building official with contact information and license information for the GC, the electrical contractor, the HVAC contractor, and the plumbing contractor that performed work on the dwelling. BCD Form 4823 (Notification of Contractor License Information) is the statewide form.
3. **CO statutory minimum content.** ORSC R110.3 requires the CO to include the permit number, address, owner name, use of building, building official's name, code edition under which the permit was issued, and any special stipulations or conditions of the permit.

The certificate is required for new dwellings, duplexes, and ADUs. It is not required for residential additions, alterations, or accessory structures like garages.

**Professional**

The temporary certificate of occupancy is discretionary under ORSC R110.4. The building official may issue a TCO before the permitted work completes, provided the occupied portion is safe to occupy. The official sets the TCO's validity period.

Typical TCO conditions across Oregon jurisdictions (Portland's program guide is the most-cited model):

- minimum fire and life-safety requirements complete and approved
- temporary erosion control in place
- all five inspection disciplines agree the TCO may be issued
- TCO issuance fee paid
- surety or security may be required for unfinished work (Portland sets surety at 120% of the value of remaining work)
- the TCO document lists outstanding items, completion date, and expiration date

Portland TCOs are valid 30 days from issuance. Extensions run in 30-day increments at the building official's discretion. Each extension requires a written rationale for the remaining items and a fee equal to the initial issuance fee. There is no statewide cap on extensions. Other jurisdictions (Bend, Eugene, Salem, county departments) use comparable 30-day or 60-day windows.

The CO and TCO do not start an owner-builder timing clock for resale. They are the gate to legal occupancy. The federal §121 capital-gains use clock starts when the owner actually occupies as a principal residence, which is the date of move-in following CO or TCO issuance, not the issuance itself.

What CO and TCO matter for, separately from the owner-builder timing question:

- legal occupancy (ORSC R110.1)
- federal §121 capital-gains exclusion (the use clock starts at move-in)
- lender disbursement (most construction-to-perm loans require CO before converting; some accept TCO with conditions)
- insurance binding (homeowner policies typically require CO or TCO before binding occupancy coverage)
- property-tax assessment (the assessor may treat substantial completion as the trigger for adding improvement value to the roll)
- address, utilities, and services (USPS, voter registration, school enrollment all require an occupiable address)

**Code**

```
Occupancy requires the certificate.
  A building or structure shall not be used or occupied, in whole or in part, until the building official has issued a certificate of occupancy.
  Source: ORSC R110.1 (2023 edition).

A passing final inspection entitles the owner to the certificate.
  After inspection with no violations found, the CO "shall issue," containing at minimum the permit number, address, owner name, building use, building official's name, code edition of the permit, and any special conditions.
  Source: ORSC R110.3.

The temporary certificate is discretionary and time-limited.
  The building official may issue a TCO before all work completes, provided the occupied portion is safe, with a set validity period.
  Source: ORSC R110.4.

The state rule adds the contractor disclosure and revocation authority.
  The building official must issue a CO before occupancy; subsection (3) requires disclosure of the GC, electrical, HVAC, and plumbing contractors' contact and license information; subsection (4) permits revocation where misrepresentation is found.
  Source: OAR 918-480-0140; BCD Form 4823, oregon.gov/bcd.

The statute delegates CO administration to the state agency.
  Uniform permit, inspection, and certificate-of-occupancy requirements, administered by BCD rulemaking.
  Source: ORS 455.055.

A building official's decision is appealable.
  Appeals go to the local appeals board.
  Source: ORSC R112.
```

## 4.9 Documents Required Before Certificate Of Occupancy

Consolidated checklist of items that must all be complete before the CO is issued.

1. final approved building inspection
2. final approved electrical inspection
3. final approved plumbing inspection
4. final approved mechanical inspection
5. final approved site-development inspection (erosion control, stormwater, sanitary, landscape per zoning or land-use review)
6. OAR 918-480-0140(3) contractor information form (BCD Form 4823 or local equivalent)
7. project-specific deferred submittal closures: engineering letters, fire-suppression sign-offs if applicable, geotechnical reports, special inspection reports
8. local jurisdiction add-ons: address verification, school-district excise tax certification, system-development charge confirmation, septic or well certificate of satisfactory completion if applicable
9. payment of any outstanding permit, plan-review, or surcharge fees

Many jurisdictions also require an as-built submittal for owner-installed septic, the well log filed with OWRD if a landowner well, the DEQ certificate of satisfactory completion for septic, and the State Fire Marshal tank installation report for any LP-gas installation.

## 4.10 The Inspection Sequence At The Legal Level

The full readiness criteria for each inspection are in the Inspection And Approval Sequence chapter. The summary below lists the sequence at the level relevant to permits, contractor licensing, and occupancy.

| Stage | Legal trigger | What the inspection records |
|---|---|---|
| Permit application | Plans submitted; approved drawings, calculations, and engineering attached | Application, plans, plan-review comments, conditions of approval |
| Pre-construction | Permit issued; homeowner statement signed; site notice posted where required | Permit posted on site; site-notice and erosion-control measures in place |
| Site and erosion control | Where required by the local jurisdiction | Erosion-control measures, site-disturbance limits |
| Footing, foundation, reinforcement, embeds | Before concrete is placed | Approved footing geometry, reinforcement, anchor bolts, embeds, sleeves |
| Foundation drainage, dampproofing, capillary break | Before backfill | Drain rock, filter fabric, perimeter drain, dampproofing or waterproofing membrane |
| Under-slab | Before slab is placed | Subgrade, capillary break, vapor retarder, soil-gas retarder where applicable, under-slab insulation, sleeves |
| Rough plumbing | Before walls or chases close | DWV pressure or static test, water-supply rough, gas piping pressure test where applicable |
| Rough electrical | Before walls close | Boxes, conductors, panel rough, grounding, bonding, low-voltage where included |
| Rough mechanical | Before walls or ceilings close | Equipment, ducts, gas piping, ventilation, exhaust, refrigerant lines |
| Insulation and air sealing | Before drywall | Insulation type, depth, continuity, air-sealing penetrations |
| Energy and blower door | Where the compliance path requires testing | Blower-door result, duct test result if applicable |
| Final building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical | Each discipline finals separately | Each discipline's final inspection record |
| Site-development final | Before CO | Permanent erosion control, stormwater, landscape, address |
| Certificate of occupancy | All disciplines final + Form 4823 | CO issued; binder closes |

If a TCO is needed before the full sequence completes, the official defines the minimum life-safety items. The TCO does not waive the remaining items. The remaining items still have to close before the full CO is issued and within the TCO's time window or its extensions.

## 4.11 Records To Save

The legal binder for an owner-built Oregon home:

- permit applications and approvals (building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, septic, well, grading, driveway access, fire where applicable)
- approved plans, including any sheets revised after issuance
- plan-review comments and the responses to each
- inspection cards, inspection approvals, and correction notices for each discipline
- the signed BCD Property Owner Statement and any local equivalent
- engineering letters, stamped sheets, calculations, and special inspection records
- product data, listings, and manufacturer installation instructions
- concrete batch tickets, soil reports, septic approvals, well records, OWRD landowner permit and well log, SFM tank installation report if applicable
- photographs taken before each cover stage
- subcontractor CCB and BCD license records and certificates of insurance
- BCD Form 4823 contractor information notification
- final approval and occupancy records (CO or TCO and any extensions)
- ORS 455.320 county-clerk recording where the structural exemption is used
- ORS 87.007 Notice of Compliance with Homebuyer Protection Act if a sale falls within the 75-day window after substantial completion

The Documentation, Maintenance, And Future Upgrades chapter specifies binder organization, retention, and digital backup in detail.

## 4.12 Owner-Builder Posture Checklist

A single page to verify before regulated work begins.

- [ ] Title held in personal name, or in tenancy by the entirety with spouse, or in a revocable trust where the trustee is also the beneficiary-occupant
- [ ] No LLC ownership of the construction-intent property
- [ ] Land-use approval and zoning verification on file
- [ ] Building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, and any specialty permits planned and applied for as the homeowner
- [ ] BCD Property Owner Statement read and signed
- [ ] Permit posted on site
- [ ] Approved plans on site and current
- [ ] Subcontractor list: CCB-licensed where required, BCD-licensed for plumbing or electrical, with insurance certificates and contracts on file
- [ ] LP gas work, septic installation, well drilling, and refrigerant work assigned to properly licensed individuals or under the appropriate owner-permit path
- [ ] Workers' compensation, liability insurance, and tax-withholding arrangements in place if any paid help is on site
- [ ] Inspection schedule integrated with the construction schedule
- [ ] Project binder set up at permit application
- [ ] Brand and LLC marketing language reviewed for engineering and architecture compliance
- [ ] Plan for occupancy: move-in date, utility account setup, voter and tax registration update
- [ ] CO/TCO requirements documented including BCD Form 4823 contractor disclosure

If any item is unchecked, resolve it before regulated work begins.
