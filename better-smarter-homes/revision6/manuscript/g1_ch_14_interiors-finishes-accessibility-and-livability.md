# Chapter 14: Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, And Livability

This chapter specifies the interior: room geometry and egress, stairs and guards, the garage-to-dwelling separation, wall and ceiling coverings, floor finishes and their fit with radiant heat, wet-area waterproofing and tile, bathroom clearances and anti-scald protection, kitchens and cabinets, doors and safety glazing, storage, accessibility, interior materials chemistry, and move-in readiness.

The concrete structure is the long-service part of the house. The finishes serve the life inside it and are designed to be durable, repairable, and replaceable without disturbing the permanent shell. Several of these interior topics carry code requirements, so the chapter separates what the residential code requires from what is a design choice.

The residential code of record is the 2023 Oregon Residential Specialty Code (ORSC), based on the 2021 IRC, mandatory since April 1, 2024. A 2026 ORSC (2024 IRC base) is anticipated near October 1, 2026 and will revise some stair, egress, and wet-area provisions; values below are for the 2023 ORSC, and the ones most likely to change are noted. Plumbing fixture rules come from the 2023 Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code (OPSC, 2021 UPC base; a 2026 OPSC on the 2024 UPC is anticipated October 1, 2026), and electrical device rules from the branch-circuit chapter.

## 14.1 Finish Philosophy And Replacement Tiers

The finishes are chosen so the permanent assembly stays protected and every serviceable point stays reachable. Materials are grouped by how long they are meant to last, and the money follows that grouping.

**Simple**

The structure lasts for generations; the finishes serve daily life and change over time. That split is useful: the structure can stay sound for many decades while the inside changes with the people in it. The rule that keeps it working is that a finish never hides a problem. No valve, junction box, cleanout, or shutoff gets buried behind permanent finish.

**Applied**

Group interior materials by expected life and design each accordingly:

| Tier | Role | Examples | Design rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Permanent | Do not cover problems | Concrete, reinforcement, foundation, masonry | Keep inspection and repair logic clear |
| Semi-permanent | Service life justifies quality | Doors, cabinets, tile, stone, hardwood, built-ins | Install well, document products, keep replacement possible |
| Consumable | Planned future refresh | Paint, appliances, faucets, some lighting | Do not overbuild items that will be replaced |

Keep samples, model numbers, finish names, paint formulas, and photos in the project record. Do not bury inspection points, valves, junction boxes, cleanouts, dampers, manifolds, or shutoffs behind permanent finish.

**Professional**

Finish durability includes moisture tolerance, heat transfer, abrasion, cleaning chemistry, attachment method, and substrate compatibility, not just material strength. The wrong finish damages a good assembly: vapor-closed flooring over a wet slab, tile without movement joints, an unvented wet room, or an unrated shower backing turns interior design into a building failure. Interior finishes respect the air barrier, vapor behavior, thermal mass, radiant heating, acoustics, service access, and fire separation established by the structural and envelope work. The adopted code, the approved plans, and the product listings govern every assembly below; a decorative decision does not override a required assembly.

## 14.2 Room Geometry: Area, Ceiling Height, Light, And Ventilation

Habitable rooms meet minimum floor area, dimension, and ceiling height, and each has natural light and ventilation or the mechanical equivalent.

**Simple**

The code sets floors under room size. A habitable room is at least 70 square feet and at least 7 feet across. Ceilings are at least 7 feet in living spaces and 6 feet 8 inches in bathrooms and laundries. Each habitable room also gets daylight through windows and either openable windows or mechanical ventilation. These are minimums. This design uses 9-foot ceilings in the main living spaces, against the 7-foot floor.

**Applied**

- Size each habitable room (other than a kitchen) to at least 70 square feet with no horizontal dimension less than 7 feet.
- Provide at least 7 feet of ceiling height in habitable space and hallways, and at least 6 feet 8 inches in bathrooms, toilet rooms, and laundry rooms.
- Where a ceiling slopes, count only the floor area under at least 5 feet of height, and provide the required height over at least half the required floor area.
- Provide glazing for natural light equal to at least 8 percent of the floor area, with at least 4 percent openable for ventilation, or provide the mechanical ventilation covered in the HVAC chapter.

**Professional**

ORSC R304 sets the 70-square-foot minimum floor area and 7-foot minimum dimension for habitable rooms other than kitchens. R305 sets the ceiling heights (7 feet habitable and hallway, 6 feet 8 inches bath and laundry), with sloped-ceiling allowances: at least half the required floor area at 7 feet or more, nothing below 5 feet counted, and a bathroom permitted up to 75 percent of its floor area below 6 feet 8 inches with the fixture clearances kept. ORSC R303 requires natural light glazing at 8 percent of floor area and openable area at 4 percent for ventilation, and mechanical ventilation under M1505 substitutes for the natural ventilation. A bathroom without bathing facilities meets either an aggregate 3 square feet of glazing with half openable or mechanical exhaust.

**Code**

```
Habitable rooms meet a minimum area and dimension.
  At least 70 square feet and no horizontal dimension under 7 feet, kitchens excepted; sloped-ceiling areas below 5 feet do not count toward the minimum.
  Source: ORSC R304 (2023 edition).

Ceiling heights meet the room-type minimums.
  7 feet in habitable space and hallways; 6 feet 8 inches in bathrooms, toilet rooms, and laundry rooms; sloped ceilings comply with at least half the required area at full height; bathrooms allowed up to 75 percent below 6 feet 8 inches.
  Source: ORSC R305 (2023 edition).

Habitable rooms have natural light and ventilation, or the mechanical equivalent.
  Glazing at least 8 percent of floor area; openable area at least 4 percent; mechanical ventilation per M1505 substitutes; a bathroom without bathing facilities needs 3 square feet of glazing (half openable) or mechanical exhaust.
  Source: ORSC R303 (2023 edition).
```

## 14.3 Emergency Escape And Rescue Openings

Every sleeping room and every basement with a sleeping room has an emergency escape and rescue opening sized for a person to get out and a firefighter to get in, operable from inside without a key or tool.

**Simple**

Every bedroom needs one opening a person can escape through in a fire. The opening has fixed minimum sizes, sits low enough to reach, and opens without a key or tool. A bedroom window usually serves. In a basement bedroom, the window needs a well outside big enough to climb out of.

**Applied**

- Provide an emergency escape and rescue opening in every sleeping room, in each basement sleeping room, and in a habitable attic; the opening leads directly to a public way, or to a yard or court that opens to a public way.
- Meet the net clear opening: at least 5.7 square feet (5.0 at grade-floor openings), at least 24 inches high and 20 inches wide, achieved by normal operation.
- Keep the sill no more than 44 inches above the floor.
- Make the opening operable from inside without a key, tool, or special knowledge; any bar, grille, or cover releases the same way.
- Provide a window well of at least 9 square feet, at least 36 inches in width and projection, with a ladder or steps where the well is deeper than 44 inches (the ladder may encroach 6 inches into the well).

**Professional**

ORSC R310 sets the opening at 5.7 square feet net clear (5.0 at grade), 24 inches minimum height, 20 inches minimum width, and a 44-inch maximum sill. Oregon amended R310.1 effective October 1, 2024, striking the 2021 IRC's new destination language and returning the section to the 2021 ORSC wording: openings open directly into a public way, or to a yard or court that opens to a public way. The amendment removed the model's 36-inch yard-width qualifier and its separate unobstructed-path exception. The amendment also states the sprinkler exception in two branches: where the dwelling has an NFPA 13D sprinkler system, basement sleeping rooms need no rescue opening provided the basement has either one R311-compliant means of egress plus one rescue opening, or two R311-compliant means of egress. Storm shelters and basements used only for mechanical equipment up to 200 square feet are exempt. A door may serve as the rescue opening where it is side-hinged or a slider, with an area well where it is below grade.

**Code**

```
Every sleeping room, basement sleeping room, and habitable attic has a rescue opening.
  Opening leads directly to a public way, or to a yard or court opening to a public way; exemptions: storm shelters and mechanical-only basements up to 200 square feet.
  Source: ORSC R310.1 (2023 edition, Oregon amendment effective October 1, 2024).

The opening meets the net clear dimensions by normal operation.
  5.7 square feet net clear (5.0 at grade-floor openings); 24 inches minimum height; 20 inches minimum width; sill not more than 44 inches above the floor; operable from inside without key, tool, or special knowledge; bars or covers release the same way.
  Source: ORSC R310 (2023 edition).

A below-grade opening gets a window well sized for escape.
  At least 9 square feet, 36 inches in width and projection; ladder or steps where deeper than 44 inches, encroaching not more than 6 inches.
  Source: ORSC R310 (2023 edition).

A sprinklered basement can omit sleeping-room rescue openings under two configurations.
  NFPA 13D system, plus either one R311 means of egress and one rescue opening, or two R311 means of egress.
  Source: ORSC R310.1 Exception 2 (2023 edition, Oregon amendment effective October 1, 2024).
```

## 14.4 Stairs, Handrails, Guards, And Landings

Stairs, handrails, and guards follow fixed geometry that keeps them safe to use and consistent from step to step. These dimensions are among the most-checked at inspection.

**Simple**

Stair falls come from inconsistency, so the code fixes the geometry. Riser heights and tread depths may not vary more than 3/8 inch in a flight. A stair is at least 3 feet wide, with a handrail between 34 and 38 inches high. Any drop over 30 inches gets a guard a small child cannot slip through.

**Applied**

- Build stairs at least 36 inches wide above the handrail, with headroom of at least 6 feet 8 inches; a handrail may project up to 4-1/2 inches into the width on each side.
- Keep risers no higher than 7-3/4 inches (and at least 4 inches) and treads at least 10 inches deep, with no more than 3/8 inch variation between adjacent risers or treads or across a flight.
- Provide a landing at least 36 inches in the direction of travel at the top and bottom of each stairway; at an exterior door, keep the interior landing within 1-1/2 inches below the threshold, and an exterior landing within 8 inches below it (with the door-swing limits).
- Provide a graspable handrail (Type I or Type II profile) 34 to 38 inches above the nosing on at least one side of any stair with four or more risers.
- Provide a guard at least 36 inches high wherever a walking surface is more than 30 inches above the floor or grade, with openings that reject a 4-inch sphere (4-3/8 inches at the tread-riser-guard triangle; 6 inches at the open side of a stair between tread, riser, and bottom rail).

**Professional**

ORSC R311 and R312 set these dimensions from the 2021 IRC: 36-inch stair width above the handrail with 4-1/2-inch handrail projections allowed, 6-foot-8-inch headroom measured from the nosing line, 7-3/4-inch maximum and 4-inch minimum riser, 10-inch minimum tread, and 3/8-inch maximum variation both between adjacent steps and cumulatively across a flight. Landings are 36 inches in the direction of travel, with the threshold-height limits at doors. Handrails run 34 to 38 inches with a Type I or Type II graspable profile for four or more risers, and guards are 36 inches with the 4-inch, 4-3/8-inch, and 6-inch sphere allowances by location. Lighting control at the top and bottom is required for stairs of six or more risers (the branch-circuit chapter states the lighting rules). The 2024 IRC revises some stair geometry, so a build permitted under the 2026 ORSC cross-checks these dimensions against the adopted edition.

**Code**

```
Stair geometry is fixed and consistent.
  Width 36 inches minimum above the handrail (handrails may project 4-1/2 inches each side); headroom 6 feet 8 inches; risers 4 to 7-3/4 inches; treads 10 inches minimum; variation not more than 3/8 inch between adjacent steps or across the flight.
  Source: ORSC R311.7 (2023 edition).

Landings are provided at the top and bottom of each stairway.
  36 inches minimum in the direction of travel; interior side of an exterior door not more than 1-1/2 inches below the threshold; exterior landings not more than 8 inches below, with door-swing limits.
  Source: ORSC R311.3 and R311.7.6 (2023 edition).

Stairs of four or more risers get a graspable handrail.
  34 to 38 inches above the nosing; Type I or Type II profile; on at least one side.
  Source: ORSC R311.7.8 (2023 edition).

Raised walking surfaces get guards.
  Required above 30 inches; 36 inches minimum height; openings reject a 4-inch sphere (4-3/8 inches at the tread triangle; 6 inches at the open side of a stair).
  Source: ORSC R312 (2023 edition).
```

## 14.5 Garage-To-Dwelling Separation

Where an attached garage adjoins the dwelling, the separation is a specific gypsum assembly with a self-latching, self-closing door, and no opening into a sleeping room. A future garage triggers this assembly only when it is built and connected.

**Simple**

A garage holds a running engine, fuel, and stored chemicals. So the wall between garage and house is a barrier against fumes and fire. The barrier is specific gypsum board plus a thick or fire-rated door that closes and latches by itself. Penetrations through it get sealed. A garage door may never open into a bedroom.

**Applied**

- Separate the garage from the dwelling with at least 1/2-inch gypsum board on the garage side; use 5/8-inch Type X on the ceiling where a habitable room is above the garage, and protect the structure supporting a dwelling above with 1/2-inch gypsum.
- Use a door between the garage and dwelling that is solid wood or solid or honeycomb-core steel at least 1-3/8 inches thick, or a 20-minute fire-rated door, and equip it to self-latch and self-close.
- Do not open a garage directly into a room used for sleeping.
- Duct through the separation in at least 26-gauge steel with no opening into the garage; keep any vibration isolator at least 18 inches from the penetration; fireblock other penetrations per R302.11.

**Professional**

ORSC R302.5 and R302.6 (Table R302.6) set the separation: 1/2-inch gypsum on the garage side, 5/8-inch Type X under a habitable room, and 1/2-inch gypsum protection on the supporting structure. The 2021 IRC (carried into the 2023 ORSC) requires the connecting door to be both self-latching and self-closing or automatic-closing, a change from the prior edition that inspectors check. R302.5.1 prohibits an opening into a sleeping room. The gypsum is prescribed protection, not a rated assembly: the thickness and type are the requirement. Where both the dwelling and garage are protected by an NFPA 13D sprinkler system, the door rating and self-closing requirements are waived, but the wall separation is not. A future garage is planned in the design without installing the door and sheathing, and the assembly is completed when the connection is made.

**Code**

```
The garage is separated from the dwelling by prescribed gypsum protection.
  1/2-inch gypsum on the garage side; 5/8-inch Type X on the ceiling where a habitable room is above; structure supporting a dwelling above protected with 1/2-inch gypsum.
  Source: ORSC R302.6 and Table R302.6 (2023 edition).

The connecting door is rated or solid, and closes itself.
  Solid wood or solid/honeycomb-core steel at least 1-3/8 inches thick, or 20-minute fire-rated; self-latching and self-closing or automatic-closing; no opening from a garage into a sleeping room.
  Source: ORSC R302.5.1 (2023 edition).

Ducts and penetrations through the separation are protected.
  Ducts at least 26-gauge sheet steel with no openings into the garage, vibration isolators at least 18 inches from the penetration; other penetrations fireblocked.
  Source: ORSC R302.5.2 and R302.5.3 (2023 edition).

A sprinklered dwelling and garage relax the door, not the wall.
  NFPA 13D protection in both: door rating and self-closing waived; gypsum separation retained.
  Source: ORSC R302.5.1 exception (2023 edition).
```

## 14.6 Wall And Ceiling Coverings

Interior coverings follow the gypsum, backer, and fastening rules, and any foam plastic insulation is covered by a thermal barrier. A concrete or masonry wall may be left exposed or furred and covered.

**Simple**

Drywall thickness follows the framing behind it, and wet areas get cement or glass-mat board instead of regular drywall. Any foam insulation on the inside of the house gets covered with drywall. Exposed foam burns fast and produces toxic smoke. Bare concrete is a legal finished wall. This house uses it where the thermal mass and the look both work.

**Applied**

- Use gypsum board of the thickness the framing spacing requires (1/2 inch standard at 16-inch on-center framing; 5/8 inch on ceilings framed wider than 16 inches on center and in fire-resistance assemblies).
- Fasten per the code table (screws at about 7 inches on center, nails at 8) and tape and bed joints; use alkali-resistant mesh tape at cement-board joints.
- Cover foam plastic insulation on the interior with a thermal barrier of at least 1/2-inch gypsum or an approved equivalent.
- Use cement board, fiber-cement board, or glass-mat gypsum, not regular drywall, as tile backer in wet areas (regular drywall is permitted only under a listed waterproofing membrane system).
- Photograph wiring, piping, blocking, and shutoffs before closing any wall.

**Professional**

ORSC R702 governs gypsum (ASTM C1396), plaster, and paneling, with the thickness and fastening matrix in Table R702.3.5. ORSC R316 requires the thermal barrier over foam plastic, which is the rule that prevents an exposed-foam interior. A required fire-resistance-rated wall is built to a tested assembly (ASTM E119 or UL 263) or a prescriptive IBC-table assembly. Exposed concrete or CMU, drywall on furring, plaster veneer, and metal-stud partitions are all valid interior wall paths; the choice follows thermal-mass goals, acoustics, cost, and service access. An exposed-concrete ceiling reflects sound and shows forming quality, so acoustic treatment and lighting are planned with it.

**Code**

```
Gypsum board thickness and fastening follow the code table.
  Gypsum board per ASTM C1396; thickness by framing spacing per Table R702.3.5 (1/2 inch at 16 inches on center; 5/8 inch on wider-framed ceilings and rated assemblies); fastener spacing per the table.
  Source: ORSC R702 and Table R702.3.5 (2023 edition).

Foam plastic is separated from the interior by a thermal barrier.
  Minimum 1/2-inch gypsum board or approved equivalent.
  Source: ORSC R316 (2023 edition).

Required fire-resistance-rated assemblies are tested assemblies.
  ASTM E119 or UL 263 tested designs, or prescriptive IBC-table assemblies.
  Source: ORSC R302.4 (2023 edition); ASTM E119; UL 263.
```

## 14.7 Floor Finishes And Radiant Compatibility

The floor finish is chosen for the room's use and for its fit with the radiant slab, and the slab's moisture is tested before any moisture-sensitive finish goes down. Polished concrete is the cleanest fit for a radiant concrete house.

**Simple**

Every floor here sits on a heated slab, so the finish has to pass heat instead of blocking it. Polished concrete is the best case: the slab itself is the floor. Tile passes heat well. Engineered wood works when the maker rates it for radiant heat. Thick carpet blocks about half the heat and is excluded. Before any wood or vinyl goes down, the slab gets a moisture test, because sealed-in dampness ruins flooring.

**Applied**

- Match the finish to the room: polished or stained concrete in living areas, kitchen, halls, and basement; large-format porcelain tile in wet areas, entry, and laundry; engineered wood or cork in bedrooms and offices.
- Confirm the finish is rated for the radiant floor and keep the tile surface temperature under the product limit (TCNA guidance caps tile at about 85°F), ramping the system up no faster than about 5°F per hour.
- Test the slab moisture before installing a moisture-sensitive finish: ASTM F2170 in-situ relative humidity at or below 75 percent, or ASTM F1869 calcium-chloride emission at or below 3 pounds per 1,000 square feet per 24 hours; expect 30 to 90 days after the pour before the slab passes.
- Install a crack-isolation membrane (ANSI A118.12) between the slab and tile over a heated slab.
- Float engineered wood over a radiant-rated underlayment without nailing into the topping slab, and keep the total finish R-value under about 1.0 for heat transfer; each additional R-1 cuts radiant output by roughly 10 percent, and carpet cuts it by about half.
- Seal polished concrete with a penetrating (non-film) sealer and plan a reseal every 3 to 5 years; the surface refinishes rather than being replaced.

**Professional**

Polished concrete preserves the thermal coupling of the radiant slab, avoids trapped moisture under a second floor, and is refinished rather than replaced. Tile and stone suit thermal mass with proper substrate preparation, crack isolation, and movement joints; through-body porcelain in large formats reduces grout exposure, and epoxy grout in wet areas removes the sealing cycle. Slab moisture testing (ASTM F2170 at 40 percent of slab depth, or F1869, per ASTM F710, with the slab at service conditions at least 48 hours before testing) is a manufacturer and warranty requirement rather than a code item. Skipping it is the most common cause of flooring failure over slab. Engineered wood over radiant is generally limited to about 80°F surface temperature with manufacturer certification (solid hardwood moves too much and is excluded); cork is capped near 80°F and conducts poorly; vinyl products are commonly capped near 85°F and their plasticizers degrade at sustained higher temperatures. Marble and some stones need slow ramp rates because of thermal-shock sensitivity.

**Code**

```
Slab moisture is verified before moisture-sensitive flooring.
  ASTM F2170 in-situ relative humidity at or below 75 percent (probes at 40 percent of slab depth), or ASTM F1869 calcium-chloride emission at or below 3 lb/1,000 sq ft/24 hr; practice per ASTM F710; slab at service conditions at least 48 hours before testing.
  Source: ASTM F2170; ASTM F1869; ASTM F710 (manufacturer and warranty requirement; the ORSC does not adopt these tests).

Tile over a heated slab gets crack isolation and a surface-temperature cap.
  Crack-isolation membrane per ANSI A118.12; tile surface temperature about 85°F maximum with ramp-up near 5°F per hour, per the TCNA radiant methods.
  Source: ANSI A118.12; TCNA Handbook methods F124, F125, F176, F189.

Finish-floor thermal resistance stays inside the radiant manufacturer's limit.
  Total finish R-value per the radiant manufacturer (about 2.0 to 2.5 maximum; near 1.0 for best transfer); manufacturer radiant rating required for wood, cork, and vinyl.
  Source: radiant and flooring manufacturer installation requirements.
```

## 14.8 Wet Areas: Waterproofing And Tile

A tiled wet area is a waterproofing system finished with tile. Tile is not waterproof by itself; the membrane, backer, slope, drain, and movement joints do the waterproofing, and the tile is selected for slip resistance.

**Simple**

Water gets through tile and grout; the layer that stops it is a membrane behind them. So a shower is built as a waterproof basin first and tiled second. The floor slopes to the drain, and the membrane turns up the walls. Soft joints let the assembly move without cracking. The tile itself is chosen so it is not slippery when wet.

**Applied**

- Finish shower walls in a nonabsorbent surface to at least 6 feet above the floor (or 70 inches above the drain, whichever is greater); tile to the ceiling is this project's standard.
- Install a listed waterproofing membrane (ANSI A118.10) over an approved backer, continuous across the shower floor and up the walls, with the shower liner turned up at least 2 inches above the finished threshold and not perforated in the watertight portion.
- Slope the shower floor 1/4 inch per foot to the drain, and use a drain assembly listed for the chosen membrane (the drain and membrane are a matched pair).
- For a curbless shower, run the membrane continuously across the threshold and at least 6 inches into the dry-area floor, and use a linear drain so the floor slopes in one direction.
- Provide movement joints (soft sealant joints) at changes of plane, at the perimeter, and at the tile-standard spacing.
- Select tile with a dynamic coefficient of friction of at least 0.42 (at least 0.50 in showers) and record the product data in the project file.
- Use tempered safety glass (typically 3/8 inch, frameless or minimum-frame) for the enclosure, and build in a bench and a tiled niche where the design calls for them.

**Professional**

ORSC R702.4 sets the wet-area minimums (nonabsorbent surface height, approved backer per Table R702.4.2), and OPSC 417.5.2 governs the shower lining. Table R702.4.2 lists three backer materials with their standards: glass-mat gypsum (ASTM C1178), nonasbestos fiber-cement board (ASTM C1288 or ISO 8336 Category C), and nonasbestos fiber mat-reinforced cementitious backer units (ASTM C1325); the Oregon table is the unamended model table. Regular gypsum is not a shower tile backer unless a listed membrane system permits it. The industry layer above the code is ANSI A118.10 (waterproofing membranes), A118.12 (crack isolation), A137.1 and A326.3 (DCOF slip resistance), and the TCNA Handbook methods that manufacturer warranties reference. Movement joints per the TCNA method (typically 8 to 12 feet on center in dry areas and 8 feet in wet areas) prevent the most common tile failure. The DCOF floor is a design minimum, not a safety guarantee: drainage, surface texture, contaminants, and footwear still govern real slip behavior. A curbless shower with a linear drain keeps the membrane continuous across the threshold, and manufacturer installation instructions govern the warranty for any specific detail.

**Code**

```
Shower walls are nonabsorbent to the required height over an approved backer.
  Nonabsorbent surface to 6 feet above the floor or 70 inches above the drain, whichever is greater; backer per Table R702.4.2: glass-mat gypsum (ASTM C1178), fiber-cement (ASTM C1288 or ISO 8336 Category C), or fiber mat-reinforced cementitious backer units (ASTM C1325).
  Source: ORSC R702.4 and Table R702.4.2 (2023 edition, unamended model table).

The shower floor is lined waterproof.
  Lining turned up at least 2 inches above the finished threshold; not nailed or perforated in the watertight portion; sloped 1/4 inch per foot to the drain.
  Source: OPSC 417.5.2 (2023 edition).

Waterproofing, crack isolation, and slip resistance follow the ANSI standards.
  Bonded waterproofing membranes per ANSI A118.10; crack isolation per A118.12; tile DCOF at least 0.42 on level interior wet floors (0.50 in showers), tested per ANSI A326.3.
  Source: ANSI A118.10; ANSI A118.12; ANSI A137.1; ANSI A326.3.

Assembly details and movement joints follow the TCNA methods.
  Movement joints at changes of plane, perimeters, and 8 to 12 feet on center (8 feet in wet areas); assembly methods and warranty conditions per the TCNA Handbook and the membrane manufacturer.
  Source: TCNA Handbook (movement joint method EJ171); manufacturer installation instructions.
```

## 14.9 Bathrooms: Fixture Clearances And Anti-Scald

Bathroom fixtures need clear space around them, and every shower, tub, and bidet has anti-scald temperature control. The temperature architecture pairs a hot storage setting with a limited delivery temperature.

**Simple**

Hot water at 140°F scalds a child in about a second, so the valves at the tub and shower limit what comes out to 120°F. The water heater can still store hotter water; the limit is set at the point of use. Fixtures also need room: space to stand at the sink, sit at the toilet, and step into the shower.

**Applied**

- Provide at least 21 inches of clear space in front of a water closet and lavatory, center the water closet at least 15 inches from any sidewall or fixture, and give it at least 30 inches of width.
- Size a shower to at least 30 by 30 inches interior with at least 24 inches of clear space at the entry and at least a 22-inch clear door opening.
- Control shower and tub-shower temperature with a balanced-pressure, thermostatic, or combination valve (ASSE 1016 or ASME A112.18.1), field-set per the manufacturer's instructions to deliver no more than 120°F, and log the set point in the project binder (the documentation chapter states the record system).
- Limit bathtub and whirlpool fill temperature to 120°F with an ASSE 1070 device where a combination tub-shower valve does not already provide it; a bidet gets its own temperature-limiting device.
- Use showerheads at or under Oregon's 1.8 gallons per minute rating.
- Provide bathroom exhaust of at least 50 CFM intermittent or 20 CFM continuous to the outdoors (80 to 100 CFM serves a bathroom with a separate soaking tub better), with a humidity-sensing or timer control.
- Specify comfort-height toilets (17-to-19-inch seat) and a handheld shower on a slide bar; both serve accessibility at no meaningful premium.

**Professional**

ORSC R307 and OPSC 402.5 set the fixture clearances (21-inch front clearance, 30-inch water-closet width, 15-inch centerline; 30-by-30-inch shower with 24 inches at the entry). The anti-scald sections in Oregon's UPC-based plumbing code are OPSC 408.3 and 409.4, not the 424-series numbers used in IPC-based states. OPSC 408.3 requires the shower or tub-shower control to be a pressure-balance, thermostatic, or combination valve complying with ASSE 1016/ASME A112.1016/CSA B125.16 or ASME A112.18.1/CSA B125.1. The valve is installed at the point of use and adjusted to a 120°F maximum mixed-water setting; the water heater thermostat is not an acceptable control. OPSC 409.4 limits bathtub and whirlpool filler discharge to 120°F through an ASSE 1070/ASME A112.1070/CSA B125.70 device, and OPSC 410.3 requires a temperature-limiting device on a bidet. The 2021 UPC base adds a water heater conforming to ASSE 1084 as an alternative limiting means for 408.3, 409.4, and 410.3. The 120°F limit on public lavatories (407.3) does not reach private residential lavatories. Oregon amended the showerhead flow to 1.8 gpm at 80 psi (OPSC 408.2, HB 2062). Storing water at 140°F for Legionella suppression while delivering 120°F through an ASSE 1017 master mixing valve at the heater, with the point-of-use devices above, is the resilient configuration detailed in the water-supply chapter. Bathroom ventilation follows M1505.5, and the electrical protection follows the branch-circuit chapter.

**Code**

```
Bathroom fixtures meet the clearance minimums.
  Water closet: 21-inch front clearance, 30-inch width, 15-inch centerline to sidewall or fixture. Shower: 30 by 30 inches interior minimum, 24 inches clear at the entry, 22-inch clear door opening. Tub: 21 inches clear at the open side.
  Source: ORSC R307 (2023 edition); OPSC 402.5 (2023 edition).

Shower and tub-shower valves are anti-scald valves set to 120°F.
```

## 14.10 Kitchens, Cabinets, And Counters

The kitchen is planned by work zone with the appliances, circuits, and exhaust frozen before the cabinets are ordered, and cabinets and counters are attached to framing and substrate that carry their load.

**Simple**

A kitchen is planned backward from the work: cooking, cleaning, prep, and storage each get a zone. The appliance models, sink, hood duct, and outlets are all decided before cabinets are ordered. Cabinets are cut to fit them. Counters are heavy; stone needs a plywood base and anchored cabinets under it.

**Applied**

- Plan the kitchen by zone (cooking, cleaning, food storage, prep, serving) and freeze the appliance models, sink location, receptacle and lighting locations, hood route, plumbing rough-in, and floor thickness before ordering cabinets.
- Provide working counter runs at the zones: about 24 inches each side of the range and sink and 18 inches at the refrigerator handle side.
- Attach wall cabinets through the back rails into framing (two screws per stud at top and bottom rails), with solid blocking behind the rails.
- Support a stone or quartz counter on a continuous 3/4-inch plywood substrate over the base cabinets, anchor the cabinets to the floor, and bracket any unsupported overhang beyond 6 inches per the fabricator's specification.
- Decide the island receptacle before the cabinet order: install a listed countertop or work-surface receptacle assembly, or rough in the junction box and pathway the electrical code requires for future installation (side-of-cabinet receptacles no longer qualify).
- Provide solid blocking in the walls for grab bars, heavy mirrors, and wall-hung fixtures, even where they are not installed now.
- Coordinate the kitchen circuits, GFCI and AFCI protection, and countertop receptacle spacing with the branch-circuit chapter.

**Professional**

The ORSC does not set cabinet attachment criteria; those come from the manufacturer and trade practice (screws into framing at each stud crossing, plywood substrate under stone, mechanical anchorage, cantilever support for overhangs, and shelf design near 30 pounds per square foot, 50 for book storage). Grab-bar blocking is standard at 33 to 36 inches above the floor at toilet and shower locations, full-width between studs with 2x backing so any screw pattern engages. Counter material follows use. Installed costs (2026): roughly $80-$150 per square foot for quartz (non-porous, no sealing, heat-sensitive), $60-$150 for granite (heat-tolerant, sealed every 1 to 3 years), and $75-$200 and up for marble (stains and etches; suited to a baking zone rather than a whole kitchen). Butcher block serves an island top with regular oiling. The 2023 electrical code made island and peninsula receptacles optional but requires rough-in provisions for future installation where omitted, ended side-of-cabinet countertop receptacles, and extended GFCI protection to all dwelling kitchen receptacles. The branch-circuit chapter states those rules, and the kitchen layout is coordinated with them before the cabinet order.

**Code**

```
Cabinet and counter attachment follows the manufacturer and the fabricator.
  Wall cabinets screwed to framing at each stud crossing; stone counters on continuous 3/4-inch plywood substrate with anchored cabinets; overhangs beyond 6 inches supported per the fabricator.
  Source: cabinet and stone manufacturer installation requirements (the ORSC does not specify).

Grab-bar and fixture blocking is built in as future provision.
  2x solid backing at 33 to 36 inches above the floor at toilet and shower locations, full width between studs.
  Source: trade practice per ANSI A117.1 mounting heights (voluntary for this dwelling).

Kitchen electrical rules are carried in the branch-circuit chapter.
  Countertop receptacle spacing, island/peninsula receptacle or future-provision rule, small-appliance circuits, GFCI and AFCI per the 2023 OESC with Oregon Table 1-E.
  Source: OESC/NEC 210.52(C), 210.8, 210.11(C), 210.12 (2023 edition; see the branch-circuit chapter).
```

## 14.11 Doors, Egress, And Safety Glazing

At least one exterior door provides the required egress, and glazing in hazardous locations is safety glazing. Interior door hardware on sleeping and bathroom rooms releases from outside without a key.

**Simple**

One exterior door must be a real exit: side-hinged, at least 32 inches of clear opening, with a low threshold. Glass anywhere a person could fall into it is tempered: doors, beside doors, around tubs and showers, along stairs. Tempered glass crumbles instead of shearing into blades. Bedroom and bathroom locks open from outside with a coin or pin, never a key.

**Applied**

- Provide at least one side-hinged exterior egress door with a 32-inch clear width (door open 90 degrees) and 78-inch clear height, with a threshold no more than 1-1/2 inches above the interior floor.
- Use safety glazing (tempered or laminated) in doors, within 24 inches of a door edge below 60 inches, in and around tubs and showers, and adjacent to stairs and landings within the defined zones.
- Use privacy hardware on bedroom and bathroom doors that releases from outside without a key; do not use a sliding or barn door as the required egress door from a sleeping room.
- Specify solid-core interior doors (1-3/8 inch standard; 1-3/4 inch with perimeter seals and automatic door bottoms where sound isolation matters), hung on three ball-bearing hinges, with lever handles.
- Install exterior doors as part of the envelope, with flashing, air sealing, and a drainage path, not as loose finish carpentry.

**Professional**

ORSC R311.2 requires the egress door (32-inch clear width, 78-inch clear height, 1-1/2-inch maximum threshold). ORSC R308 requires safety glazing at hazardous locations: in doors, within 24 inches of a door where the glass is below 60 inches, in tub and shower enclosures, within 60 inches of a tub or shower where the glass is below 60 inches, and adjacent to stairs and landings within the defined zones. Safety glazing meets ANSI Z97.1 or 16 CFR 1201. Exterior doors meet the Chapter 11 U-factor and air-leakage limits (0.5 CFM per square foot tested to AAMA/WDMA/CSA 101/I.S.2/A440), and door flashing follows ASTM E2112 integrated with the water-resistive barrier. Interior doors carry no code rating in a single-family house apart from the garage door; the solid-core specification, wider leaves (30-inch minimum at bathrooms, 32 at most rooms, 36 where the accessibility section applies), pocket doors with metal-stud pockets, and acoustic sealing are durability and livability choices. A smart lock still provides safe manual operation from inside where egress requires it.

**Code**

```
One exterior door is the required egress door.
  Side-hinged; 32-inch clear width at 90 degrees open; 78-inch clear height; threshold not more than 1-1/2 inches above the interior floor.
  Source: ORSC R311.2 (2023 edition).

Glazing in hazardous locations is safety glazing.
  In doors; within 24 inches of a door edge below 60 inches; in tub and shower enclosures and within 60 inches of them below 60 inches; adjacent to stairs and landings in the defined zones; tempered or laminated per ANSI Z97.1 or 16 CFR 1201.
  Source: ORSC R308 (2023 edition).

Exterior doors meet the energy limits and are flashed into the envelope.
  U-factor per Chapter 11; air leakage 0.5 CFM per square foot per AAMA/WDMA/CSA 101/I.S.2/A440; flashing per ASTM E2112 integrated with the water-resistive barrier.
  Source: ORSC Chapter 11 (2023 edition); ASTM E2112.
```

## 14.12 Storage, Pantry, Mudroom, And Laundry

Storage is planned space, not leftover space, and the utility rooms get the light, power, water, drainage, and ventilation their use requires. Closet lighting follows the fixture-clearance rules.

**Applied**

- Plan the entry or mudroom, pantry, closets, laundry, and basement storage for the specific loads they carry, with matching shelf strength.
- Give each area lighting, receptacles where needed, durable moisture-tolerant flooring, and exhaust where moisture collects.
- Use permitted closet fixtures at the required clearance from storage: enclosed surface LED or incandescent at 12 inches, enclosed recessed or fluorescent at 6 inches, fixtures identified for in-storage use at 0; no exposed-lamp or pendant fixtures.
- Provide a laundry standpipe, drain, and exhaust, and leak protection at the washer.

**Professional**

Built-in storage reduces dependence on furniture and keeps circulation clear, and the pantry, mudroom, and laundry are planned before the final electrical, plumbing, HVAC, and cabinet decisions because they often need water, drains, exhaust, charging shelves, or special doors. Closet lighting follows NEC 410.16, because a fixture too close to stored material is a fire risk. The clearances: 12 inches for enclosed surface-mounted fixtures, 6 inches for enclosed recessed and fluorescent fixtures, zero for fixtures identified for use within the storage space, and no pendant or exposed-lamp fixtures at all. The 2023 electrical code added the laundry to the rooms requiring a wall-switched lighting outlet. The laundry follows the plumbing and mechanical chapters for the standpipe, drain, and dryer exhaust.

**Code**

```
Closet luminaires meet the clearance-from-storage rules.
```

## 14.13 Accessibility And Aging In Place

A private single-family house is not covered by federal accessibility law, so accessibility here is a voluntary design standard the project adopts because it serves injury, aging, visitors, and daily life. It is designed in before construction.

**Simple**

Accessibility helps far more than wheelchair users: it helps with injuries, aging, carrying groceries, and moving furniture. None of it is required for a private home a family builds and owns. Most of it is nearly free at design time. A 36-inch door costs about what a 30-inch door costs, and grab-bar blocking is scrap lumber. The same features added after tile and drywall start with demolition.

**Applied**

Low-regret accessibility choices that trigger no code requirement:

- One no-step entry route into the house.
- Wider door openings (36-inch doors) on the ground floor where the layout allows.
- Reduced thresholds and a ground-floor full or half bath with maneuvering room.
- Blocking in bathroom walls for future grab bars.
- Curbless or low-threshold showers, a built-in bench, and a handheld shower on a slide bar.
- Comfort-height toilets and lever handles throughout; rocker light switches.
- Switches, outlets, and controls within a comfortable reach range (outlets at 18 inches above the floor rather than 12).
- Strong lighting at entries, stairs, halls, bathrooms, and exterior paths.
- Stacked closets aligned floor-to-floor and sized about 5 by 5 feet for a future lift or small elevator, where that is wanted.

**Professional**

The Americans with Disabilities Act does not apply to individually owned private single-family homes; it covers public accommodations and government facilities. The Fair Housing Act accessibility design requirements apply only to covered multifamily dwellings of four or more units, and a detached single-family house is not covered. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act applies only to federally funded housing. ANSI A117.1 (the 2017 edition referenced by the current code, with a 2024 edition published) is the technical accessibility standard, and for a private single-family house it is a voluntary best practice, not a requirement. Visitability (a no-step entry, 32-inch clear ground-floor doors, and a ground-floor bathroom with maneuvering room) is a voluntary framework that some jurisdictions promote through incentives. Oregon has no statewide mandatory visitability requirement for a private single-family house, so the specific site's jurisdiction is checked for any local incentive program. These features are stated as voluntary choices, and any dimension drawn from A117.1 is applied without invoking the federal regimes, as long as no federal or state housing funding flows through the project. The decision framework: the near-free items are built in every time; single-floor living, the elevator provision, and the curbless entry are weighed against the site and plan; full roll-in bathrooms and 42-inch halls are built when a specific need exists.

**Code**

```
Federal accessibility law does not reach this house.
  ADA (28 CFR Part 36): public accommodations and government facilities only. Fair Housing Act (24 CFR 100.205): covered multifamily of four or more units only. Section 504: federally funded housing only.
  Source: 28 CFR Part 36; 24 CFR 100.205; Section 504, Rehabilitation Act.

The technical standard is voluntary here.
  ANSI A117.1 (2017 edition referenced by current codes; 2024 published): applied as best practice by choice.
  Source: ANSI A117.1-2017.
```

## 14.14 Interior Materials Chemistry

Composite wood carries a mandatory federal formaldehyde limit, and finishes are selected to low-emission standards because source control is a more effective air-quality strategy than ventilation alone.

**Simple**

Glued wood products (plywood, MDF, particleboard) can release formaldehyde for years. Federal law caps that for everything sold since 2019, and the label proves it. Paints, glues, and finishes release their own fumes, so this project buys low-emission versions. Keeping the fumes out of the house beats filtering them out of the air later.

**Applied**

- Specify composite wood products (plywood, MDF, particleboard, and finished goods containing them) certified and labeled compliant with the federal formaldehyde standard, and retain the labels.
- Specify no-added-formaldehyde or ultra-low-emitting composite wood where available.
- Select paints, sealants, adhesives, and flooring meeting the California Section 01350 emission method or GREENGUARD Gold, and low-VOC content limits.
- Keep the product data and certifications in the project file.

**Professional**

TSCA Title VI (40 CFR Part 770) is the binding federal standard: all composite wood products sold or used since March 22, 2019 are certified and labeled compliant by an EPA-recognized third-party certifier. The emission limits are identical to CARB ATCM Phase 2, tested per ASTM E1333 or D6007. Solid wood is unregulated, and phenol-formaldehyde plywood is compliant by default because it emits far less than urea-formaldehyde. Above the mandatory floor, GREENGUARD Gold and FloorScore certify low VOC emissions, and the CDPH Standard Method (California Section 01350) is the chamber test behind those labels. VOC content limits follow the SCAQMD rules (paints under about 50 grams per liter for flat, sealants and adhesives under the Rule 1168 limits). Specifying products to Section 01350 for paints, sealants, adhesives, and flooring is a more effective indoor-air-quality strategy than ventilation alone.

**Code**

```
Composite wood products carry the federal formaldehyde certification.
  Certified and labeled TSCA Title VI compliant by an EPA-recognized certifier; limits equal to CARB ATCM Phase 2; tested per ASTM E1333 or D6007; applies to products sold since March 22, 2019.
  Source: TSCA Title VI (40 CFR Part 770).

Low-emission finishes are specified to the recognized test method.
  CDPH Standard Method v1.2 (California Section 01350) chamber test, the basis of GREENGUARD Gold and FloorScore.
  Source: CDPH Standard Method v1.2.

VOC content limits follow the SCAQMD rules used as the specification benchmark.
  Architectural coatings per Rule 1113 (flat paints near 50 g/L); adhesives and sealants per Rule 1168.
  Source: SCAQMD Rule 1113; SCAQMD Rule 1168.
```

## 14.15 Lighting, Move-In Readiness, And Records

Interior lighting is designed from the furnished room, and move-in readiness means every code-required safety item is present and every normal activity works. The finish record and the pre-cover documentation let a future owner maintain the house without guessing.

**Simple**

Move-in readiness is a checklist, not a feeling. Every alarm, handrail, guard, exit, and safety valve gets confirmed working before the family moves in. The finish record goes in the permanent house file. It contains paint formulas, tile and floor products, model numbers, and photographs of what is inside the walls. Years later, that record is what turns a repair into a small job.

**Applied**

- Design lighting in three layers (ambient, task, accent) placed from the furnished room and the work surface, coordinated with the branch-circuit and smart-home chapters.
- Before covering interior work, document wall and ceiling cavities, blocking, shutoffs, valve and cleanout access, electrical boxes, low-voltage pathways, radiant tubing, and waterproofing layers with photographs.
- At move-in, confirm the required safety items are present and operating: smoke and CO alarms interconnected, guards and handrails installed, egress doors and rescue openings operable, garage door self-latching and self-closing, anti-scald valves field-set and logged, ventilation balanced, blower-door test submitted, and required GFCI, AFCI, and lighting outlets working.
- Keep the finish record (paint formulas, flooring and tile products with DCOF data, cabinet and hardware models, composite-wood labels, slab moisture-test results, spare materials) and the manufacturer manuals in the permanent house file.

**Professional**

Interior final approval spans building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, energy, alarms, egress, stairs, guards, glazing, and documentation. The move-in checklist confirms both the visible quality and the hidden-system proof: doors swing and latch, cabinets and counters are secure, tile has slope and movement joints, floors are moisture-compatible, and service points are accessible. Concrete construction does not allow later access to embedded routing, so the pre-cover photographs and the complete finish record are what keep a future repair from starting with demolition. Best-practice records beyond the code minimum: the tile DCOF data, the composite-wood labels, the slab moisture-test results, and the anti-scald valve set points.

**Code**
