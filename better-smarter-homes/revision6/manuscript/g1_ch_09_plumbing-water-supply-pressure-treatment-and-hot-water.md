# Chapter 9: Plumbing: Water Supply, Pressure, Treatment, And Hot Water

This chapter contains the potable water system: the source, the service and distribution sizing, pressure control, water testing and treatment, hot-water generation and delivery, backflow protection, and freeze protection. Its scope is the water supply chassis from the source through the last fixture supply stop.

The drain, waste, and vent chapter contains waste piping, drain slope, venting, traps, cleanouts, sewer and septic connections, and basement sewage. The two systems can share a wet wall or service chase, but they are designed separately: the potable side is pressure work, and the drainage side is gravity and vent work.

The Oregon code of record for plumbing at the time of writing is the 2023 Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code (OPSC), effective February 9, 2024, based on the 2021 Uniform Plumbing Code. Residential energy provisions that affect hot-water piping and equipment come from the 2023 Oregon Residential Specialty Code (ORSC) Chapter 11. Section values in this chapter are given for those editions; the edition in force is confirmed at permit time, because a 2026 OPSC adoption and a 2026 ORSC heat-pump provision were both in progress when this was written.

## 9.1 The Supply Side Boundary

The potable system delivers pressurized water that is safe to drink, at a controlled pressure, at a usable flow, at the right temperature, without any path for contamination to travel backward into it.

**Simple**

Plumbing has two halves. Clean water comes in under pressure through small pipes. Used water leaves by gravity through larger pipes. This chapter is the clean-water half: the source, the pressure, the treatment, the hot water, and the protection that keeps the drinking water clean. The used-water half is a separate chapter.

**Applied**

In scope here: the water source, the service entry and main shutoff, pressure control, water testing and treatment, the cold and hot distribution layout, water heaters, hot-water recirculation, backflow protection, freeze protection, and the outdoor and utility water supply up to the branch point. In the drain, waste, and vent chapter: drains, vents, traps, cleanouts, sewer, septic, and basement sewage.

**Professional**

The potable system is governed by pressure, flow, developed length, elevation, backflow hazard, thermal expansion, disinfection, scald protection, and material compatibility. It is designed as its own system even where it shares a chase with drainage or mechanical runs. In a concrete house the supply system is difficult to alter after placement, so the design keeps every serviceable component (valves, unions, strainers, regulators, expansion tanks, manifolds, mixing valves, pumps, tanks, filters, and backflow devices) in an accessible service layer rather than buried in structure.

**Code**

```
Potable water work follows the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code.
  2023 OPSC, based on the 2021 UPC, effective February 9, 2024; a 2026 OPSC (anticipated effective October 1, 2026) was in adoption when this was written, so the edition in force is confirmed at permit application.
  Source: OAR 918-750-0110; 2023 OPSC.

Water piping and distribution are sized under the plumbing code's sizing provisions.
  Traditional sizing per Chapter 6 with Appendix A (Table 610.4), or the peak-demand method of Appendix M.
  Source: 2023 OPSC Chapter 6; Appendix A; Appendix M.
```

## 9.2 Wet-Zone And Service-Layer Planning

Pipe length is set by where the fixtures land relative to the source and the water heater. Layout is decided before any sleeve, blockout, or slab penetration is formed, because a concrete structure cannot be recut for a missed route without breaking structural work.

**Simple**

Bathrooms, kitchen, laundry, and the water heater belong close together when the floor plan allows. Short pipe runs cost less to build, deliver hot water faster, and hold fewer hidden joints. A spread-out plan can still work, but it adds pipe, adds hot-water wait time, and adds hidden joints.

**Applied**

Settle these before forming any penetration and record them in the project binder:

- Group bathrooms, kitchen, and laundry into a wet zone where the plan allows.
- Locate the water heater near the largest hot-water loads.
- Locate the main shutoff where it can be reached without tools or a ladder.
- Route horizontal supply in an accessible service layer, not cast in structure.
- Mark every slab and wall penetration on the plan before the pour.
- Label and locate every valve, manifold, regulator, and device for reach and service.

If a fixture group has to move away from the wet zone, treat it as a deliberate tradeoff and plan the added pipe length, wait time, insulation, and penetrations for it.

**Professional**

Wet-zone concentration reduces developed length, pressure loss, standby heat loss, hot-water wait time, penetration count, and hidden joints. It also concentrates the serviceable components (source, treatment, pumps, tanks, recirculation, and manifolds) into one controlled service path. In a concrete structure, the supply layout is coordinated with the structural, waterproofing, radon, and mechanical trades before sleeves and blockouts are placed. The penetration schedule is complete before concrete placement.

**Code**

```
Pipe passing through structure must be protected.
  A sleeve or approved protective wrap protects piping through concrete, masonry, or foundation elements from damage and corrosion.
  Source: OPSC Chapter 3 (2023 edition).

Rough-in must match the approved plans and pass inspection before cover.
  The rough plumbing inspection is approved before any piping is concealed.
  Source: OPSC 312; ORSC R109.

Freeze-exposed piping must be protected.
  Water piping in exterior or unconditioned locations requires freeze protection.
  Source: OPSC 312.6.
```

## 9.3 Water Source: Public Utility Or Private Well

The source sets the permit path, the treatment plan, the pressure strategy, and the resilience plan. A public utility usually delivers treated water at a known pressure. A private well delivers raw water that belongs to the site and has to be tested, treated, pressurized, and protected from power loss.

**Simple**

The house gets water from a public utility, a private well, stored water, or a combination. City water usually arrives treated and pressurized. Well water is raw. Its quality belongs to the site, and it is tested before any treatment or fixture is chosen.

**Applied**

Before designing the system, document:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Public utility or private well? | Sets the permit path, treatment plan, pressure source, and backup plan. |
| Sustained flow the source can deliver? | Sets pump, tank, and pipe sizing. A well below 5 GPM sustained means storage and a booster. |
| Static and flowing pressure at the building? | Determines whether a regulator, a booster, or both are needed. |
| What does the water test show? | Determines the treatment train. Nothing downstream is chosen until this is known. |
| Is there a nonpotable source? | Requires full separation from the drinking water. |
| What happens on power loss? | A well pump and any booster pump stop without power. |

Measure or obtain source pressure and flow. Do not assume the source can feed the fixture schedule directly.

**Professional**

Public-water design starts from the service size, meter capacity, static and residual pressure, utility backflow conditions, and water supply fixture unit (WSFU) load. Private-well design starts from the well log, sustained yield, static water level, drawdown, pump curve, pressure-tank drawdown, treatment, disinfection contact time, and power resilience. A storage-and-booster arrangement decouples a low or intermittent source from the peak demand of the house: the source fills an atmospheric tank slowly, and a downstream booster (often a variable-speed constant-pressure pump) feeds the house from storage. That arrangement improves pressure stability and resilience but adds water-quality, disinfection, overflow, backflow, and maintenance obligations covered later in this chapter.

**Code**

```
Wells are constructed to state standards and documented by a well log.
  Casing, cap, and contamination-source separation per the construction rules; the well log records the driller's sustained-yield pumping test and is retrieved from the OWRD well-log query system.
  Source: OAR 690-200; OWRD well-log database, oregon.gov/owrd.

Domestic well testing and cross-connection control sit with the health authority.
  Testing guidance and cross-connection oversight for domestic wells.
  Source: OAR 333-061; OHA Drinking Water Services.

A private supply may not be connected to another source without approval.
  Connection of a private water supply to any other source of supply requires approval of the Building Official, the Oregon Health Authority, or the department having jurisdiction.
  Source: OPSC 602.4.
```

## 9.4 Water-Service And Distribution Sizing

Sizing sets the meter and the building supply pipe from the fixture load, the developed length, the elevation, and the available pressure. Undersized pipe starves fixtures under simultaneous use. Oversized pipe raises cost, holds more standing water, and lengthens hot-water wait.

**Applied**

Size the service and distribution from the fixture schedule, not from memory or a generic chart:

1. Tabulate the water supply fixture units for every fixture.
2. Determine the developed length from the source to the highest, farthest outlet.
3. Subtract 0.5 psi for every foot of elevation gain from the source to that outlet.
4. Where a pressure regulator is used, size against 80 percent of the reduced pressure.
5. Look up the minimum meter and building supply pipe size, or run the Appendix M peak-demand method.
6. Check the worst-case simultaneous draw (two showers plus dishwasher plus clothes washer) against the minimum flowing pressure each fixture needs, typically 8 to 20 psi depending on the fixture.

**Professional**

Two sizing methods run concurrently under the 2023 OPSC. The traditional method tabulates WSFU per OPSC Table 610.3, applies the developed length and the 0.5 psi-per-foot elevation loss, and reads Table 610.4 for the meter and supply size. Appendix M, new in the 2023 OPSC, is a peak-demand method that assumes water-efficient fixtures and accounts for the probability of concurrent use; it generally yields a smaller meter for the same fixture count. Portland Water Bureau adopted Appendix M for residential meter sizing in 2023, and other jurisdictions are following. The minimum building supply is 3/4 inch regardless of method. The regulator margin is built into the arithmetic: a system regulated to 60 psi is sized against 48 psi, and that 20 percent margin decides pipe sizes on long developed lengths.

For a well-plus-pressurization system the sizing is layered: sustained well yield sets the pump GPM ceiling, the pump curve and total dynamic head set the pressure delivered to the tank, the pressure-tank drawdown sets the pump cycle, an atmospheric storage tank with a downstream booster is added where yield is below peak demand, and the house service is then sized on the post-booster line using Appendix M or Table 610.4.

**Code**

```
Fixture loads are tabulated in water supply fixture units.
  WSFU values by fixture type.
  Source: OPSC Table 610.3.

The traditional method reads meter and pipe size from a table.
  Sizing from WSFU, developed length, and available pressure; minimum building supply 3/4 inch.
  Source: OPSC Table 610.4 (Appendix A).

A peak-demand method is the concurrent alternative.
  Appendix M assumes water-efficient fixtures and concurrent-use probability and generally yields a smaller meter for the same fixture count; Portland Water Bureau adopted it for residential meter sizing in 2023.
  Source: OPSC Appendix M; Portland Water Bureau residential meter policy.

Regulated systems are sized on reduced pressure.
  Where a pressure regulator is installed, distribution under Table 610.4 is sized on 80 percent of the reduced downstream pressure.
  Source: OPSC 608.2.
```

## 9.5 Private Well System: Yield, Pump, Pressure Tank, And Controls

Three controls separate a code-minimum well system from a durable one: a correctly sized pressure tank, a low-water cutoff that stops the pump before it runs dry, and, where the output is chlorinated, a contact tank sized so the disinfection has time to work.

**Simple**

A well pump pushes water up into a pressure tank, a steel tank holding air and water. The compressed air pushes water out to the fixtures until the pressure drops, and the pump then refills the tank. The stored volume keeps the pump from starting at every faucet opening, and frequent starting is what wears out a pump motor. The most common cause of pump failure is running dry, when the well is drawn down faster than it refills.

**Applied**

For a private well, build in this order and record each value:

- Obtain the sustained yield from the well log. Below 5 GPM sustained is a storage-and-booster build; 5 to 10 GPM is demand-dependent; 10 GPM and above can direct-pressurize a typical house.
- Match pump GPM at or below the sustained yield so the well is not drawn dry.
- Size the pressure tank for roughly 1 gallon of drawdown per pump GPM, with at least a 1-minute run cycle. For pumps between 10 and 20 GPM, use 1.5 gallons of drawdown per GPM; a pump of 2 HP or larger needs at least a 2-minute run cycle. Oversize deliberately; a larger tank extends pump life and costs little more.
- Set the pressure-tank pre-charge 2 psi below the pump cut-in pressure.
- Install a low-water cutoff (a pressure-based well-saver, a flow switch, or a low-water electrode). Dry-running is the most common cause of pump burnout.
- Where output is chlorinated, install a contact tank sized for at least 10 minutes of contact at peak flow: contact volume in gallons equals peak GPM times 10, so a 15 GPM peak needs a 150-gallon contact tank minimum.

**Professional**

A submersible turbine pump is standard for a drilled well. Total dynamic head is the static water level plus maximum drawdown plus elevation lift to the pressure tank plus friction losses plus the target system pressure (psi times 2.31 for feet of head). A stainless-steel-body pump outlasts a cast-iron body in Oregon's mineralized groundwater. Pressure-tank drawdown is a fraction of total tank volume, not the full volume: at a 30/50 psi switch the drawdown is about 29 percent of volume; at 40/60 psi it is about 22 percent. A 10 GPM pump on a 40/60 switch needs roughly 10 gallons of drawdown, which is about a 45-gallon nominal tank. A variable-speed constant-pressure pump smooths delivery and is easier on a generator than a fixed-speed pump.

Standby power is not required by code for a single-family well, but a well pump stops without power. A 1 HP pump can draw 30 amps or more of starting inrush, which sizes any generator or inverter. A gravity feed from an elevated atmospheric storage tank is the only fully off-grid path and supports a design goal of continued function during a grid outage.

**Code**

```
The well log records the yield the design starts from.
  The driller's sustained-yield pumping test is the empirical basis for pump and storage sizing.
  Source: OWRD well log for the site, oregon.gov/owrd.

Pressure and expansion control apply to well systems the same as public supplies.
  Expansion and system pressure control on the pressurized side of a well system follow the same sections as a public supply; no Oregon code section requires a low-water cutoff or standby power for a single-family well, so both are project specifications above the minimum.
  Source: OPSC 608.2; OPSC 608.3.
```

## 9.6 Indoor Potable Storage And Booster

An atmospheric storage tank between the source and the house decouples a limited source from peak demand and adds an off-grid water reserve. It is allowed indoors in Oregon, but it triggers a layered approval and a set of construction details that keep the stored water potable.

**Simple**

A storage tank holds water. A booster pump moves it to the fixtures at a steady pressure. A pressure tank smooths the pump cycling. Stored water lets a low or intermittent source deliver steady pressure at the tap, and it adds a reserve for a power outage. It also has to be built so the stored water stays clean and so a stuck float cannot flood the room.

**Applied**

Before committing to indoor storage, confirm the design carries all of these:

- An air gap on the fill line from the source into the tank. The air gap is the backflow protection.
- A tank, liner, fittings, and gaskets certified to NSF/ANSI/CAN 61.
- A watertight, vermin-proof, secured cover with downturned, screened vents.
- An access opening sized for cleaning and inspection: 22 by 30 inches minimum, or 24 inches in diameter, gasketed and secured.
- An overflow piped to an indirect waste with an air gap, never a direct drain connection.
- A drain valve at the low point.
- Liquid-level controls and a high-level alarm.
- A floor pan or spill containment with a piped indirect drain.
- Seismic restraint per the building code.
- A written disinfection protocol run at commissioning and after any opening, repair, or media change.

**Professional**

An indoor potable storage tank is fed through an air gap, then boosted downstream. The air gap satisfies cross-connection control; a submerged or direct connection would not. Materials are specified to system-level NSF 61 certification confirmed by the manufacturer's NSF listing, not tank-only certification. Disinfection follows the OHA and OWRD well-owner protocol: super-chlorinate to 50 to 250 mg/L free chlorine, hold at least 24 hours, drain to a non-sanitary outlet, refill, and retest until the residual is below 4 mg/L and total coliform is absent. Where stored water is held beyond about two weeks, a small chemical-feed pump maintains a free-chlorine residual. A tank substantially above 1,000 gallons, or in an unconventional location, should be expected to require a stamped design from a registered design professional and sign-off from the local environmental health office.

**Code**

```
Storage between a private supply and the house requires layered approval.
  Connection of a private supply to storage or any other source requires Building Official approval with Oregon Health Authority involvement; cross-connection control between the potable system and storage follows the health rules.
  Source: OPSC 602.4; OAR 333-061.

Materials in contact with stored potable water are certified.
  Tank, liner, fittings, gaskets, and internal coatings carry system-level potable-contact certification.
  Source: NSF/ANSI/CAN 61.

The tank access opening has a minimum size.
  A readily accessible cleanout or manhole, 22 by 30 inches minimum or 24 inches diameter.
  Source: OPSC 508.2.

Overflow and pan drains discharge indirectly.
  Tank overflow and floor-pan drains run to an indirect waste through an air gap, never a direct drainage connection.
  Source: OPSC 811.0.

Tanks and equipment are seismically restrained.
  Support and restraint per the building code.
  Source: OPSC 313.1.
```

## 9.7 Water Testing

Clear water is not proof of safe water. A well can look clean and still carry bacteria, nitrate, arsenic, iron, manganese, hardness, sulfide, or tannins. The test result, not appearance, sets the treatment train, and nothing downstream is selected until the raw water is characterized.

**Simple**

Well water can look, smell, and taste fine and still carry things that hurt people slowly: bacteria, nitrate, arsenic. A laboratory test is the only way to know what is in the water. The treatment equipment is chosen from the test result, so the test comes first. Bacteria tests are repeated during the first year. A new well can test clean and then fail after the first heavy rain washes the disturbed ground.

**Applied**

Test in tiers and re-sample bacteria through the first year:

- Tier 1, always: total coliform and E. coli; nitrate and nitrite; arsenic; pH, alkalinity, hardness, and total dissolved solids; total and dissolved iron; manganese; sulfate and hydrogen sulfide.
- Tier 2, by geology: fluoride; lead and copper; radon-in-water in granitic terrain; uranium and gross alpha; chloride near the coast; tannins and total organic carbon in forested or peaty ground.
- Tier 3, by local risk: volatile organic compounds near fuel tanks, industry, or landfills; a pesticide and herbicide panel near cropland, vineyards, or orchards; a PFAS panel near bases, airports, fire-training areas, or biosolids ground; glyphosate where the standard pesticide panel does not cover it.
- Re-sample coliform after the first heavy rain following construction, after the septic install, and again about three months in.
- Baseline the Langelier Saturation Index and Ryznar Stability Index to predict whether the water is scaling or corrosive.

Use an accredited laboratory. Oregon labs are not currently accredited for PFAS, so a PFAS sample ships out of state.

**Professional**

The Oregon real-estate-transaction minimum is arsenic, nitrate, and total coliform. That is a sample of record, not a design basis. Arsenic is a statewide concern in Oregon's volcanic and geothermal geology. The corrosion indices matter for a long-service house because they drive the pipe-material choice, the anode-rod chemistry in the water heater, and the treatment pH target. Iron speciation (ferrous versus ferric versus organically bound) and the manganese load determine which oxidation and filtration media will work, so the panel distinguishes total from dissolved iron.

**Code**

```
A well sale triggers a minimum test panel.
  Arsenic, nitrate, and total coliform, analyzed by an accredited laboratory, at every real estate transaction; a floor, not a design panel.
  Source: ORS 448.271; OAR 333-061-0307.

Laboratory accreditation and sampling follow the health rules.
  Accredited-laboratory and sampling framework for domestic well water; Oregon laboratories are not accredited for PFAS, so those samples ship out of state.
  Source: OAR 333-061; OHA Drinking Water Services.
```

## 9.8 Water Treatment Train

Treatment is sequential, and the order is not interchangeable. An oxidizer ahead of a softener destroys the resin; iron ahead of a softener fouls the bed; sediment ahead of a UV lamp scatters the light; carbon ahead of reverse osmosis protects the membrane. The train is built for the test result, and each stage is placed so it does not damage the next.

**Simple**

Treatment equipment is a row of tanks and filters, and the order of the row is part of the design. Each stage removes something the next stage cannot tolerate: grit before the ultraviolet lamp, iron before the softener, chlorine before the plastic pipe. The equipment list comes from the water test, not from a catalog. Treatment for a difficult well runs $1,500-$8,000 or more (2026), which is why the land chapter prices the water question before the purchase.

**Applied**

Order the train to the raw-water test:

1. Pre-sediment filtration, 5 to 20 micron, to protect everything downstream.
2. pH neutralization if pH is below about 6.7, using a calcite or calcite-plus-Corosex media tank.
3. Oxidation with a metered chlorine feed or hydrogen peroxide into a contact tank sized for at least 10 minutes at peak flow. Aeration is an alternative for moderate iron with no manganese.
4. Iron and manganese filtration (manganese greensand regenerated with potassium permanganate, Birm, Pyrolox, or air-injection media), chosen for the iron form and manganese load.
5. Tannin removal by dedicated anion exchange where organic color or total organic carbon is high.
6. Water softening by sodium-form ion exchange, placed only after iron, oxidation, and tannins are handled.
7. Activated carbon to remove residual chlorine and chloramine, protect downstream PEX, and polish taste and odor.
8. Point-of-use reverse osmosis for arsenic, nitrate, fluoride, or uranium; whole-house RO is rarely justified.
9. UV disinfection as the last stage, where the lamp sees clear water, sized for at least 40 mJ/cm² at end of lamp life.

Retest after the train is commissioned, and label every bypass, direction of flow, and service interval.

**Professional**

Specialty cases override the general order. Arsenic in low-iron water is handled by titanium-oxide or iron-oxide adsorptive media and a point-of-use RO rather than a full train. High nitrate is handled by nitrate-selective anion exchange or by point-of-use RO, which is operationally simpler. A bacteriologically positive well is not a permanent treatment problem to design around until the source has been shock-chlorinated and the cap, casing, and separation distances have been checked; continuous disinfection is the response to a source that stays positive after the well itself is corrected. Reverse osmosis discharges reject water and a softener discharges brine, so both need an indirect waste path coordinated with the drainage design.

**Code**

```
A coliform-positive well is corrected at the source first.
  The well cap, casing, and contamination-source separation are checked and the well is shock-chlorinated before continuous disinfection is designed.
  Source: OAR 690-200.

Treatment discharges are isolated from the potable system.
  Brine and treatment discharge take air-gap or reduced-pressure protection at the potable interface; softener brine and RO reject run to an indirect waste.
  Source: OPSC 603.5; OPSC 811.0.

Treatment devices carry performance certifications.
  Filtration, softening, UV, RO, and distillation devices are listed to the matching standard and installed per the manufacturer's instructions.
  Source: NSF/ANSI 42, 44, 53, 55, 58, and 62.
```

## 9.9 Pressure Control

Oregon caps static residential supply pressure. Above the cap, a regulator and an expansion provision are required, and both have to be reachable for service. Excess pressure stresses fixtures, valves, and the water heater. Heated water expands, and where a check valve or backflow device closes the system, the expansion raises the pressure until a relief provision absorbs it.

**Simple**

Pressure is the push behind the water. Flow is how much water moves. A house can have high pressure and still deliver poor flow if a pipe is undersized or a filter is clogged. The system controls both: it holds the static pressure under the limit, and it gives heated water somewhere to expand.

**Applied**

Measure and set pressure with fixtures installed, not from the paper design:

- Static pressure with no fixtures running.
- Flowing pressure with fixtures running.
- Pressure drop across every filter and treatment device.
- Pressure at the farthest and highest fixture.
- Pressure before and after the regulator.
- Pump cut-in and cut-out, or the variable-speed setpoint.

If static pressure exceeds 80 psi, install an approved regulator with a strainer and reduce it to 80 psi or less. Install the expansion provision on the building side of any check valve or backflow device.

**Professional**

Under OPSC §608.2, where static supply pressure exceeds 80 psi, an approved pressure regulator complying with ASSE 1003, preceded by a strainer, reduces the static pressure to 80 psi or less. Regulators of 1-1/2 inch and larger are exempt from the strainer requirement under an Oregon amendment. An expansion tank is required downstream of the regulator on the cold-water distribution, and wherever a check valve, backflow preventer, or other normally closed device would trap thermal expansion, independent of water-heater type. The expansion tank complies with NSF 61 for potable contact and IAPMO Z1088 for prepressurized models, and Oregon prohibits a shutoff valve between the expansion tank and the system. Instantaneous on-demand water heaters do not require an expansion tank. A registered design professional may substitute an approved pressure relief valve set at 100 psi or less in lieu of an expansion tank. The regulator and strainer are installed aboveground or in a drained vault, protected from freezing, with the strainer serviceable without removing the regulator body. Where the regulator sits in a vault, an Oregon amendment accepts any adequate means of vault drainage in place of the model code's drain to daylight, which matters on flat sites where a daylight drain is geometrically impossible. Field delivery targets for reference at commissioning: 45 to 60 psi at showers, 60 to 80 psi at tub fills, and the appliance-specified range (typically 20 to 80 psi) at washers and dishwashers.

A high-pressure trunk with per-fixture regulators is possible only as an alternative engineered design under OPSC 301.5, which requires signed and sealed drawings from a registered design professional and Building Official approval. It is an optional engineered upgrade, not a default, and it multiplies the closed-end stub conditions that the dead-leg rule governs. Its component costs price the concept before the engineering fee: per-fixture brass regulators run $25-$40 each (2026), and a 2-inch copper trunk runs $15-$25 per foot (2026).

**Code**

```
Static pressure above 80 psi requires regulation.
  An approved ASSE 1003 regulator preceded by a strainer reduces static pressure to 80 psi or less; regulators 1-1/2 inch and larger are strainer-exempt (Oregon amendment); regulator and strainer sit accessibly aboveground or in a vault with adequate drainage (Oregon amendment), protected from freezing, with the strainer serviceable without disconnecting the supply.
  Source: OPSC 608.2 (with Oregon amendments).

Thermal expansion must have a control device.
  An expansion tank is required downstream of a regulator and wherever a check valve, backflow preventer, or other normally closed device blocks dissipation to the main; the tank complies with NSF 61 (IAPMO Z1088 for prepressurized models); Oregon prohibits a valve between the tank and the system; instantaneous on-demand heaters are excepted; a registered design professional may substitute a relief valve set at 100 psi or less.
  Source: OPSC 608.2; OPSC 608.3.

A high-pressure trunk with per-fixture regulators is an engineered alternative only.
  Signed and sealed drawings from a registered design professional and Building Official approval under the alternative-engineered-design section.
  Source: OPSC 301.5; OPSC 608.2.

Dead legs are limited and flushable.
  A dead leg cannot exceed 1.5 times the pipe diameter and requires an accessible flushing method.
  Source: OPSC 309.5.
```

## 9.10 Pipe Materials And Compatibility

The code permits a set of potable materials; the long-service goal narrows the set to those that tolerate the site water chemistry, the temperature, the concrete contact, and field repair. Material selection interacts with chlorine resistance, UV exposure, freeze tolerance, and how the pipe is joined and supported.

**Applied**

For each material in the design, verify:

- Approved for potable use and, where it carries hot water, rated for the temperature and pressure.
- Compatible with the site water chemistry, including chlorine if the water is disinfected.
- Sleeved with a nonmetallic sleeve at every concrete or masonry penetration.
- Separated from dissimilar metals to prevent galvanic corrosion.
- Joined by the method the manufacturer specifies, and supported at the specified spacing.
- Shielded from continuous UV; PEX is not rated for permanent sunlight exposure.
- Repairable from the service layer.

Do not cast a material in concrete unless the approved detail permits it, and confirm the printed material code on PEX shows the chlorine and UV class the design requires.

**Professional**

OPSC Table 604.1 permits copper (Types K, L, M), CPVC, PEX (A, B, and C), PE-RT, multilayer PEX-AL-PEX, stainless steel, and HDPE for service piping. For the buried service line, HDPE PE4710 (SDR 9 or 11) is chlorine-resistant, freeze-tolerant, and fusion-jointed with no in-ground mechanical fittings; a 14 AWG tracer wire is required with nonmetallic service pipe. Inside the house, PEX-A joined with expansion fittings is the most flexible and the most field-repairable and suits freeze-zone runs and homerun manifolds; PEX-B carries the highest chlorine resistance and is the better choice where the treated water carries a continuous chlorine residual. The printed four-digit material code should read 5306 (Class 5 chlorine, Class 3 UV) on a chlorinated system; the 1306 and 3306 grades are avoided there. PEX-C (electron-beam crosslinked) carries the smallest stabilizer reserve of the three PEX types and is not specified for this project. CPVC is code-allowed but becomes brittle with age and is intolerant of some caulks, foams, and wire jackets, so it is not specified for a long-service house. For the buried service line, soft copper Type K is the traditional alternative to HDPE; it is avoided where soils run acidic, a condition common in the Cascade foothills.

Under OPSC §604.13, PEX, PEX-AL-PEX, PE-AL-PE, and PE-RT cannot be installed within the first 18 inches of piping connected to a storage water heater; a copper or stainless flexible connector makes that first connection. The exception is an instantaneous on-demand tankless heater, where an approved plastic material may be used within the first 18 inches per the manufacturer's instructions. PEX cast in a slab is permitted and standard for radiant work; the higher-burst PEX-B is used where rebar drag or a strike edge could damage the pipe during placement.

**Code**

```
Potable pipe materials come from an approved list.
  Copper Types K, L, and M; CPVC; PEX A, B, and C; PE-RT; PEX-AL-PEX; stainless steel; HDPE for service piping.
  Source: OPSC Table 604.1.

Plastic tubing keeps a setback from storage water heaters.
  No PEX, PEX-AL-PEX, PE-AL-PE, or PE-RT within the first 18 inches of piping at a storage water heater; a copper or stainless connector makes the first connection; approved plastics are permitted at an instantaneous tankless heater per its instructions.
  Source: OPSC 604.13.

Buried nonmetallic service pipe is traceable.
  HDPE PE4710 service pipe per the material standards, with 14 AWG tracer wire on nonmetallic service piping.
  Source: AWWA C901; ASTM F714; 2023 OPSC.

The printed PEX code states the chlorine and UV class.
  The 5306 designation is Class 5 chlorine and Class 3 UV; Class 3 UV is a 6-month construction allowance, not a rating for permanent exposure.
  Source: ASTM F876; ASTM F877.

Penetrations and dissimilar metals are protected.
  Nonmetallic sleeves at concrete and masonry penetrations; dielectric separation at dissimilar-metal joints.
  Source: OPSC Chapter 3.
```

## 9.11 Water Hammer, Noise, And Movement

Water hammer is the pressure shock that hits when moving water is stopped suddenly by a quick-closing valve. It bangs pipes, stresses valves, and can loosen joints over time. Oregon exempts one- and two-family dwellings from the code arrestor requirement; this project controls the shock at the valves that cause it.

**Applied**

Control hammer and noise before the walls close:

- Size pipe so velocity stays reasonable, which reduces both hammer and flow noise.
- Support pipe at the specified spacing and isolate it from framing and masonry so vibration is not transmitted into the structure.
- Install ASSE 1010 mini-arrestors at the clothes-washer supply box, the dishwasher, the refrigerator ice-maker line, and any solenoid-actuated valve such as an irrigation interface or a treatment-regeneration valve.
- Isolate booster and recirculation pumps with vibration mounts and keep them serviceable.
- Listen during commissioning with fixtures and appliances cycling.

Do not use a capped air-chamber stub as a substitute for an arrestor; it waterlogs and stops working.

**Professional**

OPSC §609.11 requires water hammer arrestors complying with ASSE 1010 or PDI-WH 201 at quick-acting valves, but an Oregon-retained exception removes one- and two-family dwellings and townhouses from that requirement. Fast-closing solenoid valves on washers, dishwashers, ice makers, and irrigation are the common shock sources, so mini-arrestors at those points are durability-preserving even though they are not required. In a concrete house, rigidly coupling a pipe to the structure transmits pump and flow vibration into the slab and walls, so isolation, sleeves, and service-layer routing keep movement and noise under control.

**Code**

```
Arrestors are required at quick-acting valves, with a dwelling exception.
  ASSE 1010 or PDI-WH 201 arrestors at quick-closing valves; an Oregon-retained exception removes one- and two-family dwellings and townhouses from the requirement; this project installs them at appliance valves as a durability specification, and capped air-chamber stubs are not an approved substitute.
  Source: OPSC 609.11 (with Oregon exception).
```

## 9.12 Hot Water Generation

Hot water is both a comfort system and a safety system. The generator is sized to the peak-hour demand, chosen for the site's energy source and the space it sits in, and paired with the temperature controls in the next section so it can be stored hot enough to suppress bacteria and delivered cool enough to prevent scald.

**Simple**

Hot water has to be hot enough at the tank to keep bacteria from growing. It also has to be cool enough at the tap that it cannot burn someone. The generator heats and stores the water; separate valves set the temperature that reaches each fixture. The generator is chosen after the fixture count and the energy plan are settled, not before.

**Applied**

Define the load before selecting equipment:

- Number of occupants and fixture count.
- Simultaneous shower demand and tub-fill demand.
- Laundry and dishwasher timing.
- Recirculation strategy.
- Energy source and available electrical service.
- The space the heater sits in and its air volume.
- The drain pan and safe drain path.
- Service and replacement clearance.

Size storage to about 1.5 times the peak-hour demand. A two-shower morning peak in a four-person household is roughly 30 to 40 gallons, which a 65 to 80 gallon unit covers with margin.

**Professional**

A heat pump water heater (HPWH) is the default for this project. It aligns with the Energy Trust incentive structure and with the heat-pump direction the Building Codes Division approved in February 2026 for the 2026 ORSC. A HPWH cools the room it sits in, so it is either sited in unconditioned space with adequate air volume (roughly 1,000 cubic feet or more), ducted to outdoor air, or specified as a split system with the compressor outdoors and the tank indoors. The split system avoids both the cold-room effect and the small-room sizing penalty. A NEEA Northern Climate Tier 2 or higher unit at 65 to 80 gallons suits a typical single-family load; a point-of-use electric-resistance buffer tank is added only where a long branch forces it. Tankless gas is not aligned with Oregon's electrification path and shows a cold-water sandwich (a slug of cold water delivered between successive draws while the heat exchanger recovers) and a minimum-flow floor at low-flow fixtures, so it is not specified here.

**Code**

```
Heat pump water heaters are tiered by climate specification.
  NEEA Northern Climate tiers: Tier 1 (energy factor 1.8 or higher) configured for unconditioned spaces; Tier 2 and higher (2.0 or higher) for conditioned or semi-conditioned spaces.
  Source: OAR 330-070-0073.

A heat-pump direction is entering the residential code.
  The BCD Residential and Manufactured Structures Board approved heat-pump provisions for new construction in February 2026 for the 2026 ORSC; the adoption and effective date are confirmed before any design relies on them.
  Source: BCD 2026 ORSC adoption record, oregon.gov/bcd.

Water heater installation has a fixed checklist.
  Temperature-and-pressure relief valve with discharge piping, drain pan with a safe-waste path, seismic support, and service access, per the section and the unit's listing.
  Source: OPSC 504; the manufacturer's listing.
```

## 9.13 Storage And Delivery Temperature: Legionella And Scald

Storage temperature and delivery temperature are two different numbers serving two purposes. Storage has to be hot enough to suppress Legionella. Delivery has to be cool enough to prevent scald. A master mixing valve at the heater and point-of-use valves at the fixtures resolve the two requirements at once.

**Simple**

Two temperatures matter, and they are different numbers. Water stored warm but not hot grows Legionella, the bacteria behind Legionnaires' disease, so the tank stays at 140 °F. Water at 140 °F scalds skin in seconds, so mixing valves cool it on the way to each faucet and shower. The storage number and the delivery number are set at different devices. The dial on the water heater is not the safety control.

**Applied**

Set the temperatures at defined points and verify them at commissioning:

- Store at 140°F in a tank heater.
- Mix down to about 124°F for distribution with a master mixing valve at the heater outlet, which stays above the 122°F Legionella growth ceiling.
- Limit shower and tub-shower delivery to 120°F or less with the point-of-use valve, and field-set the limit stop to deliver about 110°F.
- Limit lavatory, bathtub, and bidet delivery with the appropriate point-of-use valve.
- Do not rely on the water-heater thermostat as the delivery-temperature control.

For a HPWH stored at 130 to 135°F, supplement with point-of-use mixing where a fixture needs a lower delivery temperature.

**Professional**

Legionella growth is suppressed above about 122°F and killed rapidly above about 140°F, so the standard of care stores tank water at 140°F. Delivery is controlled at the fixture: OPSC §408.3 and the parallel sections limit showers and tub-shower combinations to 120°F through an ASSE 1016 device, and limit bidets, bathtubs, and whirlpool tubs through ASSE 1070 or equivalent. The water-heater thermostat is not an accepted delivery-temperature control. The full architecture stores at 140°F, mixes to about 124°F at the heater with an ASSE 1017 master mixing valve, and delivers 110°F at each shower through a field-set ASSE 1016 limit stop and 110 to 115°F at lavatories and tubs through ASSE 1070 devices. The ASSE 1016 tolerance is about ±3.6°F at 45 psi, so a 110°F limit stop delivers 105 to 115°F, all of it below the scald line. A water heater conforming to ASSE 1084 is an accepted alternative limiting means under the same fixture sections. That architecture keeps distribution above the Legionella ceiling while eliminating scald at every fixture.

**Code**

```
Showers and tub-showers are limited to 120°F at delivery.
  An ASSE 1016 (P, T, or P/T) device with a field-set limit stop controls each shower and tub-shower combination; the water-heater thermostat is not an accepted delivery control.
  Source: OPSC 408.3.

Bidets, bathtubs, and whirlpool tubs take their own limiting devices.
  ASSE 1070 or CSA B125.3 devices; bidets limited to 110°F, bathtubs and whirlpool tubs to 120°F.
  Source: OPSC 409.4; OPSC 410.3.

A listed water heater is an accepted alternative limiter.
  A water heater conforming to ASSE 1084 may serve as the temperature-limiting means for the same fixture sections.
  Source: OPSC 407.3 family; BCD 2021 OPSC change summary.

Distribution mixing has its own device standard.
  A master thermostatic mixing valve at the water-heater outlet controls distribution temperature.
  Source: ASSE 1017.
```

## 9.14 Hot-Water Delivery And Recirculation

Hot water standing in a pipe cools, so opening a far fixture first drains the cooled water while the user waits. Short pipe runs shorten that wait directly; a recirculation loop removes it by keeping hot water near the fixtures. Demand-controlled recirculation is the default here because it cuts the wait without the energy loss and the bacterial exposure of a continuously hot loop.

**Applied**

Choose a delivery strategy and insulate the hot piping:

| Strategy | Best use |
|---|---|
| Short runs | Small wet zone with fixtures near the heater |
| Demand-control recirculation | Push-button or motion call at end-of-loop fixtures; loop sits cold until called |
| Timed recirculation | Predictable daily schedule; higher loss than demand control |
| Continuous recirculation | Hot water within 2-5 seconds at every fixture; pump energy roughly $30-$60 per year (2026) plus standby heat loss; highest bacterial exposure; avoided here |
| Point-of-use heater | An isolated remote sink or utility load |

Insulate hot-water piping to at least R-3, and insulate all recirculation piping and the first 8 feet on each side of the heater. Do not recirculate through uninsulated pipe.

**Professional**

A demand-controlled loop runs the pump only until the return reaches setpoint, then sits at room temperature until the next call, which minimizes both standby loss and the time the loop spends in the Legionella growth range. It also keeps loop water cold most of the time, which reduces chlorine attack on PEX. Modern demand systems use a crossover valve at the far fixture and the cold line as the return path during the priming pulse, so a dedicated return line is not always required. A check valve is installed between the circulator and the heater. Long stub branches are not run off the loop; they are routed as homerun PEX from a manifold so they do not create dead legs. A quarter-horsepower pump moving 6 to 10 GPM is sufficient for a residential demand loop, sized for a 1-to-2-minute loop fill on call; a continuous loop uses a far smaller 5-to-25-watt circulator at 1 to 2 GPM, because it never has to prime the loop quickly. On systems with more than one loop, balancing valves set the flow split and check valves stop thermosiphon circulation through dormant branches.

**Code**

```
Hot-water piping carries minimum insulation.
  R-3 minimum where piping is not fully within conditioned space, on the first 8 feet into and out of the water heater, and on all recirculating piping; weather-exposed insulation is protected from sunlight and moisture.
  Source: ORSC N1103.5 (2023 ORSC Chapter 11).

Dead legs are limited and flushable.
  1.5 times the pipe diameter maximum, with an accessible flushing method; demand-control loops and homerun stubs satisfy the rule.
  Source: OPSC 309.5.

The commercial energy code states the recirculation control standard.
  Recirculation shuts off automatically when there is no demand, with a check valve between the circulator and the heater; a commercial requirement applied here as project practice.
  Source: OEESC 6.4.3; ASHRAE 90.1.
```

## 9.15 Backflow And Cross-Connection Control

Backflow is contaminated water moving backward into the potable system when supply pressure drops or when a submerged connection lets it siphon. Cross-connection control matches a protection device to the hazard at each connection. The device that suits a hose bib is not the device that suits a chemical-injected irrigation system.

**Simple**

Water normally flows one way, from the supply to the fixture, and a pressure drop can reverse it. A garden hose left in a bucket of soapy water can siphon that water back into the house pipes when the main pressure dips. Backflow devices are one-way protections at every risky connection, sized to the risk. A screw-on part for a hose bib costs $10-$20 (2026); a tested assembly for a chemical connection costs $300-$800 (2026).

**Applied**

Protect each connection to the hazard it presents:

- Hose bibs: a hose connection vacuum breaker at every threaded outlet, often integral to a frost-free model.
- Landscape irrigation with no pump and no chemicals: a pressure vacuum breaker above ground.
- Irrigation with chemical injection or a pump: a reduced-pressure principle assembly.
- Hot tubs, spas, and boiler fill with treatment chemicals: a reduced-pressure principle assembly.
- Softener brine discharge and rainwater makeup from potable: an air gap.
- Yard hydrants: a self-draining sanitary type or an ASSE 1024 dual-check; underground stop-and-waste valves are prohibited.
- Any nonpotable system: an air gap or reduced-pressure assembly at every makeup connection, with nonpotable identification and signage.

Use an air gap wherever the layout allows; it is the most reliable protection and needs no testing.

**Professional**

OPSC §603 and the cross-connection table classify hazards and assign devices. A reduced-pressure principle assembly is required, with no double-check substitute, wherever chemicals or a pump can drive contamination back under pressure, which covers chemical-injected irrigation, spas, and treated boiler fill. Testable assemblies (reduced-pressure, double-check, pressure vacuum breaker, and spill-resistant vacuum breaker) are tested at installation and then annually by a certified backflow assembly tester who reports to the water purveyor. Backflow devices are installed accessibly, protected from freezing, and given a drainage and replacement path; they are not buried behind finishes.

**Code**

```
Every hazard class has an assigned device.
  Hose bibs take an ASSE 1011 hose-connection vacuum breaker ($10-$20, 2026); standard landscape irrigation takes a pressure vacuum breaker; chemical injection, pumps, spas, hot tubs, and treated boiler fill take a reduced-pressure assembly ($300-$800, 2026); a double-check assembly ($100-$300, 2026) serves only chemical-free connections where the code accepts it; softener brine and nonpotable makeup take an air gap.
  Source: OPSC 603.5.1 through 603.5.21.

Yard hydrants have a fixed rule.
  Self-draining sanitary type or ASSE 1024 dual-check; underground stop-and-waste valves are prohibited.
  Source: OPSC 603.5.17.

Testable assemblies are tested at installation and annually.
  Testing at installation, then annual testing of reduced-pressure, double-check, and vacuum-breaker assemblies by a certified backflow assembly tester who reports to the water purveyor.
  Source: OPSC 603.4.2; OAR 333-061-0070.
```

## 9.16 Nonpotable Rainwater And Outdoor Water

Rainwater is useful for irrigation, toilet flushing, and clothes washing when it is collected, filtered, disinfected to the use, and kept fully separate from the drinking water. The outdoor supply that leaves conditioned space is part of the supply chassis and is specified here through the branch point; the specific end-use equipment connections are detailed with the equipment they serve.

**Simple**

Rain collected from the roof can flush toilets, run a clothes washer, or water the yard. It is not drinking water unless it is treated and approved as drinking water. A nonpotable system is kept physically separate from the potable system, labeled, and protected so nothing can flow from it back into the drinking water.

**Applied**

A nonpotable rainwater system carries:

- Gutters, downspouts, leaf screens, and a first-flush or sediment diverter.
- A durable, watertight, labeled storage tank with a screened, secured cover.
- An overflow to a safe discharge, kept away from the foundation, the septic drainfield, and the neighbor's property.
- A separate pump and separate piping, labeled nonpotable throughout.
- Sediment plus UV at minimum for toilet or clothes-washer use.
- An air gap or reduced-pressure assembly at any potable makeup connection.
- Freeze protection for winter.

Do not connect rainwater to the potable system except through an approved air gap or reduced-pressure assembly.

**Professional**

The 2023 OPSC contains nonpotable rainwater catchment in Chapter 16 (mandatory statewide), potable rainwater in Appendix K (an alternate method requiring OHA involvement, rarely used for a single-family home), and broader alternate nonpotable sources in Chapter 15. Permitted nonpotable end uses include water closets and urinals, trap primers, clothes washers, irrigation, and water features. A single-family system whose outlets, piping, and components are entirely exterior needs no registered-design-professional design and no permit; a system with any component, piping, or fitting inside the building requires a certified plumbing contractor or RDP designer and a permit. That permit is plumbing permitting under the OPSC, obtained through the same building department as the rest of the plumbing work; unlike graywater reuse, nonpotable rainwater carries no separate DEQ permit. Rooftop catchment for on-site use does not require an OWRD water right under the ORS 537.141 rooftop exemption, but catchment that diverts or impounds surface flow does. Some cities publish their own rainwater guidance under the OPSC framework, so the local layer is checked before final design.

For the outdoor and utility supply, this chapter specifies the trunk run and isolation valving to the branch point, the reduced-pressure backflow device at any equipment branch, the freeze protection and insulation for any branch leaving conditioned space, and the frost-free hose-bib specification. The specific equipment connections (workshop sinks, wash stations, and similar loads) and any dedicated point-of-use heaters are detailed with those end uses, cross-referenced back to this supply boundary.

**Code**

```
Nonpotable rainwater catchment is regulated statewide.
  Chapter 16 governs nonpotable rainwater; Chapter 15 governs other alternate nonpotable sources; Appendix K (potable rainwater) is an alternate method involving OHA, rare for a single-family home.
  Source: 2023 OPSC Chapters 15 and 16; OPSC Appendix K.

Design and permit requirements switch at the building line.
  A certified plumbing contractor or registered design professional designs the system, with a permit, where any component is inside the building; a single-family system entirely exterior needs neither; aboveground irrigation at 360 gallons or less of storage needs no professional design.
  Source: OPSC 1601.

Nonpotable systems are labeled and valved to standard.
  "CAUTION: NONPOTABLE RAINWATER, DO NOT DRINK" signage; diverter valves per IAPMO PS 59 (2 to 4 inch) or IAPMO IGC 352 (6 to 12 inch) with an upstream filter.
  Source: OPSC 1603.

Rooftop collection needs no water right.
  Rooftop rainwater collected for on-site use is exempt from water-right permitting; diverting or impounding surface flow is not exempt.
  Source: ORS 537.141.

Exterior hose outlets are frost-protected and check-protected.
  Frost-free hose bibs with the required dual-check and self-draining features.
  Source: OPSC 603.5.17; ASSE 1019; ASSE 1052.
```

## 9.17 Freeze Protection And Concrete Penetrations

A frozen pipe splits, and a pipe cast into concrete cannot be reached to repair. Freeze protection is decided by routing and detailing before concrete closes around the penetrations, not corrected afterward.

**Applied**

Verify before cover:

- Supply piping runs inside the conditioned envelope wherever the layout allows.
- Any pipe in an exterior wall or unconditioned space is insulated and the cavity is air-sealed so outdoor air cannot reach it.
- Hose bibs are frost-free where they serve exterior walls.
- Every concrete or masonry penetration is sleeved with a nonmetallic sleeve.
- Dissimilar metals are separated.
- Pipe can move as it expands and contracts, without abrading against the sleeve edge.
- Shutoffs and drains are accessible.
- Any heat tape is listed and its connection is accessible.

Do not route supply pipe through a cold exterior location just to shorten the run.

**Professional**

Freeze protection is the combination of routing, insulation, air sealing, a heat source, and flow; a pipe in a warm room still freezes if outdoor air leaks to it through an unsealed sleeve or rim detail. A pipe through concrete is protected against freezing, corrosion, movement, and abrasion by a nonmetallic sleeve with clearance, and the penetration is coordinated with the structural, waterproofing, air-sealing, vapor-control, and radon details so a single opening satisfies all of them. The below-grade waterproofing and radon interface at these penetrations is detailed in the foundation and envelope chapters; this chapter specifies the pipe protection and the freeze provision.

**Code**

```
Freeze-exposed piping must be protected.
  Water piping in exterior walls, unconditioned spaces, and other freeze-exposed locations requires protection from freezing.
  Source: OPSC 312.6.

Pipe through structure is sleeved.
  Piping through concrete, masonry, and foundations takes a sleeve or approved protection against damage and corrosion, with clearance for movement.
  Source: OPSC Chapter 3.

Exterior hose bibs are self-draining or frost-free.
  Self-draining or frost-free type with integral backflow protection.
  Source: OPSC 603.5.17.
```

## 9.18 Inspection, Testing, Commissioning, And Records

The supply system is proven before it is hidden and again under real use before the house is occupied. A leak found before drywall costs a fitting and a repair visit; the same leak found after finishes adds demolition and refinishing to the same repair. The records let a future owner find every shutoff, device, and serviceable fitting without opening a wall.

**Applied**

Before cover, record and photograph:

- The approved rough-plumbing inspection.
- The pressure-test result.
- Pipe material, size, and printed material code.
- Valve, manifold, and shutoff locations.
- Regulator make and setpoint, and the expansion-tank location.
- The water-heater make, model, and listing.
- Mixing-valve makes and setpoints.
- Backflow device makes, locations, and test tags.
- Filter and treatment models, media, and replacement intervals.
- Photographs of any buried or concealed piping before it is covered.

At commissioning, run the system under real use and confirm the result:

- Multiple fixtures running against the worst-case simultaneous draw.
- Hot-water wait time at the farthest fixture.
- Delivery temperature at each fixture against the scald limit.
- Pressure drop across the treatment train.
- Water hammer with appliances cycling.
- Water-heater recovery and recirculation balance.
- A post-treatment water retest and, for a new well, a coliform re-sample.

**Professional**

The final acceptance shows the system is leak-free, supported, protected from freezing and corrosion, accessible, labeled, and built to the approved plan and the sizing basis. The commissioning record documents the measured pressures, temperatures, and flows, not just the design values, so a future service call starts from what the system delivered at handover. The water-quality record and the retest results stay with the permanent house file alongside the well log, pump data, treatment design, and permit record.

**Code**

```
Supply piping is tested and inspected before cover.
  Pressure test and rough-inspection approval precede concealment of any piping.
  Source: OPSC 312; OPSC 609.4.

The water heater and system take a final inspection.
  Relief-valve discharge, pan and safe-waste path, and temperature controls are verified at final.
  Source: OPSC 504; OPSC 507.

Backflow assemblies are tested at installation and annually.
  Testable assemblies are retested annually by a certified tester.
  Source: OPSC 603.4.2; OAR 333-061-0070.
```
