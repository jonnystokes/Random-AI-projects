# Chapter 19: Testing, Commissioning, And Quality Control

This chapter specifies the testing and commissioning program: which tests are required and by whom, what the owner-builder may test versus what a certified party must, the concrete and soil testing framework, the plumbing and electrical tests, the envelope and energy tests, HVAC and smart-home commissioning, radon and water-quality testing, the owner's commissioning plan, and the records the program produces. The system-specific values (mix design, pressures, R-values, flows) are stated in the trade chapters; this chapter is the testing and commissioning program that ties them together.

Two framing points control the chapter. First, Oregon's code structure is not the IRC's: the ORSC adopts the residential mechanical and energy chapters but not the plumbing and electrical chapters. Plumbing tests therefore cite the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code (OPSC) and electrical tests the Oregon Electrical Specialty Code (OESC), never ORSC plumbing or electrical sections. Second, several tests have no standing unless a certified party performs them, and two tasks (refrigerant work and backflow-assembly testing on a public connection) are gated by federal or state certification regardless of the owner-builder exemption. The chapter is written to keep the owner's role inside its lawful boundary.

## 19.1 Scope And The Testing Program

Testing checks assumptions before the work is buried, commissioning proves the finished systems work together, and quality control keeps the build from drifting from the approved design. Every test is scheduled early enough that its result can still change the work, and every test produces a record.

**Simple**

Testing answers one question: did the house do what the design said it would? The answer is found early, while the work is still reachable, not at move-in. Some tests the owner-builder can run. Others only count when a certified technician or an accredited lab performs them, and the plan respects that line.

**Applied**

Run the program across the tracks, and record every result:

| Track | What it checks |
|---|---|
| Soil and site | Bearing and drainage screening (design values require a geotech) |
| Concrete | Acceptance testing where required (by a certified agency) |
| Plumbing | Drain-waste-vent, supply, shower pan, gas, and backflow |
| Electrical | Polarity, protection devices, grounding, and labeling |
| Envelope and energy | Air leakage and duct leakage on the chosen compliance path |
| HVAC | Airflow, charge, ventilation balance, and controls (charge by a certified tech) |
| Radon and water | Radon test and well-water panel |
| Smart home | Local operation, fallback, alerts, and backups |
| Occupancy | Whole-house readiness and the records |

**Professional**

The program distinguishes tests the owner performs, tests a certified party performs, and diagnostics that guide fixing but do not establish compliance. It sequences the tests to the build (pre-cover tests before drywall, code tests before the certificate of occupancy, and post-occupancy tests after move-in), and it feeds every result into the commissioning binder. The inspection gates that witness the code tests are in the inspection chapter; this chapter is the program that prepares for them and adds the commissioning and monitoring the code does not require.

**Code**

```
Plumbing and electrical tests cite their own Oregon codes.
  The ORSC adopts the residential mechanical (Part V) and energy (Chapter 11) chapters, not plumbing or electrical; plumbing tests cite the OPSC, electrical tests the OESC.
  Source: ORSC R101.2 (2023 edition); OAR 918-750 (OPSC); OAR 918-260 to 918-311 (OESC programs).

Every test result enters the commissioning binder.
  Owner-performed, certified-party, and diagnostic results recorded with dates, photographs, and signatures.
  Source: project documentation standard (see the materials and documentation chapters).
```

## 19.2 Which Tests Are Required, And By Whom

Most testing on a prescriptive single-family house is limited, and much of what people assume is required is condition-triggered or optional. The required-by-source matrix keeps the program honest.

**Simple**

A prescriptive house requires fewer tests than most people expect. The plumbing tests and the alarm checks are mandatory everywhere. Concrete testing, soil testing, duct testing, and radon construction each trigger only under specific conditions. The rest is either the manufacturer's warranty condition or the owner's own quality program.

**Applied**

Sort every test by its source:

- Code-mandated: the OPSC plumbing tests (drain-waste-vent, supply, shower pan), the gas-piping pressure test, the smoke and CO alarm function check, and the energy-path tests where a testing option or a testing-based measure is chosen.
- Condition-triggered: concrete acceptance testing only where the engineer, the plans, or the building official requires it; a soils investigation only where the official determines weak or questionable soils are likely; duct leakage only where ducts leave the conditioned envelope; radon-resistant construction only in the seven mandated counties.
- Utility- or provider-required: the backflow test on a public water connection, under the supplier's cross-connection program.
- Manufacturer-required (warranty-gating): water-heater and heat-pump startup and commissioning per the product instructions.
- Building-official discretion: any test the official requires as evidence of compliance where the evidence is otherwise insufficient.

**Professional**

The building official's authority to require a test at the permit holder's expense (ORSC R104.11.1) is the legal hook behind every "the inspector asked for a test" situation, and per-county practice varies, so the project confirms the county's testing expectations and its chosen energy-compliance path at the design pass. The structural engineer's general notes, where the project uses engineered concrete or masonry, override every default in this chapter and name the tests, frequencies, and testing agency directly.

**Code**

```
The building official may require tests as compliance evidence.
  Tests at the permit holder's expense where evidence of compliance is insufficient; methods per the code or recognized standards.
  Source: ORSC R104.11.1 (2023 edition).

The engineer's general notes control the testing program for engineered elements.
  Tests, frequencies, and the named testing agency in the stamped documents override the chapter defaults.
  Source: ORSC R106.1 construction documents; the project's stamped structural notes.
```

## 19.3 The Owner-Builder Testing Boundary

Some tests only have standing when a certified technician or an accredited lab performs them, and two tasks are gated by certification the owner-builder exemption does not waive. The owner's role on those is scheduling, access, and support, not running the test.

**Simple**

The owner can run many checks, but two lines are hard. Refrigerant work is federal: only an EPA-certified technician may connect, charge, or recover it. Backflow testing on a public water connection is state-certified work. Owner-run concrete or soil tests are useful screening, but they carry no legal weight for acceptance.

**Applied**

- Do not treat owner-performed concrete slump, air, or cylinder results as acceptance; a certified agency performs acceptance testing, and the owner provides the specimen curing environment.
- Do not treat any do-it-yourself soil test as a design value; screening informs whether to call a geotechnical engineer.
- Hire an EPA Section 608-certified technician for all refrigerant work, including connecting a mini-split line set, attaching gauges, and charging.
- On a public water connection, schedule the certified backflow-assembly test at installation and annually through the supplier's program; on a private well, install the OPSC-required backflow protection and test as maintenance practice.
- Keep every owner-performed test to de-energized work or a device's own listed test feature.

**Professional**

Owner-performed concrete tests have no acceptance standing because acceptance testing requires an ASTM C1077-conforming agency with certified field and lab technicians (ACI 318 §26.12.1.1); the owner's job on an engineered shell is the ASTM C31 initial-curing logistics and the pour scheduling. Do-it-yourself soil screening (ribbon, jar, hand-auger, pocket penetrometer, dynamic cone) is uniformity and red-flag screening under the visual-manual practice, never a source of bearing values, settlement predictions, expansive-soil parameters, seismic site class, or a liquefaction conclusion. Refrigerant work is gated by EPA Section 608 under 40 CFR 82 Subpart F. Backflow-assembly testing sits in the drinking-water rules, which apply to public water systems. The supplier must ensure testing at installation, after repair or relocation, and at least every 12 months, by an Authority-certified backflow assembly tester or a journeyman or apprentice plumber with the approved training. The tester delivers the report to the supplier within 10 working days. A home on its own private well is outside those rules; its backflow devices are an OPSC installation requirement enforced through the plumbing permit, and periodic testing is maintenance practice rather than a state duty. The water-supply chapter states the device tiers and specifications the tests apply to.

**Code**

```
Concrete acceptance testing is a certified-agency activity.
  Agency per ASTM C1077; ACI-certified field (Grade I) and laboratory technicians; owner performs the ASTM C31 initial-curing logistics only.
  Source: ACI 318, Section 26.12.1.1; ACI 301; ASTM C1077.

Soil design values come from an approved agency.
  Do-it-yourself methods are screening under the visual-manual practice; bearing, settlement, site-class, and liquefaction values require the geotechnical professional.
  Source: ASTM D2488-26; ORSC R401.4 (2023 edition).

Refrigerant work requires federal certification.
  Line-set connection, gauge attachment, charging, and recovery by an EPA Section 608 certified technician.
  Source: Clean Air Act Section 608; 40 CFR Part 82, Subpart F.

Backflow testing on a public connection is certified work on a fixed schedule.
  At installation, after repair or relocation, and at least every 12 months; by an Authority-certified tester or journeyman/apprentice plumber with approved training; report to the water supplier within 10 working days. Private-well systems are outside these rules; their devices are OPSC installation requirements.
  Source: OAR 333-061-0070(6), (16); OAR 333-061-0072; OAR 333-061-0010 (scope).
```

## 19.4 Concrete Acceptance Testing

Concrete acceptance testing is a certified-technician, accredited-lab activity that is usually not required on a prescriptive house and is invoked by the engineer, the plans, or the building official. Where it applies, the owner's role is the curing environment and the schedule.

**Simple**

Concrete strength is proven by crushing sample cylinders in a lab at 28 days. The sampling and the crushing only count when certified people do them. On this build, the owner's part is small and real. The fresh cylinders stay warm and undisturbed on site and reach the lab on schedule. A cylinder break costs about $25-$50 (2026).

**Applied**

- Where the engineer or official requires acceptance testing, budget the certified agency per pour and provide the secure, temperature-controlled initial-curing box on site.
- Where the owner wants field-testing skills for quality control, take the ACI Concrete Field Testing Technician Grade I course ($400-$600 including the exam, valid 5 years, 2026) and self-perform slump, air, and temperature as internal checks; ship cylinders to an accredited lab for the compression tests ($25-$50 per cylinder).
- Use field-cured cylinders for form-removal and loading decisions, never for acceptance.

**Professional**

Where acceptance testing is required, the testing agency conforms to ASTM C1077 (typically accredited, under a professional engineer's direction), the field technician holds the ACI Concrete Field Testing Technician Grade I credential, and the lab technician holds the corresponding certification (ACI 318 §26.12.1.1 and ACI 301). The field tests in that scope are ASTM C172 sampling, C1064 temperature, C143 slump, C138 density and yield, C231 or C173 air content, and C31 specimen making, with C39 compressive strength (and C42 cores where a question arises) at the lab. Acceptance cylinders are standard-cured per C31 with the initial on-site curing temperature controlled and recorded; field-cured cylinders inform form-removal and loading decisions, not acceptance. Testing errors on C31 and C39 read almost always low, which is why the accredited-lab quality standard matters. On an engineered shell the agency is named in the engineer's general notes and budgeted per pour. The mix design, exposure class, and target values are in the structural and foundation chapters.

**Code**

```
The acceptance-testing agency and technicians are certified.
  Agency per ASTM C1077 under professional direction; ACI Grade I field technician; certified laboratory technician.
  Source: ACI 318, Section 26.12.1.1; ACI 301; ASTM C1077.

The field and laboratory test methods are the ASTM concrete series.
  Sampling C172/C172M-25; temperature C1064/C1064M-23; slump C143/C143M-26a; density C138/C138M-24a; air C231/C231M-26 or C173/C173M-26; specimens C31/C31M-26a; compressive strength C39/C39M-26; cores C42/C42M-20 (editions current 2026; C31 revises nearly yearly, rechecked at publication).
  Source: ASTM C172, C1064, C143, C138, C231, C173, C31, C39, C42.

Acceptance cylinders are standard-cured; field-cured cylinders serve construction decisions.
  Initial on-site curing temperature controlled and recorded per C31; field-cured specimens inform form removal and loading only.
  Source: ASTM C31/C31M-26a; ACI 301.
```

## 19.5 Soil And Geotechnical Testing

Prescriptive foundation design rests on the code's presumptive bearing values, and any value above them or any settlement, site-class, or liquefaction determination is a professional geotechnical activity. Do-it-yourself screening tells the owner whether to call a geotechnical engineer, not what the soil will carry.

**Simple**

The code lets a house be designed on conservative assumed soil strengths, about 1,500 pounds per square foot for clay. Claiming better soil than that takes a geotechnical engineer and lab tests. The owner's soil checks (a jar test, a hand auger, a probe) exist to spot trouble early, not to prove capacity.

**Professional**

ORSC and IRC R401.4 leave the decision to require a soils investigation to the building official where expansive, compressible, or shifting soils are indicated, with the test by an approved agency and method; otherwise the presumptive values of Table R401.4.1 apply (about 1,500 psf for clay and silt). Where the seismic design category is C or higher and soil testing is performed, the geotechnical report includes the seismic site class, which is directly relevant in western Oregon. The professional tests are the visual-manual and laboratory soil classification (ASTM D2488-26 and D2487), Atterberg limits (D4318-17e1, withdrawn 2026 with the successor pending, as the soil chapter states), the Proctor compaction reference (D698-12(2021) or D1557-12(2021)), field density (D1556-24 sand cone or D6938-23 nuclear gauge), and the dynamic cone (D6951-18(2023)). Do-it-yourself screening supports using the conservative presumptive row, flags the conditions (plastic clays, organics, uncontrolled fill, groundwater) that require the geotechnical call, and documents conditions for the pre-pour inspection; compaction acceptance itself requires a laboratory Proctor plus certified field density testing. The soil chapter states the screening procedures and the red-flag list.

**Code**

```
Prescriptive design uses the presumptive bearing values.
  About 1,500 psf for clay and silt per the table; a soils investigation at the building official's determination, by an approved agency and method.
  Source: ORSC R401.4 and Table R401.4.1 (2023 edition).

The professional soil tests are the ASTM geotechnical series.
  Classification D2488-26/D2487; Atterberg D4318-17e1 (withdrawn 2026, successor pending); Proctor D698-12(2021)/D1557-12(2021); field density D1556-24/D6938-23; dynamic cone D6951-18(2023).
  Source: ASTM standards as listed (editions current 2026).
```

## 19.6 Plumbing Testing

Plumbing tests are held under the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code, and the drain-waste-vent, supply, shower-pan, and gas tests are witnessed at the rough and final inspections. Plastic drainage is never air-tested.

**Simple**

Every pipe system is proven under pressure before it is hidden. The drains hold a 10-foot column of water, the supply holds test pressure on a gauge, and the shower pan holds standing water. Plastic drain pipe is never tested with air, because plastic under air pressure fails explosively.

**Applied**

- Test the drain-waste-vent rough with a 10-foot water head above the highest fitting for 15 minutes (or an approved air test); do not air-test plastic drainage.
- Test the water supply at working pressure or 50 psi for 15 minutes, with the gauge holding when the inspector arrives, and install a pressure-reducing valve where static pressure exceeds 80 psi.
- Test the shower pan by plugging the drain and holding at least 2 inches of water for 15 minutes.
- Test gas piping to Oregon's criteria: at least 10 psi held at least 15 minutes with no perceptible drop for a standard system (the fuel-gas section of the HVAC chapter states the full Oregon test regime).
- On a public water connection, have the certified tester test the backflow assembly at installation and annually; on a private well, test as maintenance practice.
- Commission the water heater per the manufacturer (fill before power or fire, purge, check the temperature-and-pressure relief valve, set the temperature, and precharge the expansion tank).

**Professional**

The OPSC (2021 UPC base) sets the drain-waste-vent test (UPC 712), the supply test (UPC 609), and the shower-pan test, and it prohibits air-testing plastic drainage because a brittle failure under air is explosive. Gas piping is tested to the Oregon-amended criteria (ORSC G2417.4.1: 10 psi for 15 minutes under 14 inches water column; 60 psi for 30 minutes for welded or higher-pressure systems), not the model code's 3 psi, and the rough piping inspection precedes any gas turn-on. Backflow assemblies on a public connection are tested at installation, after any repair or relocation, and annually, by a state-certified tester under the drinking-water rules, with the report to the supplier within 10 working days. Water-heater and pump startup follow the manufacturer's model-specific procedures, which are collected at specification time and gate the warranty. The inspector witnesses the code tests; the owner's binder keeps the timestamped gauge photographs and the pass record. The supply pressures, pipe sizing, and treatment detail are in the water-supply chapter, and the drainage detail in the drain-waste-vent chapter.

**Code**

```
The drain-waste-vent system tests under a water head.
  10-foot head above the highest fitting (or approved air test), held 15 minutes; plastic drainage is not air-tested.
  Source: OPSC 712 (2023 edition, 2021 UPC base).

The water supply tests at pressure.
  Working pressure or not less than 50 psi, held 15 minutes; pressure-reducing valve where static pressure exceeds 80 psi.
  Source: OPSC 609 (2023 edition); OPSC 608 (pressure regulation; see the water-supply chapter).

The shower pan holds standing water.
  Drain plugged; at least 2 inches of water held 15 minutes.
  Source: OPSC 417.5 (2023 edition).

Gas piping tests to the Oregon criteria.
  At least 10 psi held at least 15 minutes, no perceptible drop (under 14 inches water column); 60 psi/30 minutes welded or higher-pressure.
  Source: ORSC G2417.4.1 (2023 edition, Oregon amendment; see the HVAC chapter's fuel-gas section).

Backflow assemblies on public connections test on the certified schedule.
  At installation, after repair or relocation, and every 12 months, by a certified tester; report to the supplier within 10 working days.
  Source: OAR 333-061-0070(6), (16); OAR 333-061-0072.
```

## 19.7 Electrical Testing

Electrical testing is cited to the Oregon Electrical Specialty Code, and the owner's test set is limited to de-energized work and a device's own listed test feature. Anything requiring opening energized equipment is a hired-electrician task.

**Simple**

The owner's electrical checks are the safe ones. A plug-in tester goes in every outlet, the TEST button gets pressed on every protective device, and a torque screwdriver sets every termination. Anything that means opening live equipment belongs to an electrician. The protective devices get their TEST button pressed monthly for life.

**Applied**

The owner's defensible electrical test set:

- Verify receptacle polarity and ground on every device with a tester.
- Verify GFCI and AFCI protection with the device's own TEST button (a plug-in tester does not reliably trip an AFCI), and repeat monthly per the manufacturer.
- Check voltage at the panel and the farthest outlets, and the continuity of grounding and bonding connections.
- Verify the panel-directory accuracy and the smoke and CO alarm interconnection function.
- Torque terminations to the manufacturer's value with a calibrated tool.

Leave earth-resistance testing, thermal panel scans under load, and any test that opens energized equipment to a hired electrician.

**Professional**

The OESC (NEC base) governs, and the owner-level tests are on de-energized work or the listed test features. The grounding-electrode requirement is met without earth-resistance testing by driving the supplemental rod under the two-rod alternative (NEC 250.53(A)(2)), which is the practical residential answer; actual electrode testing uses professional equipment. Torque of terminations follows the calibrated-torque requirement (NEC 110.14(D)), and the panel directory follows the circuit-identification requirement (NEC 408.4). Insulation-resistance (megger) testing is not a residential code requirement. Structured cabling is certified to the TIA-568 family with a cable certifier where resale documentation is wanted; a function verifier suffices otherwise. The service, grounding, and branch-circuit detail is in the electrical chapters.

**Code**

```
Owner electrical testing stays de-energized or on listed test features.
  Polarity and ground verification; GFCI/AFCI by the device TEST button (plug-in testers do not reliably trip AFCIs), monthly per the manufacturer; energized diagnostics by a hired electrician.
  Source: OESC/NEC 90.4 program administration (2023 edition); device manufacturer instructions.

Terminations, directories, and electrodes follow their sections.
  Calibrated torque per NEC 110.14(D); panel directory per NEC 408.4; the two-rod alternative (NEC 250.53(A)(2)) meets the electrode requirement without resistance testing.
  Source: OESC/NEC 110.14(D), 408.4, 250.53(A)(2) (2023 edition).
```

## 19.8 Envelope And Energy Testing

The envelope air-leakage test is one of two compliance options, not an unconditional statewide mandate, and the chosen compliance path determines whether a blower-door test is required at all. Duct leakage is tested where ducts leave the conditioned envelope.

**Simple**

The blower door is a calibrated fan in a doorway that measures how leaky the whole house is. Oregon's base code allows either a checklist path or the measured path. Whether the test is required depends on the compliance path the project chose. This project tests anyway, before drywall, because a leak found then costs caulk instead of demolition.

**Applied**

- Determine the energy-compliance path at design; air sealing is met either by the prescriptive sealing criteria or by a blower-door test at or below the path target.
- Where the testing path is chosen, have an approved third-party tester run the multi-point blower-door test, and file the result on the state form before the certificate of occupancy.
- Test duct leakage where any duct runs outside the conditioned envelope, and design ducts and the air handler inside the envelope to avoid the test.
- Use smoke tracing and thermal imaging as diagnostics to find and fix leaks and insulation voids, not as compliance evidence.

**Professional**

The 2023 ORSC N1104.8.2 makes sealing compliant either by the Table N1104.8 criteria or by a blower-door result at or below 3.25 ACH50. The separate high-achievement path is Additional Measure 7, a third-party-tested 2.75 ACH50 paired with heat-recovery ventilation at 66 percent sensible efficiency, and a local reach code may set a lower target. The test follows ASTM E779-19 or E1827-22 (with ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380-2022 as the program standard), with ACH50 computed as CFM50 times 60 divided by the conditioned volume, and the result filed on the BCD form. Field water-spray testing of windows (ASTM E1105-15(2023)) is a hired forensic tool, not a code requirement on a house; the code path is manufacturer-instruction flashing verified at inspection. Thermographic inspection follows ASTM C1060-23 as a diagnostic. The envelope values, the blower-door procedure and costs, and the assemblies are in the envelope chapter.

**Code**

```
Air sealing complies by criteria or by measured result.
  Table N1104.8 criteria, or a blower-door result at or below 3.25 ACH50; Additional Measure 7 is the separate 2.75 ACH50 third-party-tested path with 66 percent sensible heat recovery.
  Source: ORSC N1104.8.2; Table N1101.1(2), Measure 7 (2023 edition).

The blower-door test follows the fan-pressurization standards.
  ASTM E779-19 or E1827-22; ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380-2022 program standard; ACH50 = CFM50 × 60 ÷ conditioned volume; result on the BCD residential form before the certificate of occupancy.
  Source: ASTM E779-19; ASTM E1827-22; ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380-2022; BCD blower-door form.

Ducts outside the envelope are leak-tested; diagnostics are not compliance.
  Duct-leakage testing where ducts leave the conditioned envelope; thermal imaging per ASTM C1060-23 and window water-spray per ASTM E1105-15(2023) serve diagnosis and forensics.
  Source: ORSC N1105.3 (2023 edition); ASTM C1060-23; ASTM E1105-15(2023).
```

## 19.9 HVAC Commissioning

HVAC commissioning verifies that the installed system delivers its design performance, following the residential grading standard, with the refrigerant work performed by a certified technician. The owner installs everything mechanical except the refrigerant circuit.

**Simple**

A heat pump sized right and installed wrong loses its rated performance, so the finished system is measured, not assumed. Five checks make up the standard: the design paperwork, the duct leakage, the blower's airflow, the blower's power draw, and the refrigerant charge. The charge check belongs to the certified technician.

**Applied**

- Verify the equipment was sized by a load calculation (Manual J) and selected within the oversize limit (Manual S), with the duct design to Manual D.
- Commission to the five grading-standard tasks: design review, total duct leakage, blower-fan airflow, blower-fan watt draw, and refrigerant-charge verification.
- Have the EPA-certified technician connect the line set, evacuate, charge, and verify the charge; the owner installs the rest.
- Balance the ventilation: verify the whole-house balanced ventilation rate, the heat- or energy-recovery balance to the manufacturer's tolerance, and the local exhaust flows with a flow box.
- Pressure-test hydronic and radiant tubing before and during the slab pour with the gauge visible, then purge, fill, and verify the temperature difference.
- Verify the condensate trap, slope, and overflow switch, and walk through the heat, cool, and auxiliary staging and the smart-thermostat configuration.

**Professional**

The residential commissioning standard is ANSI/ACCA/RESNET/ICC 310-2020, whose five tasks form the commissioning checklist, with ANSI/ACCA 5 QI-2015 as the quality-installation specification behind it (a proposed revision entered ANSI public review in late 2025, so the edition is rechecked at publication). The refrigerant boundary is a bright line: any line-set connection, gauge attachment, charging, or recovery requires EPA Section 608 certification, with substantial daily penalties for uncertified work. The owner installs everything mechanical and hires the certified technician for the refrigerant circuit and the charge verification, which also preserves the manufacturer's warranty. The ventilation rates, equipment selection, and radiant detail are in the HVAC chapter; this chapter is the commissioning that confirms the installation performs.

**Code**

```
Residential HVAC commissioning follows the grading standard's five tasks.
  Design review, total duct leakage, blower-fan airflow, blower-fan watt draw, refrigerant-charge verification.
  Source: ANSI/ACCA/RESNET/ICC 310-2020; ANSI/ACCA 5 QI-2015 (revision in ANSI review, 2025).

Loads, selection, and ducts are documented to the ACCA manuals.
  Manual J load, Manual S selection within the oversize caps, Manual D ducts.
  Source: ORSC M1401.3 (2023 edition); ACCA Manuals J, S, D.

The refrigerant circuit is certified-technician work.
  Connection, evacuation, charging, and recovery under EPA Section 608; the owner installs the non-refrigerant balance.
  Source: Clean Air Act Section 608; 40 CFR Part 82, Subpart F.
```

## 19.10 Radon, Indoor Air, And Water Quality

Radon-resistant construction is required in seven Oregon counties and is inexpensive to rough in everywhere, and the house is tested for radon after occupancy. A new well gets a full water panel before the plumbing is commissioned.

**Simple**

Radon is a soil gas that causes lung cancer, and the only way to know a house's level is to test after move-in. The sub-slab piping that handles it costs little during the pour and a lot after. Well water gets a full lab panel before anyone drinks it: bacteria, nitrate, arsenic, and the local extras.

**Applied**

- Build radon-resistant construction (a passive sub-slab vent rough-in) in the seven mandated counties, and rough it in elsewhere because it is inexpensive at build time.
- Test radon after occupancy with a closed-house test per the measurement standard (winter preferred), activate the passive system with a fan only if the result is at or above the action level, and retest periodically.
- Test a new well for the full panel (bacteria, nitrate, arsenic, and the site-appropriate items) before commissioning the house plumbing, using an accredited lab.
- Treat moisture and mold checks (crawl-space humidity, attic sheathing, window condensation) as maintenance-schedule observations, not tests.

**Professional**

Radon-resistant new construction applies in Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, and Yamhill counties under the ORSC-adopted radon appendix, and radon is measured per the ANSI/AARST closed-house protocol with the EPA action level at 4.0 pCi/L (mitigation considered at 2 to 4). Oregon does not license radon professionals and points to nationally certified providers. Well water is governed at sale by the Domestic Well Testing Act (ORS 448.271, OAR 333-061), which requires arsenic, nitrate, and total-coliform testing with results to the health authority and the buyer, valid one year. Outside a sale the health authority recommends annual coliform and nitrate and periodic arsenic, with thresholds of 10 ppb arsenic, 10 mg/L nitrate, and coliform absent. The radon rough-in detail and test costs are in the envelope chapter, and the well-water treatment detail is in the water-supply chapter.

**Code**

```
Radon-resistant construction applies in the seven mandated counties.
  Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, Yamhill; passive sub-slab vent rough-in per the radon appendix; measurement per the ANSI/AARST closed-house protocol, EPA action level 4.0 pCi/L.
  Source: ORSC radon provisions (2023 edition; see the envelope chapter); ANSI/AARST measurement standards.

Well water is tested at sale and on the recommended cycle.
  Arsenic, nitrate, and total coliform at sale, results to the health authority and buyer, valid one year; ongoing practice: annual coliform and nitrate, periodic arsenic (thresholds 10 ppb arsenic, 10 mg/L nitrate, coliform absent).
  Source: ORS 448.271; OAR chapter 333, division 61.
```

## 19.11 Smart-Home Commissioning

No residential code or consensus standard governs smart-home commissioning, so this is practice guidance, and its center is proving the house works with the internet disconnected. The detailed architecture and the full commissioning checklist are in the smart-home chapter; here it is one track of the whole-house program.

**Applied**

Commission the smart-home layer by test, not by assumption:

- Local operation: disconnect the wide-area connection and confirm every controlled load still works from a physical control.
- Automation fallback: confirm a manual switch or override for every automated device.
- Camera and lock behavior: confirm motion events record and retain, and confirm the mechanical key, power-loss behavior, and low-battery behavior on every lock.
- Backup power: measure the rack runtime under real load and confirm a clean automatic shutdown.
- Alerts and safety chains: confirm end-to-end notification for leak, freeze, smoke-interconnect, and power-loss events, and confirm the leak-sensor-to-shutoff-valve chain with a wet sensor.

**Professional**

The only citable anchors are the product listings and the consumer-IoT baseline guidance (NIST IR 8425), so the smart-home commissioning is framed as practice guidance and recorded in the binder, and this section has no Code block because there is no statute, rule, or consensus standard to cite. It is the same internet-out, power-out, and local-control testing detailed in the smart-home chapter, run here as part of the whole-house commissioning so the "works without the internet" design is verified rather than assumed.

## 19.12 The Owner's Commissioning Plan And Records

The house is commissioned against an owner's plan assembled from the code tests, the HVAC grading tasks, the manufacturer startups, and borrowed program checklists used only as internal quality assurance. The plan claims no certification label it has not earned, and it produces the record set that carries into warranty, insurance, and resale.

**Simple**

The commissioning plan is a calendar: pre-drywall tests, pre-occupancy tests, a 30-and-90-day round after move-in, and a one-year review. Every result lands in the binder with a date, a photo, and a signature. Years later, the binder answers the questions a buyer, an insurer, or a repair crew will ask.

**Applied**

Sequence the commissioning plan:

1. Pre-drywall: the rough plumbing and electrical tests, and photographs of the insulation and air barrier before cover.
2. Pre-occupancy: the final code tests, the filed energy forms, and the trade finals.
3. Move-in plus 30 and 90 days: the radon test, an indoor-air baseline, and system tuning.
4. One-year review: the seasonal tuning and the whole-binder review.

Keep, organized by system with dates, photos, and signatures: the inspection record and filed state forms, the plumbing and gas gauge photos, the concrete batch tickets and any lab reports, the soils report, the radon results, the well tests, the HVAC commissioning sheet (with the technician's EPA 608 number), the electrical panel directory and torque log, the product serials and warranties, and the pre-cover photographs of every concealed assembly.

**Professional**

Formal commissioning standards and certification programs (ENERGY STAR, DOE Zero Energy Ready, PHIUS, and HERS ratings) are sources of excellent checklists, but claiming their labels requires the program's own verifiers, so the owner's plan borrows the checklists as internal quality assurance and does not imply certification. Post-occupancy, the program continues as monitoring: an energy log compared seasonally, a water meter and leak-sensor check, indoor humidity kept roughly 30 to 60 percent, a radon retest on the recommended cycle and after any foundation or HVAC change, an annual well panel, and the routine equipment logs and safety-device checks. Oregon's well-test and radon-disclosure environment means these records convert directly into a smoother sale, and the closeout binder is retained per the materials and documentation chapters.

**Code**

```
The owner's plan borrows program checklists without claiming their labels.
  ENERGY STAR, Zero Energy Ready, PHIUS, and HERS checklists as internal quality assurance; certification claims require the program's own verifiers.
  Source: the named programs' certification requirements.

The records carry into disclosure and resale.
  Well tests (valid one year) and radon results feed the seller's property disclosure; the binder is retained per the documentation chapter.
  Source: ORS 448.271; ORS 105.464 (see the legal and documentation chapters).
```
