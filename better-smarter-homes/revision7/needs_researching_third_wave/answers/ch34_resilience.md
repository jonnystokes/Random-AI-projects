**1. What does base isolation cost, buildable in rural Oregon, for a reinforced CMU/concrete single-family shell rather than the lighter Japanese timber houses the existing commercial systems (Ichijo/Bridgestone) target, and does any U.S. supplier sell a system sized for a project like this one?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Both leads are confirmed but neither is a solution buildable or purchasable for this project as specified. Ichijo (with Bridgestone) sells a real, commercially deployed sliding-plate-plus-rubber-bearing base-isolation system for single-family houses, warranted for 60 years, that has been installed on more than 1,800 Japanese homes since gaining Japanese Ministry of Construction blanket approval in 2000 (extended to soft-ground sites in 2002); reported added cost is roughly $20,000 on a 1,200 sq ft two-story house selling for about $400,000 (Fine Homebuilding, "Houses That Roll With the Punches," https://www.finehomebuilding.com/2007/04/01/houses-that-roll-with-the-punches). This is a Japan-market product for a light timber house; no source found in this pass shows it sold, imported, or engineered for a U.S. buyer, and it is sized for wood-frame dead loads, not a reinforced CMU/concrete shell's far greater mass, which changes the bearing and sliding-plate load design entirely. The Stanford program is a research effort, not a commercial product: a 2012-2014 NSF-funded ($1.3 million) two-year Stanford study tested full-scale wood-frame structures with re-centering dish-shaped and flat high-friction sliding isolators (high-strength plastic on galvanized steel) and estimated a total system cost near $15,000 on a $400,000 wood-frame house — again a light-timber target, and a research demonstration, not a purchasable product with a named U.S. supplier (Stanford Digital Repository, https://purl.stanford.edu/rk084kb2577; BRANZ Build summary, https://www.buildmagazine.org.nz/articles/show/residential-base-isolators). No U.S. supplier selling a base-isolation system engineered and rated for a reinforced-CMU/concrete single-family shell's mass was found in this pass; the only U.S. installations of Bridgestone's system found are large institutional/commercial buildings (San Francisco and Oakland city halls, USC's Leavey Library), not a product line addressing a house-scale but heavy masonry structure. Commercial-scale elastomeric/friction-pendulum base isolation (the technology those buildings use) is available from U.S. suppliers (e.g., Dynamic Isolation Systems, Taylor Devices) but is priced and engineered for commercial/institutional projects, not packaged or costed as a residential product; a project-specific quote from such a supplier, with a project-specific structural engineer's isolator design for this shell's actual mass, would be the only way to get a real number, and this pass found no such quote.

**7. What low-damage, self-centering structural concepts from New Zealand's post-Christchurch research have been adapted to light residential construction, and is any part of that thinking transferable to a reinforced-CMU or cast-in-place concrete residential shear wall, even as a partial-wall or single-wall-line application?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** This item duplicates `ch06_structural.md` item 14 in full. The PRESSS (Precast Seismic Structural Systems) program, coordinated by UC San Diego in the late 1990s, originated post-tensioned, unbonded, self-centering "rocking wall" technology in precast concrete: a wall panel rocks on its foundation under seismic load, is re-centered by unbonded post-tensioning tendons, and dissipates energy through separate, often replaceable, mild-steel elements rather than through damage to the primary structural material. Following the 2011 Christchurch earthquake, New Zealand researchers extended the concept to engineered timber ("Pres-Lam," using LVL, glulam, or CLT), and this timber adaptation is confirmably more mature in the published literature for light/residential-scale buildings than any concrete- or masonry-specific residential adaptation found in this pass. The rocking/self-centering principle is conceptually system-agnostic (it originated in concrete before moving to timber), so it is not foreign to this project's material, but no documented, built residential-scale reinforced-CMU or CIP-concrete application exists, even as a partial-wall hybrid; translating the commercial precast precedent down to a single-family wall line would be genuinely novel engineering, not a documented option to specify. A "0.5 percent of total building cost" figure sometimes cited for this technology could not be independently verified and should not be used; the only verified adjacent figure is a roughly 7% net construction-cost effect from a three-months-shorter schedule on one Christchurch low-damage commercial building (a schedule-driven savings, a different metric).

Source: ASCE Journal of Architectural Engineering, "Cyclic Testing of Three-Story Mass Timber Building Structure with Coupled Self-Centering Rocking Walls," https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/JAEIED.AEENG-2173; Canterbury Earthquakes Royal Commission, Low-Damage Building Technologies Final Report, https://canterbury.royalcommission.govt.nz/vwluResources/FinalReportVol3Print/$file/Final_Report_Volume_3_Web.pdf; both checked 2026-07-12 per `ch06_structural.md`.

Lands in: Structural System and Concrete Design (full derivation in `ch06_structural.md` item 14).

**8. What does the research and post-earthquake damage literature say about the actual failure modes of reinforced CMU and cast-in-place concrete residential construction specifically, and which of those failure modes is this project's current SDC D0-D2 prescriptive/engineered specification already closing versus still exposed to?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** This item duplicates `ch06_structural.md` item 15 in full. Documented reinforced/confined-masonry failure modes (distinct from unreinforced masonry's simple out-of-plane collapse): inadequate detailing at tie-column-to-tie-beam joint zones; absence of ties (stirrups) in end columns concentrating damage at joint zones; absence of ties combined with inadequate anchorage causing shear failure at the joint; and buckling of longitudinal reinforcement where tie size/spacing was inadequate to restrain bars under compression reversal. The 2010 Chile reconnaissance documented nominally similar, nearby buildings showing sharply different damage outcomes, traced specifically to these detailing differences (the same case-study finding item 79 below revisits). This project's design (fully grouted, vertically and horizontally reinforced CMU with continuous bond beams per §6.6, ACI 318 §25.4 development-length laps per §6.8) already closes these documented failure modes at the design-intent level; what it does not automatically close is the field-execution risk — the same congestion-and-consolidation failure mode where reinforcement present on paper is undermined by a field detailing or placement gap — which is exactly why the manuscript's §6.16 inspection hold-point discipline exists.

**10. What automatic seismic gas shutoff valve products are available, what triggering threshold and reset procedure does each use, and where does Oregon plumbing/gas code place them relative to the existing fuel-gas piping and pressure-test material?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** This item duplicates `ch21-24_electrical-solar-hvac-smart.md` item 13 in full. The trigger mechanism is consistent across the product category per national safety-standard guidance (ASCE 25): a mechanical valve (no electricity required) uses a small ball balanced on a pedestal that rolls off and physically blocks the gas line when ground motion reaches a threshold commonly cited as equivalent to a magnitude 5.2-5.4 earthquake (roughly 0.11g sustained horizontal acceleration), deliberately not triggering on passing-truck or construction vibration (https://www.thewaterheatercompany.com/blog/how-a-seismic-gas-shutoff-valve-works; https://www.jaspector.com/wiki/earthquake-shutoff-valve/). **12. What can a homeowner who understands their own house's structural design safely self-assess after a major earthquake, using the ATC-20 green/yellow/red framework, before a professional inspector can reach a rural property in a region-wide event?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** ATC-20 ("Postearthquake Safety Evaluation of Buildings"), published by the Applied Technology Council in 1989 with California OES/OSHPD and FEMA funding, is the standard rapid/detailed post-earthquake building-safety evaluation framework, resulting in one of three placards: green ("INSPECTED" — no apparent structural hazard, normal occupancy permitted), yellow ("LIMITED ENTRY/RESTRICTED USE" — moderate damage or hazardous conditions restrict full occupancy, entry limited to specific purposes, further evaluation usually required), or red ("UNSAFE" — severe damage or imminent collapse risk, no entry without special authorization) (Applied Technology Council, https://www.atcouncil.org/atc-20; ATC-20-1 Field Manual, https://www.atcouncil.org/pdfs/ATC201tocv2.pdf). ATC-20 evaluations are formally intended to be performed by trained engineers, architects, or building officials, not homeowners — a genuine, official ATC-20 placarding is not something a resident can self-certify, and this research found no ATC-20-branded homeowner self-assessment product or officially sanctioned lay-user checklist. What a homeowner who understands their own house's structural design (the manuscript's own §6.16 hold-point records and structural drawings) can realistically and safely do before a professional reaches a rural property in a region-wide event is an informal pre-screening using the same categories of evidence ATC-20 evaluators look for: visible wall racking, cracking through (versus surface-only) a structural element, out-of-plumb walls, visible foundation displacement or settlement, roof/wall separation at the bond-beam line, and whether doors and windows still operate — checked against the pre-event baseline documentation the household should keep on hand (item 86 below). This self-screening's purpose is narrow: deciding whether to re-enter the house at all, and whether to treat it as probably safe, possibly unsafe (stay out of specific areas, do not run gas or electrical systems), or clearly unsafe (do not enter, evacuate to a known-safe structure or the safe room if it is separately verified sound) — pending an actual professional ATC-20 evaluation, which should still be obtained as soon as one is reachable, since a lay assessment is never a substitute for the real placarding.

Source: Applied Technology Council, ATC-20 overview and placard definitions, https://www.atcouncil.org/atc-20 and https://www.atcouncil.org/placards-and-evaluation-forms, checked 2026-07-14; FEMA, ATC-20 program page, https://www.fema.gov/emergency-managers/risk-management/earthquake/training/atc-20, checked 2026-07-14.

**15. Does ORS 455.446/455.447's "essential facility" and "special occupancy structure" definition ever reach a single-family owner-built dwelling, under any circumstance, or is a single-family home categorically outside the statute's restricted-construction list?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** This item duplicates `ch03_land-site-and-feasibility.md` item 1 (and `ch04_legal_part1.md` item 1) in full. The premise needs correcting: the exact phrases "essential facility" and "special occupancy structure" do not appear anywhere in ORS chapter 455. ORS 455.446 only authorizes DOGAMI to map tsunami inundation zones and imposes no construction restriction itself. ORS 455.447 is the operative section, and it applies only to a "major structure" (defined at 455.447(1)(c) as a building over six stories with 60,000+ sq ft aggregate floor area, any building over 10 stories, or parking structures as BCD defines by rule) or to a building designated Tsunami Risk Category III or IV under Table 1604.5 of the Oregon Structural Specialty Code (the commercial code, adapted from ASCE 7's Risk Category I-IV system). A detached single-family dwelling is categorically Risk Category II under both the IRC/ORSC and IBC/OSSC framework — not Category III (substantial hazard to human life if it fails) or Category IV (essential facilities: hospitals, fire/police stations, emergency operations centers) — and is nowhere near the "major structure" size/height thresholds. No circumstance the question raises (home occupation, accessory-structure use, future use change) converts an ordinary single-family dwelling into a Risk Category III/IV structure or a "major structure," since those are occupancy-based classifications tied to public/emergency function, not to how a family uses its own house.

**17. What do FEMA P-646's structural design concepts for a tsunami vertical-evacuation refuge imply for a private single-family home sited in a tsunami zone, even where the home is not required to meet P-646 as an institutional refuge?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** This item duplicates `ch06_structural.md` item 17 in full. FEMA P-646 ("Guidelines for Design of Structures for Vertical Evacuation from Tsunamis," 3rd edition) is guidance for a structure or earthen mound strong and tall enough to serve as a life-safety refuge for evacuees who cannot reach high ground before tsunami arrival — normally a community facility, not a code-mandated single-family requirement anywhere in Oregon. **37. What does a realistic multi-week household routine look like once "day five fatigue" sets in: what specific tasks, checks, or stored-supply drawdowns should be pre-planned and written into a household outage procedure so decisions are not made from scratch under stress?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** The "day five" threshold is a documented, real pattern in prolonged-outage and disaster-psychology literature, not an arbitrary framing: by day four or five, physical discomfort stops being a mere inconvenience and becomes the thing that makes everything else harder — decision-making, patience, sleep, and household relationships all degrade under the accumulated strain (The Savvy Survivalist, https://www.thesavvysurvivalist.com/how-fast-society-changes-during-a-long-term-power-outage/). A named real-world example of day-five-specific infrastructure compounding: during one documented Midwest outage, the fire department went door to door on day five specifically to warn residents about potential sewage backup and to request minimal bathroom-facility use, illustrating how a household's own systems (septic, in this project's case) can develop new failure modes only once the outage has run long enough. A second documented failure mode at this stage is uncoordinated resource-sharing: a household that shares supplies with neighbors without a pre-set plan can find itself down to a fraction of its stored reserve by day five, specifically because saying no in the moment, without a pre-agreed policy, feels wrong — precisely the kind of decision this project's household outage procedure should settle in advance, not negotiate in real time. **38. What snow-load and ice-accumulation failure case studies exist for Oregon or Pacific Northwest roofs, and does this project's roof structural specification already exceed the failure threshold those case studies identify?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** This item duplicates `ch06_structural.md` item 19 in full. The best-documented Oregon case is a December 2008 East Multnomah County (Portland-area) warehouse collapse: a truss roof designed to 1997 UBC and completed in 2001 experienced witness-reported snow accumulation of 3-5 ft at parapets and 2-3 ft over interior areas, and 24 roof trusses collapsed. Forensic investigation (Portland State University engineering faculty) found the collapse resulted from actual snow load surpassing the design load, wind-driven snow drift accumulating against tall parapets beyond the design assumption, construction defects, and progressive collapse (each failing truss dumping load onto its neighbor); of the failed trusses, 18 failed by connection weld failure and 6 by concrete breakout at the connection — i.e., the documented failure mode was the connection detail, not the truss members themselves. Additional Oregon/PNW cases identified: a 2014 manufacturing-plant roof collapse and the 2025 North Medford High School gym roof collapse under a reported 350 tons of snow (KOIN/KGW news coverage). The identifiable engineering lesson (connection-design margin and drift-load assumptions at parapets/discontinuities, not the base structural material) differs materially from this project's manuscript §6.10 roof options (a concrete deck + PMR roof has no truss connection to fail this way at all; the manufacturer-engineered wood-truss option carries the manufacturer's own stamped connection design), and the manuscript's own snow-load sourcing (site-specific ground snow load per the OSU/SEAO lookup tool with a 25 psf minimum, plus an ASCE 7 ponding-instability check) already requires the site-specific, drift-inclusive load the 2008 case shows was missing. This is a design-intent conclusion, not a certification — drift loading at any parapet, valley, or roof-step condition in the project's actual roof geometry should still be specifically checked against ASCE 7's drift provisions by the engineer of record.

**40. What does the passive-survivability literature's dynamic-simulation compliance approach require as a deliverable, and would running this project's design through that kind of simulation be a useful voluntary verification step even though Oregon has no such mandate?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Passive survivability — a building's ability to maintain safe, livable interior conditions during an extended utility outage without active mechanical intervention — is assessed in the building-science literature by simulating interior conditions during a modeled power/heat/cooling outage in dynamic building-energy-modeling software (e.g., WUFIplus), producing a time-series curve of interior temperature (and sometimes humidity) decay against outdoor design-condition weather data, rather than a single static number; this is the same category of deliverable item 30 above already establishes cannot be produced for this project without a finalized envelope and an actual model run (Building Enclosure Online, https://www.buildingenclosureonline.com/blogs/14-the-be-blog-building-enclosure/post/91125-assessing-heat-stress-for-passive-survivability). Passive survivability is not named in either major passive-house standard (Passive House Institute or PHIUS) but the same envelope strategies that produce PHIUS's energy performance (continuous insulation, high airtightness, thermal-bridge-free detailing) are documented to also produce the resilience/passive-survivability benefit during outages, and PHIUS-certified buildings are explicitly noted to provide superior resilience during power outages as a documented side effect of the certification's own envelope requirements (https://www.phius.org/assessing-resiliency-and-passive-survivability-multifamily-buildings). A formal compliance pathway exists in LEED (Pilot Credit 100, "Passive Survivability and Backup Power During Disruptions"), which offers PHIUS or Passive House Institute certification, combined with effective natural ventilation, as one of its compliance paths — meaning the "deliverable" a project pursuing formal recognition would produce is either a full PHIUS/PHI certification package or a project-specific dynamic simulation run demonstrating maintained habitable conditions over a defined outage duration. For this project: since Oregon has no such mandate, running a dynamic simulation is a genuinely useful *voluntary* verification step specifically because it would give the household an actual, project-specific decay-time number (rather than the qualitative "mass slows the decay" finding of item 30) to plan the deep-store durations (items 48-76) against — but it requires the finalized envelope design and a modeler competent in a tool like WUFIplus or an equivalent hourly energy model, which is a design-development-stage task, not something this research pass can produce now.

**53. What water storage container/tank material performs best for a buried, multi-decade, freeze-safe cistern serving this project's long-life philosophy, and does an owner-poured concrete cistern compete on cost and durability against a manufactured tank?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** This item duplicates `ch18_water-independence.md` items 23 and 28 in full. For a residential-scale cistern, the honest tradeoff between formed (owner-poured) concrete and ferrocement runs toward formed concrete for this project specifically: ferrocement saves material (thin 1-2 in. shells, no heavy formwork) but costs labor and skill (continuous hand-application of mortar through mesh layers, a two-plus-person operation demanding trowelling skill to avoid cold joints), while an owner-poured formed tank costs more material/formwork but reuses the same form-rebar-pour skill set this project already commits to for the foundation and walls, and tolerates staged, discrete-session owner-building the way continuous ferrocement application does not. **64. What propane tank size and NFPA 58 setback geometry fits this project's site while supporting weeks, not days, of backup generation, cooking, and any propane-fired backup heat, and how does tank size interact with the buried-versus-above-ground tank decision?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** This item duplicates `ch21-24_electrical-solar-hvac-smart.md` item 19 in full, with additional cost and consumption detail added here. NFPA 58 (the LP-Gas Code), which Oregon's State Fire Marshal enforces by reference alongside NFPA 54 (https://www.oregon.gov/osfm/industry/pages/lpg.aspx), sets above-ground tank separation distances that scale with water capacity: roughly 10 ft from important buildings and buildable property lines for a tank in the 125-500 gallon bracket (covering the common single-family 250/500 gallon sizes), stepping up to roughly 25 ft for the 501-1,000 (and, per secondary sources with lower confidence since the 2,000-gallon bracket was not independently verified against the primary text, likely 501-2,000) gallon bracket. Underground tanks carry a flat, smaller separation requirement of roughly 10 ft from buildings and property lines regardless of size, with burial depth minimums of 6 in. (18 in. within 10 ft of a vehicle path) (https://propanedeal.com/tanks/propane-tank-distance-rules; https://propanedeal.com/installation/distance-rules). This produces a real site-planning interaction: on a tight or narrow rural parcel, moving from a 500-gallon to a 1,000-gallon above-ground tank costs roughly 15 additional feet of clearance the site may not have, while the same size increase costs a buried tank nothing in setback terms, making burial the more site-flexible option specifically where lot geometry is constrained.

**Sizing to weeks rather than days:** a residential-scale (20-24 kW) standby generator commonly burns roughly 2 gal/hr at 50 percent load and 3.6-4 gal/hr at full load (https://www.dynamic-electric.com/blog/2018/11/27/i-plan-to-run-my-generator-on-propane-how-large-a-tank-do-i-need); a 500-gallon tank (roughly 400 gallons usable at the standard 80 percent fill limit) is commonly cited as good for roughly 8-10 days of dedicated generator-only run time, meaning that reaching a genuine multi-week reserve once cooking and any propane-fired backup heat draw from the same tank (and winter heating load can turn a tank lasting a full month in spring into one needing refill within 2-3 weeks in a cold January) points toward the 1,000-gallon size rather than 500, which is exactly the size threshold that triggers the larger 25-ft above-ground setback. Buried tanks also cost roughly two to three times more to install (roughly $3,000-5,000+ including excavation and corrosion protection, versus roughly $500-2,500 above-ground) and last longer with cathodic protection (30-40 years versus 20-30), but an inadequately anchored buried tank can float in a flood-prone location, a real caution for a coastal or floodplain-adjacent parcel (https://propanedepot.com/blog/installing-above-and-underground-propane-tanks/; https://www.lgjordanoil.com/underground-vs-aboveground-tanks/).

**66. Where does a dedicated, code-compliant fuel-storage structure or bunker belong on this project's site plan relative to the house, the propane tank, and any wildfire defensible-space zone, so the fuel store itself is not a fire-spread or wildfire-ignition liability?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** No single consolidated code section governs this exact combined siting question (fuel-storage building relative to house, propane tank, and defensible-space zone together); the answer is assembled from several separately-governed rules that must each be satisfied at once. For a gasoline/diesel storage building specifically, OSHA's flammable-liquids framework (29 CFR 1910.106, and the parallel construction-site rule 1926.152(f)(3)) is the closest analog, requiring at least 50 ft between any source of ignition and stored flammable liquids on a construction site, and NFPA 30 (the Flammable and Combustible Liquids Code OSHA incorporates by reference) governs container/quantity limits (drums up to 60 gallons, portable tanks up to 660 gallons, are the individual-container categories covered) and general storage-building requirements (https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.152; https://legalclarity.org/flammable-liquid-storage-osha-and-nfpa-30-requirements/). These OSHA figures are written for workplaces and construction sites, not residential accessory buildings, so a residential fuel-storage shed's exact required setback from the house is genuinely a local building/fire-code-amendment question the local AHJ must confirm, not a single fixed national number. Layered on top of that, Oregon's wildfire defensible-space framework (item 29 above) treats any outbuilding, including a fuel-storage shed, as its own defensible-space center: guidance generally recommends detached sheds sit at least 50 ft from the home (further if storing combustible items) and that a 10 ft clearance to bare mineral soil with no flammable vegetation be maintained around the shed's own exterior, exactly as required around the house and around the propane tank itself (https://www.readyforwildfire.org/prepare-for-wildfire/defensible-space/; https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/defensible-space-protection-against-wildfires). The practical site-plan conclusion: a dedicated fuel-storage structure should sit far enough from the house to satisfy the larger of the applicable ignition-source setback and the wildfire defensible-space distance (roughly 50 ft is a reasonable planning distance against both), far enough from the propane tank that neither installation's own required clearance zone overlaps the other's stored fuel, and with its own independent 10 ft noncombustible perimeter exactly as the house and the propane tank each require — meaning the fuel store is not a lesser-regulated appendage to the house's own defensible space but a third independent hazard center needing its own clearance ring.

**67. What does a realistic multi-week battery-plus-solar-plus-generator dispatch strategy look like on paper?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** This item duplicates `ch21-24_electrical-solar-hvac-smart.md` item 21 in full. The settled method, drawn from the automatic-generator-start (AGS) two-wire integration and the critical-loads framework covered elsewhere in that chapter: (1) Tier 1 critical loads (well pump, freeze protection, refrigeration, life-safety) run continuously off battery, topped up by whatever solar the array produces each day; (2) battery state-of-charge is monitored against a set low threshold (commonly 20-30 percent of usable capacity in off-grid-forum practice, a configurable rather than fixed setpoint); (3) when state-of-charge crosses that threshold — which on a partial-cloud winter day may happen daily, or every few days, depending on actual solar reaching the array — the two-wire AGS signal starts the generator automatically, which both recharges the battery and carries the live house load directly (this is why sizing the generator at roughly 2x inverter continuous output matters elsewhere in that chapter's findings, since the generator does both jobs at once); (4) the generator runs until state-of-charge climbs back above a set high threshold, or for a fixed minimum run time to avoid short-cycling the engine, then shuts down and the house reverts to battery-plus-solar until the next low-SOC trigger. Fuel burn per day of partial-cloud winter operation is a direct function of the specific generator's fuel-consumption curve at partial load (published in the manufacturer's spec sheet as gallons/hour at 25/50/75/100 percent load) multiplied by however many hours per day the dispatch logic calls for it to run; this is not a number this research can supply without this project's final generator model and a realistic estimate of how many low-SOC-trigger events a typical partial-cloud winter day produces, both of which depend on the finalized array/battery sizing that chapter leaves as an owner decision. A separate, documented practitioner method for validating whatever dispatch strategy is finally chosen: a "solar system stress test" — picking a poor-weather week, starting around 80 percent battery charge, running normal daily loads (well pump, washing machine, and other real household loads stacked together) without leaning on the generator, and tracking state-of-charge each evening to see whether the battery bank actually holds up (https://www.anoffgridlife.com/solar-system-stress-test-for-off-grid) — is directly useful groundwork for this project's own annual resilience drill (item 85 below).

**69. What root-cellar volume, ventilation duct sizing, and floor treatment delivers the 32-40°F/85-95 percent RH target using this project's own foundation mass, without mechanical refrigeration or dehumidification?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** The target range itself is a settled, consistently documented figure across root-cellar design literature: 32-40°F and 85-95 percent relative humidity, conditions that slow produce respiration and ripening enough to extend storage life for months (https://www.newlifeonahomestead.com/root-cellar-temperature-humidity-ventilation/; https://commonsensehome.com/root-cellars-101/). **71. What passive stack-effect ventilation duct sizing and placement is standard root-cellar practice, and does it conflict with or complement this project's overall tight-envelope, mechanically-ventilated design philosophy?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Duct sizing and placement are given in full in item 69 above (paired 4-inch insulated intake-low/exhaust-high ducts, each screened and dampered). The conflict with this project's tight-envelope, mechanically-ventilated design philosophy is real but resolvable, and the resolution is a siting principle rather than a ventilation-hardware compromise: a root cellar's passive stack-effect ventilation depends on genuine, continuous air exchange with the outside, which is the opposite of what a Passive-House-grade airtight envelope with balanced mechanical ventilation is designed to do everywhere else in the house, and building the cellar as an airtight space specifically to "keep things cold" is a documented mistake, since some produce gives off ethylene gas that accelerates ripening and rot if not vented, and excess trapped humidity in an over-sealed cellar causes mold rather than the intended preservation (https://www.ecohome.net/en/guides/4143/how-to-build-a-root-cellar-in-your-basement-for-food-storage/). The documented resolution in the same source: locate the root cellar physically outside the house's airtight thermal envelope (a genuinely unheated, non-insulated-to-the-earth space bounded by earth on at least two sides), with its own independent passive ventilation system separate from the house's mechanical ventilation, rather than trying to run passive stack ventilation through a wall that is also supposed to be part of the sealed, mechanically-ventilated envelope; the cellar works specifically because it sits outside the pressure and airtightness boundary the rest of the house's ventilation strategy manages, not because its ducts are engineered to coexist with that boundary. This means the root cellar's envelope detailing (where its walls, ceiling, and access door meet the conditioned house) is the genuine design-coordination point requiring care — the access door itself should be insulated and reasonably well-sealed against the heated basement/house side specifically so the cellar's passive ventilation losses are not also the house's mechanical ventilation system's losses — while the cellar's own two ducts penetrate only the unconditioned exterior earth-facing side, not the house's managed airtight boundary.

**74. What are the real, legal constraints on stockpiling prescription medication for a multi-week isolation scenario in Oregon, and what workaround strategies do chronic-condition patients in disaster-prone areas use?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Oregon law does not permit open-ended prescription stockpiling; it instead provides three specific, narrower mechanisms, read directly from the controlling statute and rule text. **ORS 743A.063** requires a health benefit plan to reimburse up to a 90-day supply of a covered prescription drug once an initial 30-day supply has already been dispensed to the member and the quantity does not exceed what the prescriber authorized through refills, but this section explicitly does not apply to Schedule II controlled substances (https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_743a.063). **OAR 855-041-1120(2)** lets a pharmacist dispense an emergency refill without contacting the prescriber only where the practitioner is unavailable and the pharmacist's professional judgment finds a genuine emergency need, capped at a 72-hour supply and explicitly excluding controlled substances, with the practitioner "promptly notified" afterward (https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_855-041-1120). **OAR 855-007-0090** is broader but only active during a formally declared emergency or in an area engaged in disaster assistance: a pharmacist may then dispense up to a 30-day supply without a valid prescription where professional judgment finds the drug essential to health or continuation of therapy, with the dispensing recorded and marked as an Emergency Prescription and the patient told no prescriber authorization was obtained; controlled-substance refills under this provision require DEA permission (https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_855-007-0090). None of these three mechanisms authorizes a household to simply stockpile weeks of medication in advance of a disaster; they are all response-time mechanisms activated only once an actual emergency need or declared emergency exists.

**Documented real-world workaround strategies** used by chronic-condition patients in disaster-prone areas, drawn from disaster-preparedness and clinical-guidance sources rather than from Oregon law itself: asking the prescribing physician directly for a 90-day-supply prescription written specifically for preparedness purposes (using the ORS 743A.063 mechanism as intended, proactively rather than reactively); for insulin-dependent patients, keeping older, unpatented human insulins (NPH and Regular), which are available over-the-counter, are substantially cheaper, and can survive without refrigeration for up to 28 days at 59-86°F, as a lower-cost, non-prescription-dependent backup to a patient's usual analog insulin; keeping paper and photographed copies of every prescription and insurance card in a waterproof container so an emergency refill (mechanism two or three above) can actually be processed quickly if evacuation or a pharmacy disruption occurs; and using a small cooler with gel ice packs for any medication needing refrigeration during an evacuation. A documented real-world consequence of not planning ahead: after Hurricane Ivan (2004), 53 percent of pharmacies in affected areas had depleted supplies, and at least 26 percent had to prioritize distribution — a real, quantified illustration of why the proactive 90-day-supply route is materially more reliable than counting on the emergency-refill mechanisms after the fact.

**78. Where specifically does concrete's fire-spalling risk apply to this project's actual specified mix designs, and does the project's long-life mix increase or decrease spalling susceptibility relative to ordinary residential concrete?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** This item duplicates `ch06_structural.md` item 23 in full. Explosive spalling occurs because heated concrete generates internal vapor pressure as pore water vaporizes; low-permeability concrete cannot vent that pressure fast enough through its own pore structure, so pressure builds until a surface layer violently detaches, and the governing research variables are water-cement ratio, heating rate, matrix strength, and permeability. Critically, and counter to ordinary durability intuition, high-strength, low-permeability concrete is *more* susceptible to explosive spalling than normal-strength concrete at elevated temperature specifically because its dense matrix traps evaporating water more effectively — one cited study found that *raising* the w/c ratio measurably *reduced* spalling risk at high temperature, the opposite direction from ordinary durability practice, where a lower w/c ratio is unambiguously better. Applied to this project's own specified mix (manuscript §6.13): the project's long-life target mix (f'c 4,000-5,000 psi versus 2,500-3,000 psi standard; maximum w/c 0.40-0.45 versus 0.55 standard; SCM replacement) is chosen specifically to densify the paste and reduce permeability for corrosion/durability reasons, and per the spalling research this is precisely the mix profile that *increases* susceptibility to explosive spalling relative to ordinary residential concrete, not decreases it — the same density that gives the project its 100-plus-year corrosion-resistance target is the property that raises fire-spalling risk. This is a genuine, documented trade-off inherent to the project's own durability strategy, not a hypothetical concern, and the same source literature identifies polypropylene fiber addition (roughly 0.1 vol%) as a documented mitigation that improves spalling behavior without abandoning the low-permeability durability mix.

**79. What real-world documented case shows two structurally similar reinforced-masonry or concrete residential buildings with sharply different earthquake outcomes traced to a specific, nameable detailing difference, in enough construction detail to draw a direct lesson for this project's own detailing decisions?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** This item duplicates `ch06_structural.md` item 24 in full. The 2010 Maule, Chile earthquake (magnitude 8.8, February 27, 2010) reconnaissance literature documents exactly this pattern: nominally identical, physically adjacent masonry/confined-masonry buildings in some neighborhoods stood side by side where some structures showed no damage at all while immediately neighboring structures showed severe, brittle damage or, in a smaller number of cases, partial collapse. **81. What does "repairable after damage" mean and cost for this project's specific wall systems after a moderate seismic or fire event, and how does repairability compare against the total-loss-and-rebuild reality of a damaged wood-framed house?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Documented repair technique for moderately damaged reinforced concrete or masonry: epoxy-injection repair of cracked beam-column joints and wall elements, a technique with real, tested performance data — static and dynamic testing shows epoxy-resin injection repair restores, and in some documented tests increases, the flexural strength and stiffness of a damaged reinforced-concrete joint relative to its pre-damage condition, not merely a cosmetic fix (a Springer Nature-published study on epoxy-repaired braced partial-infilled RC frames; a ResearchGate-indexed study on seismic repair of RC beam-column subassemblages by epoxy injection). **The comparison against wood-frame total loss is the more clearly documented half of this question**: a well-known pre-1940 wood-frame failure mode, cripple-wall collapse (the short stud wall between the foundation sill and the first-floor diaphragm lacking adequate shear-panel capacity), is a documented total-structural-compromise failure mode with no analogous "epoxy-inject and continue" repair path — a collapsed or racked cripple wall generally means the entire structure above it has shifted off its foundation-level support, a fundamentally different, much more severe damage category than a cracked concrete/masonry joint that retains its original geometric alignment and load path even while damaged. This is the structural basis for the manuscript's own resilience premise (and for items 77's flood-recovery findings, and item 41's ICC 500 discussion): concrete/masonry construction's realistic post-event outcomes cluster around "repair the specific damaged elements while the building remains standing, functional, and geometrically intact," while a comparably-aged, comparably-exposed wood-frame structure's realistic post-event outcomes include a genuine risk of the "total loss, rebuild from the foundation up" category this project's structural philosophy is specifically chosen to avoid — though this comparison is a documented structural-behavior pattern, not a claim that concrete/masonry construction is immune to total loss in a sufficiently severe event.

**83. What ham radio license class, equipment, and antenna installation gives this specific rural property a realistic communication range to the nearest emergency-services repeater network and to family outside the region?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** License class: a Technician-class FCC amateur license (the entry-level class) already grants full transmitting privileges on the VHF and UHF bands most commonly used for local, repeater-based, and emergency communications, which is the practical minimum for reaching a local emergency-services repeater network; a General-class license (built on top of Technician) additionally opens the HF bands, which is what actually enables long-distance, out-of-region communication with family rather than only local/regional VHF/UHF range (https://www.hamradioschool.com/post/tech-general-or-extra-license-what-s-the-difference). Range reality for VHF/UHF: signals do not typically travel beyond the radio horizon (limited by terrain and earth curvature) on their own, but repeater stations sited on towers, hills, or mountains retransmit at higher effective power and from a much higher vantage point, extending real usable range substantially — rural areas are specifically noted to have more 2-meter repeaters than dense urban areas precisely because rural terrain rewards this extended-horizon effect (https://hidarg.org/index.php/8-public-content/18-what-is-ham-radio). Oregon has active, documented wide-area repeater infrastructure: the Amateur Radio Relay Group (ARRG) operates the K7RPT statewide repeater system (18-plus VHF/UHF repeaters), with confirmed coverage into Central Oregon towns (Madras, Redmond, Prineville, Bend, Sisters) and along Highways 26, 97, and 126 (https://www.arrg.org/) — this is a real, existing network a rural Central Oregon property could plausibly reach, though coverage for any *other* specific candidate region of the state depends on that region's own local repeater infrastructure and terrain, which this pass could not verify in the abstract. County-level ARES (Amateur Radio Emergency Services) groups are the documented organizing structure connecting individual operators to actual emergency-services communication during a disaster (https://www.tualatincert.org/amateur-radio). For a specific property, the realistic communication range to both the nearest emergency-services repeater and to out-of-region family depends on the parcel's actual terrain, elevation, and line-of-sight to the nearest repeater site, and on antenna height/type at the house itself — all site-specific facts requiring an actual site survey (a signal-propagation check against the specific candidate parcel's terrain) once a site is chosen, not a generalizable answer.

**84. Does a satellite messenger or personal locator beacon make sense as the household's redundant secondary communication layer, and what ongoing subscription cost does that add against its value during a road-out, cell-tower-down scenario?**

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Both device categories are real and currently marketed, and they serve genuinely different roles rather than being interchangeable options. A **personal locator beacon (PLB)** operates on the internationally reserved 406 MHz distress frequency through the government-operated Cospas-Sarsat satellite search-and-rescue network, requires no subscription (a one-time device purchase plus free NOAA registration, https://beaconregistration.noaa.gov/), and is genuinely free to use in an actual emergency, but is one-way only: it sends a distress signal and GPS location to rescuers and cannot send or receive ordinary messages, confirm receipt (except on newer models with Return Link Service), or provide routine check-in communication. A **satellite messenger** (Garmin inReach, Zoleo, SPOT) is two-way, supports routine non-emergency text messaging and check-ins in addition to SOS alerting, and runs on the commercial Iridium (or equivalent) satellite network with true global coverage, but requires an ongoing subscription, commonly $15-65/month depending on plan and message volume, with some providers (Garmin) offering a pause option between periods of active use (https://gearjunkie.com/technology/best-satellite-messengers; https://www.saltandprepper.com/learn/communications/satellite-communicators). Both devices are specifically designed to keep working when cell towers and terrestrial infrastructure fail, which is documented in the same source material as a predictable failure pattern worth planning around: cell networks are noted to "fail early, fail in the same geographic areas most affected by the emergency, and fail when you most need them" (https://readyguidance.com/gear/best-satellite-communicators-emergency/). For this project's stated redundant-secondary-layer framing (alongside the ham-radio primary layer of item 83), a two-way satellite messenger is the better fit specifically because it supports the routine check-in and non-emergency coordination a household drill (item 85) and an actual road-out event would realistically need, not only a last-resort distress signal, which is what a PLB alone provides; a PLB remains a reasonable, subscription-free supplement for a household member who leaves the property alone (hiking, working a remote section of land) rather than a replacement for the messenger's routine two-way capability at the house itself.

**87. What insurance disclosure or hazard-zone notification obligations does Oregon law place on an owner-builder at permit application or at eventual resale for a parcel in a mapped seismic, tsunami, landslide, or wildfire hazard zone, and does meeting or exceeding IBHS/FORTIFIED-class voluntary standards change any disclosure obligation or liability exposure?**

The bill also requires at least 24 months for an insured to repair, rebuild, or replace property damaged by a fire subject to an Emergency Conflagration Act order, and mandates 30-60 days' advance written notice before any wildfire-related non-renewal or premium increase. Separately, and already established in items 21-22 above, IBHS's Wildfire Prepared Home designation (Essential and Enhanced levels) is a confirmed, currently-available, Oregon-eligible third-party certification with named participating carriers (Mercury, State Farm, CSAA, USAA, Allstate, Farmers) and documented discounts commonly cited in the 10-20 percent range, alongside general Bend-area reports of 10-15 percent reductions tied to Firewise-standard compliance with documented defensible space and a 2-5 year payback period on mitigation cost (https://wildfireprepared.org/incentives/; https://insurepacific.com/blog/wildfire-mitigation-save-money-oregon-homeowners). At the underwriting-criteria level specifically, insurers and their agents are documented to look for concrete, physically verifiable features rather than a jurisdiction's code-minimum status alone: noncombustible ground cover within the 0-5 ft Zone 0 (river rock, gravel, paver stones, or concrete, not bark mulch or vegetation), ember-resistant venting (1/8-in. metal mesh or better, per items 26/29 above), and clean roofs and gutters free of needle/leaf litter (https://insurepacific.com/blog/wildfire-mitigation-save-money-oregon-homeowners).
