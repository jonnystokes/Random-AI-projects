## Sequencing Principles for a Solo Build

**1.** Does a construction-trade-specific learning curve (Wright's Law-style percentage improvement per doubling of repetitions) exist for masonry unit-laying, and if so what rate, so the manuscript can replace or refine its existing flat 1.5x first-attempt labor multiplier with a real curve for repeated tasks?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** The general Wright's Law/learning-curve model is well established (time or cost per unit falls a consistent percentage each time cumulative output doubles; Wright first described this in 1936 for airplane manufacturing at roughly a 20 percent drop per doubling, i.e., an "80 percent curve") but the construction-specific evidence for masonry/blockwork specifically is mixed, not a single clean rate. Two peer-reviewed studies give opposite answers: Ugulu and Allen (2018), observing seven blockwork craft gangs on a 17-storey tri-tower project in Nigeria, found "an average learning rate of 94.21 per cent resulting in 5.79 per cent improvement gains" for repetitive blockwork labor (Ugulu & Allen, "Using the learning curve theory in the investigation of on-site craft gangs' blockwork construction productivity," *Built Environment Project and Asset Management* 8(3), 2018, https://doi.org/10.1108/BEPAM-09-2017-0067) — a real but much shallower improvement curve than manufacturing's classic 80 percent rate. Jarkas (2016), analyzing repetitive blockwork data from 52 multi-storey residential buildings, reached the opposite conclusion: "the patterns emerged from this study provide little evidence" that learning curve theory usefully predicts blockwork productivity, and most buildings observed showed "either an increase or a negligible reduction in labor inputs" across recurring floor cycles (Jarkas, "Learning effect on labour productivity of repetitive concrete masonry blockwork: Fact or fable?," *International Journal of Productivity and Performance Management* 65(8), 2016, https://doi.org/10.1108/IJPPM-08-2015-0119). Both studies concern multi-crew, multi-storey commercial blockwork with trade disruptions between floors (a materially different situation from one owner repeating the same unit-laying motion daily on a single-story house), so neither transfers directly. The honest, non-invented replacement for the manuscript's flat 1.5x multiplier is: expect some real per-repetition improvement (the Ugulu/Allen figure, ~6 percent cumulative gain per doubling, is a defensible floor), but do not assume a manufacturing-grade 80 percent curve, and use the build's own logged hours (already committed to in the existing chapter's "build's own labor database," section 17.13) to fit an actual curve from the front half of the masonry phase rather than import an unverified external rate (see also item 77, which addresses the data-format question this implies).

**Lands in:** Build Sequence and Site Coordination (Sequencing Principles).
**SETTLED** for the existence and range of construction-specific learning-curve findings (both studies real, both directly cited); **NOT-RESEARCHABLE-PRO** for a single defensible replacement rate for THIS project's owner-made-block and masonry-laying tasks specifically, which the conflicting evidence shows must come from the owner's own logged first-half-of-phase hours, not a borrowed literature figure.

**2.** What lift height and inter-lift timing window (from real ACI 305.1/306R-compliant practice) lets a 3-4 person crew pour a residential foundation or basement wall in lifts without risking a cold joint, and how does that get scheduled against the concrete truck's paid wait-time clock?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** ICF-industry placement guidance (a directly transferable source for lift-based wall pours generally, since the placement mechanics are the same regardless of the permanent form) confirms a lift height of **3 to 4 feet**, placed continuously around the wall's full perimeter before returning to add the next lift, with the internal vibrator following the concrete placement immediately so each lift is consolidated into the one below it while both are still plastic (ICF Builder Magazine, "Timing For Concrete Placement," https://icfmag.com/2023/07/timing-for-concrete-placement/). The same source states a below-grade wall must be placed as one continuous pour with no cold joint at all (backfill lateral pressure makes a cold joint structurally unacceptable there), while an above-grade wall can tolerate a cold joint only at a planned break point (commonly the top of a story). Crew size for this continuous-lift method is confirmed at a **recommended minimum of four people**: one person placing concrete, one following with the internal vibrator, and one or two monitoring the wall/forms from the ground for blowouts or bulging (same source); a related source describes a fuller crew of five to six (three on the scaffold, one on the hose, two on the vibrator) for a more comfortable pace (Amvic ICF US Design Guide, https://alleguard.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Alleguard-ICF-US-Design-Guide.pdf). Scheduling against the truck's paid clock (item 18 below has the fee figures): the lift-and-return method is inherently slower per cubic yard placed than a single unrestricted pour, because the crew works the whole perimeter at each lift rather than filling one section to full height and moving on, so the pre-pour walk-through and crew-readiness discipline (existing chapter, section 17.9's Applied block) is what keeps this method from running into wait-time charges, not a change to the truck's own delivery rate.

**Lands in:** Build Sequence and Site Coordination (Sequencing Principles; cross-ref existing chapter section 17.9, which already cites ACI 305.1/306R for temperature control but did not previously state the 3-4 foot lift figure or the four-person crew minimum).

**3.** Working backward from the date the mason needs the first cured, lab-passed pallet of owner-made block, how many weeks of lead time does the block-production campaign need, and what buffer protects the wall schedule if a lot fails testing and has to be remade?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** No single source gives a complete worked lead-time schedule for this exact case, so this answer states the components the owner must chain together rather than inventing a total figure. From the owner-made-block research (`ch12_blocks_part1.md`): units are demolded immediately (no lead time there) but need a **moist-cure window commonly referenced as the first 3-7 days** before they gain enough strength to be stacked and handled without damage (item 65, citing the same dry-cast/CEB curing mechanism as item 4), and the **standard 28-day ASTM C39/C140 benchmark** is the design/test-reporting age for full compressive strength, even though units are commonly laid well before that date once a project-specific earlier-strength threshold is confirmed (item 67). Durability qualification, if the project runs ASTM D559 (wetting-drying) or D560 (freezing-thawing) cylinders rather than relying on compressive strength alone, adds roughly a month per test program, per PCA guidance (item 10, `ch12_blocks_part1.md`, citing https://matestlabs.com/test-standards/astm-d559/). Layered onto this: the R104.11 alternative-materials approval process itself (compression-test data reviewed by the local building official before the material may be used structurally) has no published, dated turnaround figure — BCD's own Alternate Method process page states no processing-time commitment (Oregon BCD, https://www.oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/pages/alternate-method-process.aspx), which `ch12_blocks_part2.md` item 115 already flags as NOT-RESEARCHABLE-AHJ. Chaining these settled pieces: the campaign's lead time is at minimum (days to produce the pallet's unit count at the owner's actual achievable rate, per items 5-14 below) **plus** the 3-7 day stacking cure **plus** the 28-day design-strength cure before the units the mason actually lays are provably at spec **plus** whatever calendar time the specific building official's R104.11 review takes (a local, un-datable variable). The buffer for a failed lot is not a percentage found in any source; it is the same chain run twice (remake, re-cure, re-test), so the practical planning rule is to start the pallet the mason needs first far enough ahead that a full 28-day-plus-test-turnaround remake cycle still lands before the wall-start date, not to assume a shorter buffer will absorb a failure.

**4.** What does the UK self-build "critical path" concept, and the Australian "lock-up" milestone concept, add to how this manuscript should name and track the true minimum-duration chain of this specific house's build, distinct from parallel work that does not gate anything downstream?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Both concepts are real, well-documented UK/Australian construction-industry terms, not folklore, and both add a naming precision the existing chapter's dependency-map language (section 17.1) does not currently state explicitly. **Critical path** (UK self-build press): "the critical path is made up of the tasks that can only be completed in sequence, the total duration of which will define how long the project will take to build. The critical path excludes any stages that can be undertaken simultaneously" (Homebuilding & Renovating, "A Typical Self Build Schedule," https://www.homebuilding.co.uk/advice/self-build-schedule; the same source notes masonry/block construction specifically sits ON the critical path in a way a timber frame or SIP superstructure would not, because "the superstructure has to be built on site, brick by brick, and must be fully completed before any internal works can commence" — directly relevant to this project's concrete-and-masonry-heavy scope, where the wall system genuinely gates every downstream trade rather than merely preceding it in a flexible schedule). **Lock-up** (Australian owner-builder press): the point at which "the external structure is complete enough to be physically secured... usually once the walls, roof, windows and external doors are installed," at which point the building is commonly described as roughly 80 percent complete on the outside and "the property is enclosed and safe from the elements, pests, and theft" even though the interior is roughly half-finished (Build.com.au, "What is the 'lockup stage'?," https://build.com.au/what-lockup-stage/; ASBIR, "Lock Up Stage: What It Means and Its Importance," https://www.asbir.com.au/lock-up-stage/). The existing chapter already names the same milestone functionally (its "dry-in" phase, section 17.10) but does not currently name it as a distinct, trackable, security-relevant event the way Australian practice does. The concrete addition for this manuscript: (a) explicitly label the concrete-and-masonry wall system as this project's critical path (not merely "the structure phase"), since unlike a timber-frame alternative it cannot be parallelized with other trades; and (b) adopt "lock-up" as this manuscript's named term for the dry-in milestone specifically because it carries the security and insurance implication (item 68/79 in the existing chapter's storage-and-security discussion) that "dry-in" alone does not evoke.

**7.** How should the production slab/pad be designed for a home block yard (size, flatness, especially for an egg-layer, surface hardness, and drainage), and how does it differ if using a stationary press versus a mobile machine?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** `ch12_blocks_part1.md` item 58 (egg-layer machines) directly answers the flatness/surface question: an egg-layer casts blocks directly onto the ground rather than onto pallets, and documented ground-preparation requirements are that the site be "neat and flat" because the machine's wheels (most models three-wheeled) cannot tolerate bumpy ground; either a well-compacted earthen surface or a roughly 5 cm (2 in.) thick cement/concrete slab is documented as acceptable — a full engineered slab is not strictly required (brickmachinesupplier.com, https://brickmachinesupplier.com/brick-laying-machine/). This is the key difference from a stationary press: a stationary machine casts onto pallets that are then moved to a separate curing area, so the pad under the press itself only needs to support the machine and operator, while the curing/drying yard beyond it needs to be flat and well-drained for pallet or ground-stacking; an egg-layer, by contrast, needs its entire travel path to be uniformly flat and hard enough for the machine's own wheels for the full length of each casting row, since the units cure in place where they were cast rather than being relocated immediately. No source in this pass gave a specific drainage-slope figure (percent grade) for a block-curing yard specifically; ordinary construction-yard drainage practice (positive slope away from the curing area, no ponding) applies by extension but was not separately verified for this application. Documented egg-layer output figures (item 58: roughly 4,000-17,000 units/day across cited commercial models) are all well above this project's ~100-units/day target, meaning an egg-layer is likely over-scaled equipment for this project regardless of pad design.
