66. Does the National Wetlands Inventory's free mapping tool produce enough false negatives (real wetlands it misses) on typical Oregon rural parcels that a buyer should budget for a site-specific delineation as a routine step rather than only when the map shows something? [PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [TOOLS]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Yes, and Oregon's own mapping agency says so directly. The Department of State Lands (DSL), which maintains Oregon's Statewide Wetlands Inventory (SWI, built on the federal National Wetlands Inventory base plus state additions), states that NWI/SWI mapping in Oregon is incomplete: it under-represents "smaller, seasonal and forested wetlands that may not be detected on aerials," and by policy the NWI methodology excludes certain "farmed wetlands" as defined under the federal Food Security Act even though many of those areas meet wetland criteria on the ground. DSL's own limitations document states that wetland and water boundaries shown on the map are approximate and that "conducting on-the-ground wetland delineations is the only way to verify wetland boundary locations." DSL's guidance treats a clean (no-wetland) map result as a planning-level screen only, not a final answer, and directs users toward field verification regardless of what the map shows, precisely because a clean result can be a false negative on forested or seasonally wet ground, which describes most of the rural building-site terrain this project is likely to be sited on. A buyer should therefore budget for a qualified wetland delineation as a routine due-diligence line item on any candidate parcel with forest cover, seasonal wet areas, or drainage features, not only when the SWI/NWI map already shows a mapped wetland.
Sources: [DSL Statewide Wetlands Inventory](https://www.oregon.gov/dsl/ww/pages/swi.aspx); [DSL SWI Layer Limitations](https://www.oregon.gov/dsl/wetlands-waters/Documents/DSLSWILayerLimitationsFinal.pdf); [DSL Local Wetlands Inventories](https://www.oregon.gov/dsl/ww/Pages/Inventories.aspx).

71. For a forested parcel, what does a professional timber cruise cost and how is its result (board-foot volume and species-based stumpage value) used to negotiate the purchase price down by the value of timber the buyer does not intend to harvest, or up to reflect timber value the buyer does intend to harvest? [COST] [METHOD]
   Leads: Oregon Department of Forestry publishes quarterly stumpage price data (unverified, needs direct confirmation of current publication and access).

**Answered (2026-07-12):** A professional timber cruise (a field inventory estimating species mix, tree sizes, and merchantable volume) typically costs $8-$15 per acre from a consulting forester, with most consulting foresters setting a practical minimum charge of $300-$500 for small tracts under 30-40 acres; for larger properties the total cost commonly runs $300 to $2,000 or more depending on acreage, terrain, stand density, and road access (steep or dense ground costs more to cruise than flat, accessible ground). The cruise result (board-foot volume by species and diameter class) is combined with a current stumpage price (dollars per unit volume paid for standing timber) to produce a dollar estimate of standing timber value on the tract; the lead about Oregon Department of Forestry quarterly stumpage/pond-value price data is partially confirmed and partially outdated: ODF historically published price reports back to 1977, but as of the sources checked, ODF has stopped independently soliciting quarterly pond values from mills and now relies on a subscription log-price service for its own Timber Appraisal System, meaning a buyer's forester will typically need a paid stumpage-price subscription or a direct forester relationship rather than a free current ODF web table. On negotiation mechanics: no documented case study of a specific Oregon land purchase where a cruise-derived timber value was used to move the asking price was found in this pass; the general real-estate-forestry practice (not independently verified for Oregon specifically) is that a buyer who does not intend to harvest treats standing timber as a cost input to a below-asking counteroffer, while a buyer who does intend to harvest treats it as a near-term, largely liquid asset that can justify a higher offer or faster close since the timber value can partially fund the harvest-and-clear cost.
Sources: [OSU Extension, Planning a timber cruise and using it](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/forests/cutting-selling/planning-timber-cruise-using-it); [Century Forest Management, Timber Valuation, Cruising & Appraisal Services in Oregon](https://www.centuryforestmgmt.com/services/timber-valuation-cruising-appraisal); [US Forest Service, Oregon Timber Price Information](https://research.fs.usda.gov/srs/centers/fep/timberor); [Oregon Department of Forestry, Timber Sales](https://www.oregon.gov/ODF/Working/pages/TimberSales.aspx).

74. What is a realistic, sequenced due-diligence checklist and timeline (title, survey, septic evaluation, well/water-rights check, zoning confirmation letter, wetland screen, hazard-map screen) that fits inside a typical 30-60 day Oregon raw-land inspection period without cutting corners? [METHOD]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Combining the individually sourced steps found across this research pass and the earlier-numbered items in this chapter, a realistic sequence that fails fast on dealbreakers before spending money on paid reports is: (1) Day 1-3, immediately upon going under contract: order the preliminary title report from a title company (cheap/free, fast) and pull the free public-tool screens in parallel — county GIS/assessor RMV and zoning class, OWRD well-log and water-rights/critical-groundwater-area search, DOGAMI SLIDO landslide layer, FEMA flood-zone lookup, National Wetlands Inventory/Statewide Wetlands Inventory map, and DLCD's Measure 49 analyzer if the parcel's history suggests a claim. (2) Day 3-10: request a zoning confirmation letter from the county planning department (often a paid, multi-week-turnaround item, so start it early) and schedule the septic site/perc evaluation with the county's onsite-wastewater program, since this is typically the single longest-lead paid item and gates the whole feasibility question (see item 1, elsewhere in this chapter). (3) Day 7-20: order the boundary survey if the parcel's legal description or access is at all ambiguous, and commission a wetland delineation if the NWI/SWI screen or a site walk shows any forested, seasonal-wet, or drainage-feature ground (see item 66 above; do not rely on a clean map result alone). (4) Day 15-30: get well drawdown/yield test results if an existing well is on the parcel, or a driller's professional opinion on likely yield if drilling new; follow up any DOGAMI or FEMA flag with the recommended professional consultation. (5) Day 30-45+: finalize based on all returned reports, renegotiate or exercise walk-away rights per the contract's contingency deadlines before the inspection period closes. This sequencing puts the free/fast screens first (to kill obviously bad parcels at zero cost) and the slow/paid items (septic evaluation, zoning letter, survey) started immediately in parallel rather than sequentially, which is what makes a 30-60 day window workable; a shorter 30-day window is workable only if the septic evaluation and zoning letter are ordered on day one.

76. What happens under Oregon contract law if a septic or well test result comes back marginal rather than clearly pass or fail, inside a contingency period, and the buyer wants to renegotiate price instead of walking away entirely; is a price-renegotiation right something that has to be written into the contingency clause explicitly, or is it available by default? [CODE] [METHOD]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** No specific Oregon statute or reported case addressing a "marginal" septic/well result under a contingency clause was found; this is a contract-drafting question governed by ordinary Oregon contract law rather than a specific statutory rule. Under general contract principles, a due-diligence or inspection contingency in Oregon gives the buyer only the rights the contract's own text grants: typically the right to terminate and receive earnest money back if the contingency is not satisfied by its deadline. Oregon contract law does not supply a default, implied right to instead demand a price reduction; if the parties want price-renegotiation (rather than only terminate-or-proceed) to be an available buyer option on a marginal result, that right must be drafted into the contingency clause explicitly (for example, language allowing the buyer to submit a written request for a price credit or seller-funded remediation within a stated number of days, with a further walk-away right if the seller declines). Absent that language, a buyer facing a marginal result has, by default, only the binary choice the contract's contingency clause actually states (typically: waive the contingency and proceed at the agreed price, or terminate under it), and any price renegotiation the buyer attempts is an out-of-contract request the seller is free to refuse.
Sources: general Oregon contract law principle; no Oregon-specific statute or case on point was located in this pass. Cross-reference: [Fynk, Due Diligence Contingency Explained](https://fynk.com/en/clauses/due-diligence-contingency/) for the general structure of due-diligence contingencies.
NOT-RESEARCHABLE-PRO (have an Oregon real estate attorney draft explicit price-renegotiation contingency language before relying on it; do not assume it is available by default)

77. What specific terms should a buyer insist on in an Oregon land sale contract beyond price, rate, and down payment (a due-on-sale clause, a right to pay off early without penalty, escrow of taxes and insurance, a specific default-cure procedure referencing ORS 93.910/93.915) to avoid a bad-faith forfeiture attempt later? [CODE] [METHOD]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** ORS 93.910 and ORS 93.915 are real, current Oregon statutes and directly control the default-and-forfeiture procedure on any Oregon land sale contract (contract for deed), regardless of what the contract itself says. ORS 93.910 provides that whenever a land sale contract has a forfeiture remedy (self-executing or optional), the seller may enforce forfeiture of a defaulting buyer's interest ONLY after giving the statutory notice of default required by ORS 93.915, "notwithstanding any provision in the contract to the contrary" — meaning a seller cannot contract around this protection. ORS 93.915 requires the seller to give written notice of default, served under Oregon Rule of Civil Procedure 7 D(2)/7 D(3) or by both first-class and certified mail return-receipt, to the buyer, to any occupant of the property, and to any person who has recorded a request for notice with the county clerk; the buyer then generally has a statutory right-to-cure period (commonly described as 60-120 days depending on how much of the purchase price has already been paid) before forfeiture can actually be completed, and during forfeiture proceedings a court may appoint a receiver or issue a restraining order, except that a receiver may NOT be appointed over a single-family residence occupied as the principal residence of the purchaser, spouse, or minor dependent children at the time notice is given. Beyond ensuring the contract does not attempt to waive these statutory protections (which would be void under ORS 93.910's own text), a buyer should insist on: an explicit due-on-sale disclosure confirming the seller has no underlying mortgage that a land sale contract would trigger into default (see item 80 below), a written right to prepay principal at any time without penalty, escrow or a clear, contract-stated procedure for who pays property taxes and insurance during the contract term (a common forfeiture trigger is unpaid taxes, not just missed payments), and a default-cure procedure that expressly cross-references and does not attempt to shorten the ORS 93.915 notice-and-cure timeline.
Sources: [ORS 93.910, Enforcement of forfeiture remedy after notice of default](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_93.910); [ORS 93.915, Notice of default; contents; recordation; time of forfeiture; interim measures](https://law.justia.com/codes/oregon/volume-03/chapter-093/section-93-915/); [Oregon Revised Statutes Chapter 93](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors093.html).

78. Does an Oregon land sale contract (contract for deed) create any complication for later getting a construction loan or a permanent mortgage, given that the seller retains legal title until the contract is paid off? [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Yes, and this is a well-documented structural issue, not a minor technicality. Under an Oregon land sale contract, no deed passes from seller to buyer at closing; the seller retains legal title while the buyer holds only equitable title, with a "Fulfillment Deed" conveyed and recorded only once the contract is paid in full. A construction lender or permanent mortgage lender needs to secure its loan with a recorded deed of trust or mortgage against the buyer's own legal title to the real property; a buyer who holds only equitable title under an unpaid land sale contract typically cannot grant that clean, first-position security interest, which materially complicates or blocks getting a construction loan while the land contract is still outstanding. In practice, this means a buyer intending to build should expect to need the land sale contract paid off (or restructured, e.g., refinanced into a seller-carried trust deed/note instead of a land sale contract, which does convey title immediately) before a construction lender will fund, or should negotiate the underlying land purchase using a trust deed/promissory note structure from the outset rather than a land sale contract if construction financing is a near-term goal.
Sources: [Querin Law, Seller-Carried Trust Deeds vs. Land Sale Contracts: Which to Use & When?](https://q-law.com/seller-carried-trust-deeds-vs-land-sale-contracts-when/); [Querin Law, Mortgages, Trust Deeds, Land Sale Contracts, and Their Foreclosure](https://q-law.com/mortgages-trust-deeds-land-sale-contracts-foreclosure/).

What is the practical process and cost difference between a property-line adjustment (for combining or reshaping adjoining parcels) and a full partition, in a specific target county, and when does a county require the more expensive partition process even though no new parcel is being created? [METHOD] [COST]

94. What exact current statutory language, template dimensions, and productivity-tier lot/dwelling-count thresholds govern the small-tract (template) forestland dwelling path under ORS 215.750, confirmed against current text? [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Confirmed directly from current ORS text. ORS 215.750 allows a dwelling on a forest-zoned lot or parcel that is too small to qualify under the large-tract path (ORS 215.740) if the surrounding area is already developed at a minimum density, tiered by soil productivity and by eastern versus western Oregon. In WESTERN Oregon (subsection 2): a parcel with soils capable of 0-49 cubic feet/acre/year of wood fiber qualifies if at least three OTHER lots or parcels that existed on January 1, 1993 are within a 160-acre square centered on the subject tract's centroid, AND at least three dwellings existed on those other lots/parcels as of that date; the "at least 7 other lots/parcels" tier applies for 50-85 cubic feet/acre/year soils; the "at least 11 other lots/parcels" tier applies for soils producing MORE than 85 cubic feet/acre/year (each tier still requiring at least three existing dwellings on the qualifying lots). In EASTERN Oregon (subsection 3), the same three-tier, three-dwelling structure applies but at lower productivity breakpoints: 0-20, 21-50, and more-than-50 cubic feet/acre/year, with the same 3/7/11-other-lots tiers respectively. The template area itself is a "160-acre square centered on the center of the subject tract" (subsection 2/3 opening text), where "center of the subject tract" is defined in subsection (1) as the tract's mathematical centroid; subsection (6) allows substituting a "160-acre rectangle that is one mile long and one-fourth mile wide," aligned to the maximum extent possible with an existing (pre-January-1-1993) road, where the tract abuts such a road, and subsection (7) provides a further variant for tracts 60+ acres abutting a road or perennial stream. Subsection (5) layers additional conditions onto any qualifying dwelling: no dwellings may exist on other lots/parcels making up the tract and subject to ORS 215.740 deed restrictions (5)(c); the tract must not already include a dwelling (5)(d); the specific lot/parcel must have been lawfully established (5)(e); any property line adjustment to the lot/parcel must have complied with ORS 92.192 (5)(f); and — the provision directly relevant to item 97 below — any property line adjustment to the lot/parcel AFTER January 1, 2019 must NOT have had the effect of qualifying the lot/parcel for a dwelling under this section (5)(g), with a related bar in (5)(h) on tracts where no dwelling existed as of January 1, 2019.
Sources: [ORS 215.750, Alternative forestland dwelling; criteria, via revision6_code_library/ors/ors215.html (Oregon Legislature official chapter HTML)](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors215.html).

96. What is the practical process to draw and verify the ORS 215.750 template (the 160-acre square or 1-mile-by-quarter-mile rectangle) against actual county parcel and dwelling records for a specific candidate tract, and can a buyer do this before an offer using public GIS data alone? [METHOD] [TOOLS]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The practical process is: locate the subject tract's mathematical centroid (its "center" as defined in ORS 215.750(1)); construct either the 160-acre square centered on that point, or, if the tract abuts a pre-January-1-1993 road, the alternative 160-acre-equivalent rectangle (one mile long by one-quarter mile wide, aligned to the maximum extent possible with that road) per subsection (6); then, using county assessor parcel data and building/dwelling records (available through most county GIS portals and the assessor's property database), count how many OTHER lots or parcels that existed on January 1, 1993 fall wholly or partly within that shape, and how many dwellings existed on those other lots/parcels as of that same date, checking the count against the productivity-tiered threshold that applies to the tract's soil class (see item 94 above: 3/7/11 other lots and at least 3 dwellings, tiered by cubic-feet-per-acre-per-year productivity and by eastern/western Oregon). A buyer CAN attempt this before an offer using public county GIS parcel data and historical aerial imagery/assessor records alone, since none of the inputs (parcel boundaries, approximate dwelling locations, the 1993 date cutoff) legally require a county-issued determination to calculate informally. However, this is a real litigation-tested area, not a mechanical formality: LUBA has directly overturned county decisions for failing to properly explain the centroid/template calculation (Eng v. Wallowa County, 79 Or LUBA 421 (2019), where the county erred by not explaining its basis for establishing the tract's center point), and has drawn a hard technical line that only LAWFULLY created lots count toward the "other lots" total, even though neither ORS 215.750(1) nor the general ORS 92.010 lot/parcel definitions say so explicitly on their face (Reeves v. Yamhill County, 53 Or LUBA 4 (2006), and its companion holding that a county cannot use its own, looser local definition of "lot" to recognize lots ORS 215.750 itself would not recognize). This means a buyer's own pre-offer GIS-based template count is a genuinely useful screening exercise, but is not a substitute for the county's own formal template-dwelling application review, where lawful-creation status of each counted "other lot," the exact centroid methodology, and the 1993 dwelling-existence proof can all become contested, appealable issues.
Sources: [ORS 215.750, via revision6_code_library/ors/ors215.html]; [LUBA Headnote 8.8, Forest Dwellings, ORS 215.750 and OAR 660-006](https://www.oregon.gov/luba/docs/headnotes/8.8.pdf); [LUBA Headnote 33.2, Land Divisions – Partitions](https://www.oregon.gov/luba/docs/headnotes/33.2.pdf) (Reeves v. Yamhill County, 53 Or LUBA 4 (2006)); Eng v. Wallowa County, 79 Or LUBA 421 (2019) (LUBA opinion, [oregon.gov/LUBA/docs/Opinions/2019/02-19/18077.pdf](https://www.oregon.gov/LUBA/docs/Opinions/2019/02-19/18077.pdf)).

102. What is the exact current completion deadline a subsequent (non-original-claimant) owner faces after acquiring a Measure 49 home-site authorization, and from what triggering date does that clock start? [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Confirmed directly from DLCD's own official Measure 49 FAQ document (fetched and read in full). There is NO time limit on when the ORIGINAL claimant may carry out the development of the property; the 10-year clock applies only to a SUBSEQUENT owner. Specifically: "After receiving an approval, a claimant is free to pass the property to a subsequent owner who must complete the development within 10 years." If the property passes through multiple subsequent owners, there is no limit on the number of transfers, and "[t]he 10-year time limit begins with the first owner subsequent to the claimant" — it does not restart with each new transfer. For Measure 49 purposes, "owner" is specifically defined to mean: (a) the holder of fee title per the county deed records; (b) the purchaser under a recorded, in-force land sale contract; or (c) if the property is held by a revocable trust, the settlor of that trust (only the trustee becomes the "owner" once the trust becomes irrevocable). Special triggering-date rules apply around trusts and the claimant's death: transferring the property into a revocable trust with the claimant as settlor does NOT start the 10-year clock; transfer to the claimant's spouse also does not trigger it; but once a revocable trust becomes irrevocable upon the claimant's death, the subsequent owner then has 10 years from that event to establish the parcels and dwellings. If the claimant dies on or after December 6, 2007 (the date Measure 49 took effect) and before DLCD's final order issues, the claim is processed to completion using the claimant's own facts, and the subsequent owner then has 10 years from the date the final order was issued. If the claimant dies AFTER the final order has already issued, the subsequent owner has 10 years from the date of the claimant's death. (If the claimant died BEFORE December 6, 2007, no subsequent owner receives the right to prosecute the claim at all.) The controlling law is ORS 195.300-336 and OAR 660-029 and 660-041; this 10-year subsequent-owner rule and its trigger-date variations are DLCD's own official, current guidance, not a secondary summary.
Sources: [DLCD, Measure 49 Frequently Asked Questions (official PDF, fetched and read in full 2026-07-13)](https://www.oregon.gov/lcd/Measure49/SupportingDocuments/M49_FAQ_2023.3.pdf), page 2-3 ("DOES MY MEASURE 49 APPROVAL EXPIRE? WHAT HAPPENS IF I TRANSFER OWNERSHIP?" and "May a claimant sell the property with the Measure 49 approval?" sections); [DLCD Measure 49 program page](https://www.oregon.gov/LCD/MEASURE49/pages/index.aspx).
