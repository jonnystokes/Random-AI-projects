**1. Flooring over a slab, moisture-vapor-emission failures. Why glue-down and rubber-backed floors blister and delaminate over concrete, the RH-probe / MVER testing that must precede installation, the under-slab vapor retarder as the real prevention, and topical moisture-mitigation coatings as the retrofit.**
Lands in: Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, and Livability

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Glue-down and rubber-backed flooring fails over concrete because residual and rising moisture vapor re-emulsifies water-based adhesives and softens rubber backing, causing blisters, bond loss, and delamination; the mechanism is moisture moving up through the slab by capillary action and vapor diffusion, not surface wetness the installer can see or feel. Two ASTM test methods quantify the risk before installation: ASTM F2170, in-situ relative-humidity probes inserted into holes drilled to 40 percent of slab depth (drying from one side) and equilibrated at least 24 hours before reading; and ASTM F1869, the calcium-chloride moisture-vapor-emission-rate (MVER) test, a sealed dish left on the slab for 60-72 hours and weighed for water gain ([IFTI, "ASTM F2170 vs F1869: Which Concrete Moisture Test Is Right?"](https://ifti.com/astm-f2170-vs-f1869-choosing-the-right-concrete-moisture-test-method-2/)). ASTM F710, the standard practice for preparing concrete floors to receive resilient flooring, sets the default acceptance ceiling most manufacturers reference: MVER not to exceed 3 lb/1,000 sq ft/24 hr per F1869, and slab internal RH not to exceed 75 percent per F2170, "unless otherwise specified by the flooring or adhesive manufacturer" ([IFTI, "ASTM Standards Every Designer Should Know for Flooring Systems"](https://ifti.com/astm-standards-every-designer-should-know-for-flooring-systems/)); some manufacturers publish looser thresholds (one cited example allows up to 6 lb MVER at 75 percent RH), so the specific flooring and adhesive manufacturer's own published limit is the controlling number, not a generic rule. Because these are manufacturer/warranty requirements rather than an ORSC-mandated test, skipping them voids the flooring warranty and is the single most common cause of documented resilient-flooring failures, not a code violation as such.

The real prevention is upstream of any test: a continuous under-slab vapor retarder (a polyethylene sheet placed under the slab before the pour, per ASTM E1745 practice) blocks ground moisture from ever entering the concrete, so the slab dries to an acceptable RH/MVER level and stays there. A topical moisture-mitigation coating (a two-component epoxy or urethane vapor-reduction coating, e.g. LATICRETE VAPOR BAN E, applied directly to the slab surface before underlayment or flooring) is the retrofit for a slab that tests high after the fact, or where no under-slab retarder was installed: it seals the top of the existing slab and lets flooring installation proceed despite an out-of-spec RH/MVER reading, at the cost of an added labor and material step and its own cure time before flooring goes down ([LATICRETE, VAPOR BAN E](https://www.laticrete.com/products/vapor-ban-e); [Polyguard, "Preventing Water & Vapor Transmission in Concrete Slabs"](https://polyguard.com/blog/moisture-through-concrete-slab/)). For this project, specifying the under-slab vapor retarder at pour time (a Structural System / Foundation-chapter decision) is the low-cost fix; the topical coating is the fallback for any area where that step was missed or where post-occupancy testing surprises the builder.

Lands in: Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, and Livability

Saw-cut control joints in a slab-on-grade are cut as soon as the concrete can support the saw without raveling and before uncontrolled shrinkage cracking begins, typically within 4-12 hours of finishing for early-entry dry-cut saws and up to 24 hours for conventional wet-cut saws; joint spacing is a function of slab thickness (a common rule of thumb is spacing in feet no more than 2 to 3 times the slab thickness in inches, though the project's structural engineer sets the actual spacing for this specific slab design, not a generic rule). Stain/dye color is a design choice covered under Aesthetics; sealing and maintenance costs are answered in items 12 and 95. The radiant-tubing coordination question is the genuinely distinct piece: any saw-cut or grinding/polishing pass must stay above the top of the embedded tubing with a documented safety margin, which means the radiant loop's burial depth (set in the HVAC/radiant-floor design) and the finish grind depth (typically 1/16 to 1/8 inch of material removed per polishing pass, several passes deep) must be reconciled on the shop drawings before the slab is poured, not decided at finishing time; no source in this pass gives a single universal minimum cover number for this specific project, because it depends on the tubing manufacturer's own minimum-cover specification and the polishing contractor's planned cut depth, both of which should be confirmed against each other before the pour, not assumed.

**3. Curbless / wet-room showers with a slab, waterproofing failure points. Why tile and grout are not the waterproofing, pre-slope plus bonded/sheet membrane vs surface tile, single-plane linear-drain slope, the bonding-flange drain connection, and recessing the slab for a flush entry, decided before the pour.**
Lands in: Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, and Livability / Plumbing: DWV, Sewage, Venting, Slope, Distance, and Fixtures

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Tile and grout are both water-permeable; grout in particular absorbs and passes water at every joint, so the actual waterproof layer must sit beneath the tile, not be the tile itself. A curbless shower needs two slopes built in sequence: a structural pre-slope (typically 1/4 inch per foot minimum) poured or mortared under the waterproofing membrane so that any water that gets past the tile still drains toward the linear drain, and then the finish mortar bed/tile itself sloped the same direction on top of the membrane. The membrane is either a fully bonded sheet membrane or a liquid/bonded waterproofing membrane applied over the pre-slope and turned up the curb-less threshold and side walls, with all seams, inside corners, and the drain connection detailed as the documented failure points; an un-detailed corner, seam, or drain connection — not the field of tile — is where curbless showers actually leak. The linear drain uses a bonding-flange connection: the drain body's flange is mechanically bonded/clamped to the waterproofing membrane so the membrane and drain form one continuous water-tight plane, rather than relying on caulk or sealant at the drain edge. For a curbless entry specifically, the slab must be recessed (stepped down) in the shower zone before the pour by an amount equal to the pre-slope mortar bed plus membrane plus finish tile thickness, so the finished shower floor and the adjacent room floor end up flush; this recess dimension has to be fixed on the structural/foundation drawings, because it cannot be added after the slab is poured ([Austin Tile Pro and craftingwithconcrete guidance, as cited in the question's own leads, describes this same sequence]). This item is substantially the same buildable detail asked for again in items 61 and 62 below (the zero-threshold entry and its interaction with the shower recess); see those items for the fully worked dimensional detail.

**4. Radon-resistant new construction, build the passive stack now, add a fan only if a test says so. Sub-slab clean aggregate plus gas-tight membrane plus a 3-inch-plus vent riser cast into the slab, sealed penetrations, Oregon's high-radon-area guidance, and the test-after-occupancy-then-activate logic.**
Lands in: Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, and Livability (cross-ref Build Sequence and Site Coordination)

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Oregon's radon-resistant-construction requirement is county-specific, not statewide: it is mandatory only in Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, and Yamhill counties, per Section AF101.1 of the ORSC's radon appendix, with the statutory basis at 2010 Oregon Laws Chapter 83 (Senate Bill 1025), Section 2, which also lets the state boards add counties after consulting the Oregon Health Authority ([OHA, Appendix F Radon Control Methods PDF](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/PH/HEALTHYENVIRONMENTS/HEALTHYNEIGHBORHOODS/RADONGAS/Documents/Appendix%20F_Radon%20Control%20Methods.pdf), full text read directly; [OHA Radon Mitigation page](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/PH/HEALTHYENVIRONMENTS/HEALTHYNEIGHBORHOODS/RADONGAS/Pages/Radon-Mitigation.aspx), same county list confirmed independently). Outside those seven counties the appendix is not mandatory, so the project's specific county must be checked against this list before deciding whether the passive system is a code requirement or a voluntary best practice (the done_researching_second_wave research on Appendix AT/AX preemption, item_16, confirms the current 2023 ORSC still carries this appendix, now lettered "AF" in the current code's appendix list, alongside AE, AH, AK, AR, AS, AU, AW — the manuscript should verify the current letter designation against the 2023 ORSC table of contents rather than the 2011-dated PDF text quoted here, which predates the appendix relettering).

The full, directly-read requirement set (AF103): a gas-permeable layer under the slab, either 4 inches minimum of clean aggregate passing a 2-inch sieve and retained on a 1/4-inch sieve, or 4 inches of sand overlain by geotextile drainage matting; a soil-gas-retarder of minimum 6-mil polyethylene (or 3-mil cross-laminated) placed on top of that layer before the pour, sheets lapped at least 12 inches and sealed at all punctures; a minimum 3-inch-diameter ABS, PVC, or equivalent gas-tight vent pipe embedded vertically into the sub-slab aggregate before the slab is cast, with a "T" fitting or equivalent to keep the pipe opening within the permeable layer, extended up through the building and terminating at least 12 inches above the roof surface, at least 10 feet from any window or opening less than 2 feet below the exhaust point; all floor penetrations, control/isolation/construction joints, and joints between slab and foundation wall sealed with polyurethane caulk or equivalent elastomeric sealant; and — the specific "add a fan later" provision the question describes — the vent pipe must be accessible for future fan installation through an attic or other non-habitable area (AF103.8), the pipe must be labeled "Radon Reduction System" on each floor and in accessible attics (AF103.9), and a dedicated electrical circuit terminating in an approved box must be installed during construction in the attic or other anticipated fan location specifically to power a future active system, with power also accessible at any anticipated alarm location (AF103.12) (all quotes and section numbers from the OHA Appendix F PDF, read directly, cited above). This is exactly the "build the passive stack now, wire for a fan, test after occupancy, activate only if needed" sequence the question describes, and it is the code text itself, not merely a strategy — worth noting for the manuscript that even outside the seven mandatory counties, adopting this appendix voluntarily costs little at construction time and is good practice statewide given radon's geological distribution is not confined to political county lines.

**7. Hanging things and building in on concrete/CMU walls. Fastening into concrete/masonry (mechanical/adhesive anchors, and embeds/blocking planned before the pour), plus designing cabinetry, TVs, and storage for walls you can't just drive a screw into.**
Lands in: Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, and Livability

**Answered (2026-07-14):** This item is the umbrella question for the entire anchor/embed cluster (items 20-35, 76-87) below; rather than duplicate, this answer states the organizing framework those items fill in with specifics. Two fundamentally different strategies exist for attaching anything to a finished concrete or CMU wall: post-installed anchors (mechanical anchors — wedge, sleeve, screw types — or adhesive/epoxy anchors, both driven or set after the wall exists, sized per manufacturer load tables for the specific substrate and load), and planned embeds (wood nailers, plywood inserts, steel plates, or unistrut/channel cast or built into the wall or CMU cells before it is finished, so a screw or bolt lands in ordinary wood or metal rather than requiring a masonry-rated anchor at all). **9. Non-structural seismic bracing beyond the code-required water heater strap: what does a full house-wide bracing pass look like (tall shelving, the pantry/food-storage racks, the mechanical-room equipment stack, the battery bank, kitchen cabinet latches) and which products/methods exist for a concrete/masonry house where most bracing anchors into concrete or CMU rather than wood stud framing?**
Lands in: Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, and Livability (cross-ref Resilience and Disaster Independence)

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Seismic bracing hardware is a mature commercial product category (Unistrut, TOLCO, and B-Line all make systems purpose-built to resist seismic forces, and Zip-Clip and Gripple make cable-based seismic bracing kits) and the anchors used to attach that bracing to a concrete or CMU wall or ceiling must specifically be anchors "prequalified for seismic applications" — rated for use in cracked concrete under seismic loading, not just static pull-out — with the actual design and anchor selection requiring a qualified structural engineer for anything beyond a simple strap ([BHB Engineers, "Seismic Bracing of Non-Structural Components"](https://www.bhbengineers.com/articles/seismic-bracing-of-non-structural-components)). For the water heater strap specifically, the same principle applies at small scale: where the strap anchors into concrete rather than wood framing, 1/4-inch expansion bolts (not lag screws) are the documented substitution ([JMC Inspections, water heater seismic bracing guide](https://jmcinspections.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/Water-Heater-Seismic-Braces.pdf); [California Seismic Safety Commission, "Guidelines for Earthquake Bracing of Residential Water Heaters"](https://www.ssc.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2020/08/waterheaterbracing_08-11-04.pdf)). For tall shelving, pantry racks, and mechanical/battery equipment stacks specifically, no single consolidated "house-wide bracing pass" checklist product or publication was found in this research pass; the documented pattern from the sources above is that each individual item (a tall shelf, an equipment rack, a battery enclosure) needs its own strap or bracket rated for the item's mass and anchored with a seismic-rated masonry anchor, sized by whoever specs that equipment (the HVAC/solar chapters for mechanical and battery equipment, this chapter for shelving and cabinetry), rather than a single generic bracing product covering the whole house. Kitchen cabinet latch hardware exists as an off-the-shelf child-safety-lock-style product repurposed for seismic use (widely sold, e.g. "earthquake cabinet latches"); no seismic-rating standard specific to cabinet latches was found, so this remains a common-sense retrofit item rather than a code-verified one. This item is correctly cross-referenced to Resilience and Disaster Independence, which should own the whole-house bracing checklist; this chapter's job is the anchor-into-masonry detail specifically.

**21. What is the practical, owner-buildable method for locating a solid bearing point (a face shell over a cross web, or a grouted cell) behind an already-finished CMU interior wall, without exploratory drilling?**
Lands in: Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, and Livability

**Answered (2026-07-14):** No single non-destructive tool reliably tells an owner-builder both "is this cell grouted" and "exactly where is the cross web" the way a wood stud finder locates a framing member; the documented methods split into three tiers. A basic magnetic or electronic stud finder ("the Zircon stud finder should be able to locate reinforcement but that is about all it can tell") can find embedded steel (rebar, ties) but not distinguish a grouted cell from an ungrouted one or locate a plain concrete cross web reliably ([Eng-Tips forum discussion, "Existing CMU – Determining Reinforcing"](https://www.eng-tips.com/threads/existing-cmu-determining-reinforcing.288283/)). The professional non-destructive method is infrared thermography: grouted cells have different thermal capacitance than air-filled cells, so they heat and cool at a different rate than the surrounding wall and become visible as a temperature-contrast pattern at certain times of day (cooler than the field in the morning, warmer in the evening) ([The Snell Group, "Verifying Grouted Cells in Concrete Masonry Walls"](https://www.thesnellgroup.com/featured-tips/verifying-grouted-cells-concrete-masonry-walls)); this is a hired-inspector service (an infrared thermographer with the right camera and timing), not an owner-buildable DIY method. The practical owner-buildable answer, and the one this chapter should actually recommend: item 22's fastening/embed map, recorded at construction time before the wall is finished, is the reliable non-destructive method, because it records the actual coursing, grouted-cell locations, and web positions while they are still visible, so no later detection method is needed at all. Where no map exists (an inherited or already-finished wall), a single small-diameter pilot hole drilled at the calculated coursing-module location (using the block's known nominal dimensions to predict web spacing) remains the fallback, minimally destructive method; a rebar/cover-meter scanner (item 31) can add confidence before drilling but does not eliminate the need for it on an unmapped wall.

**105. Extending the branch-circuit chapter's general dimmer/LED-compatibility note, which specific fixture types and drivers does this chapter's actual layered lighting plan need, and where do compatibility problems (flicker, minimum-load issues, buzzing) actually show up in real installations?**
Lands in: Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, and Livability (cross-ref Electrical: Branch Circuits, Wire Size, Voltage Drop, Lighting, Low Voltage, and Future Power)

**Answered (2026-07-14):** The documented root cause of nearly all real-world LED dimmer problems is a mismatch between dimmer switching technology and LED driver technology, not a defect in either component alone: "most homeowners run into trouble because they are using a 'Leading Edge' dimmer (designed for the 1970s) with 'Trailing Edge' bulbs (the 2020s)" — a traditional leading-edge (forward-phase, TRIAC-based) dimmer chops the front of the AC waveform in a way that works for old-style resistive incandescent loads but causes an LED driver's internal electronics to "misfire, resulting in buzzing from unstable current" ([Repenic, "Why Is My Dimmer Switch Buzzing or Flickering with LEDs?"](https://www.repenic.com/blogs/hot/why-is-my-dimmer-switch-buzzing-or-flickering-with-leds)). Flickering specifically arises from a minimum-load mismatch: LED fixtures draw far less power than the incandescent loads dimmers were originally designed around (often under 10 watts per fixture), so an older-technology dimmer "can't maintain a stable arc" at that low a load, and when the chopped waveform drops below the driver's own minimum operating voltage, "the power supply rapidly shuts down and restarts," which is perceived as a visible strobing flicker (same source). Buzzing specifically is described as "coil whine" — the LED driver's internal coils and capacitors vibrating audibly under the electrical stress of a leading-edge dimmer's sharp voltage-chopping waveform. The documented, real-world fix: pair modern LED drivers specifically with reverse-phase (electronic-low-voltage/ELV, trailing-edge) dimmers rather than legacy leading-edge TRIAC dimmers, and where flicker persists, adjust the dimmer's own low-end trim setting to raise the circuit's minimum voltage floor above the point where the driver cuts out ([same Repenic source]; consistent guidance at [Bees Lighting, "Why LED Light Bulbs Flicker & Buzz: Dimmer Compatibility Guide"](https://www.beeslighting.com/blogs/ideas-advice-blog/flickering-led-light-bulbs-dimmer-compatibility)). For this project's own layered lighting plan (item 98), this means Electrical's branch-circuit and dimmer specification should standardize on ELV/trailing-edge dimmers matched to whatever specific LED driver each fixture manufacturer specifies as compatible, checked fixture-by-fixture against the manufacturer's own published dimmer-compatibility list, rather than assuming any modern dimmer works with any LED fixture — a genuinely common, well-documented, and entirely avoidable real-installation problem.
