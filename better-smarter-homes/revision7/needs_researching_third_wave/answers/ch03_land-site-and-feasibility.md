1. Septic type selection on a hard site (Oregon DEQ terms). For high water table / shallow or slow-perc soil, walk the ladder: conventional gravity vs pressure-distribution vs sand filter vs ATU vs mound/capping fill, with siting, required reserve/replacement area, and the cost climb. What does the site/perc evaluation decide? [PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [COST] [CODE]
   Leads: Oregon DEQ onsite/subsurface wastewater program, soil and perc evaluation gates the whole feasibility question; reserve area is mandatory.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The controlling rule set is OAR chapter 340, division 71 (Onsite Wastewater Treatment Systems), administered by DEQ and, in most counties, delegated to the county health or community-development department. The site/soil evaluation (a licensed evaluator digs test pits and classifies soil texture, effective soil depth, depth to seasonal water table, and slope) decides two things: which system type the site can support, and how many linear feet of absorption trench (or equivalent) that system needs. The trench-length tables are explicit: OAR 340-071-0220 Table 4 sets minimum absorption-trench length per 150 gallons of daily flow by soil group (A/B/C) against effective soil depth (18-24 in.: 125/150/175 linear ft; 24-36 in.: 100/125/150; 36-48 in.: 75/100/125; 48 in. or more: 50/75/125), and Table 5 sets the same by depth to temporary (seasonal) groundwater (24-48 in.: 100/125/150; 48 in. or more: 50/75/125). A permanent water table within 4 feet of the absorption facility's bottom (24 inches in DEQ-designated areas with a supporting groundwater study) forces the system up the ladder.

The ladder itself, from least to most site-tolerant (and cheapest to most expensive), as reflected in DEQ's own construction-installation permit fee tiers (OAR 340-071-0140, Table 9B, current at the time this 2011-dated fee schedule was issued): standard subsurface absorption trenches, absorption trenches in saprolite, evapotranspiration-absorption, redundant systems, and steep-slope systems form the first (cheapest) tier; alternative treatment technologies, capping fill, pressurized distribution, and tile dewatering form the second tier; recirculating gravel filter and sand filter (residential or commercial) form the third (most expensive) tier. A high water table or slow/fast-perc soil pushes the design toward pressurized distribution (dosed, even distribution over the trench to compensate for uneven soil acceptance), then capping fill or mound systems (importing fill above the natural soil surface to gain separation from a high water table or shallow limiting layer), then a sand filter (an engineered filter bed with 2+ feet of sand media pretreating effluent before it reaches a smaller drainfield) or an ATU (aerobic treatment unit, a mechanical system that aerates effluent before it discharges to a smaller/simpler drainfield) for the most difficult sites.

A reserve (replacement) area equal to the primary system's footprint is a standard DEQ requirement so a failed system can be rebuilt without a new site evaluation; this project's site plan must reserve that second footprint from day one (see the site-plan-sequencing discussion at item 27 below).

Cost: DEQ's own 2011-dated fee schedule (the most recent one located in this pass; current 2026 fees are confirmed to be different, see below) shows a single-family first-lot site evaluation fee of $680, a site evaluation report review fee of $640, and construction-installation permit fees ranging from roughly $1,008 (standard subsurface, the cheapest tier) to roughly $1,235 (alternative treatment technologies/capping fill/pressurized distribution) to roughly $1,520-$1,808 (sand filter). These are DEQ permit fees only, not the installed system cost, which runs far higher and was not itemized in this pass (the Wave 3 seed material's $8,000-$15,000 standard-system and $20,000-$50,000 alternative-system figures, cited in the writing style guide's own Exemplar A, are the total-installed-cost figures to use in the manuscript). DEQ's own rulemaking page confirms onsite/septic fees are increasing 33% (a 3% base increase plus a 30% legislatively authorized recalibration) effective July 1, 2026, so the 2011 dollar figures above are directionally useful but not current; the manuscript entry should cite DEQ's current fee table (OAR 340-071-0140, or its successor after the July 2026 recalibration) rather than the 2011 figures.
   Source: OAR 340-071-0220 Table 4 and Table 5 (trench length by soil group and effective soil depth/water table depth), local file `revision6_code_library/os-Table1MinSepDistances.pdf` (downloaded DEQ document, dated 01-04-10, fee tables marked "Effective January 3, 2011"), read in full 2026-07-12; DEQ Onsite Wastewater Management Program overview, oregon.gov/deq/residential/pages/onsite.aspx; DEQ Water Quality Fees 2026 rulemaking page, oregon.gov/deq/rulemaking/pages/wqfees2026.aspx (confirms the 33% fee increase effective July 1, 2026), checked 2026-07-12.

2. Well siting and yield, the independence question at the source. Interpreting a drawdown/yield test, OWRD exempt-groundwater limits, required separations from septic/property lines/contamination sources, and the low-yield-well fix (large storage cistern + booster pump buffering a trickle well). [PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [CODE] [CREATIVE-IDEA]
   Leads: OWRD exempt-use groundwater framework; low-yield homesteads commonly solve gpm shortfalls with a cistern buffer feeding a pressure pump.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Every newly constructed well must be tested for yield and drawdown by bailing, pumping, or air testing for not less than one hour (Oregon well-construction standards, OAR chapter 690, division 210); a test that does not record drawdown during the test period is not considered a valid yield determination. The exempt-use ceiling most relevant to a single-family homestead is ORS 537.545(1)(d): "single or group domestic purposes in an amount not exceeding 15,000 gallons a day" requires no registration, permit, or water-right certificate (see item 13 below for the full current exempt-use list and its pending 2027 amendment).

Well-placement setback distances are set by OAR 690-210-0030 (Placement of Water Supply Wells): a well may not be constructed within 50 feet of any septic tank, within 100 feet of a septic drainline or sewage disposal structure, within 50 feet of a closed sewage or storm drainage system, within 100 feet of a sewage sludge disposal area, within 50 feet of a confined animal feeding or holding area or an animal-waste pond/lagoon, within 5 feet of a permanent structure or its roof/eaves/overhang, within 500 feet of a hazardous-waste storage/disposal/treatment facility (absent written OWRD director permission), within 25 feet of a residential petroleum storage tank, or within 50 feet of a commercial petroleum storage tank; a 20-foot separation from power poles is recommended (not required) for future equipment access. DEQ's septic-side Table 1 (OAR 340-071-0220) sets the mirror-image separation from the septic side: a well or other groundwater supply must be at least 100 feet from the subsurface absorption area (or 50 feet where OWRD has granted special well-construction standards) and at least 50 feet from the septic tank and other treatment units.

The low-yield-well fix: a storage cistern (commonly 300 to 5,000 gallons, with roughly 1,100 gallons cited by secondary industry sources as a lending-standard minimum for real-estate transactions) is filled slowly by the low-producing well over time, decoupling the well's slow recharge rate from the household's peak-demand rate; a booster pump then draws from the cistern and pressurizes the household plumbing on demand, with run-dry protection on both the well pump and the booster pump. This is a widely documented, commercially supported configuration (multiple Oregon-based pump/water-system installers offer it as a standard product), not a novel or unverified idea, though no single authoritative Oregon-specific engineering standard was located in this pass; component sizing (cistern volume against household peak-day demand, booster pump curve, well recovery rate) is a site- and household-specific engineering calculation for whoever designs the system, not a fixed code value.
   Source: OAR 690-210-0030, oregon.public.law/rules/oar_690-210-0030, checked 2026-07-12; ORS 537.545(1)(d), local file `revision6_code_library/ors/ors537.html`; OAR 340-071-0220 Table 1, local file `revision6_code_library/os-Table1MinSepDistances.pdf`; low-yield cistern/booster-pump configuration, multiple secondary/industry sources (H&M Water Solutions, ClearWater Pump Services (Oregon-based), ampland/Cistern Living, The Driller trade publication), checked 2026-07-12, cross-checked for consistency across independent sources.

3. The driveway and site access that quietly break the budget. Grade, culvert, and drainage; base-rock depth to carry concrete and pump trucks; fire-apparatus turnaround/access; and why the durable driveway usually gets built first. [PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [COST] [METHOD]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The fire-apparatus-access requirement is the one hard, code-sourced number in this item. The Oregon Fire Code (OFC) Appendix D (Fire Apparatus Access Roads), as adopted for driveways serving three or fewer dwelling units, allows a reduced driving-surface width of 12 feet, but only within a 20-foot-wide unobstructed corridor (vegetation and obstructions cleared for the full 20 feet even though the paved/graveled running surface itself is 12 feet). A 12-foot-wide road's turning radii must be not less than 44 feet inside and 56 feet outside. Where an access road exceeds 400 feet in length, turnouts 10 feet wide and 30 feet long are required at intervals of not more than 400 feet, in addition to the required road width. A dead-end fire apparatus access road longer than 150 feet requires an approved turnaround. These are Appendix D provisions; Appendix D is adopted for use at the discretion of the jurisdiction having authority (per its own text, it is "a tool for jurisdictions... in establishing access requirements"), so the exact width, turnaround, and turnout requirements a specific candidate parcel's driveway must meet depend on which edition of the Oregon Fire Code the county or fire district has adopted and whether Appendix D applies as written or with local amendment; this must be confirmed with the local fire marshal or fire district at the specific candidate parcel.

Base-rock depth to carry concrete trucks and pump trucks (rated at up to roughly 66,000-80,000 lb gross vehicle weight for a loaded ready-mix truck) is not set by a single Oregon code value; it is a geotechnical/civil-engineering design question driven by the subgrade soil's bearing capacity, the design vehicle's axle loads, and the drainage design, sized by a civil engineer or a road-building contractor against the specific site's soil, not looked up in a table. No authoritative fixed depth figure was found in this pass and none should be invented; this is a site-specific engineering determination, not a code citation.

Culvert sizing and headwall/outlet protection for a driveway crossing a watercourse is governed by the county road department's private-road/driveway-access standard (a county engineering standard, not a single statewide Oregon rule) and, where the crossing affects a regulated waterway, potentially by Oregon Department of State Lands (DSL) removal-fill permitting; both are jurisdiction-specific and were not resolved to a single statewide figure in this pass.

The "durable driveway gets built first" point is a widely repeated owner-builder practice, not a codified requirement: a construction-grade driveway must exist before delivery trucks (concrete, block, lumber, equipment) can reach the building pad, so sequencing the driveway and its drainage/culvert work ahead of the house build avoids the common failure mode of a driveway that washes out or ruts under the first loaded truck, which then delays or damages the build itself; this is a project-management/method point drawn from general construction practice rather than a single documented source.
   Source: Oregon Fire Code (OFC) Appendix D (Fire Apparatus Access Roads), 2019/2022 editions, up.codes/viewer/oregon/or-fire-code-2022/chapter/D/fire-apparatus-access-roads and codes.iccsafe.org/content/OFC2019P1/appendix-d-fire-apparatus-access-roads, checked 2026-07-12.

4. Water independence as layered redundancy, well + rainwater + storage. Legality of potable roof-rainwater in Oregon, cistern sizing for dry-season autonomy, the first-flush + multi-stage filtration + UV train, low-pH corrosion control, and cross-connection/backflow rules when mixing well and rain sources. [CREATIVE-IDEA] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [CODE]
   Leads: permies / AZ1863 / NTO Tank, first-flush + cartridge stages + UV for potable; low-pH rain corrodes copper (neutralize); design sources so they can't cross-connect; Oregon permits roof-collected rainwater.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Legality is settled and broader than a simple "roof rainwater is legal" rule: ORS 537.141(1)(h) exempts from any water-right application, permit, or certificate requirement "the collection of precipitation water from an artificial impervious surface and the use of such water." The exemption's text is not limited to roofs; it covers any artificial impervious surface, and it exempts both the collection and the use of that water from Oregon's water-right permitting system entirely (this is a state water-rights exemption, distinct from and additional to the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code's separate requirements for treating and distributing that water once collected, addressed below). No structure-purpose limitation (such as a rule confining the exemption to a structure whose primary purpose is something other than water collection, reportedly a feature of Washington State's parallel rule) appears in the Oregon statute's text; a purpose-built, non-habitable collection roof (for example, a large carport-style structure built mainly to catch water) reads as within the same exemption on its face, since the statute asks only whether the surface is "artificial" and "impervious," not what else the structure is for. This is a plain reading of the statute text, not a confirmed regulatory interpretation from OWRD; if this project pursues that specific configuration, confirming OWRD's own reading before relying on it is prudent.

Cross-connection and backflow prevention, where a rainwater cistern is plumbed into the same distribution system as a well (or any other source), falls under the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code (OPSC), Sections 602.3 and 603.3 (backflow prevention devices, assemblies, and methods), and under the Oregon Health Authority's drinking-water cross-connection rule, OAR 333-061-0070 (Cross Connection Control Requirements) and OAR 333-061-0071 (Backflow Prevention Assembly Installation and Operation Standards). The practical rule reported by Oregon plumbing-industry secondary sources: a property with a private well, a storage tank, a rainwater-harvesting system beyond simple gravity-fed landscape use, or a stormwater/groundwater reuse or graywater/blackwater reuse system is required to have a reduced-pressure-principle (RP) backflow assembly on the water service connection. This prevents backpressure (downstream pressure exceeding supply pressure) or backsiphonage (negative pressure drawing contaminated water backward) from carrying rainwater-sourced or non-potable water into the well side, or vice versa. The design principle stated in the leads (source systems built so they physically cannot cross-connect, for example via an air gap plus a manual or automated switchover rather than a single shared header) is a sound engineering practice consistent with these code requirements, though the OPSC/OAR text itself does not use that specific phrase; it sets the backflow-assembly requirement and leaves the specific plumbing topology to the designer.

Cistern sizing for dry-season autonomy is a household-specific water-budget calculation (roof catchment area x average precipitation x collection efficiency, checked against the length of the driest expected stretch); it depends on the specific parcel's climate station data (see item 9 below) and cannot be given a generic number.

The first-flush diverter, multi-stage cartridge filtration, and UV disinfection train, and low-pH corrosion control for copper plumbing fed by naturally acidic collected rainwater, are standard, widely documented potable-rainwater-system design practices (the AZ1863/University of Arizona rainwater harvesting guidance and NTO Tank-style commercial systems cited in the lead are consistent with mainstream rainwater-harvesting engineering guidance), but they are design practices, not Oregon code-mandated specifications; no Oregon-specific code section mandating a UV stage or a specific pH-neutralization method for a private rainwater system was found in this pass. Where a rainwater system is intended as a public water system (serving, for example, a home-based business with public access) rather than a single-family private supply, Oregon Health Authority Drinking Water Services rules would likely apply and change the analysis; that scenario needs its own research pass if the project's plans include any public-facing water use.
   Source: ORS 537.141(1)(h), local file `revision6_code_library/ors/ors537.html` (lines ~2977-2979), read in full 2026-07-12; OPSC Sections 602.3 and 603.3, and OAR 333-061-0070/333-061-0071, per Oregon Plumbing Authority secondary summary (oregonplumbingauthority.com) and the OHA rule text pages (oregon.gov/oha and oregon.public.law/rules/oar_333-061-0070, oar_333-061-0071), checked 2026-07-12.
   Lands in: Water Independence chapter.
SETTLED for the water-right exemption text (537.141(h)) and the existence of the backflow-prevention requirement; OWNER-DESIGN-CHOICE for the specific filtration/UV/pH-control train (a design decision among several workable approaches, not a single mandated method); NOT-RESEARCHABLE-PRO if the project's water system could be classified as a public water system (confirm with OHA Drinking Water Services before finalizing any shared or business-adjacent water use).

5. Does ORS 455.446/455.447's "essential facility" and "special occupancy structure" definition ever reach a single-family owner-built dwelling, under any circumstance (home occupation, accessory structure use, future use change), or is a single-family home categorically outside the statute's restricted-construction list? [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** A single-family owner-built dwelling is categorically outside ORS 455.447's reach under ordinary circumstances. ORS 455.447 (Regulation of certain structures vulnerable to earthquakes and tsunamis) applies its special site-evaluation, review, and surcharge requirements only to two defined categories: (1) a "major structure," statutorily defined as "a building over six stories in height with an aggregate floor area of 60,000 square feet or more, every building over 10 stories in height, and parking structures as determined by [DCBS] rule" (ORS 455.447(1)(c)); and (2) a building or structure designated Tsunami Risk Category III or IV for design, where Risk Category is the classification defined in Table 1604.5 of the Oregon Structural Specialty Code (itself adapted from ASCE 7's Risk Category I-IV system). A detached single-family dwelling is, under ordinary use, Risk Category II (the standard/ordinary-hazard category under ASCE 7 Table 1.5-1), not Category III (substantial hazard to human life in the event of failure) or Category IV (essential facilities, such as hospitals, fire and police stations, and emergency operations centers). It is also nowhere close to the "major structure" size and height thresholds (six-plus stories and 60,000-plus square feet, or ten-plus stories), which were plainly written for large commercial and institutional buildings.

The statute's own defined terms are "major structure" and "Tsunami Risk Category III or IV," not "essential facility" or "special occupancy structure" as freestanding statutory terms; those two phrases (used in the question as the researcher's own shorthand, consistent with how ASCE 7/IBC commentary sometimes describes Risk Category IV and Category III occupancies informally) do not appear as ORS 455.447 defined terms themselves. This project's home-occupation, accessory-structure, or future-use-change scenarios do not change this outcome: none of those uses converts an ordinary single-family dwelling into a Risk Category III or IV structure or into a "major structure" by the statute's own size/height definition. A change of use that did introduce a genuine Risk Category III or IV occupancy (for example, if a portion of the property were later built out as a licensed care facility or an emergency-response function) would need its own case-by-case building-code occupancy analysis at that time, but that is a generic building-code point, not something specific to ORS 455.447.

Separately, ORS 455.446 (a shorter, related section) simply directs DOGAMI to establish and adopt, by rule, the tsunami inundation zone boundary; it does not itself impose construction restrictions.
   Source: ORS 455.446 and ORS 455.447, local file `revision6_code_library/ors/ors455.html` (lines 8156-8315), read in full 2026-07-12.

6. For any coastal or estuarine Oregon parcel this project might consider, what does the DOGAMI regulatory tsunami inundation map show for depth and modeled arrival time at that specific location, and how does that compare to the evacuation time available to reach mapped high ground on foot? [CODE] [METHOD]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** This is answerable only in method, not in a specific number, because no candidate coastal parcel has been named. The tool is DOGAMI's Tsunami Inundation Map (TIM) series, developed with NOAA funding and available through Oregon HazVu (the Statewide Geohazards Viewer, oregon.gov/dogami/hazvu/Pages/resources.aspx) and the dedicated OregonTsunami.org map portal. Each TIM publication for a given coastal segment includes two plates: a local-source (Cascadia Subduction Zone) scenario and a distant-source (Alaska-Aleutian Subduction Zone) scenario, each showing modeled inundation extent and depth, a projected wave-height time-series chart, and a measurement of community exposure. The companion evacuation maps color-code two hazard zones (a distant-source "orange" zone and a local Cascadia "yellow" zone) and include a separate tsunami evacuation walk-time map showing estimated on-foot evacuation time from any point in the inundation zone to mapped high ground, alongside the estimated arrival time of the first wave. For a local (Cascadia) event, the standard guidance embedded in these maps is that the first wave can arrive in a matter of minutes, well before any external warning could be issued, which is why the walk-time map (not a siren or alert system) is the tool DOGAMI designed for a Cascadia-specific self-evacuation decision.

To use this for a specific candidate parcel: pull the TIM plate and walk-time map for that coastal segment, read the parcel's location against the inundation depth contours (local and distant scenarios separately) and against the walk-time zone it falls in, and compare that walk time to the actual on-the-ground terrain and route to the nearest mapped high-ground/assembly point (the map's straight-line or path-based walk-time estimate should be field-checked against the parcel's real access route, since a mapped walking path may not match the parcel's actual driveway or trail).

7. For a property with a single-access rural driveway crossing a culvert or seasonal watercourse, what culvert sizing, headwall/outlet protection, and inspection routine prevents the washout failure mode described in Part 1, and what does a second/alternate emergency-access path (a graded but unpaved secondary route, a neighbor easement, a helicopter-accessible clearing) cost to establish at design time versus after a road-out event? [METHOD] [COST] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Culvert sizing for a private driveway crossing is set, in practice, by the county road department's private-road/access-permit standard, not by a single Oregon statewide rule; county standards typically size the culvert to convey a design storm event (commonly a 25-year or 50-year peak flow, though the specific return period and methodology varies by county) with headwall and outlet protection (riprap or an engineered energy dissipator) sized to prevent the erosion/washout of the fill around the pipe. Because this varies by county and, on a watercourse the state or the county considers "waters of the state," may also require an Oregon Department of State Lands removal-fill permit or an Army Corps of Engineers permit if the crossing disturbs the streambed or banks, this project's exact culvert size and permitting path cannot be answered generically; it must be confirmed with the specific candidate county's road/public-works department and, if applicable, DSL.

A basic inspection routine that prevents the washout failure mode (debris blocking the culvert inlet during a storm, forcing water to overtop and erode around the crossing) is a matter of maintenance practice rather than code: walking the crossing before and after storm events, clearing inlet debris, and checking headwall/riprap condition seasonally are standard rural-road-maintenance practices, not sourced to a specific Oregon regulation in this pass.

The cost comparison between establishing a second/alternate emergency-access path at design time versus after a road-out event was not found as a documented, itemized comparison in any source located in this pass; this is a reasonable planning principle (a graded secondary route or a formalized neighbor easement is normally far cheaper to arrange before an emergency, when negotiation is unpressured and equipment mobilization is routine, than to arrange during or after a washout, when access itself may be the constraint), but no dollar figures or real project case study were located to substantiate specific numbers.

Is there any meaningful legal difference in Oregon between water rights for a drilled well versus a developed spring versus rooftop rainwater catchment versus a pond, given all four might realistically be considered on a rural parcel, and does combining more than one of them on the same property trigger any additional water-right filing beyond what each source needs on its own? [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Yes, the four source types sit under materially different legal treatment in Oregon, and this project's site-selection and water-design planning should treat them as four separate legal questions, not variations on one rule.

**Drilled well (groundwater).** Governed by ORS 537.505 to 537.795 (groundwater rights) with the ORS 537.545 exempt-use categories discussed above (items 2, 13, 15, 16) as the relevant carve-out for an ordinary domestic well; a well drawing more than the exempt thresholds needs a standard groundwater right permit and certificate.

**Developed spring.** Springs receive a specific, separate statutory test. Where a spring's water, under natural conditions, does not form a natural channel and does not flow off the property of origin at any time of year, developing and using it is treated as exempt from the standard permitting process (this is reported by a secondary legal-practice source, not independently verified against the exact ORS section in this pass, and needs direct confirmation). Where a spring's water does flow off the originating property under natural conditions, it is legally surface water belonging to the public under Oregon's prior-appropriation system, and a standard surface-water-right permit application is required, the same as for a stream. This means two springs on two different rural parcels can have entirely different legal treatment depending purely on whether their natural outflow crosses a property line, a fact the buyer needs to establish for any specific candidate spring before assuming it is exempt.

27. How should the site plan resolve conflicts when the septic reserve area, the well setback, the solar array's shade-free envelope, and a desired outbuilding location overlap, and what is the right order to lock each of those onto the site plan first? [METHOD] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]

The underlying logic: elements with the least locational flexibility, because they are dictated by fixed site conditions (soil, geology, sun geometry) that cannot be relocated by decision, should be placed on the site plan first; elements with genuine locational flexibility, because the owner can choose among several workable positions, should be placed last, fitted around whatever the fixed elements leave available.

By that logic, the general order is: (1) the septic primary absorption area and its mandatory reserve/replacement area, since a site evaluation (item 1 above) identifies specific soil conditions that determine where a system can go at all, and DEQ's own separation-distance table (Table 1, OAR 340-071-0220, item 2 above) then radiates a fixed set of no-build/no-well/no-structure setback rings outward from that footprint (10 feet to a property line, 5 feet to a septic tank/treatment unit, 10 feet to a foundation line from the absorption area); (3) the house footprint itself, sited to respect both sets of setbacks while also capturing the solar-access requirement (a south-facing wall/roof plane with adequate winter-sun access, addressed elsewhere in this question bank), since the house is the largest single fixed element and the one every other element (driveway approach, outbuilding placement, solar array orientation) is measured relative to; (4) the driveway and any required fire-apparatus turnaround, since access requirements (item 3 above) are close to fixed once the house location is set; (5) the solar array's shade-free envelope, which may be a ground-mount array sited independently of the house roof and therefore has more genuine placement flexibility than the house/septic/well cluster, though it still needs its own protected sun-angle envelope reserved early rather than treated as an afterthought, since a future tree or building placed without checking shading can quietly ruin array performance; What is the exact current ORSC/OSSC section and text for the Oregon 4-foot retaining-wall engineering and permit threshold, including the surcharge trigger that applies even under 4 feet, and how does "surcharge" get defined for a wall that supports a driveway, a block-yard pad, or a building pad rather than plain soil? [CODE]
   Leads: general and Oregon-specific sources converge on a 4-foot footing-to-top threshold plus a surcharge trigger; exact ORSC/OSSC section number and current text unverified.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Confirmed and quoted directly from the current (2023) ORSC. Section R101.2.2, item 2 (the ORSC's permit-exemption/local-ordinance provision addressing retaining walls) states: "A municipality may adopt an ordinance to regulate other retaining walls, provided that the threshold established for requiring a permit does not include retaining walls 4 feet (1219 mm) or less in height, when measured from the bottom of the footing to the top of the wall, except where the retaining wall supports ascending slopes exceeding 3:1 or where the retaining wall supports a nonsoil surcharge." This confirms the lead's figures: the 4-foot threshold is measured from the bottom of the footing (not from finished grade) to the top of the wall, and it sets a floor below which a local jurisdiction's own ordinance is not permitted to require a building permit, subject to two exceptions that can pull a shorter wall back into permit-required territory regardless of its height: (1) where the wall retains an ascending slope steeper than 3:1 (three horizontal to one vertical) above it, and (2) where the wall supports a "nonsoil surcharge."

"Surcharge" in this context is the retaining-wall-engineering term for any additional load imposed on the soil behind or above the wall beyond the weight of the retained soil itself; a "nonsoil surcharge" is a load from something other than plain earth, such as a driveway (vehicle live load plus the pavement structure's dead load), a building pad (the structure's foundation and dead/live loads bearing near or above the wall), a block-yard storage pad (stacked material load), a parking area, or any other structure or use that adds concentrated or distributed load pressing on the retained soil mass. The ORSC text itself does not further define or quantify "nonsoil surcharge" with a specific load threshold in the section quoted; whether a specific driveway, block-yard, or building-pad condition counts as a triggering surcharge for a wall under 4 feet is a determination the local building official makes case by case, informed by the geotechnical/structural loading actually present, not a fixed numeric test stated in the code text itself. Because R101.2.2 frames this as a floor on what a municipality's own ordinance can require (a municipality "may adopt an ordinance," and that ordinance's permit threshold "does not include" walls at or under 4 feet absent the two exceptions), the exact permit trigger for a specific retaining wall ultimately depends on the specific city or county's adopted ordinance language, confirmed at permit time with that jurisdiction's building department.
