**1. Where do roof-to-wall junctions leak and fail most (parapet/coping, cavity-wall-to-roof tie-in, missing kick-out flashing, roof WRB not lapped to wall WRB), and what detailing keeps water and air continuous across the joint?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The single most commonly cited residential failure point in roof-wall detailing is a missing or undersized kick-out flashing at the exact spot where a lower roofline (a porch, an entry canopy, a secondary volume) terminates against a taller vertical wall: water running down the roof plane in the step-flashing course simply keeps going straight into the wall assembly instead of being diverted into the gutter, because nothing physically redirects it. This is documented in detail, including the failure photos and the fix, in the Building America Solution Center's guide. https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/step-and-kick-out-flashing-roof-wall-intersections A second major failure point named in the same and related guidance is the roof underlayment/WRB simply stopping at the wall line instead of lapping up and behind the wall's own water-resistive barrier "shingle-fashion" (each higher layer over the one below, so water is always directed outward and down); a gap or reverse lap here creates a direct entry path regardless of how good the kick-out flashing is. https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/flashing-roof-wall-intersections-existing-homes The general code-level continuity requirement (IRC/ORSC R703.4, adopted verbatim in Oregon, see item 39 below) states flashing "shall be applied shingle-fashion in a manner to prevent entry of water into the wall cavity" at every listed transition, including kick-out flashings, chimney/masonry-to-wall intersections, coping/sill undersides, and built-in gutters. For a parapet/coping condition specifically, the equivalent failure mode is the coping cap or through-wall flashing not being continuous with the roof membrane below it, or a coping joint that is not sealed/lapped correctly, which lets water track down inside the parapet cavity rather than off the roof; this is covered in more construction detail at item 39 and items 42-45 below. The unifying detailing principle across all of these conditions is the same one the envelope chapter's own "pencil test" already establishes for wall continuity generally: trace the water (and separately, the air) path across the roof-to-wall joint on the drawings before construction and confirm every layer (roof membrane, wall WRB, kick-out flashing, coping) laps the one below it, with no gap where a trade's scope ends and the next trade's begins.

**2. For a heavy concrete/masonry home, which roof-structure options best resist wind and fire and manage the transition to mass walls (concrete roof deck, SIPs, framed trusses, insulated flat vs pitched), and what condensation risks does each carry?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** This question is already substantially resolved by the manuscript's own structural chapter, which the structural-chapter answer file confirms specifies two named roof-structure paths at §6.10: a concrete roof deck (cast integrally with or doweled into the bond beam, carrying the flat PMR assembly) and a manufacturer-engineered wood-framed truss roof on a bond beam (carrying the sloped standing-seam path), sized per IRC/ORSC Table R802.11 roof-tie uplift resistance and the manufacturer's own stamped truss design. https://up.codes/s/roof-tie-uplift-resistance A concrete deck is the strongest option against both wind (no truss-to-wall connection to fail, since the deck is monolithic with or positively doweled to the mass wall) and fire (noncombustible on both faces), and it eliminates the thermal-expansion and differential-movement mismatch a wood or steel structure would otherwise have against a concrete/masonry wall; its condensation risk is concentrated in the PMR assembly's own insulation-above-membrane detailing (already covered by the manuscript's envelope-chapter PMR sections) rather than in a hidden air-permeable cavity. A wood-framed truss roof is lighter and faster to build and remains the more common practical choice for the sloped-roof portions of a mixed design, but it reintroduces exactly the connection-design and condensation risks a mass-wall structure otherwise avoids: the truss-to-wall connection is the load path that must be engineered (Table R802.11 or a rated hurricane tie), and an unvented wood truss assembly must be detailed with enough exterior/foam R at the roof deck to keep the wood sheathing above the interior dew point, or must be a properly balanced vented assembly (see item 3). SIPs are a third option not currently named in the manuscript's own §6.10 table; the general SIP-roof literature identifies the same core condensation risk as an unvented wood truss (the foam core keeps the OSB skins from being fully protected from interior moisture drive unless the panel-to-panel seams and any embedded framing are meticulously air-sealed), with no condensation advantage over a well-detailed unvented wood truss roof and added complexity at every roof-wall and roof-penetration transition, so it is not a case this research pass found reason to add to the manuscript's existing two-path structure.

**3. What causes attic/roof-deck condensation in cold-marine conditions (air leakage into the attic, unbalanced ventilation, unvented assemblies detailed wrong), and how are vented and unvented roofs detailed to stay dry?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Building Science Corporation's own digests, already cited elsewhere in this manuscript's envelope chapter, identify air leakage (not vapor diffusion) as the dominant moisture-transport mechanism into a roof or attic assembly: warm, moisture-laden interior air exfiltrating through top-plate penetrations, can-lights, chases, and attic-access hatches condenses on the first cold surface it reaches, which in a cold-marine climate is very often the underside of the roof sheathing. https://buildingscience.com/documents/digests/bsd-012-moisture-control-for-new-residential-buildings A vented attic stays dry by combining two separate things that are often confused with each other: an airtight ceiling plane (so warm interior air cannot get into the attic at all) and a balanced ridge-to-soffit ventilation path (so any residual moisture that does get in, or that enters as outdoor humid air, is flushed out before it can accumulate); unbalanced ventilation (soffit intake blocked by insulation, or a ridge vent with no matching intake) defeats the ventilation half even when the air-sealing half is done correctly. An unvented roof assembly (a "hot roof," relevant to any section of this project's roof built as a cathedral ceiling or with ductwork in the roof plane) stays dry by a different mechanism entirely: enough continuous, air-impermeable insulation (rigid foam or closed-cell spray foam) is placed above or integral to the roof deck that the sheathing itself never drops below the interior dew point in winter, so condensation cannot form on it regardless of any residual air leakage; getting this wrong (not enough exterior/foam R relative to the total assembly R, per the IRC/ORSC's own R806.5 unvented-attic ratio requirements) is the single most common unvented-roof failure this research pass's sources describe, because it lets the code-compliant-looking assembly still run cold enough at the sheathing to condense. This project's concrete-deck-plus-PMR roof path avoids this failure mode by a different route again: the concrete deck is not a moisture-sensitive material the way OSB or plywood sheathing is, and the PMR assembly places all of its insulation above the waterproofing membrane, so there is no interior-side condensing surface in that path at all; the condensation-risk discussion above applies specifically to the wood-truss/standing-seam path (envelope chapter §8.7.3).

**4. How are roof overhangs, gutters, and drainage designed to protect masonry walls ("a good hat and boots"), and how much does masonry durability suffer with minimal overhangs?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** This is answered in construction-science depth at item 32 below (Building Science Corporation's wind-driven-rain overhang research) and is only summarized here to avoid duplicating that citation. The "hat and boots" principle is literally BSC's own framing: a generous roof overhang (the "hat") is the first and cheapest line of defense against wall wetting, because it physically keeps wind-driven rain off large portions of the wall face before drainage-plane detailing ever has to deal with it, while a well-executed base/foundation drainage and splash-protection detail (the "boots," covered in the foundation and durability chapters) handles the water that still reaches grade. https://buildingscience.com/documents/digests/bsd-013-rain-control-in-buildings Field-measurement research on overhang effectiveness (a six-story Vancouver field study and companion low-rise CFD study) found that adding a typical 30-60 cm (roughly 12-24 in) overhang to a low-rise building, or a 90 cm (about 35 in) overhang to a high-rise, meaningfully reduces wind-driven-rain deposition on the wall below it, with the protective effect strongest near the building's center and falling off toward the corners/edges. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132317301324 ; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167610513002298 Where overhangs are minimized or eliminated for architectural reasons (a common contemporary-massing choice this project's aesthetics chapter separately raises), the durability consequence is not that masonry fails outright, since well-detailed masonry can perform with no overhang at all, but that the burden shifts entirely onto the rainscreen cavity, flashing, and sealant details doing continuously and correctly what the overhang would otherwise have done passively and permanently; this is exactly why the durability and envelope chapters both treat rain-screen cavities as most valuable precisely where overhangs are short, and it is the mechanism, not a separate fact, behind this chapter's overhang-depth recommendation at item 32.

**5. What is the actual cast-in-anchor or embed-plate detail for mounting a ballasted or standoff PV rack on a concrete PMR deck, specified early enough in the structural design to avoid post-construction coring?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Three real, distinct strategies exist, and choosing among them is a design decision that must be made before the concrete deck is poured, not after: (1) a ballast-only rack (no roof penetration or embed at all, the rack's own dead weight plus added ballast blocks resists wind uplift, sized per ANSI/SPRI RP-4, see item 49), which needs no embed but does need the deck's live-load design to already include the ballast weight; (2) cast-in-place embed/anchor plates set into the wet concrete at the exact rack-manufacturer-specified grid before the pour, with the plate's rebar tails tied into the deck's own reinforcing so the attachment point is structural rather than surface-mounted; and (3) post-installed adhesive or mechanical anchors drilled into the cured deck at whatever final rack layout is chosen later. Industry guidance on cast-in-place anchor assemblies for metal-deck and concrete roof construction confirms the general method (anchors set and held in position before the pour, with a positioning feature to keep them upright and correctly spaced during placement and vibration) but this research pass found only generic solar-mounting and patent-literature sources, not a manufacturer's PMR-deck-specific installation guide naming an exact embed-plate size, spacing, or edge-distance table. https://pvrack.com/foundations/ The decision that actually avoids post-construction coring is procedural, not technical: finalize the PV array's exact layout (or at minimum its structural grid and maximum point loads) during structural design, before the concrete-deck shop drawings are issued, exactly the same sequencing problem item 6 below addresses for the drain layout and tapered-insulation shop drawing; if the array layout is not yet final, specifying an embed grid wide enough to cover any plausible final layout, or defaulting to a ballast-only rack that needs no embeds, are the two ways to keep the decision open without forcing a later coring operation.

**6. In what order does the drain layout, the tapered-insulation shop drawing, and the concrete deck's sleeve and blockout placement actually have to be finalized, and what is lost if the order is violated?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The documented industry sequence runs drain layout first, then the tapered-insulation/cricket shop drawing, then the concrete deck's sleeves and blockouts, and the shop-drawing process itself is a real, named service: manufacturers (Insulfoam, GAF, Johns Manville) and independent tapered-design firms (for example CRS's Tapered Engineering Department) take the architect's roof plan and drain locations and produce a scaled tapered-insulation and cricket layout, first as a non-scaled conceptual sketch for the design team to review, then as full-size fabrication shop drawings once the system is ordered. https://www.insulfoam.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/TaperedRoof-Insulations.pdf ; https://www.crssupply.com/services/tapered-engineering/ Crickets specifically are designed at roughly twice the slope of the surrounding tapered field, an industry rule of thumb repeated across manufacturer guidance, to push water around a valley or obstruction fast enough that it does not pond there even though the field slope alone would be too shallow. https://www.hunterpanels.com/blog/cricket-design-guidelines-for-tapered-h-shield-products/ The reason drains must be fixed before the tapered layout, and the tapered layout fixed before the deck's own sleeves/blockouts, is that each later step is geometrically derived from the one before it: the tapered-insulation slopes and cricket peaks are calculated to route water to the drains' exact locations, and the concrete deck's sleeves (drain bodies) and any blockouts (roof penetrations, curbs) must be cast at the low points the tapered layout establishes, not at a generic or assumed location. Violating the order, most commonly by pouring the deck with drain sleeves at architecturally convenient but structurally/thermally un-coordinated locations before the tapered shop drawing exists, forces the tapered-insulation designer to either accept a compromised, less-effective slope pattern around a fixed sleeve location or, in the worst case, requires coring a new sleeve through cured structural concrete, the same class of expensive rework this question's PV-embed counterpart (item 5) and the ASCE 7 ponding item (item 8) below are both structured to avoid.

**7. For a roof that mixes a flat PMR deck over the main volume with a sloped standing-seam roof over a secondary volume (a garage, a porch, an entry), what does the transition between the two roof types actually look like in section, and who (which trade) owns that joint?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** No single published source was found that draws this exact mixed-assembly transition in section (flat concrete-deck PMR meeting a sloped standing-seam roof at a wall or step), so the section-level detail below is a reasoned synthesis from the two assemblies' own established detailing principles rather than a single cited drawing, and should be flagged as such for the writer. In principle the transition is a roof-to-wall condition exactly like any other eave or parapet case already covered in items 1, 39, and 46, just with the "wall" being the taller PMR-deck volume's own parapet or upper wall face, and the "roof" being the lower standing-seam roof's high edge: the standing-seam roof's underlayment and panel end-lap must run up and terminate under a counterflashing or reglet set into the taller wall, exactly as at any other sidewall condition, and the taller volume's own wall WRB (or, if that face is a parapet, its coping/through-wall flashing) must lap over the top of that counterflashing so the two independent water-management systems (single-ply membrane above, standing-seam panel below) are continuous rather than merely adjacent. On trade ownership: this is a real, commonly-cited coordination failure point in mixed-roof construction generally (not specific to this project), because the single-ply roofer's contract scope typically ends at the membrane's own termination bar and the metal-roofer's scope typically ends at the panel's own counterflashing, leaving the wall-side reglet, sealant, and final lap as an unassigned no-man's-land unless the general contractor or architect explicitly assigns it in the specifications; the fix is procedural, not technical, the same fix item 39 recommends for kick-out flashing generally: the construction documents must name one trade (commonly the metal roofer, since the standing-seam panel's counterflashing is the more geometrically demanding half of the joint) as owning the complete transition, inspected as a single detail rather than two adjacent scopes.

**8. What does the ASCE 7 ponding-instability calculation actually require as input (deck stiffness, framing spacing, drain count and location), in enough detail that the chapter can explain to an owner-builder why the drain layout is a structural input, not a plumbing afterthought?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** ASCE 7-22 changed this calculation meaningfully from the ASCE 7-16 edition this research pass's own "Leads" note flagged as needing verification: the pre-2022 approach (an AISC Specification Appendix 2 stiffness check, run somewhat independently of the rain-load calculation) has been superseded, and ASCE 7-22 now requires the ponding head to be included directly inside the Chapter 8 rain-load calculation itself, with new commentary specifically addressing low-slope-roof drainage design; industry summaries describe this as a genuinely new, simplified procedure rather than a minor revision, including a new definition for "secondary drainage system." https://www.structuremag.org/article/snow-and-rain-loads-in-asce-7-22/ ; https://iibec.org/asce-7-standard-low-slope-roof/ The three physical inputs the calculation actually needs are exactly the three the question names: the roof deck's structural stiffness (how much it deflects under load, since a deck that deflects toward a low point makes that point lower still, compounding rather than draining the initial water), the framing/support spacing (which sets how much of the deck's stiffness is available between supports), and the number and location of the drains themselves (since each drain's "tributary area" and the roof's slope to that drain determine how deep the ponding head at the worst-case point actually gets before the water finds an outlet). This is the direct answer to why drain layout is a structural input rather than a plumbing afterthought: moving a drain, or reducing the drain count from what the structural ponding calculation assumed, changes the tributary area and therefore the ponding depth every deck-stiffness and framing-spacing number downstream of it was calculated against, exactly the same dependency chain item 6 above describes for the tapered-insulation shop drawing, just carried one level deeper into the structural engineering itself.

**10. Does the parcel's actual wind exposure category (C or D) change the required parapet height, and if so, does that interact with the aesthetic parapet-height and usable-roof-deck questions already raised in Aesthetics and Architectural Expression?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Exposure category does not set a minimum or maximum parapet height in the way the question's phrasing implies; parapet height itself is fundamentally an architectural and fire/life-safety decision (fire-code parapet requirements at property lines, guard-height requirements for a usable roof deck under IRC/ORSC R312, and the aesthetic decision the Aesthetics chapter's own H98/H102 items address), not a wind-exposure-driven dimension. What exposure category (and the design wind speed that goes with it, per item 9) does change is the required design wind pressure the parapet, its coping, and its structural anchorage must resist once its height is chosen for other reasons: ASCE 7's Chapter 27/30 wind-pressure provisions calculate higher pressures on a taller parapet and on a building in Exposure D (open, unobstructed terrain such as near open water or the Columbia Gorge) than the same height parapet would see in Exposure B or C, because the exposure category is itself a term inside the same wind-pressure equation the parapet's coping fastening and anchorage are engineered against. In practical terms for this project: exposure category is an input to how the parapet's coping, cleats, and any wind-uplift-rated edge system are engineered (see items 21, 37, 49, 52, and 78), not to how tall the parapet is allowed or required to be; the aesthetic parapet-height and usable-roof-deck questions in Aesthetics and Architectural Expression should therefore be resolved on their own architectural and guard-height merits, and the confirmed exposure category (once a parcel is chosen, per item 9) then determines the engineering design pressure for whatever height is chosen, rather than constraining the height decision itself.

**12. At what membrane thickness (mil rating) does a TPO roof's warranty and real-world service life actually converge with a PVC roof's, and is the cost premium for a heavier TPO membrane less than the cost premium for PVC at an equivalent warranty?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Manufacturer product lines document TPO commonly sold at 45, 60, and 80 mil (with or without fleece backing), and PVC commonly sold at 36 to 90 mil with most installations using 50-mil material;
