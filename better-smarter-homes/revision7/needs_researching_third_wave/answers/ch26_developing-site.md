### Site Development Overview and Phasing

1. Does Oregon or the relevant rural county allow an accessory structure (a shop or garage) to be built and occupied before a primary dwelling exists on the parcel, and does living in it before its own certificate of occupancy create a code or insurance problem?
Lands in: Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules / Developing the Site. [CODE] [REAL-WORLD-EXPERIENCE] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]
Leads: multiple owner-builder forum threads describe "build the shop first, live in it" as common practice nationally, with at least one flag that many jurisdictions technically require the primary residence first; unverified for Oregon specifically.

**Answered (2026-07-13):** This is a zoning-ordinance question decided county by county in Oregon, not a statewide building-code rule, and the two halves of the question (building/occupying an accessory structure first, versus legally living in it) resolve differently.

Building an accessory structure before the house exists is generally allowed under the state building code (ORSC R105.2's permit exemption for small non-habitable accessory structures, confirmed at item 2 below, does not condition itself on a primary dwelling already being present), but county zoning ordinances commonly do impose a "dwelling-first" or "one dwelling per parcel" sequencing rule, and enforcement of that rule varies. A documented, concrete Oregon example: Klamath County's planning FAQ states directly that a primary dwelling must be built prior to any accessory structure, but describes two real workarounds landowners use: (1) obtaining a Temporary Use Permit (TUP) to live in an RV on-site while the house-building permit stays active (Klamath County's fee: $450 to apply, $150 per year to renew, separate from building-permit fees; without a TUP, on-site camping is capped at 21 days in any 6-month period), or (2) building the shop first with a small legal apartment inside it (kitchen, bath, one conditioned room), which requires the shop itself to be permitted and occupied as the one dwelling the parcel is allowed, then removing the apartment's dwelling features once the house is finished, since most rural residential zones cap a parcel at one dwelling. Source: Klamath County Planning Department FAQ, https://www.klamathcounty.org/Faq.aspx?QID=163, checked 2026-07-13.

Whether a specific target county follows Klamath County's exact sequencing rule, its exact TUP mechanism and fee, or a different rule entirely is a direct, county-specific confirmation this project must make once a candidate parcel is identified; Oregon has 36 counties and this research pass did not survey all of them. The general pattern (a "dwelling-first" zoning rule paired with an RV/TUP workaround, or a "build the shop with a legal apartment, then convert it back" workaround) recurs across multiple counties' planning FAQs and owner-builder forum threads nationally, so it is a reasonable expectation, not a settled Oregon-wide fact.

The insurance half of the question is a separate, non-code matter: whether a homeowner's or builder's-risk policy treats occupancy of an uncompleted or uncertified structure as a coverage exclusion or a disclosure requirement is an insurer-specific policy question, not something a code citation resolves; confirm directly with the specific insurer and specific policy before relying on early occupancy.
   Source: Klamath County Planning Department FAQ ("I have a piece of bare land; how do I begin the build"), https://www.klamathcounty.org/Faq.aspx?QID=163, checked 2026-07-13; general owner-builder forum pattern (multiple threads, not individually itemized) corroborating the "shop-first, RV-workaround, or apartment-then-convert" pattern nationally.

2. What is the actual ORSC section and current text for the non-habitable detached accessory-structure permit exemption (200 sq ft general, 400 sq ft on a 2-plus-acre parcel with a 20-foot setback), and does it change under the anticipated 2026 ORSC adoption?
Lands in: Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules / Land, Site, and Feasibility. [CODE]
Leads: general-source figures found (200 sq ft / 15 ft height; 400 sq ft on 2+ acres with 20 ft setback); exact current ORSC section number and the 2026-edition status are unverified.

**Answered (2026-07-13):** Already fully verified in this research wave; reproduced here for this chapter's self-containedness rather than re-researched. From `ch03_land-site-and-feasibility.md`, item 26: ORSC Section R105.2, item 1 (Building), exempts from a building permit: "Nonhabitable one-story detached accessory structures, provided that the building area does not exceed 200 square feet (18.58 m²) and does not exceed a height of 15 feet (4572 mm) measured from grade plane to the average height of the highest roof surface," with an exception: "Where the structure is located on a parcel of 2.0 acres or greater in area, and the structure is located a minimum of 20 feet (6096 mm) from all property lines and regulated structures, the building area may be increased to 400 square feet (37.16 m²)."

This is a building-permit exemption only; it does not exempt the structure from zoning setback/use compliance (a separate county or city zoning confirmation) or from electrical, plumbing, or mechanical trade permits where those systems are added. It is a real lever for this chapter's sequencing logic (item 1 above, item 22 below): a first practice outbuilding, or a shop sized to stay just under 400 sq ft on a 2+ acre parcel with the required setbacks, can go up without plan review before the house design is finalized. Whether this carries forward unchanged into the anticipated 2026 ORSC (based on the 2024 IRC) was not confirmed in the original pass and remains open; recheck once the 2026 ORSC is adopted and published.

In what order should a solo owner-builder build driveway, temporary utilities, shop, block-yard, and house shell, given this project's specific site, and what is lost or gained by compressing that sequence (for example, running the block-yard concurrently with foundation work instead of finishing it first)?
Lands in: Developing the Site / Build Sequence and Site Coordination. [METHOD] [OWNER-DESIGN-CHOICE] [REAL-WORLD-EXPERIENCE]

**Answered (2026-07-13):** No single authoritative source prescribes one official sequencing order for a solo owner-builder's site development; the order below is a reasoned synthesis from general construction-management logic and recurring patterns in owner-builder accounts, not a citation to one document, and should be presented to the reader as a recommended default, not a fixed rule.

The recurring real-world pattern (multiple owner-builder accounts, not individually itemized) is: (1) driveway and site access first, because every later phase depends on trucks (excavation equipment, concrete, block, aggregate, lumber) being able to reach the site at all, and a durable access road built first avoids the washout/rutting failure mode already documented for an under-built driveway (see item 60-61 below); (2) temporary utilities (power, water) next, so tools, lighting, and a pump or mixer have a power source and the crew has water without hauling it for every task; (3) the shop or a covered work area next, giving the owner a dry place to keep tools and stage material out of the rain before committing to the block campaign or the foundation; (4) the block-yard and production run, since a multi-month campaign benefits from being finished, or well underway, before the block is needed for the walls; (5) the foundation and house shell, with outbuildings, site walls, and landscaping filled in around the house schedule as time, money, and weather allow.

What compressing this sequence trades away: running the block-yard concurrently with foundation excavation (item 8 below) can save real calendar time by overlapping two long-duration activities, but it multiplies coordination problems on a small rural site with one crew and one access road, since delivery trucks for both efforts (cement, aggregate, rebar, ready-mix, pump trucks) compete for the same driveway and turnaround, mud from excavation can contaminate the flat, clean production pad a block campaign needs, and dust from the block operation can foul wet concrete work nearby. A solo or near-solo owner-builder, with only one set of hands to supervise both operations, is also more exposed to whichever activity gets neglected while attention is on the other. The judgment call is genuinely site- and schedule-specific: a site with distinctly separated shop/block-yard and foundation areas, and enough site access to keep truck traffic apart, gains real time from overlap; a tight or single-access site risks enough mutual interference that finishing one phase before starting the next is the safer default.

4. What does a realistic laydown and staging plan look like for a rural owner-builder site across the whole build, not just one phase: where do cement, aggregate, block, rebar, lumber, and mechanical/electrical rough-in materials each sit at each stage so nothing blocks truck access or gets buried under the wrong pile?
Lands in: Developing the Site / Build Sequence and Site Coordination. [METHOD] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]

**Answered (2026-07-13):** General construction-management practice (not homestead-specific) treats a site utilization/laydown plan as a living document redrawn for each phase rather than a single fixed diagram for the whole build: each phase gets its own staging area, its own access route, and its own work zone, and the laydown area for incoming material is kept physically distinct from the active working footprint so delivery trucks and material stacks never block the crew or each other. No single published source was found that lays out a rural owner-builder-specific version of this plan with exact zone dimensions, so the following is this pass's reasoned application of that general principle to this project's material list, not a citation to a homestead-specific plan.

The practical implication for this project's specific material list: bulk, heavy, weather-tolerant materials that arrive early and get consumed over a long campaign (aggregate, block-making sand, bagged or bulk cement under cover) belong near the block-yard and shop, not near the house footprint, since they are drawn on daily during the block campaign and would otherwise sit in the way of foundation and framing trucks. Cured block, once made, needs its own staging path from the curing yard to wherever the house wall being built currently is, ideally a short, flat, all-weather route that does not cross the concrete-truck turnaround. Rebar (bundled by size and use) and lumber (framing dimensional stock, sheet goods) both need to be kept off bare ground, on dunnage or a temporary rack, and under cover in Oregon's wet season, or rebar rusts and lumber warps or absorbs moisture before use. Mechanical/electrical rough-in material (pipe, conduit, wire spools, fixtures) is the most theft- and weather-sensitive category and benefits from lockable, roofed storage (the shop, once it exists, or a lockable job box before that) rather than open yard staging. The general failure mode this plan avoids: a pile of one material (commonly aggregate or block) placed for early-phase convenience that later blocks the only truck access to the foundation or framing stage, forcing a costly re-handle.

**Answered (2026-07-13):** The underlying principle is a straightforward, widely repeated piece of construction-sequencing logic, not a codified rule: a trench that is already open costs only marginally more to make wider or to add another conduit run in, while a trench reopened later, after landscaping, a driveway, or a fence has gone in over it, costs far more in excavation, restoration, and disruption than the up-front conduit would have. No specific, Oregon-verified per-foot cost figure comparing "extra conduit run now" against "reopened trench later" was found in this pass, so this project should treat the general principle as settled and the specific dollar comparison as a site-specific estimate to get from a local excavation contractor, not a number to invent.

Applying that principle to this project's specific outbuilding set (shop, block-yard/future-reuse pad, generator shed, battery shed, pump house, greenhouse, woodshed, and any future ADU footprint, item 68 below): the practical answer is to run spare conduit and sleeves to every outbuilding location already fixed on the site plan (item 66 below) at the same time as the main house utility trench is dug, even for a structure not planned for years, because the trench is open regardless and the location is already decided. What owners commonly report regretting, per general owner-builder forum accounts rather than a single citable source: running a single conduit sized only for the load known at the time, rather than an oversized conduit or a second empty sleeve that leaves room to pull additional or larger conductors later without any new excavation at all; and not running a data/communications conduit alongside power to a shop or future ADU location, which then requires a second trench solely for network wiring once that structure is finally built.

What real quantitative shade-analysis method (a solar pathfinder reading, a sun-path diagram, or software) should this project run at the site-planning stage to guarantee no future outbuilding, tree, or fence shades the solar array at its mature height, and how far out (in years and in feet) should "future" be modeled?
Lands in: Solar, Battery, and Energy Independence / Developing the Site. [METHOD] [TOOLS] [TEST-TO-PROVE]

**Answered (2026-07-13):** Two commercially established, purpose-built tools do this job, both real and currently sold, not creative-idea speculation: the Solar Pathfinder (a reflective-dome instrument that captures a full panoramic year of sun/shade data at a site in a single reading, read against a printed sun-path diagram for the site's latitude), https://solarpathfinder.com/, and the Solmetric SunEye (a handheld electronic tool combining a fisheye camera, electronic compass, tilt sensor, and GPS to measure and log shading by day, month, and year), https://www.solmetric.com/product/suneye-210-shade-tool/. Both are standard solar-industry site-assessment tools, not experimental or unverified methods.

Both tools, and the shading-analysis software built around them, explicitly support modeling hypothetical future scenarios: an object (a building, a tree) can be added to the site model at a size larger than its current state, letting the analysis show what the array's shading looks like once that object reaches a specified future size, per Solmetric's own documentation, https://www.solmetric.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/UnderstandingTheSolmetricSunEye-March2011.pdf. No single authoritative source was found specifying a required number of years or feet to model "future" growth as a fixed rule; the practical, industry-common approach (drawn from general shading-analysis guidance rather than a single mandated figure) is to model any nearby tree at its mature height for its specific species (a fast-growing Douglas fir or bigleaf maple common in western Oregon can reach 60-100-plus feet at maturity, a figure this project should get from an arborist or a species-specific growth reference for whatever tree species is actually on or near the site, not from a generic assumption) and to model any future structure (an outbuilding not yet built) at its maximum plausible height and footprint under the site plan, not at a smaller placeholder size. Given a solar array's multi-decade service life, modeling for the full mature/maximum condition of anything within realistic shading distance, rather than a partial-growth snapshot, is the conservative and defensible approach.

The schedule logic is real and straightforward: a block-making campaign large enough to supply a whole house's walls commonly runs weeks to months (see the Owner-Made Blocks chapter's own production-rate research), and foundation/early-sitework excavation typically runs on its own multi-week timeline; if the two activities do not depend on each other's output (the block campaign does not need the foundation finished, and the foundation does not need finished block), running them in parallel can compress the project's overall calendar length by roughly the shorter of the two durations, since the two would otherwise stack end to end.

10. Pole-barn kit versus stick-built versus steel-frame versus shipping-container shop: for a roughly 1,200-2,400 square foot owner-built shop in rural Oregon, what is the real all-in cost (kit or materials, slab, doors, electrical, insulation) for each path in 2026 dollars, and what does each trade off in build time, DIY-feasibility, and long-term durability?
Lands in: Developing the Site / Materials, Suppliers, Procurement, and Substitution. [COST] [OWNER-DESIGN-CHOICE] [MATERIALS/INGREDIENTS]

**Answered (2026-07-13):** Figures below are 2026 national secondary-source ranges (multiple pole-barn-industry and container-conversion cost guides, cross-checked for rough consistency across sources), not an Oregon-specific quote; a real Oregon bid from a local pole-barn supplier or contractor is needed before this project budgets against any of these numbers.

**Pole-barn kit**, a 30x40 shell (1,200 sq ft): kit materials only (posts, trusses, steel roofing/siding, trim, fasteners) run roughly $12,000-$22,000 from a major supplier (Morton, Lester, or similar), per homeguide.com and polebarncosts.com, https://homeguide.com/costs/pole-barn-kit-prices and https://polebarncosts.com/guides/30x40-pole-barn-cost/, checked 2026-07-13; adding a concrete slab, one overhead door, and basic electrical brings an owner-assembled shell to roughly $18,000-$35,000-plus; a crew-erected kit (materials plus $10,000-$18,000 labor) runs higher, and a full turnkey contractor build for the same footprint runs roughly $36,000-$56,000. Pole-barn kits are explicitly designed for owner erection, and a two-person crew with a tractor and a boom or forklift can reportedly raise a 30x40 shell in a long weekend; this is the fastest, most DIY-feasible, and generally cheapest path to a large clear-span shop, with post-frame construction giving decades of service life when posts are properly protected from ground contact and moisture.

**Stick-built** (conventional wood-framed) and **steel-frame** shops were not separately cost-quoted with current figures in this pass; general construction-cost knowledge (not a specific source) holds that a stick-built shop of the same footprint typically costs more than a pole-barn kit of equivalent size because of the added footing/foundation wall and framing labor a post-frame building avoids, while a pre-engineered steel-frame building (bolted rigid-frame, not a pole-barn's post-and-truss system) sits in a similar or somewhat higher cost band than a pole-barn kit with a longer service life on the frame itself but less DIY-feasibility, since steel-frame erection commonly requires a crane and a specialized erection crew rather than an owner-assembled kit. This portion of the answer is a reasoned cost-ordering, not a sourced dollar figure, and should be verified against actual Oregon quotes before publication.

**Shipping-container shop**, a single 40-foot high-cube container (roughly 320 sq ft of interior floor area, well under this project's 1,200-2,400 sq ft target on its own, so a real shop-sized build needs multiple containers or a hybrid container-plus-addition): the container itself runs $1,800-$7,000 depending on new/used condition, per Well Built Florida and Metal Buildings guidance, https://wellbuiltflorida.com/cost-40-foot-shipping-container/, checked 2026-07-13; insulating one container runs roughly $2,000-$4,000 for rigid foam board or $5,000-$9,000 for closed-cell spray foam; a fully outfitted, insulated, electrified single-container workshop conversion runs roughly $25,000-$54,000 all-in per Conexwest's cost breakdown, https://www.conexwest.com/blog/much-cost-modify-shipping-container-2025-real-numbers-breakdown, checked 2026-07-13, with mid-grade remodels averaging roughly $80-$85 per square foot, a materially higher per-square-foot cost than a pole-barn kit at this project's target size. Reported trade-offs: fast delivery (weeks, not months) and a genuinely secure, lockable shell from day one, against a narrow roughly 8-foot clear interior width per container before any multi-container cut-and-join work, and its own condensation/insulation design problem for a steel shell in Oregon's wet, temperature-swinging climate (item 16 below). The general maintenance pattern that keeps a gravel driveway's base performing over the life of a house: maintaining a crowned cross-section (the driveway surface built slightly higher in the center than at the edges) so water sheds to the sides rather than pooling or running down the length of the driveway surface, where it would otherwise erode the surface material and eventually work into the base; maintaining clear roadside ditches so shed water actually has somewhere to go rather than backing up against the driveway edge; and periodically inspecting and cleaning culverts of accumulated silt and debris, since a partially blocked culvert backs water up against the driveway fill and increases both the flooding risk and the erosion risk at that specific point. Washboarding (rhythmic ripples across the driving surface) and edge rutting are commonly described general gravel-road failure patterns resulting from traffic combined with insufficient surface material or an insufficiently crowned/compacted base, generally addressed by periodic grading (re-blading the surface to restore the crown and smooth the ripples) and, where the underlying base itself has degraded rather than just the surface, adding and re-compacting fresh base material rather than only grading the top layer. Culvert siltation (sediment accumulating and reducing a culvert's effective capacity over time) is addressed by periodic inspection and cleanout, ideally before the wet season each year, so the culvert's full design capacity is available when storm flows are highest.
