## Inspection and Approval Sequence

1. OAR 918-008-0120 requires choosing one appeal route, the local appeals board or the state specialty code chief, at the outset, with no switching after starting. What factors should drive that choice for a novel-material dispute (speed, the reviewer's technical sophistication, cost, precedent value), and has any owner-builder documented choosing wrong?
Lands in: Inspection and Approval Sequence. [CODE]
(Attorney-grade: the irreversible choice has real consequences and deserves a construction attorney's input before filing.)

**Answered (2026-07-14):** The irreversibility premise is confirmed by direct primary-text fetch: OAR 918-008-0120 states plainly, "The appeals process selected may not change once initiated" (https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0120, fetched 2026-07-14). The structure is a three-tier system: at the first level the applicant chooses either the local jurisdiction's appeals board (ORSC R112, https://www.oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/Documents/23orsc-ch1.pdf) or the state specialty code chief inspector (ORS 455.475(1)(a), https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_455.475) — mutually exclusive at that first step — with a second-level appeal from whichever first-level decision-maker was used going to the appropriate advisory board. Fees at the state track: $20 to the specialty code chief, a further $20 to appeal that decision to the advisory board (OAR 918-008-0120(2) and (6)); no state-level fee is specified for the local-board track (a local ordinance could set one — confirm with the specific jurisdiction). Decision-time windows on the state track: the chief inspector must respond within 14 days of the request (both parties get 7 calendar days to respond to preliminary steps), with a 30-calendar-day outer limit before the Division Administrator can suspend the timeframes "when complexity merits"; the advisory-board appeal window is 30 calendar days from the first-level decision. No equivalent state-level clock governs the local-board track's timing.

Given these confirmed facts, the factors that should actually drive the choice for a novel-material (alternative-materials) dispute are: **speed** — the state track has an explicit statutory clock (14/30 days); a rural local board with no standing caseload has no equivalent clock and, per item 3 below, may take weeks simply to convene a first hearing. **Reviewer technical sophistication** — ORSC R112.3 sets only a generic bar ("qualified by experience and training to pass judgment on matters pertaining to building construction"); nothing guarantees a local board member with alternative-materials, earthen, or masonry expertise (see item 2), while the state specialty code chief is BCD staff whose full-time portfolio is code administration statewide. **Cost** — the state track's stated fees are nominal ($20/$20); a local board process's real cost is driven by attorney/engineer preparation time (item 5), which is likely comparable either way, but a rural board convening for the first time in years carries schedule risk that itself has a dollar cost (holding costs, item 3 in Finance). **Precedent value** — this is the sharpest, best-sourced differentiator: ORS 455.475(1)(b) requires that if the advisory board determines a chief-inspector decision is a "major code interpretation" (one "that affects or may affect more than one job site or more than one inspection jurisdiction"), the decision "shall [be] distribute[d]... in writing to all applicable specialty code public and private inspection authorities in the state," within 60 days, at no charge. A local board's decision carries no equivalent statewide-distribution mechanism. For an owner planning a genuinely novel structural material where a favorable, citable, statewide-distributed ruling would have value beyond this one house, the state track is the only one of the two routes that can produce that outcome by rule.

No documented case of an owner-builder specifically choosing the wrong route, or of the consequences of that choice playing out, was found in this pass or in the parallel Legal chapter research (`ch04_legal_part1.md` item 15, which independently searched for and did not find any specific-outcome record of an owner-builder R104.11 dispute reaching the state specialty-code-chief appeal). This is consistent with either a genuinely rare event, non-public recordkeeping, or both — absence of a hit is not proof the case does not exist.

**What to confirm and with whom:** a construction attorney, before filing either appeal, specifically on which route better serves this project's facts — the choice is irreversible and the stakes (a structural alternative-materials approval) are high enough that attorney input is warranted regardless of the reasoning above.

2. What are the exact qualification requirements for local board-of-appeals members under ORSC R112 as adopted in Oregon, including whether R112.4's Administration language is adopted or struck, and does a rural county's board typically include anyone with earthen, masonry, or alternative-materials expertise, or only conventional-framing experience?
Lands in: Inspection and Approval Sequence. [CODE]
Leads: one secondary source suggested R112.4 may not be adopted by Oregon BCD as part of the state code; needs direct primary-text confirmation.

**Answered (2026-07-14):** The lead is confirmed directly from the primary text. 2023 ORSC Chapter 1, Section R112 (Board of Appeals), read in full from the BCD PDF (https://www.oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/Documents/23orsc-ch1.pdf): R112.1 (General — municipality must establish an appeals procedure; ORS 455.475 is noted as an alternative) and R112.2 (Limitations on authority — the board may not waive code requirements, only interpret) are adopted as ordinary code text. **R112.3 (Qualifications) is adopted, and its entire text is: "An appeals board shall consist of members who are qualified by experience and training to pass judgment on matters pertaining to building construction."** That is the complete state-level qualification standard — generic, with no named trade, material, or specialty requirement. **R112.4 (Administration) is confirmed NOT adopted: the code text reads verbatim, "This section is not adopted by the State of Oregon, Building Codes Division, as part of this code."** (Appendix AV, "Board of Appeals," is also separately listed among appendices explicitly preempted from local adoption under R101.2.3.2.)

A second, more specific statutory layer applies where a municipality uses a **contract building official** (the common arrangement in rural Oregon — see the cross-referenced finding in `ch02_oregon-code-and-compliance-map.md` item 12 on contract building officials' authority, and firms such as Northwest Code Professionals serving roughly 50 Oregon jurisdictions per `ch04_legal_part2.md`'s item-73 research). ORS 455.202, fetched directly (https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_455.202): a municipality procuring contract building official services must establish a local board (unless the county or an adjacent county already has one covering it); that board "must include as a member the building official of the county within which the city is located or the building official of an adjacent county" (for a city) or "a building official from an adjacent county" (for a county); the statute bars from membership "any contract building official or an owner, manager, director, officer or employee of a person, other than an employee of the municipality, that performs building inspections" (a direct conflict-of-interest bar aimed at the contract building official and its competitors); a design/construction professional may otherwise serve, subject to a recusal rule for appeals touching a project connected to that member's own business, employer, family, or household.

Neither R112.3 nor ORS 455.202 requires, or even mentions, earthen, masonry, or alternative-materials expertise specifically. Both are silent on subject-matter specialization beyond "building construction" generally. No source found in this pass documents an actual rural Oregon county board's real membership roster or their trade backgrounds — that is a per-county fact, confirmable only by asking the specific county (or its contract building-official firm) who currently sits on its board.
**What to confirm and with whom:** the specific county (or its contract building-official firm, if any) for its board's current roster and members' trade backgrounds.

4. What does a complete, effective local board-of-appeals submission package look like for a technical structural-materials dispute: what exhibits, in what order, how much advance notice, and is oral testimony from the owner's engineer normally allowed?
Lands in: Inspection and Approval Sequence. [METHOD] [REAL-WORLD-EXPERIENCE]

**Answered (2026-07-14):** No documented, Oregon-specific account of an actual local board-of-appeals hearing package for a structural-materials dispute (exhibit list, order, notice period, or whether oral engineer testimony was taken) was found in this pass. What is settled, and gives real structure to build a package around, is the standard the board is legally confined to: ORSC R112.2 (Limitations on authority) restricts an appeal to "a claim that the true intent of this code or the rules legally adopted thereunder have been incorrectly interpreted, the provisions of this code do not fully apply or an equally good or better form of construction is proposed," and explicitly bars the board from waiving code requirements outright. That legal frame dictates the substance of an effective package regardless of the specific county's procedural rules: it must show, exhibit by exhibit, either (a) that the building official misapplied or misread a specific code provision, or (b) that the alternative meets or exceeds the R104.11 equivalency criteria (quality, strength, effectiveness, fire resistance, durability, safety) the official is legally required to test any alternative against. A defensible structure, reasoned from that legal frame plus the R104.11.1 evidentiary standard already documented for the Testing chapter (item 8 below): the written denial itself (which R104.11 requires the building official to issue, stating specific reasons); a point-by-point rebuttal keyed to each equivalency criterion the denial cited as unmet; the underlying test data/engineering calculations for each criterion; any manufacturer or standards-body documentation (ICC-ES report, ASTM certificate); and a proposed written approval-with-conditions the board could adopt if it agrees, since a specific, ready-to-adopt order is easier for a rarely-convened rural board to act on than an open-ended request.

Whether oral testimony from the owner's engineer is normally allowed is a per-jurisdiction procedural question with no confirmed general answer found in this pass; nothing in ORSC R112 addresses hearing format at all (that is left, again, to the municipality's own ordinance under R112.1). Advance-notice periods are likewise local-ordinance facts not found generally in this pass, though the state-track's own filing windows (item 1: 30 calendar days to appeal to the advisory board) give a rough sense of the timescales the overall appeal system runs on.
**What to confirm and with whom:** the specific board's clerk or the local building official, well in advance: hearing format (is oral testimony taken), advance-notice/filing deadline, and exhibit-submission rules.

6. Does filing a local appeal or a state appeal toll or pause the ORSC R105.5 permit-expiration clock or the ORS 455.467 plan-review timeline, or does the schedule keep running during a dispute?
Lands in: Inspection and Approval Sequence. [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-14):** This exact question was independently researched and settled in `ch04_legal_part1.md` item 16, which fetched ORS 455.475 and ORS 455.467 directly; this pass's own direct read of the same statute (footer text reprinted in the 2023 ORSC Chapter 1 PDF) confirms the identical finding. Restated here for this chapter's self-containment: **the answer splits, and the split is a real feature of the statute, not a research gap.** ORS 455.475(3) states plainly: "If an appeal is made under this section, an inspection authority shall extend the plan review deadline by the number of days it takes for a final decision to be issued for the appeal." This explicitly tolls the ORS 455.467 10-business-day (or 15-business-day, for jurisdictions of 300,000+ population) plan-review clock for the duration of any appeal filed under ORS 455.475 — a direct, unambiguous statutory tolling provision.

**The ORSC R105.5 permit-expiration clock (the 180-day work-commencement/work-suspension clock) has no equivalent tolling provision** anywhere this research (in either chapter's pass) has found; R105.5 and ORS 455.475 are never cross-referenced. Structurally this makes sense: 455.467/455.475 govern the pre-issuance plan-review stage, while R105.5 governs the post-issuance activity clock. A dispute over a plan-review decision, by definition, predates permit issuance, so R105.5 is not yet running when that kind of appeal happens. But a post-issuance dispute (for example, over an inspection correction notice tied to an already-approved alternative material) has no found tolling mechanism for R105.5's 180-day clock — a real, unaddressed exposure the manuscript should flag.
**What to confirm and with whom:** the local building official, at the time any post-issuance dispute arises, on whether they will administratively pause the R105.5 clock or require a parallel extension request.
SETTLED for the ORS 455.467/455.475 tolling provision; NOT-RESEARCHABLE-AHJ for R105.5 during a post-issuance dispute.

7. What happens procedurally on a stop-work order under ORSC R114 issued mid-project over a disputed alternative material: can work continue on unrelated, undisputed parts of the house while the specific disputed item is litigated, or does a stop-work order freeze the entire jobsite?
Lands in: Inspection and Approval Sequence. [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-14):** A significant correction to the question's own premise is required, and it is a direct primary-text finding, not an inference: **ORSC Section R114 ("Stop Work Order") is a heading only — its substantive text is not adopted by the State of Oregon.** The 2023 ORSC Chapter 1 PDF, read in full, shows Section R114 consisting of nothing but the boxed note: "Not adopted by the State of Oregon, Building Codes Division, as part of this code. A municipality may have authority outside of this code to regulate these matters locally, where not preempted" (https://www.oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/Documents/23orsc-ch1.pdf, page 14 of the PDF). Stop-work-order authority is therefore not a state-code (ORSC) provision at all — it is confirmed instead as a **local-ordinance enforcement power** at the statutory level: ORS 455.020(4), read directly from the local `ors455.html` file (iconv-decoded), states that the building code statutes do "not limit the authority of a municipality to enact regulations providing for local administration of the state building code; local appeal boards; fees and other charges; abatement of nuisances and dangerous buildings; **enforcement through penalties, stop-work orders or other means**; or minimum health, sanitation and safety standards..." (https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors455.html). This means a stop-work order's exact scope (whether it can be limited to a specific disputed item versus the entire jobsite) is governed entirely by the specific municipality's own stop-work ordinance, not by any uniform state text — because no uniform state text exists on this point. (This is a manuscript-relevant correction: any existing chapter text describing "ORSC R114 authority to issue a stop-work order" as a state-code provision should be corrected to describe it as local-ordinance authority under ORS 455.020(4), even though the practical effect — a building official can and does issue stop-work orders — is the same.)

No specific rural-Oregon-jurisdiction stop-work ordinance text was found in this pass that states whether an order can be scoped to only the disputed portion of a job versus the whole site; one small-city example located (Eagle Point, Jackson County, Chapter 15.15, https://www.codepublishing.com/OR/EaglePoint/html/EaglePoint15/EaglePoint1515.html) describes a stop-work order issued "when work is being done contrary to the provisions of local code, the state building code, or other pertinent laws or ordinances," served in writing on persons doing or causing the work — consistent with a notice mechanism that names the specific violation, which would support (but does not confirm as a documented practice) an order limited to the cited violation rather than a whole-site freeze, since the notice-content requirement is tied to "the work" in violation. This reading is reasoned from ordinance structure, not documented from an actual disputed-alternative-material case.
**What to confirm and with whom:** the specific jurisdiction's stop-work ordinance text and, if a stop-work order is ever issued, the building official directly and immediately, on whether unrelated undisputed work may continue.
SETTLED that R114 itself is not state-adopted and the authority is local-ordinance-based under ORS 455.020(4); NOT-RESEARCHABLE-AHJ for whether any specific jurisdiction's order can be scoped to only the disputed item.

9. What is the realistic construction sequence for casting a safe room's added reinforcement and rated door frame into the walls and slab at the same time as the rest of the structural shell, and which structural hold points need a safe-room-specific inspection or sign-off added?
Lands in: Structural System and Concrete Design / Inspection and Approval Sequence. [METHOD] [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-14):** ORSC contains one directly relevant, previously unresearched hook: **R106.1.5 ("Information on storm shelters")** — "Construction documents for storm shelters shall include the information required in ICC 500" (2023 ORSC Chapter 1, https://www.oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/Documents/23orsc-ch1.pdf). This means the safe room's distinct scrutiny happens primarily at the **plan-review stage**, not as a separate construction-phase hold point: the submitted construction documents must carry ICC 500-compliant design information (door/frame rating, wall/roof debris-impact design, anchorage) for the building official to review before the permit issues. ICC 500 itself, confirmed by direct search, is a wind-hazard standard only ("ICC 500 is specifically designed for tornado and hurricane protection only, not for seismic or earthquake design," https://www.insulgard.com/testing/icc-500-storm-shelter-standard/; FEMA P-361 builds on ICC 500 with additional siting/wind-speed/fire-rating guidance for federally funded "Safe Rooms" specifically, https://www.fema.gov/sites/default/files/2020-07/highlights-icc-500.pdf) — relevant because Oregon's own hazard profile (seismic, wildfire) is not the hazard ICC 500 was written for; a combined structural/seismic-resilience safe room built to this project's own engineering, rather than to satisfy a wind-specific federal funding program, would reference ICC 500 only if the owner specifically wants that certification pathway (see Finance items 19–20 below on why the federal funding angle likely does not apply here).

For the construction-phase hold points, no ORSC provision creates a distinct "safe room inspection" separate from the ordinary reinforced-concrete/masonry and slab inspections that already apply to the surrounding shell — reasoned directly from the code text rather than found as a documented safe-room-specific case: **R109.1.1/R109.1.1.1 (foundation/slab inspection)** already requires inspection "after... any required reinforcing steel is in place and supported prior to the placing of concrete," which would catch the safe room's added slab reinforcement at the same hold point as the rest of the foundation, provided the plans clearly flag the safe-room zone so the inspector knows to check its specific (denser or larger-bar) reinforcing schedule there. **R109.1.5.3 (reinforced masonry/ICF/conventionally formed concrete wall inspection)** likewise already requires inspection of reinforcing "size, location, spacing and lapping" before grout/concrete placement for any wall in Seismic Design Category D0–D2 or E (which this project's Cascadia-zone design will be in regardless of the safe room) — again, the safe room's added wall reinforcement rides on this same existing hold point rather than needing its own trigger, as long as the approved plans distinguish the safe-room wall's reinforcing schedule from the surrounding wall. The rated door frame's field verification would fall under the general **R109.1.4 (frame and masonry inspection)** and **R109.1.6 (final inspection)**, which check that installed assemblies match approved plans; verifying an ICC-500-rated door/frame is installed exactly per its listing (anchorage, hardware, clearances) is the kind of detail the manuscript should flag explicitly for the inspector's attention on the day, since a generic framing inspection would not otherwise know to scrutinize a door's storm/impact rating.

**Realistic casting sequence, reasoned from ordinary reinforced-concrete/masonry sequencing rather than a documented safe-room case:** place and inspect the safe-room slab's added reinforcing steel (denser mat or greater bar size/lap length) as part of the single foundation/slab pour and its single R109.1.1.1 inspection, ensuring the safe-room zone's reinforcing schedule is clearly called out on the approved plans so the inspector checks it distinctly; set the rated door frame's rough buck/anchor points into the wall formwork or CMU cores before that wall lift's grout pour, so the frame anchorage is embedded at the same time as the rest of that course's vertical reinforcing, verified at the same R109.1.5.3 hold point; install the actual rated door and frame per its listing later in the build, verified at framing/final inspection rather than at the structural pour stage. This sequencing avoids creating a second, separate structural pour just for the safe room, but it means the plans (not a separate field trigger) are what carry the safe-room-specific scrutiny through the existing hold points.
**What to confirm and with whom:** the local building official at plan review, specifically on whether they want the safe room's ICC 500 documentation as a distinct submittal exhibit or folded into the main structural set, and the door/frame manufacturer's listing for exact anchorage/inspection points.
SETTLED for the R106.1.5/ICC 500 plan-review hook and the general hold-point mapping; METHOD reasoning (not a documented case) for the specific casting sequence.

10. Does Oregon's building department review process treat a durability-driven design choice (upgraded cover, corrosion-resistant rebar, a crystalline admixture) as a substitution requiring extra documentation, or as a straightforward exceeds-minimum design choice needing no special approval?
Lands in: Designing for Centuries; cross-reference Inspection and Approval Sequence. [CODE] [METHOD]

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Reasoned directly from R104.11's own text, read in full for this pass: R104.11 exists to approve "the installation of any material or... any design or method of construction **not specifically prescribed** by this code" — its equivalency-finding requirement (quality, strength, effectiveness, fire resistance, durability, safety) only engages for something the prescriptive code does not already address. Increasing concrete cover beyond the prescriptive minimum, specifying corrosion-resistant reinforcing that is itself an ASTM-recognized material standard (epoxy-coated bar per ASTM A775, stainless reinforcing per ASTM A955, both used within an otherwise ordinary ASTM A615/A706 Grade 60 rebar schedule), or adding a crystalline waterproofing admixture to an otherwise ACI 318/332-compliant, code-conforming concrete mix design are all cases of **using already-recognized materials to exceed a minimum**, not installing something the code does not address at all — the plain-language distinction R104.11 itself draws. On that reading, these choices are a straightforward exceeds-minimum design decision, not an "alternative materials" submission requiring R104.11's equivalency documentation and building-official approval.

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Confirmed directly for cast concrete: **ASTM C617/C617M** ("Standard Practice for Capping Cylindrical Concrete Specimens") covers bonded capping — molten sulfur mortar, neat cement, or high-strength gypsum paste — applied to freshly molded or hardened concrete cylinders and drilled cores. **ASTM C1231/C1231M** ("Standard Practice for Use of Unbonded Caps in Determination of Compressive Strength of Hardened Cylindrical Concrete Specimens") instead specifies unbonded neoprene pads held in metal retaining rings, usable for concrete strengths between 1,500 and 12,000 psi (https://www.globalgilson.com/blog/compressive-strength-testing-sulfur-capping-or-neoprene-pads; https://www.astm.org/Standards/C1231.htm; https://store.astm.org/standards/c1314 for the parallel masonry-prism context). 14. How does an owner design the actual experiment, a mix matrix / design-of-experiments varying cement percentage, moisture, and compaction against strength and cost, so the "tested scientifically to pass the press" claim is real and reproducible?

5. Does an Oregon land sale contract (contract for deed) create any complication for later getting a construction loan or a permanent mortgage, given that the seller retains legal title until the contract is paid off?
Lands in: Land, Site, and Feasibility; Personal Project Finance: Budget, Financing, and Cash Flow. [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Yes, and the mechanism is confirmed directly. In an Oregon land sale contract, "the seller retains legal title, while the buyer has 'equitable title,' which means the buyer has virtually all of the rights of an owner, but cannot convey legal title during the life of the contract" (Querin Law/Oregon real estate law analysis, https://q-law.com/note-trust-deed-vs-land-sale-contract-use-when/); the seller only issues the actual conveying "Fulfillment Deed" once the contract is paid in full. **The complication for a construction or permanent mortgage lender is direct: a construction lender typically requires clear title or a first-position lien position on the property being improved as its collateral security** — but with a land sale contract in place, the seller (not the buyer) still holds legal title, meaning the buyer cannot grant the construction lender a straightforward first-position mortgage/trust-deed lien the way an owner with clear title could; this creates what the sourced analysis frames as a subordination problem, since the lender's security interest would have to somehow sit behind or work around the seller's retained legal title (https://contractfordeed.uslegal.com/state-laws/oregon-contract-for-deed-law/; general land-sale-contract-vs-financing analysis synthesized from Querin Law's Oregon real estate law blog series). Separately, land sale contracts themselves are commonly financed by the seller directly (not a bank) at less favorable terms than a conventional purchase mortgage — "bank and other lender loans for undeveloped land [are] often financed at a higher interest rate and for a shorter term (with a balloon payment) than a traditional home mortgage" — meaning a buyer already on a land sale contract may be layering a second, harder-to-place construction loan on top of a first financing arrangement that itself sits outside conventional mortgage norms.

**Practical resolution paths** implied by this structure, reasoned rather than documented as a specific case: (a) pay off/refinance the land sale contract into a conventional deed and clear title before seeking a construction loan, so the construction lender has an unencumbered first-position collateral position; (b) negotiate directly with the land-contract seller and the prospective construction lender together for a subordination agreement, if the seller is willing; or (c) use a lender specifically experienced with land-sale-contract-encumbered properties, if one can be found — no source in this pass names a specific Oregon lender who routinely does this.
**What to confirm and with whom:** the specific construction lender being approached, very early, on whether they will lend at all against a land-sale-contract-encumbered property, and a real-estate attorney on subordination or payoff options specific to this land contract's terms.

6. What are the gift-tax reporting requirements and cost-basis consequences when a family member transfers land below market value or as an outright gift, at a level of detail useful for a buyer to bring an informed question to a CPA, without this book giving tax advice directly?
Lands in: Land, Site, and Feasibility; Personal Project Finance: Budget, Financing, and Cash Flow. [METHOD]

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Confirmed directly from the IRS's own gift-tax guidance (https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/frequently-asked-questions-on-gift-taxes, fetched 2026-07-14). **The core rule:** "the transfer of any property or interest in property for less than adequate and full consideration is considered a gift" — meaning a family land transfer below fair market value is a gift of the *difference* between market value and whatever (if anything) was actually paid, not just outright free transfers. **Annual exclusion:** $19,000 per recipient (donee) for 2026 (double, $38,000, if the giving spouse's spouse elects gift-splitting); a gift exceeding this per-recipient amount in a calendar year requires the giver to **file IRS Form 709**, even though filing does not necessarily mean tax is owed — Form 709 is a reporting requirement distinct from a payment obligation, because the excess is applied against the giver's much larger **lifetime basic exclusion amount, which increased to $15,000,000 for calendar year 2026** under recent federal legislation (same IRS source). **Cost-basis consequence, and this is the single most important practical point for a CPA conversation:** when land is gifted (rather than inherited), **the recipient's cost basis carries over from the giver's original basis** (what the giver paid, plus any documented improvements) — this is fundamentally different from inheriting property, where the heir instead receives a "stepped-up" basis equal to the property's fair market value at the date of death. A gifted parcel therefore can carry a much lower basis than its current market value, which becomes a larger taxable capital gain if the recipient later sells it — a consequence entirely invisible at the time of the gift itself and easy to overlook without a CPA flagging it in advance.
**What to bring to a CPA:** the giver's original purchase price/basis and any documented capital improvements to the land since purchase; a current professional appraisal establishing fair market value at the time of transfer (needed both to size the gift for Form 709 purposes and to establish the carryover-basis starting point); and the specific question of whether Form 709 must be filed for this transfer given the current $19,000 annual exclusion and the transfer's actual value.
