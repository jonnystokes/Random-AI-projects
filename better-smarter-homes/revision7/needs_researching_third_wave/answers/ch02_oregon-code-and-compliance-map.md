1. Does ORSC R104.11 as adopted in Oregon, distinct from the base IRC text, require the building official to give a written response with stated reasons when an alternative-method request is denied, and what does that written denial need to contain to be useful for an appeal? [CODE] (controlling agency: Oregon Building Codes Division and the local building official)
   Leads: the base IRC R104.11 text outside Oregon reportedly requires a written response with reasons; Oregon's own adopted text needs a direct check.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Yes. The 2023 Oregon Residential Specialty Code (ORSC, based on the 2021 IRC) adopts R104.11 unchanged from the base IRC text on this point. The approval standard: the building official may approve an alternative material, design, or method of construction where the proposal "is satisfactory and complies with the intent of the provisions of this code" and is equivalent in quality, strength, effectiveness, fire resistance, durability, and safety to what the code prescribes. The denial requirement is explicit: "Where the alternative material, design or method of construction is not approved, the building official shall respond in writing, stating the reasons why the alternative was not approved" (ORSC R104.11). The code text itself does not specify further content requirements (no mandated citation format, no mandated reference to a specific code section) beyond "stating the reasons." For a denial to be useful on appeal to the local board of appeals or, where none exists, to the appropriate state advisory board (see ORS 455.690 and the appeal path referenced at ORS 455.475 area), the reasons stated should identify which equivalency criterion (quality, strength, effectiveness, fire resistance, durability, or safety) the building official found unmet, since the appeal body will be reviewing against that same standard. This is a drafting recommendation, not a code-stated content list.
   Source: ORSC R104.11 (2023 edition), text confirmed via up.codes/viewer/oregon/irc-2021 (Oregon-adopted chapter 1), checked 2026-07-12.

2. Does R104.4's acceptance of written, certified reports of inspection by approved agencies or individuals extend to an out-of-state evaluation report, such as an ICC-ES report, an IAPMO UES report, or another state's earthen-building-code compliance finding, as evidence toward an Oregon R104.11 approval, or does Oregon require independent, in-state testing regardless of any outside report? [CODE] (controlling agency: Oregon Building Codes Division and the local building official)

**Answered (2026-07-12):** ORSC R104.4 states: "The building official shall make the required inspections, or the building official shall have the authority to accept reports of inspection by approved agencies or individuals. Reports of such inspections shall be in writing and be certified by a responsible officer of such approved agency or by the responsible individual. The building official is authorized to engage such expert opinion as deemed necessary to report on unusual technical issues that arise" (ORSC R104.4, 2023 edition). The text does not restrict "approved agencies" to Oregon-based or in-state agencies; the operative word is "approved," and approval is the local building official's discretionary act. This means an ICC-ES report, an IAPMO UES report, or another jurisdiction's compliance finding can be submitted as supporting evidence toward an R104.11 alternative-method approval, but nothing in R104.4 or R104.11 obligates the building official to accept it: the official decides, case by case, whether that outside report satisfies them that the proposal is "satisfactory and complies with the intent" of the code. Oregon does not have a blanket rule either requiring acceptance of ICC-ES/IAPMO UES reports or requiring independent in-state testing regardless of outside reports; the outcome is discretionary and building-official-specific. A builder relying on an out-of-state report should bring it to a pre-application meeting with the specific building official and ask directly whether it will be accepted as sufficient evidence, or whether supplemental Oregon-specific testing or an engineer's letter will be required.
   Source: ORSC R104.4 and R104.11 (2023 edition), up.codes/viewer/oregon/irc-2021, checked 2026-07-12.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The list is browsed at the Building Codes Division's "Statewide alternate methods" page, oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/pages/alternate-methods.aspx, which links out to the individual ruling documents; the request process that generates new rulings is described separately at oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/pages/alternate-method-process.aspx. 4. Does a granted Alternate Method Ruling apply automatically and immediately in every Oregon jurisdiction, or does a local building official retain any discretion to decline to apply a statewide ruling to a specific project? [CODE] (controlling agency: Oregon Building Codes Division; enforcement duty on local jurisdictions under OAR 918-008-0110)

**Answered (2026-07-12):** A granted Statewide Alternate Method Ruling applies automatically in every Oregon jurisdiction within the ruling's stated scope; the local building official does not retain discretion to decline it. OAR 918-008-0110 (Enforcement) states: "All jurisdictions administering and enforcing the state building code must enforce statewide code interpretations and allow the use of alternate method rulings consistent with the original scope of the ruling." This is mandatory language ("must enforce," "must allow"). The Statewide Alternate Method ruling documents themselves state the same rule from the applicant's side: "Building officials shall approve the use of any material, design or method of construction addressed in a statewide alternate method" (example: SAM No. 15-01). The rule also carries an enforcement mechanism against building officials: failure to comply can result in revocation or suspension of the certifications held by building officials, plans examiners, and inspectors. The one real limit on automatic application is scope: the ruling applies only "consistent with the original scope of the ruling," so a building official retains the ordinary judgment call of whether a specific project's proposal actually falls inside the ruling's stated technical scope (for example, whether a specific CLT wall configuration matches SAM 15-01's stated parameters); that is a scope-matching determination, not a discretionary refusal to apply the ruling.
   Source: OAR 918-008-0110, oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0110, checked 2026-07-12; SAM No. 15-01, oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/Documents/sam-15-01-crosslaminatedtimber.pdf.

6. For a county where BCD directly administers the building program with no local department, does that change the alternative-materials process at all: does a BCD field inspector hold the same R104.11 approval authority as a local building official, or does every alternative-materials request in a BCD-direct county route through Salem? [CODE] (controlling agency: Oregon Building Codes Division)

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Where BCD directly administers a county's program under ORS 455.148(6) (using state employees or state agencies because the county did not assume the program), the state-employed building official filling that role functions as "the building official" for purposes of ORSC R104.11 in that county: the code text assigns the R104.11 approval authority to "the building official," a defined role, not to a county employer specifically. OAR 918-098 (building official certification rules) does not create a separate, reduced approval authority for a state-employed building official versus a county-employed one; certification and duties (OAR 918-098-1470) apply uniformly by role, not by employer. A request does not automatically route through Salem headquarters merely because BCD administers the county directly; it is decided by whichever certified building official BCD has staffed to serve as the local building official for that jurisdiction, following the same R104.11 process (a written approval or a written denial with stated reasons) as in a county-run department. Where that BCD-direct building official is themselves uncertain or wants a second opinion on a novel proposal, they can request a Statewide Code Interpretation (OAR 918-008-0085) or refer the matter to the appropriate advisory board, the same escalation path available to any building official regardless of employer.
   Source: ORS 455.148(6); OAR 918-098-1470 (duties of certified building officials, inspectors, plans examiners), oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-098-1470; OAR 918-008-0085 (Statewide Code Interpretation Process), checked 2026-07-12; ORSC R104.11.
   Lands in: Oregon Code and Compliance Map.

7. Under what conditions, which wind or seismic design values, which building height, would a residential project in western Oregon's seismic design categories cross into an OSSC-governed special-inspection trigger rather than staying under the ORSC, and is that a realistic risk for a two-story single-family concrete or masonry home at this project's scale? [CODE] (controlling agency: Oregon Building Codes Division; ORSC versus OSSC applicability)

**Answered (2026-07-12):** A detached one- or two-family dwelling stays within the ORSC's scope (Oregon Residential Specialty Code, based on the 2021 IRC) by occupancy classification regardless of its structural system; ORSC applicability is not itself keyed to seismic design category. What seismic design category and above-grade wall material do control is whether the ORSC's own prescriptive provisions still apply or whether the project must use nonprescriptive (engineered) design under ORSC R301.1.3, while still being a residential-code (not OSSC) project administratively.

The specific trigger for a concrete or masonry above-grade wall: ORSC R301.2.2.5 requires that "buildings with exterior above-grade concrete walls shall comply with PCA 100 or shall be designed in accordance with ACI 318," with an exception allowing detached one- and two-family dwellings in Seismic Design Category C to use the ORSC's own prescriptive insulating-concrete-form provisions (Section R608) instead. Outside SDC C (that is, in SDC D0, D1, or D2, which cover most of western Oregon including the Willamette Valley and coastal areas), that prescriptive exception does not apply, so an above-grade concrete wall must be engineered under ACI 318 (or masonry under the parallel masonry provisions and ACI 332/TMS 402 as applicable), triggering R301.1.3 nonprescriptive design. Separately, ORSC R301.2.2.7 caps wood-framed construction at three stories above grade plane or the limits in Table R602.10.3(3), and wood-framed buildings in SDC D2 exceeding two stories must be designed under R301.1.3 as well; this wood-specific height trigger does not itself apply to a concrete or masonry structural system, which is already engineered under ACI 318/332 rather than relying on wood-frame prescriptive bracing tables.

Practically: for this project's two-story single-family concrete or masonry home in a western Oregon SDC (virtually all of western Oregon is SDC C or higher, and most of the Willamette Valley and coast range from SDC C into D-category territory depending on site soil class), triggering engineered (nonprescriptive) design under ACI 318/332 is the realistic and expected outcome rather than an edge case, since the exception that would keep concrete walls prescriptive (R608, SDC C only) is narrow. This does not mean the project leaves the ORSC and becomes an OSSC-governed commercial project; the dwelling remains under ORSC administratively (permitting, inspections, occupancy) while its structural design method is nonprescriptive engineering referencing OSSC-adopted standards (ACI 318, ACI 332, ASCE 7) via R301.1.3. Whether a true jump into full OSSC governance (as opposed to ORSC-administered engineered design) could occur would depend on occupancy or use changes (for example, a duplex/triplex above the ORSC's scope limits), not on seismic category alone; no such crossing was confirmed as a realistic risk for a single detached dwelling in this research pass.
   Source: ORSC R301.1.3, R301.2.2.5, R301.2.2.7 (2023 edition, based on 2021 IRC), text confirmed via up.codes/viewer/oregon/irc-2021, checked 2026-07-12.
   Lands in: Structural System and Concrete Design chapter (primary), cross-referenced from Oregon Code and Compliance Map by topic.

8. Do ACI 318 or ACI 332-20, already cited in the existing code-map chapter as the engineered-design basis for above-grade concrete and masonry walls in Oregon's higher seismic design categories, themselves require a form of special inspection or a testing frequency as part of using that design basis, independent of whether the ORSC separately mandates it? [CODE] (controlling agency: the engineer of record, applying ACI 318 or ACI 332-20 as the referenced standard)

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Yes, both standards carry their own inspection/testing content, though it works differently in each.

ACI 318 (Building Code Requirements for Structural Concrete) contains its own inspection chapter, Chapter 26 (Construction Documents and Inspection) in the 2019/2022 editions. Section 26.13 sets inspection requirements by category: continuous inspection is required during concrete placement, tensioning of prestressing steel and grouting of bonded tendons, installation of adhesive anchors, and placement of reinforcement in certain conditions (ACI 318 Section 26.13.3.2); periodic inspection is required for reinforcement and embedment placement, curing method and duration, formwork construction and removal, and reshoring sequence (Section 26.13.3.3). ACI 318 also requires verification of in-place concrete strength before post-tensioning stress release and before removing shores/formwork from beams and structural slabs. These are the requirements the engineer of record specifies on the construction documents; they are enforced through the special-inspection provisions of the adopted building code (in Oregon, OSSC Chapter 17 for OSSC-governed projects) rather than being self-executing.

ACI 332-20 (Code Requirements for Residential Concrete) is the narrower, residential-scoped sibling standard, covering footings, foundation walls, and slabs-on-ground for one- and two-family dwellings and townhouses. It addresses quality and testing of materials primarily by reference to the applicable ASTM standards (concrete mix, aggregate, and testing standards) rather than by prescribing its own continuous/periodic special-inspection matrix the way ACI 318 Chapter 26 does; where a residential concrete element exceeds ACI 332's scope or limits, ACI 318 governs instead, pulling in ACI 318's fuller inspection requirements.

The practical answer for this project: the special-inspection and testing requirements are not something the ORSC separately invents; they arrive already embedded in ACI 318 (and by ASTM cross-reference, in ACI 332) as soon as R301.1.3 nonprescriptive design under ACI 318/332 is triggered (see item 7 above). The engineer of record is responsible for stating the specific inspection and testing program on the construction documents per ACI 318 Chapter 26, and the local building official enforces it as part of plan review and field inspection.
   Source: ACI 318-19/-22, Chapter 26 (Construction Documents and Inspection), sections 26.13.3.2 and 26.13.3.3, as summarized in secondary code-reference material (UpCodes chapter index and ICC Building Safety Journal commentary), checked 2026-07-12; ACI 332-20 scope description, concrete.org product page and ICC codes.iccsafe.org listing. The full ACI 318 and ACI 332-20 text is a purchased standard (see `revision6_code_library/README.md`, "What Cannot Be Obtained Without Purchase"); this answer is built from free chapter indexes, tables of contents, and secondary summaries, not the complete purchased text.

12. Does a contracted building-department firm's inspector or plan reviewer carry the same Oregon-certification requirements and the same legal authority, such as stop-work orders, correction notices, and final sign-off, as a directly employed county building official, or is there any functional difference in what a private-firm reviewer can and cannot decide? [CODE] (controlling agency: Oregon Building Codes Division certification standards apply regardless of employer)

**Answered (2026-07-12):** No functional difference in authority was found; certification and legal authority attach to the individual's Oregon certification and licensed role, not to their employer. Oregon Administrative Rules (OAR 918-098) define a "contract building official" as "an owner, manager or employee of a person that the director has licensed to perform specialty code inspections and plan reviews under ORS 455.457 and that engages in the business of providing the services described in ORS 455.148(3) and 455.150(3) to one or more municipalities to which the director has delegated a building inspection program." A contract building official must hold the same Oregon Inspector Certification (and, for the building official role specifically, complete the same building-official certification training within six months of hire, per OAR 918-098) as a directly employed county building official; the certification and duties rule, OAR 918-098-1470, applies uniformly by certified role ("Persons who hold an Oregon Inspector Certification or an Oregon Code Certification must act in the public interest in performing their duties as a building official, inspector, or plans examiner, including administering and enforcing building inspection program requirements established in ORS Chapters 455 and 479") without differentiating contract employees from municipal staff. ORS 455.080 confirms the same point from the statutory side: "any inspector, including a specialty code inspector licensed under ORS 455.457, authorized by ORS 455.150 or 455.153 to determine compliance with the requirements of the state building code" carries that compliance-determination authority; the statute does not create a lesser authority for a specialty-code-licensed inspector working for a private firm. Separately, ORS 455.148(9) requires that a municipality administering a program "shall recognize and accept" the performance of state building code activities by businesses and persons authorized under ORS 455.457 "as if the activities were performed by the municipality" (with the caveat in ORS 455.148(10) that the municipality/department accepting such inspections bears no liability for the licensee's activities, which is a liability allocation rule, not an authority reduction). No source found distinguishes stop-work-order authority, correction-notice authority, or final sign-off authority by employer type; these are attributes of the certified role (building official, inspector, plans examiner) as defined and enforced under ORS chapter 455 and OAR 918-098, regardless of whether the paycheck comes from the county or from a contracted firm.
