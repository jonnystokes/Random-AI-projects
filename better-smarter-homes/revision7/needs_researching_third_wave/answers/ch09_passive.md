1. What is the actual solar-noon altitude at the winter and summer solstice for this project's specific candidate parcel latitude, computed and verified rather than assumed from a generic Willamette Valley figure? [METHOD] [TEST-TO-PROVE]
   Leads: winter noon altitude approximately 90 minus latitude minus 23.5 degrees, summer approximately 90 minus latitude plus 23.5 degrees; unverified computation for a 44-45 degree latitude gives roughly 21-22 degrees winter, 68-69 degrees summer.

**Answered (2026-07-13):** The formula is confirmed and the lead's numbers check out. Solar elevation (altitude) at solar noon equals 90 degrees minus the absolute difference between latitude and solar declination; PVEducation's standard reference states it as "the maximum solar elevation angle depends on the latitude of the location and the day of the year, and can be calculated" from latitude and declination, with declination ranging plus-or-minus 23.45 degrees between the solstices. Applying this across the actual range of Willamette Valley latitudes rather than one generic number: Eugene sits at approximately 44.05 degrees N (winter-solstice noon altitude approximately 22.5 degrees, summer approximately 69.4 degrees), Salem at approximately 44.94 degrees N (winter approximately 21.6 degrees, summer approximately 68.5 degrees), and Portland at approximately 45.5 degrees N (winter approximately 21.0 degrees, summer approximately 68.0 degrees). A high-desert eastern Oregon site (Bend, approximately 44.06 degrees N) computes almost identically to Eugene. The practical takeaway for design is that the lead's "21-22 winter / 68-69 summer" figure is a reasonable planning number across the whole Willamette Valley latitude band, but the exact value shifts by roughly half a degree of altitude per half-degree of latitude, so the actual candidate parcel's GPS latitude (not a city-center assumption) should be run through this same formula, or through a free calculator (NOAA Solar Calculator, https://gml.noaa.gov/grad/solcalc/), once a parcel is under contract, since overhang and window-head-height sizing (items 34-35) are sensitive to this number at the inch level.
   Source: PVEducation, "Elevation Angle," https://www.pveducation.org/pvcdrom/properties-of-sunlight/elevation-angle (checked 2026-07-13); latitude values for Eugene, Salem, Portland, Bend from standard geographic reference (checked 2026-07-13); formula independently computed for this answer.

2. Which sun-path analysis tool or method (Solar Pathfinder, Solmetric SunEye, a printed sun-peg chart, or a free web sun-path tool) is the right fit for a mostly-solo owner-builder doing the site's own solar-access survey, and what does each actually cost and require in skill? [TOOLS] [COST] [METHOD]
   Leads: Solar Pathfinder roughly $300-500, analog, works in any weather; University of Oregon's Baker Lighting Lab keeps a 44-degree-north sun-peg chart, a real Oregon-calibrated resource worth contacting directly.

**Answered (2026-07-13):** Four real options exist, and they split cleanly on cost, weather-dependence, and skill. (1) **Solar Pathfinder**: a non-electronic reflective dome-and-overlay device that photographs the site's actual tree and building obstructions and reads shading directly off a latitude-specific chart; it works in any weather because it does not need direct sun, and is the standard tool taught by Solar Energy International. Exact 2026 pricing was not published on the vendor site in this pass (solarpathfinder.com); historic retail has run in the $300-500 range the lead cites, consistent with SEI's shop listing it as an affordable, one-time-purchase tool; skill requirement is low (level the tool, photograph the dome, trace the treeline) but interpreting the resulting shading percentage against a design decision takes some learning. (2) **Solmetric SunEye**: an electronic fisheye-camera shade-measurement tool that computes a numeric solar-access percentage automatically; its original manufacturer (acquired by Vivint Solar in 2014) discontinued public sales in 2015, but the company was bought back by its founders in 2021 and now sells the SunEye-210 and SunEye-360 again independently (solmetric.com); used units also circulate on eBay. It is faster to read than the Pathfinder but needs sun or at least a clear sky to shoot through, and is a more expensive, more electronic (and therefore more failure-prone over a multi-year owner-build) instrument. (3) **A printed sun-peg chart** for the project's actual latitude, used with a simple sighting tool (a peg, a protractor, or a smartphone compass-and-inclinometer app) to plot obstructions by hand against the chart: this is free once printed and is the method University of Oregon's own Baker Lighting Lab teaches and stocks (44 degrees N charts, matching the Willamette Valley almost exactly), alongside a Pilkington Sun Chart, a heliodon, and loanable Solar Pathfinder and SunEye units; the lab's page gives no public contact information or confirmed public-access policy, so contacting the University of Oregon Department of Architecture directly to ask about lab access or purchasing/copying a chart is the next step, not something this pass could settle. (4) **Free web sun-path tools**: the University of Oregon's own online Sun Chart Program and Sun Path Chart (solardat.uoregon.edu, solardata.uoregon.edu) generate a location- and date-specific sun-path chart with no equipment cost at all, and SunCalc.org and the NOAA Solar Calculator do the same for any GPS coordinate; these are the zero-cost, zero-skill starting point for early site planning, but none of them substitutes for an on-site shading survey once actual trees, neighboring structures, and terrain exist to obstruct the sun, which is what the Pathfinder or SunEye are actually for. For a mostly-solo owner-builder, the practical sequence is: use the free UO/NOAA web tools during parcel shopping and early design (zero cost), then buy or borrow a Solar Pathfinder for the final on-site survey once a specific building footprint is staked (moderate cost, works in Oregon's frequently overcast weather, one-time purchase with no batteries or software to maintain over a multi-year build).
   Source: Solar Pathfinder, https://solarpathfinder.com/ and https://www.solarenergy.org/shop/tools/solar-pathfinder/ (checked 2026-07-13); Solmetric history, "What Happened to Solmetric Products?," https://www.solaris-shop.com/blog/what-happened-to-solmetric-products/, and https://www.solmetric.com/ (checked 2026-07-13); Baker Lighting Lab, "Site and Solar Analysis Tools," https://baker.uoregon.edu/lab-resources/site-and-solar-analysis-tools/ (checked 2026-07-13); UO Solar Radiation Monitoring Laboratory, https://solardata.uoregon.edu/SunPathChart.html (checked 2026-07-13).

**Answered (2026-07-13):** Confirmed with a real seasonal split, not just an annual average. Western Regional Climate Center's Oregon climate narrative states that within the Willamette Valley, prevailing wind "directions are aligned north-south with the valley" (the valley itself channels flow along its long axis), and station-level data for Eugene's Mahlon Sweet Field airport (hourly data, 1992-2002, via WRCC's prevailing-wind-direction tool) resolves that channeling into an actual seasonal pattern: south winds prevail from roughly November through March (the winter storm-track months) and switch to northwest winds from roughly April/May through September (the dry-season months), with the transition months (April, September) more mixed. This is a textbook two-season valley wind pattern, not a single annual-average direction, and it has direct siting consequences: a winter windbreak (item 52) needs to block wind arriving from the south (not the more commonly assumed west or northwest), while operable windows sited for summer cross ventilation should be oriented to catch the prevailing northwest summer flow, meaning the two design elements face genuinely different, largely opposite directions rather than reinforcing each other, which is also the direct basis for the windbreak-versus-summer-breeze conflict raised separately in item 53. A specific candidate parcel outside the immediate valley floor (a foothill site, a site with its own local terrain channeling, or an eastern Oregon high-desert site outside this wind regime entirely) should not assume this pattern without checking the nearest representative station; this figure is a Willamette-Valley-floor generalization, not a universal Oregon figure.

What building footprint and aspect ratio actually balances this project's two competing goals: a compact, low-surface-area form that minimizes total heat loss (the Passive House priority) against an elongated east-west form that maximizes south-wall area for solar gain (the classic passive-solar priority)? [METHOD] [OWNER-DESIGN-CHOICE]
   Leads: one source cites an optimum elongation near 1:1.3 along the east-west axis, unverified; Passive House form-factor targets run at or below about 0.8 per meter surface-to-volume, unverified.

**Answered (2026-07-13):** Both leads are corroborated as real, named guidelines, though from two genuinely different design traditions that this project has to choose between rather than reconcile with one number. The Passive House form-factor (surface-to-volume, S/V) guideline is confirmed at a threshold "at or below about 0.8" per meter for detached houses, with the design principle stated plainly: "the more compact a building is, the easier it is to meet an energy-efficiency standard," and the S/V ratio drives that compactness; a compact two-story 10x10 m square footprint computes to roughly 0.77 (already under the 0.8 threshold), while a genuinely elongated single-story rectangle pushes the ratio up (worse) because a longer perimeter wall wraps the same floor area. The classic passive-solar elongation guideline (an east-west-elongated rectangle, roughly 1.3-to-1.5 length-to-width, ridge running east-west) is independently confirmed across multiple passive-solar design sources as the standard recommendation to maximize south-wall area relative to floor area, though this pass did not find the specific "1.3" figure traced to one named authoritative document; it appears repeatedly as received wisdom in secondary passive-solar design literature rather than as a single citable primary-source number, so the exact ratio (1.3 vs. 1.4 vs. 1.5) should be treated as an approximate range, not an exact target. These two guidelines are in real, direct tension for this specific project: elongating the footprint to gain south wall area necessarily increases total perimeter wall area per square foot of floor, which moves the S/V ratio in the wrong direction for the Passive-House-style superinsulated envelope this project has already committed to elsewhere in the manuscript. 8. Does a two-story or partial-basement massing change the surface-to-volume calculation enough to change the compact-versus-elongated recommendation for this specific house program, given the project's daylight-basement and multi-story options already discussed in the foundation chapter? [METHOD]

**Answered (2026-07-13):** Yes, directionally and by a large enough margin to matter, confirmed by the same S/V source material used in item 7. Going vertical (two stories, or a full daylight basement counted as usable conditioned volume) is one of the most effective ways to lower S/V, because it adds floor area and volume without adding a proportional amount of new exterior wall and roof/foundation surface: the worked example already cited shows a compact two-story 10x10 m footprint at roughly 0.77 S/V versus a larger, taller compact four-story block at roughly 0.37 (the more floors stacked on the same footprint, the lower, or more favorable, the ratio becomes), because roof area and foundation footprint are shared across more floor area while wall height (and therefore wall area) grows more slowly than volume does. Applied to this project: a two-story or daylight-basement massing option directly counteracts the elongation penalty raised in item 7, because it lets the design add floor area vertically instead of by stretching the footprint east-west, which is the more S/V-efficient way to get both a reasonable south-wall length and a compact overall form at the same time. This reinforces, rather than changes, the item 7 recommendation: a two-story or daylight-basement massing is the better tool for reconciling the compact-versus-elongated tension than footprint elongation alone, because it buys south-wall length (more stories means more south-wall area at the same footprint width) without the elongation's S/V penalty. No source in this pass ran the specific numeric case for this project's actual (still undetermined) floor area and program, so the exact S/V improvement number for this house is not yet calculable; the direction and mechanism are well established.

9. How does a masonry or concrete gable end, versus a hip roof, change shading self-obstruction on east and west walls, and does the roof form chosen for structural or aesthetic reasons in the structural and wall-system chapters accidentally help or hurt the passive design? [METHOD]

**Answered (2026-07-13):** Confirmed as a real, documented effect, not just a plausible-sounding claim. A hip roof, because its overhang wraps continuously around all four sides at a roughly consistent depth, gives east and west walls a real (if partial) overhang shading benefit "shading windows nicely in summer," in the words of one passive-solar design source describing exactly this comparison. A gable roof, by contrast, has no overhang self-shading benefit at all on its gable-end wall (the triangular wall under the ridge, where the roof plane runs away from the wall rather than out over it), which specifically becomes a problem "with large gables on the front and back" when those gable ends face east and west, because there is no roof geometry left to block the low morning or afternoon sun on that wall the way a hip roof's continuous eave does. Both forms share a separate, harder limit that overhang design cannot solve regardless of roof type: low-angle east and west sun cannot be blocked by any horizontal overhang, hip or gable, because the sun is coming in nearly sideways rather than from overhead; this is precisely why item 35 (vertical fins/brise-soleil) and item 39 (exterior roller shades) exist as the real answers for east and west glare and heat, not roof form. Practically for this project: if the structural and wall-system chapters' roof form is chosen mainly for structural or aesthetic reasons without checking this interaction, an unlucky pairing (a gabled end deliberately or accidentally facing east or west, with significant glazing on that wall) will accidentally hurt the passive design by removing the one shading benefit a hip roof would have given for free; the fix is not to force a hip roof for its own sake, but to check, once the actual roof form and orientation are fixed, whether any gable end lands on the east or west face with meaningful glazing, and if so, size that wall's glazing down or add a dedicated vertical shading device rather than relying on the roof.
   Source: GreenBuildingAdvisor, "How do we take advantage of passive solar energy in this 1964 raised ranch from only the gable end?," https://www.greenbuildingadvisor.com/question/how-do-we-take-advantage-of-passive-solar-energy-in-this-1964-raised-ranch-from-only-the-gable-end (checked 2026-07-13); Inspectapedia, "Passive Solar Roof Overhangs," https://inspectapedia.com/Solar/Passive_Solar_Overhangs.php (checked 2026-07-13).
   Lands in: Passive and Bioclimatic Design, shading subsection; cross-reference to Structural System and Concrete Design and The Wall System, Foundation to Roofline (roof-form decisions) and Roof System and Roof-Wall Junctions.
