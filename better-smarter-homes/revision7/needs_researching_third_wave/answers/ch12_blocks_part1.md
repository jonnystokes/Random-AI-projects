### From the owner-made and alternative materials research (draft A)

**1. What is the actual recipe of a "zero-slump / no-slump" dry-cast concrete — target water/cement ratio, aggregate gradation, and fines content — that holds its shape the instant the mold is lifted?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Dry-cast (zero-slump/negative-slump) concrete is defined by ASTM C143 slump-test behavior, not by a fixed recipe: it is a mix stiff enough that the slump cone leaves essentially zero measurable slump, and adding more water would not raise the slump reading because the mix is governed by particle-packing friction, not fluid rheology. The commonly cited water-cementitious-materials (w/cm) ratio range for dry-cast block/pipe/paver production is roughly 0.30-0.38, with some precast sources citing as low as ~0.22 for specialized dry-cast products; the National Precast Concrete Association (NPCA) states most dry-cast specifications set a lower limit near 470 lb of cementitious material per cubic yard. Aggregate gradation favors smaller, more rounded coarse aggregate — pea gravel is the NPCA-cited default — because larger angular aggregate accelerates wear on dry-cast press/mold equipment and resists the immediate-demold packing behavior the process needs; a well-graded sand fraction (the "fines") fills the void structure so the compacted mass holds together in an unsupported state the instant the mold lifts. The mechanism that actually makes the "recipe" work is not the water/cement ratio alone but low w/cm plus mechanical energy (static pressure, vibration, or both) applied during compaction, per item 2; a dry-cast mix without adequate compaction energy will not hold shape regardless of water content.

Source: NPCA, "Demystifying Dry Cast Concrete," https://precast.org/precasttoday/q2-2024/demystifying-dry-cast-concrete/, checked 2026-07-12; NPCA, "Top 10 Facts about Dry-Cast Concrete" (0.30-0.38 w/c range, ASTM C143 zero-slump behavior, freeze-thaw resilience under ASTM C1262), https://precast.org/blog/top-10-facts-about-dry-cast-concrete/, checked 2026-07-12.

**2. Which combination of static pressure and vibration frequency produces full compaction of a zero-slump mix, and can those parameters be reproduced at owner scale?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Commercial block-machine practice combines static pressing force with vibration rather than using either alone. One frequently cited industry figure is a static pressing force on the order of 75 kg/cm² (roughly 1,070 psi) combined with vibration frequencies in the 25-50 Hz range during compaction; some higher-output machines run dual, independently controlled vibration systems (one on the mold box, one on the pressure/stripper head) with higher-frequency components (cited up to 75-120 Hz) to work the finer cement-paste fraction while the lower-frequency mold-box vibration settles the coarse aggregate. A peer-reviewed study of vibropressed paving blocks (referenced in the question's own leads) confirms the low-w/b, moderate-static-pressure-plus-vibration mechanism increases compressive strength with increasing compaction energy up to a point of diminishing return. At owner scale, the static-pressure component (75 kg/cm² on a standard block face, roughly 90-130 cm², is on the order of 7-10 tonnes of force) is achievable with a hydraulic shop press or a purpose-built hydraulic CEB press (see item 36), while the vibration component is achievable with a DIY vibrating table (item 53); reproducing the exact commercial dual-frequency, dual-head system is not realistic at owner scale, but the underlying principle — some static consolidation force plus some vibration, applied to a genuinely low-w/cm mix — is reproducible, and is the basis of most owner-scale CEB/CSEB and dry-cast approaches documented in the rest of this chapter.

Source: static-pressure/vibration-frequency figures per Qunfeng Group's block-machine vibration guidance, https://www.qunfenggroup.com/Vibration-Block-Machine, and REIT Machine's vibration-system breakdown, https://www.reitmachine.com/2026/03/04/block-machine-vibration-explained/, checked 2026-07-12; vibropressing compaction research per AIMSpress, "The effect of vibropressing compaction process on the compressive strength based concrete paving blocks," https://www.aimspress.com/article/id/5153, checked 2026-07-12.

**3. For an owner without a commercial block plant, which is more achievable for immediate demold — high static hydraulic pressing (no vibration) or vibration-plus-modest-pressure — and what press tonnage does each route need for a standard block footprint?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Both routes are documented as achievable at owner/small-operator scale, and they map onto two different equipment traditions rather than one being categorically superior. The high-static-pressure, no-vibration route is the CEB/CSEB press tradition (CINVA-Ram and its many hydraulic descendants): the Auram Press 3000, for example, is documented delivering up to 15 tons of force through a lever-multiplied hydraulic ram to compact a soil-cement block with no vibration at all, and reaches immediate demold because the mix is a stiff, damp soil-cement rather than a wet concrete. The vibration-plus-modest-pressure route is the commercial CMU/paver-block-machine tradition (item 2): a static press of roughly 75 kg/cm² (a few tonnes on a standard block face) plus 25-50 Hz vibration substitutes vibration energy for some of the static tonnage a pure-press CEB machine would otherwise need. For a standard CMU-class footprint (roughly 8x8x16 in., a face area of roughly 128 in² / 826 cm²) achieving even a modest 75 kg/cm² static-only equivalent would require on the order of 60+ tonnes of force with no vibration assist — well beyond a bottle-jack or single-cylinder shop press and into purpose-built hydraulic press territory — while the same face gets meaningful compaction from a vibrating table plus a shop-press-scale few-tonne head, which is why nearly every documented owner-scale build (items 47-58) uses vibration plus modest pressure for CMU-scale hollow block, and reserves pure high-static pressing for the smaller-footprint solid CEB/CSEB block (roughly 240x115x100 mm on the Auram, well under half a CMU face) where the smaller area makes 7-15 tons of force sufficient.

Source: Auram Press 3000 force and compression-ratio figures per Auroville Earth Institute, https://dev.earth-auroville.com/specifications-of-the-press-3000/, and Aureka, https://aureka.com/auram-press-3000/, checked 2026-07-12; block-machine static-pressure/vibration figures as in item 2.

**4. After immediate demold, what curing regime (moist cover, fog, steam, or sealed) brings a dry-cast unit from green strength up to a loadbearing target, and on what day-by-day schedule?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** NPCA's dry-cast guidance states the freshly stripped unit "will stand under its own weight to complete the curing process" without the overnight form-supported cure conventional wet-cast concrete needs, but standing on its own is not the same as reaching target strength; NPCA also states dry-cast products "can comply with specified material strength requirements in 24 hours" when production curing (commonly low-pressure/atmospheric steam, item 29/64) is used, versus the much longer schedule ambient moist curing alone requires. For ambient (non-steam) curing, the general schedule documented across sources in this chapter (items 63-67) is: keep the green unit under a moisture-retaining cover (plastic sheeting or a moist tarp) starting within the first hour after demold to prevent surface drying and crazing; maintain damp/fog conditions for at least the first 3-7 days, which is when the bulk of early hydration strength develops; the unit becomes safely stackable somewhere in that first-week window once it can carry the weight of units stacked on it (a field unit-weight/handling check, not a fixed calendar day, per item 67-68); and full design strength for ordinary portland-cement-based dry-cast concrete is conventionally referenced at 28 days, consistent with standard ASTM C39/C140 compressive-strength testing practice, even though the unit was handled and stacked well before that date. A day-by-day numeric strength-gain curve specific to a given owner mix was not found in this pass and would need to come from the owner's own test-cylinder program (ASTM C39) run in parallel with production, not a generic literature curve, since strength gain rate is mix- and temperature-dependent.

Source: NPCA dry-cast curing and 24-hour strength claims per https://precast.org/blog/top-10-facts-about-dry-cast-concrete/ and https://precast.org/precasttoday/q2-2024/demystifying-dry-cast-concrete/, checked 2026-07-12.

**5. What does a low-mold, high-throughput production line look like for one or two people making ~100 units/day — cycle time per unit, mold count, curing-yard layout, and unit handling before the material can bear its own stacking load?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** No single documented source lays out this exact one/two-person, ~100-unit/day line end to end, so this answer synthesizes across the throughput figures found elsewhere in this chapter rather than citing one design. At the low end, a manual lever press (Auram-class) documents roughly 30-40 blocks/hour for one operator and up to ~50/hour semi-automatic with an electric vibrator (item 12); at 100 units/day that implies roughly 2.5-3.5 hours of actual press time, leaving the rest of a working day for mixing, demolding/handling, and curing-yard stacking — which is why the realistic constraint on a one/two-person ~100-unit/day line is less the press cycle time itself and more the demold-to-cure-yard handling loop (each green unit must be lifted off the mold or off the press platen onto a pallet or curing rack without slumping or cracking, per item 63) and the mixer's ability to keep the press fed (item 60). Commercial low-mold African/Asian manual-machine data (item 35) shows single-mold manual machines (e.g., a Hydraform-class static manual unit) producing on the order of 35-60 "drops" (mold cycles) per hour with a 5-8 person crew — well above one/two-person capacity — which is the practical evidence that a genuine one/two-person ~100-unit/day target needs either a single-cavity press run at a sustainable pace across most of a working day, a small multi-cavity gang mold (item 41) to multiply units per cycle without multiplying operators, or acceptance that ~100/day is a burst rate rather than a steady-state rate for a true solo operator (cross-reference item 105 in The Owner-Builder's Body and Mind). Mold count follows directly from cycle time and curing-yard turnover: if a mold cannot be reused until the previous unit is off the curing rack, the owner needs enough molds (or gang-mold cavities) in rotation to bridge the gap between "just demolded, too weak to reuse the mold's follower plate on the next cycle" and "moved to a rack, mold free again," which in the documented owner builds (item 53, item 141 in Tools/Equipment) is typically solved with a bank of steel or plywood mold boxes rather than one mold doing 100 cycles a day.

Source: synthesis of items 2, 12, 35, 53, and 60-63 below, each individually cited; no single source documents the complete one/two-person 100-unit/day line.

**6. What cement percentage (by dry soil mass) does CSRE need to clear a chosen structural target, and what is the strength-vs-cement curve across the common 5% / 7% / 10% band?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Published cement-stabilized rammed earth (CSRE) studies show a clear, roughly monotonic (though non-linear and soil-dependent) strength increase with cement content across the 5-10%+ band, but the absolute numbers scatter widely by soil type, so no single curve applies to an unknown Oregon soil without site-specific testing. Representative published data points: one study reported compressive strength of 1.766, 2.85, 4.79, and 7.61 MPa at 0%, 5%, 10%, and 15% cement content respectively; another reported cement contents of 6%, 8%, and 10% producing strength increases of 41%, 80%, and 92% over an unstabilized reference mix whose own strength was 1.15 MPa; a soil with 30% clay/silt, 40% sand, 30% gravel went from about 2.68 MPa unstabilized to about 9.30 MPa at 9% cement. Across a broader literature review, cement contents around 10% are reported to reach compressive strengths over 5 MPa and in some cases up to 10 MPa. The New Mexico Earthen Building Materials Code's structural floor of 300 psi (~2.07 MPa) — cited in the question's own lead — sits near the low end of what even modest (5-6%) cement content reaches in most of these studies, meaning 5-7% cement is plausible for that specific regulatory floor on a reasonably good soil, but hitting a higher, engineer-specified structural target (and the durability-driven minimum in item 10, which commonly exceeds the strength-only minimum) requires project-specific soil testing (a Proctor curve, item 7, plus ASTM D1633 soil-cement cylinders) rather than reading a percentage off a literature curve.

**7. How is the optimum moisture content for CSRE compaction found (standard/modified Proctor), and how tightly must field moisture track that optimum to hit target density and strength?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Optimum moisture content (OMC) is found with a standard laboratory compaction (Proctor) test: a soil sample is compacted at each of roughly five different water contents using a controlled compactive energy, the dry unit weight is plotted against water content, and the peak of that curve is the OMC/maximum-dry-density point for that specific soil and that specific compactive energy. CEB/CSEB-specific sources confirm this same standard-Proctor logic is used directly to guide block production: the static OMC for a given soil sample is determined in the lab and then used to set the moisture target for the press. Once OMC is established, field moisture must be checked against it because compaction (and therefore both density and cement hydration/strength) falls off on both sides of the peak — too dry and the press cannot fully consolidate the mix even at rated force, producing crumbly, low-density blocks; too wet and the mix cannot support itself on demold (loses the "immediate demold" advantage central to this chapter's throughput goal) and can also under-compact because excess water occupies volume the press force should be closing. This research pass did not find a single universally cited numeric tolerance band (e.g., "±2% of OMC") specific to CEB/CSEB field production; the practical field proxy used in place of a lab moisture meter is the "snowball"/hand-squeeze test (item 62), which is a qualitative substitute for tracking OMC precisely enough for owner-scale production, not a substitute for establishing OMC itself in the first place via a real Proctor test on the owner's actual soil.

Source: Proctor test mechanism per Dirt Connections, https://www.dirtconnections.com/what-is-a-proctor-compaction-test/, and Geoengineer.org, https://www.geoengineer.org/education/laboratory-testing/compaction-test, checked 2026-07-12; CEB-specific application of static OMC per compressed-earth-block quality-control literature search results, checked 2026-07-12.
   Lands in: Owner-Made Blocks and Alternative Concretes.

**8. How much compaction energy does CSRE need, and what tool delivers it for a fast-demold pressed block — hand tamper vs pneumatic rammer vs hydraulic press — with the density-to-strength relationship for each?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The general mechanism is well established even though a single numeric "energy required" figure is not: compaction increases both dry density and compressive strength for a given cement content, and the strength benefit of increased compactive effort compounds with the strength benefit of increased cement content rather than substituting for it (i.e., higher compaction cannot fully make up for too little cement, and vice versa). Danso's peer-reviewed study on compacting-rate influence on CEB properties (cited in the question's own leads and confirmed in this pass) directly addresses this relationship, finding that varying the compacting rate/energy measurably changes the resulting block's mechanical properties. For tool choice: hand tamping (a manual steel-plate or wood tamper on a long handle) is the traditional, lowest-cost, lowest-energy route, historically used for both rammed earth and early hand-tamped concrete block (item 70) and still viable at very small scale but limited in achievable density and heavily dependent on operator consistency (item 52); pneumatic rammers deliver substantially more, and more consistent, compaction energy per stroke than hand tamping, at the cost of purchasing or renting powered equipment and managing compressed-air supply; hydraulic presses (CINVA-Ram-class lever presses through Auram/AECT-class powered presses, items 12, 47-51) deliver the highest and most consistent compaction force and are the standard route for anyone targeting fast, repeatable, immediate-demold block at real production volume, which is why nearly every commercial and most serious owner-scale CEB/CSEB operations documented in this chapter use a press rather than hand tamping alone. This research pass did not find a single published table converting "hand tamper vs. pneumatic rammer vs. hydraulic press" directly into a density-to-strength number for a common soil; that comparison would need to be run as a controlled trial on the owner's own soil (parallel ASTM D1633 cylinders from each compaction method) rather than assembled from disparate studies using different soils and different cement contents.

Source: Danso et al., "Influence of Compacting Rate on the Properties of Compressed Earth Blocks," Advances in Materials Science and Engineering, https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2016/8780368, checked 2026-07-12.

**9. Can CSRE be run as a pressed, immediately demolded *block* (rather than monolithic in-wall rammed earth), and does it hold enough green strength at demold to be handled and stacked?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Yes — this is exactly what a CEB (compressed earth block) or CSEB (compressed stabilized earth block) press does, and it is a mature, widely documented technique distinct from monolithic in-wall rammed earth: CSRE material (soil plus cement stabilizer, compacted near optimum moisture) is pressed in a press mold (CINVA-Ram-style lever press or a hydraulic press such as the Auram or AECT lines, items 12, 47-51) and demolded immediately, the same way dry-cast CMU is demolded immediately (item 4), because the low-water, high-compaction mix holds its own shape as soon as press pressure is released. Green (freshly pressed, uncured) CSEB units do have enough initial cohesion to be lifted off the press and moved to a curing area without collapsing — this is the entire basis of the CEB production industry documented throughout items 12-13 and 47-58 — but green strength is well below cured strength, so handling protocol still matters (item 63): freshly pressed blocks are typically set down gently on a flat curing surface or rack rather than tossed or dropped, and are not stacked under load until they have gained enough strength to carry the units above them, which for cement-stabilized block is commonly referenced as several days into the cement-hydration curing period, not the moment of demold. This confirms the question's own premise directly: CSRE is not limited to monolithic in-wall use, and unit-block CSEB production is in fact the more common form of cement-stabilized earth construction worldwide.

**10. How durable is CSRE in wet western/central Oregon — wet/dry cycling, freeze/thaw, and rain erosion — and what minimum cement content or surface treatment is needed for durability (which may exceed the content needed for strength alone)?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** No source found in this pass tested CSRE specifically in a western/central-Oregon climate, so this answer states the governing durability tests and the general finding that durability, not compressive strength, is usually the governing design criterion for stabilized earth in a wet climate — it does not manufacture an Oregon-specific pass/fail number that was not found. The controlling US test methods for this exact question are ASTM D559 (wetting-and-drying) and ASTM D560 (freezing-and-thawing) for compacted soil-cement mixtures: both measure soil-cement mass loss, water-content change, and volume (swell/shrinkage) change under repeated wet-dry or freeze-thaw cycling, both are run on specimens compacted to maximum density at optimum moisture per ASTM D558, and both were developed specifically to be used with the Portland Cement Association's Soil-Cement Laboratory Handbook criteria for determining the minimum cement content needed to resist field weathering — i.e., these standards exist precisely because the cement content needed to survive wet/dry and freeze/thaw cycling is a separate, and often higher, threshold than the cement content needed to hit a bare compressive-strength number, directly confirming the question's own premise. Practically, both D559 and D560 test programs take about a month to run per PCA guidance, which is why durability qualification is a real schedule item, not a quick check. No specific minimum cement-content number calibrated to Oregon's wet, mild-freeze climate (as distinct from the harsher freeze-thaw regions where most published soil-cement durability data originates, or the arid New Mexico context behind the 300 psi/8% figures cited elsewhere in this chapter) was found in this pass; that number would come from actually running D559/D560 on the owner's stabilized mix, or from an engineer's judgment call informed by the closest analogous published climate data, not from a literature figure claiming to already answer the Oregon case.

**11. Which cheap additives raise CSRE strength or durability without killing fast demold — hydrated lime, fly ash, a splash of sodium silicate, or short fibers — and at what dose does each help vs hurt?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Each of the named additives has documented, but individually distinct, effects and doses in the CEB/CSRE and fly-ash-composite literature:

**Hydrated lime**, combined with cement, is documented to improve the *long-term* strength of CSEB — one study title directly names "Role of Lime with Cement in long-term strength of Compressed Stabilized Earth Blocks" — because lime supplies additional calcium hydroxide that continues a slow pozzolanic reaction with clay/silica in the soil well past the point cement hydration alone plateaus; this is a durability/long-term-strength benefit rather than a fast-demold benefit, and lime does not need a specific hurt-threshold dose caution the way sodium silicate does, but it is not a fast-set accelerant either.

**Fly ash** functions as a pozzolan/partial cement or lime replacement rather than a pure additive; one cited optimum raw-material composition (a different fly-ash-based composite, not necessarily this project's soil) is 68% fly ash, 20% sand, 12% hydrated lime, illustrating fly ash can be a primary binder component at high dose in some systems, not just a minor addition.

**Sodium silicate** is documented in the 0.25-1.5 wt% range relative to fly ash in cement-fly-ash systems, with a preferred 0.5-1.0 wt% range, and compressive-strength gains that plateau (stop improving) above roughly 1.5 wt%; in CEB stabilization specifically, sodium silicate is documented to increase ductility of the block (a shift toward more gradual, less brittle failure) compared to lime-stabilized blocks, which failed in a more brittle mode — a durability/toughness benefit distinct from raw compressive strength.

**Short fibers** (natural or synthetic) are covered in depth under items 25-26 below; the short version is that natural fibers are typically dosed at 0.25-1.5% and raise toughness/crack resistance more than raw compressive strength, and do not themselves accelerate or slow demold timing the way a chemical additive can.

None of these four additives is documented in this pass as *hurting* fast demold at the doses cited; the dose-dependent hurt documented is specifically sodium silicate's compressive-strength plateau above ~1.5 wt%, which wastes material rather than damaging the block.

**12. How do the CEB press classes compare for an owner — manual CINVA-ram, mid-range manual/mechanical, and hydraulic machines — on compaction pressure, blocks/hour, resulting green strength, and price?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Three real, well-documented press classes:

**Manual lever press (CINVA-Ram class).** The original CINVA-Ram, designed by Chilean engineer Raúl Ramírez at the Inter-American Housing Center (CINVA) in Bogotá in 1952, uses a hand lever requiring roughly 80 lb (36 kg) of applied force, multiplied through toggle-lever geometry, to compact a block roughly 290x140x100 mm; documented cured block bearing strength is roughly 200-500 psi (14-35 kg/cm²). The machine itself weighs roughly 140 lb (63 kg) and is designed to be fabricable by a local welder from plate steel, which is why it spread globally. Modern manual descendants such as the Auram Press 3000 (India) apply up to 15 tons of force through a similar lever-hydraulic mechanism, with documented output of roughly 30-40 blocks/hour manually operated, rising to roughly 50/hour with an added electric vibrator, against a theoretical maximum near 106-125 blocks/hour under ideal stroke-count conditions.

**Mid-range manual/mechanical presses**, such as Hydraform's static manual models, are documented at roughly 35-60 mold cycles ("drops") per hour but require a 5-8 person crew, not a solo/pair operation, which is the key throughput-vs-labor tradeoff at this tier.

**Hydraulic/powered machines**, such as the AECT Impact 2001A (7 HP diesel-powered hydraulic), are documented producing roughly 300 blocks/hour (roughly 2,400 blocks in an 8-hour day); AECT's larger 2500HD/3500-class machines are documented up to 480 blocks/hour.

**14. Is a one-part "just add water" geopolymer (dry, pre-blended solid activator) practical for an owner, and what strength does it reach vs the messier two-part liquid-activator route?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Yes, one-part ("just add water") geopolymer systems are a real and actively researched category, not a theoretical concept: a solid aluminosilicate precursor is pre-blended with a solid alkali activator, and water is added at the point of use exactly like ordinary portland cement, which removes the need to store, meter, and handle a corrosive liquid alkaline activator on site — directly practical for an owner-scale operation from a safety and logistics standpoint (item 15 covers just how corrosive the two-part liquid route is). **15. What is a workable two-part activator recipe and its handling/safety protocol — NaOH molarity, sodium-silicate-to-hydroxide ratio, silicate modulus, mixing order, PPE, and heat of solution?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Published two-part geopolymer activator formulations cluster in reasonably consistent ranges, though exact optima vary by precursor (fly ash vs. slag vs. metakaolin) and by which property (strength vs. durability) is being optimized:

**NaOH molarity:** commonly 10-14 M in the studies surveyed (10 M, 12 M, and 14 M NaOH are directly cited as tested concentrations in comparative studies).

**Sodium-silicate-to-NaOH ratio:** studied across roughly 1.0-3.0, with specific tested points at 1.0, 1.4, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 2.7, and 3.0; a ratio in the 1.6-2.3 band is reported to improve durability (reduced deterioration in simulated marine/aggressive exposure) relative to ratios outside that band. The question's own lead cites ~0.4 for a NaOH/Na2SiO3 ratio, which is the inverse framing of the same relationship (i.e., roughly consistent with a silicate-to-hydroxide ratio in the low single digits).

**Silicate modulus (SiO2/Na2O):** the optimal molar ratio is reported as roughly 0.8-1.2 in one source (too high a ratio makes the solution too viscous and hinders the reaction; too low reduces ion concentration needed for reaction); the question's own lead cites a modulus of 1.31-1.43 for a different reported system, illustrating this parameter is genuinely mix-specific rather than fixed.

**Mixing order and heat of solution:** this pass did not find a single authoritative step-by-step mixing-order protocol specific to owner-scale geopolymer batching, but the general chemistry is unambiguous and directly transferable from standard NaOH-handling practice: dissolving solid NaOH in water is strongly exothermic, and the safety literature is explicit that concentrated NaOH and concentrated sodium silicate solutions are both highly alkaline (pH > 13) and corrosive, causing chemical burns and eye damage, and requiring specialized storage and protective equipment even at industrial scale, let alone owner scale. Standard safe-handling practice for concentrated caustic solutions generally (not geopolymer-specific, but directly applicable) is to add the solid alkali slowly to water — never water to the solid — in a well-ventilated area, wearing chemical-splash goggles, face shield, chemical-resistant (not ordinary nitrile) gloves, and protective clothing, and to allow the heat of solution to dissipate (often by preparing the activator solution well ahead of the mix, since it also needs time to cool before combining with the aluminosilicate precursor in most published procedures).

**16. How much ground-granulated slag must go into a fly-ash geopolymer to get ambient-cure early strength (avoiding the heat-cure that pure fly-ash systems need), and what 1-day and 28-day strengths result?**

At a 30% slag replacement level in a fly-ash-slag mortar, one study reports 3-day compressive strength jumping from 1.5 MPa (near-zero slag) to 33.5 MPa, and 28-day strength rising from 34.7 MPa to 73.4 MPa — an order-of-magnitude change in early strength from slag alone. For ambient-cured geopolymer *bricks* specifically (as distinct from paste/mortar), documented Class F fly-ash-and-slag brick strengths at 28 days range 16.7-25.3 MPa in one study and 30.5-51.7 MPa in another, both well above typical CMU structural targets, confirming slag-assisted ambient-cured fly-ash geopolymer brick is a real, achievable owner-relevant strength class, not just a lab curiosity. **18. What causes geopolymer efflorescence and how is it mitigated, and what is the long-term durability of ambient-cured geopolymer in a wet climate?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Geopolymer efflorescence is caused by unreacted alkali (sodium, from NaOH and/or sodium silicate activator that did not fully consume in the geopolymerization reaction) migrating to the surface with moisture and carbonating or crystallizing as a visible white salt deposit — mechanistically similar to portland-cement efflorescence (soluble calcium salts migrating and carbonating) but driven by residual activator alkali rather than calcium hydroxide. This pass did not independently verify a specific numeric efflorescence-reduction figure beyond the qualitative direction (slag helps, lower silicate modulus helps) already stated in the question's own lead, and did not find a long-term (multi-year, field-exposure) durability study of ambient-cured geopolymer specifically in a wet, temperate climate comparable to western Oregon; the bulk of the published durability literature on geopolymer is either short-duration lab simulated-aging (wet-dry cycling, sulfate exposure) or field data from hot/arid or tropical climates, not the mild wet climate this project needs.

**19. How do natural hydraulic lime (NHL) grades — NHL 2, 3.5, 5 — differ in set time and strength, and can an owner reach a structural target with NHL plus pozzolan?**

**22. What is the sulfur concrete mix and melt/mix procedure, and what are the real safety constraints (SO2/H2S fumes, ignition, hot handling) for an owner working at ~130-140 °C?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Sulfur concrete mix proportions are documented reasonably consistently: elemental sulfur (refined to at least ~99.5% purity in most sources) at roughly 12-30% of the mix by weight (the question's own lead cites ~20% sulfur, 32% fine aggregate, 48% coarse aggregate, sometimes with ~5% silica flour added as a filler/plasticizer aid), with the balance made up of ordinary sand and gravel aggregate; no water or hydraulic cement is used at all, which is the material's defining difference from portland-cement concrete. Because pure sulfur is brittle, modifiers such as dicyclopentadiene (DCPD) or styrene are commonly added at roughly 1-5% relative to the sulfur content to improve ductility and long-term stability. The process procedure is: melt the sulfur (melting point 115.21 °C / 239.38 °F) and heat to a working/mixing temperature of roughly 130-140 °C, blend in the preheated dry aggregate at that temperature (aggregate is typically preheated so it does not chill and prematurely solidify the molten sulfur on contact), cast into molds while still molten and fluid, and allow to cool and solidify — the "cure" is a physical phase change (cooling/freezing), not a chemical hydration reaction, which is why strength gain is fast (see item 23) and why the material is fully re-meltable/recyclable.

**Real safety constraints:** molten sulfur is a fire hazard, capable of igniting above roughly 232 °C or in the presence of contaminants; any sulfur fire produces toxic and corrosive sulfur dioxide (SO2) and sulfur trioxide (SO3) fumes, which further react with moisture to form sulfuric acid (H2SO4); heating sulfur above roughly 300 °F (~149 °C, i.e., not far above the normal 130-140 °C working range if overheated or if a hot spot develops) can produce harmful quantities of toxic gases including hydrogen sulfide (H2S) as well as SO2. This means the owner's real operating margin between the normal working temperature and a hazardous over-temperature condition is narrow — perhaps only 10-20 °C — making continuous temperature monitoring (not just a one-time check) and good ventilation (fume extraction, since both SO2 and H2S are toxic by inhalation) genuine, non-optional safety requirements, alongside the obvious molten-material burn hazard of any hot-pour process.

**24. What steel-fiber dose and geometry meaningfully raise flexural strength and toughness of an owner mix, and does it change the compression-test result the permit relies on?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Typical steel-fiber dosage in structural/industrial concrete practice is documented at roughly 20-50 kg/m³ (about 30-95 lb/yd³ at the upper end for industrial slab work), with hooked-end fiber specifically tested at 20, 40, and 60 kg/m³ (0.25%, 0.50%, and 0.75% by volume) in comparative research; hooked-end geometry (a mechanical anchor at each fiber end) is the dominant commercial geometry because the hook, not just fiber length or count, is what generates the crack-bridging pullout resistance that produces the flexural-toughness benefit. **26. For natural fibers (basalt, sisal, jute, coir), what dose helps vs hurts compressive strength, and how is the long-term fiber-rot / alkaline-degradation problem solved (treatment or pozzolan matrix)?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Natural fibers (jute, sisal, coir) are typically dosed at 0.25-1.5% by the applicable mix measure, with some studies using 1-1.5% treated sisal specifically; at these doses, workability drops (a stiffer, harder-to-place mix) while mechanical properties improve. On compressive strength specifically: concrete blocks reinforced with 2% by weight of cellulosic/mineral fiber blends showed improved compressive performance in one study, and treating sisal fiber with a pozzolanic material improved compressive strength by an average of 21.75%, versus a 12.83% average improvement from alkaline treatment alone, both relative to an untreated control — meaning treatment method, not just dose, materially changes the strength outcome, and pozzolan treatment outperformed alkaline treatment in this comparison. The long-term fiber-rot/alkaline-degradation problem (natural cellulose fiber degrading in cement's highly alkaline pore-water environment over time, losing the reinforcement benefit and potentially leaving voids) is real and documented as a genuine durability limit, not a solved non-issue: studies note sugarcane and coconut fibers specifically "could not prevent cracking very significantly due to fiber degradation in the alkaline environment of cement." Two mitigation routes are documented: **treatment of the fiber itself** before mixing — alkali treatment (NaOH solution), thermal treatment (150-200 °C), or calcined-bentonite treatment are all shown to remove lignin and surface impurities that would otherwise be attacked, improving both bond and durability; and **a lower-alkalinity binder matrix** — since the degradation mechanism is driven specifically by the very high pH of portland-cement pore water, a lime-pozzolan or geopolymer matrix (both generally lower or differently buffered in alkalinity than pure portland cement, though geopolymer's own high-pH activator is a separate consideration per item 15) may be inherently gentler on natural fiber than straight portland cement, though this pass did not find a study directly comparing natural-fiber survival in a portland matrix versus a lime-pozzolan or geopolymer matrix side by side.

**27. How do accelerators compare for an owner — calcium chloride vs non-chloride (calcium nitrate/formate) — on dose, early-strength gain, and downsides (shrinkage, corrosion, discoloration)?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Calcium chloride (CaCl2) is dosed at roughly 1-2% by weight of cement (e.g., 1-2 lb of CaCl2 per 100 lb of cement), with 2% treated as a practical ceiling; exceeding that range is documented to risk excessive shrinkage, reinforcement corrosion, and compromised long-term strength. CaCl2's real downsides are well documented: chloride ions actively stimulate corrosion of embedded steel over time (a serious concern for any reinforced application, including rebar-doweled or wire-reinforced owner-made block), and CaCl2-dosed concrete shrinks and creeps more during curing, which can crack the surface. Non-chloride accelerators (calcium nitrate, calcium formate, and similar chemistries) provide comparable set-acceleration and early-strength development without the corrosion risk or the discoloration some chloride-based products can cause, at a higher material cost than CaCl2 — cost is the primary reason CaCl2 remains "the first choice for non-reinforced concrete" per industry guidance, since its corrosion risk is irrelevant where there is no embedded steel to corrode. The question's own lead cites non-chloride accelerator doses of roughly 0.5-2% or 10-45 fl oz per 100 lb of cement, which this pass found consistent in direction (non-chloride products are typically liquid-dosed by fluid ounces per hundredweight of cement rather than by dry percentage) but did not independently re-verify against a specific manufacturer's data sheet. **For this project specifically:** because owner-made block intended for a loadbearing wall will very likely contain embedded steel (rebar dowels, bond-beam steel, or wire reinforcement, per the Masonry Craft and Structural System chapters), the corrosion risk makes calcium chloride the wrong default choice for any reinforced application even though it is cheaper; a non-chloride accelerator is the safer default wherever steel is present, with CaCl2 reserved only for genuinely unreinforced elements (if any exist in this project's design) where its lower cost has no corrosion downside to weigh against.

**29. What owner-scale steam or hot-box curing setup (insulated chamber, temperature, humidity, schedule) gets same-day or next-day strength without cracking or delayed-ettringite problems?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Standard precast-industry steam-curing schedule structure — directly applicable to an owner-scale insulated hot-box, at reduced scale — has four phases, each with a documented target range: a **delay period** before steam exposure begins, minimum 30 minutes and more typically two to three hours (or until the concrete reaches initial set), because early steam exposure before set is documented as detrimental to final strength and durability despite being common practice; a **ramp period**, limited to a temperature rise of no less than 20 °F (11 °C) per hour and no more than 40 °F (22 °C) per hour — exceeding 40 °F/hour risks thermal-shock cracking, while going below 20 °F/hour needlessly slows the cure; a **hold period** at the target temperature, held until the concrete reaches its desired strength (duration depends on the specific mix and steam temperature, not a fixed universal number); and a **cooling period** back to ambient, which the source material does not detail with a specific rate but which follows the same thermal-shock-avoidance logic as the ramp-up. The critical upper temperature limit, directly relevant to the DEF (delayed ettringite formation) risk this chapter's own questions flag (items 89-90, cross-referenced to Designing for Centuries): NPCA's Quality Control Manual states ambient curing temperature "shall not exceed 150 °F (65 °C) unless measures to prevent delayed ettringite formation are employed," and separate research confirms curing steam temperatures above roughly 80 °C measurably degrade concrete microstructure and later-age strength via DEF — meaning an owner-scale hot-box should be built and monitored to stay at or below roughly 150 °F / 65 °C internal concrete temperature as a default safe ceiling, not simply "as hot as the box can get," and any design pushing higher needs specific DEF-mitigating measures (e.g., a lower-C3A cement, per the Designing for Centuries cross-reference) that this pass did not further specify. This pass did not find a specific, complete, owner-built (non-commercial) hot-box design (materials, insulation R-value, heat source, humidity control) documented step by step; the schedule parameters above are transferable from commercial precast practice, but the actual box construction is a DIY-fabrication question addressed more directly in item 64 below.

**30. Is CO2 carbonation curing feasible at owner scale for dry-cast blocks (a chamber flooded with combustion or bottled CO2), and what strength and speed does it add?**

**37. How is a mold designed for genuine fast demold and reuse — draft angles, split/gang molds, release agents, liners — so a handful of molds can turn ~100 units/day?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Draft (the taper built into a mold's internal faces so the cast/pressed unit releases without binding) is the foundational design element: the commonly cited draft angle for casting concrete generally is roughly 5-15 degrees, with about 10 degrees a comfortable average, while paver-scale concrete molds use a shallower 1-2 degree sidewall taper to prevent binding and reduce demolding force — the shallower angle at paver scale reflects the much smaller, shallower cavity depth (less total wall-to-part contact area to overcome) compared to a taller block or general casting shape. NPCA's dry-cast guidance (item 4) independently confirms this principle in commercial practice: dry-cast products are given a slight taper, typically 1/8 to 1/4 in., specifically to enable immediate form removal and reduce friction — a smaller absolute taper than the "5-15 degree" general figure, but consistent with the same shallow-face, fast-strip logic as the paver figure. **Split/gang molds** (item 41 covers this in more depth) let a single mold-handling cycle produce multiple units, directly multiplying units-per-cycle without multiplying operators — the mechanism by which commercial block machines and larger CEB presses reach the 100+/day range covered in item 35 without a proportionally larger crew. **Release agents** are documented to meaningfully improve strip reliability — one source cites a mold-release agent boosting casting success rate by 70% — and **liners** (steel facing on a wood mold, an HDPE liner, or a cast rubber liner per item 44) both improve release quality and protect the base mold material from the abrasive wear a zero-slump mix inflicts on repeated cycles (item 39-40 cover the material-durability side directly). Put together, a mold built to turn ~100 units/day with only a handful of physical molds needs: adequate draft on every internal face (not just the obvious ones), a release agent applied on a defined reapplication schedule (item 143 in Tools/Equipment covers buildup/tolerance drift from over-infrequent reapplication), and either a multi-cavity gang layout or a fast enough single-cavity cycle time (item 5) to cycle through the day's target without idle mold time waiting on the curing yard.

**HDPE**: rigid, low-friction (a coefficient of friction close to Teflon), and machinable with ordinary woodworking tools, giving genuinely good release quality without a separate liner; HDPE does not corrode or absorb moisture the way plywood does, but this pass did not find a specific reuse-count figure for HDPE block molds under repeated compaction/vibration abrasion specifically (as distinct from HDPE's general durability reputation).

**42. What dimensional tolerance must an owner-built mold actually hold so finished blocks lay true in a wall — allowable variation in height, length, and squareness, and how much does the mortar joint (or dry-stack interlock) forgive?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The controlling US commercial-manufacturing benchmark is ASTM C90 (Standard Specification for Loadbearing Concrete Masonry Units): overall unit dimensions (width, height, length) may vary by no more than ±1/8 in. (3.2 mm) from the specified dimension, and molded features (ribs, scores) may vary by no more than ±1/16 in. (1.6 mm), with architectural/textured units (split-face, slump block) explicitly exempted to a looser, supplier-determined tolerance because random texture is the intended appearance. The question's own lead cites a much tighter commercial die-gap tolerance (±0.2-0.5 mm with a 0.8-1 mm die gap) — this pass found that figure is consistent with precision steel-mold manufacturing tolerance (how accurately the mold itself is machined), not the ASTM C90 finished-unit tolerance the wall-construction trade actually works to; these are two different tolerance layers (mold-machining precision vs. finished-unit acceptance tolerance), and ASTM C90's ±1/8 in. is the one directly relevant to whether a block "lays true in a wall." A conventional mortared masonry joint is explicitly designed to absorb exactly this class of unit-to-unit variation: standard mortar joint thickness (commonly 3/8 in. in US masonry practice) has enough range to take up small unit-height and unit-length variation course to course, which is precisely why ASTM C90's ±1/8 in. tolerance is workable in ordinary mortared construction — the joint is the forgiveness mechanism. Dry-stack/mortarless interlocking systems (item 46, item 101) forgive far less: because there is no mortar bed to absorb variation, dry-stack CSEB/ICEB systems require tighter dimensional consistency unit to unit than mortared CMU, which is exactly why commercial CEB/CSEB presses (Auram, AECT, item 12) are built to a much tighter dimensional-repeatability standard than a simple hand-tamped block — a home-built dry-stack mold would need to hold much closer to the mold's own machining tolerance, with far less mortar-joint margin to hide any owner-built mold's dimensional drift.

**46. Do interlocking / dry-stack (mortarless, ICEB-style) block molds change mold complexity and required press force versus plain CMU, and is the layup-speed payoff worth the harder mold for an owner-builder?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Yes to the first half: interlocking/dry-stack compressed stabilized earth block (ICSEB) molds are documented as more geometrically complex than a plain rectangular CMU/CEB mold, because they must form matching tongue-and-groove or peg-and-socket interlocking features on the block faces in addition to the plain block outline, and hollow interlocking units (which also carry the perforations used for grout/reinforcement introduction, per the question's own lead) add still more internal mold geometry — more cavity faces, more draft-angle surfaces to get right per item 37, and tighter dimensional-repeatability requirements per item 42, since a dry-stack system has no mortar bed to absorb unit-to-unit variation the way mortared CMU does. This pass did not find a source directly quantifying whether required *press force* itself changes for an interlocking versus plain mold at the same block volume (the interlocking geometry is a shape complexity question more than a compaction-force question, and the underlying soil-cement or CMU material being compacted is unchanged), so no citable force-difference figure is given here. On the layup-speed payoff: the structural literature is genuinely favorable to interlocking systems on more than just speed — one study found dry-stacking alone (0% mortar) versus using 20.4% mortar volume in an otherwise-similar wall showed the mortared assembly with 46% higher flexural bond strength and 23% higher shear strength than the pure dry-stack case, meaning interlocking dry-stack construction is documented to reduce, not eliminate, mortar's structural contribution — a real design trade-off, not simply "faster for free." Separately, interlocking CSEB is documented reducing both conventional mortar thickness/quantity needed and unskilled-labor requirements, and is credited with greater in-plane and out-of-plane shear resistance than a comparable non-interlocking system in the same research. **54. What vibration frequency and amplitude best consolidate a zero-slump block mix, how do you measure and tune them, and what are the signs of over-vibration (segregation, laitance, slumped edges on strip)?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** For stiff/low-slump mixes specifically, low-frequency, high-amplitude vibration is documented as the correct target, the opposite tuning direction from a fluid, high-slump mix — a directly relevant finding since it confirms the question's own implicit premise that zero-slump mix needs different vibration tuning than ordinary wet-cast concrete, not just "more of the same" vibration. This is consistent with item 2's commercial block-machine figures (roughly 25-50 Hz for the primary mold-box vibration, with higher-frequency 75-120 Hz components reserved for working the fine paste fraction specifically, not the bulk compaction task). This pass did not find a specific, simple field method for an owner to measure frequency/amplitude directly (no low-cost tachometer-and-accelerometer procedure was documented in the sources reviewed); the practical tuning method documented across sources is empirical/observational rather than instrumented — running a test batch and visually/physically checking the compacted result. **Signs of over-vibration**, drawn from general concrete-vibration literature (not zero-slump-specific, but directly transferable): laitance (a weak, cement-and-fines-rich layer rising to the surface, typically associated with excess water being driven upward by vibration); aggregate segregation (heavier aggregate settling while lighter paste rises), though the same general guidance notes well-proportioned, properly-consistent mixes are not very susceptible to segregation from over-vibration — it is specifically an excess-mortar or excess-water mix that segregates under prolonged vibration, again tying back to the low-w/cm discipline central to item 1's dry-cast recipe; and, per general vibration-duration guidance, stiffer/lower-slump mixes are recommended toward the longer end of a roughly 30-65-second-per-point vibration-duration range rather than the shorter end reserved for more fluid mixes — meaning under-vibration, not over-vibration, may be the more common practical risk for a genuinely stiff zero-slump mix, since it takes measurably longer to fully consolidate than a wetter mix does. **57. How do you size the vibrator motor (kW, rpm, eccentric moment) to the combined mass of a loaded mold plus table so consolidation is actually effective rather than just noisy?**

**59. Why does a stiff/zero-slump block mix need a pan or planetary (forced-action) mixer rather than a tilting drum, and how badly does a drum actually fail on this mix?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The mechanism is well documented and mechanically unambiguous: a drum mixer relies on gravity — the rotating drum lifts material with internal fins and lets it tumble/fold back down under its own weight — which works for a flowable, wet mix but generates essentially no useful mixing action on a genuinely stiff, low-water mix, because a stiff mix does not tumble or fold under gravity the way a fluid mix does; industry sources state this plainly: "drum mixers cannot handle low-slump or zero-slump mixes... the drum rotation simply does not generate enough shear force to hydrate stiff concrete." A pan (forced-action) mixer instead uses rotating paddles or blades within a stationary or slowly rotating pan to actively force material through a shearing action regardless of whether it flows under gravity, which is why pan mixers are documented producing consistent mixes "from zero-slump dry-cast pipe concrete all the way up to high-slump architectural mixes" — the forced-action design works across the whole slump range, while a drum mixer is fundamentally gravity-dependent and therefore range-limited to wetter mixes. On "how badly" a drum fails: the sources are explicit that a drum mixer does not partially or poorly mix a zero-slump batch — it essentially fails to mix it at all in any useful sense, since there is no gravity-driven tumbling action for a mix stiff enough to hold its own shape; this is a categorical failure (wrong tool for the material), not a degraded-but-workable outcome, and is the direct, mechanistic reason every dry-cast, CEB, or CSEB batching operation documented in this chapter's other items (whenever mixer type is specified) uses a pan or forced-action mixer rather than a rotating-drum concrete mixer of the kind a homeowner would already own for ordinary wet-cast concrete work.

**62. How do you control mix water for zero-slump — the "snowball"/hand-squeeze field test for optimum moisture, and how do over- and under-watering each show up in demold behavior and final strength?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The hand-squeeze ("snowball") test is documented as a genuine, standard field method: a handful of the mix is squeezed by hand, and the result is judged by whether it forms a stable, cohesive ball — correct moisture holds together without crumbling apart and without leaving visible free water or oozing moisture on the hand; too dry, and the squeezed handful crumbles apart rather than holding a ball shape; too wet, and it oozes visible water or leaves the hand noticeably wet/muddy. This is explicitly a qualitative substitute for laboratory Proctor-curve moisture measurement (item 7), not a replacement for establishing the actual optimum moisture content (OMC) in the first place — the snowball test tells an operator whether today's batch feels close to a moisture target already established by proper testing, it does not itself determine what that target should be. On demold and strength consequences: **under-watering** (too dry) leaves insufficient moisture for the press/tamper to achieve full particle rearrangement and consolidation even at rated compaction force, producing a lower-density, weaker, more crumbly block that may not even hold together on demold; **over-watering** (too wet) causes the mix to lose the cohesion and internal friction that make immediate demold possible at all (item 1's core dry-cast mechanism), so an over-wet unit is prone to slumping, bulging, or collapsing right at or immediately after demold, and even where it does hold shape briefly, excess water increases the paste's ultimate porosity (a higher effective water-cement ratio) which lowers final cured strength — the same fundamental w/c-vs-strength relationship underlying item 1's entire dry-cast recipe logic, just observed here from the too-wet failure side rather than the recipe-design side. Because both directions of error degrade the result (through different mechanisms — under-compaction on the dry side, lost cohesion and higher effective w/c on the wet side), the snowball test's practical value is exactly that it gives an immediate, no-equipment field check before a full batch is committed to the press, rather than discovering a moisture problem only after a bad block fails to demold cleanly or crumbles later.

**68. What day-to-day field QC proves compaction is holding without breaking a specimen — the weigh-a-block / target-unit-weight method, moisture checks, dimension checks — and what reject criteria should trigger a stop?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The underlying principle behind the weigh-a-block method is well documented even where a specific numeric procedure was not found spelled out step by step: dry density (and therefore unit weight, for a fixed mold volume) increases directly with the weight of soil/mix placed in the mold and with increasing compaction energy for a given moisture content, meaning a properly compacted unit of known mold volume should consistently weigh within a predictable range once the mix design and target density are established — a unit weighing meaningfully less than that target range is, by direct physical implication, under-compacted (more voids, lower density, lower strength) even without breaking it to test compressive strength directly. This makes weigh-a-block a genuine non-destructive proxy for compaction quality: weigh a sample unit right after demold (or at a consistent, repeatable moisture state), compare against the established target weight for that mold volume and mix design, and treat a significant negative deviation as a compaction-quality flag. Dimension checks (height, length, squareness against the mold's own nominal dimensions, item 42) catch a different failure mode — mold wear, mix flow/slump problems, or press misalignment — rather than compaction density directly, and moisture checks (the snowball test, item 62, or a more precise field moisture meter if available) catch the input-variable most likely to be driving an out-of-spec weight or dimension result in the first place. This pass did not find a specific, citable numeric reject-criteria threshold (e.g., "reject any unit below X% of target weight") for owner-scale CEB/block production; the correct, honest answer is that the reject threshold should be established by the owner (or their engineer) from their own established target-weight/target-density baseline for their specific mix and mold, calibrated against periodic actual ASTM C39/D1633 compression testing (item 67) to confirm that a given weight/dimension deviation actually correlates with a strength shortfall for their specific material — a borrowed percentage from unrelated literature would not be a safe substitute for that calibration.
