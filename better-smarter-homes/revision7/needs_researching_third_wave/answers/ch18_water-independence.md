**1.** For each roof material this project is actually considering (standing-seam metal, bare or coated flat concrete deck, membrane roofing), what is the verified runoff coefficient and the verified water-quality profile (pH, metals, organic load) from a primary source rather than a secondary aggregator?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Two primary sources carry this: the Texas Water Development Board's *Texas Manual on Rainwater Harvesting* (3rd ed., 2005) for collection efficiency, and the Washington State Department of Ecology's *Roofing Materials Assessment: Investigation of Toxic Chemicals in Roof Runoff* (Publication 14-03-033, the Round 2 study, 20 rain events on pilot 4-by-8-ft panels) for measured pH and metals.

*Runoff coefficient / collection efficiency.* Smooth metal is the benchmark: standing-seam/Galvalume metal is the most efficient catchment surface and the TWDB manual's worked examples use 0.85 as a conservative whole-system collection efficiency across materials, with metal at the top of the range. Concrete/clay tile is docked roughly 10 percent for porosity, texture, and evaporation (i.e., an effective coefficient near 0.80-0.85) unless painted or sealed; composite/asphalt shingle likewise loses ~10 percent. TWDB manual, pp. 8-9 and Chapter 4, `https://www.twdb.texas.gov/publications/brochures/conservation/doc/RainwaterHarvestingManual_3rdedition.pdf`. A bare or coated *flat concrete deck* is smoother than tile and would run closer to the metal figure than the tile figure, but no primary study measured a flat cast-in-place concrete roof's coefficient specifically; treat 0.80-0.90 as the defensible design band and confirm against the actual finished surface.

*Measured water-quality profile (WA Ecology Round 2, median across 20 events; glass control ~pH 4.6-5.2, i.e. Puget Sound rain is acidic):*
- Standing-seam / Zincalume (Galvalume) metal: runoff pH near-neutral, but median total zinc 114 ug/L and painted-galvanized 52 ug/L — both statistically elevated over the ~4 ug/L glass control; copper and lead low. Zinc is the flag for a bare metallic/galvanized surface. (Painted or baked-enamel Galvalume mitigates this, per TWDB.)
- Concrete tile (CTI): the cleanest of the elevated-pH group — highest median runoff pH at 7.2 (the calcium/magnesium matrix neutralizes acidic rain), median zinc 4.3 ug/L, copper 0.63 ug/L, lead 0.32 ug/L, all statistically indistinguishable from the glass control; no elevated metals or organics. Concrete tile "did not release elevated levels of the metals or organic compounds evaluated."
- Membrane: EPDM (EPD) released zinc (median 57 ug/L, elevated) and had runoff pH ~5.0-5.4; TPO *without* brominated flame retardant did NOT release elevated metals or organics (median zinc 3.5 ug/L); PVC released arsenic. So among membranes, TPO-without-BFR is the clean choice and EPDM is not.
WA Ecology study, extracted tables ES-2 (metals) and 10 (pH), `https://apps.ecology.wa.gov/publications/documents/1403033.pdf`.

Bottom line for this project: a bare/coated flat concrete deck and a TPO-without-flame-retardant membrane both read as low-leaching, near-neutral-to-alkaline potable-intent surfaces; bare galvanized/Galvalume and EPDM both leach zinc and are better as painted/coated versions or restricted to non-potable use; asphalt shingle is not appropriate for potable catchment (TWDB) due to organic/toxin leaching. Every material still requires first-flush + filtration + disinfection before potable use.
Lands in: Water Independence.

**2.** How does a bare concrete roof's runoff pH and alkalinity change over the first several years as the surface carbonates, and does a concrete sealer or coating change that trajectory in a way that matters for downstream treatment design?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** New concrete surfaces in contact with water leach lime (calcium hydroxide) and drive runoff/stored-water pH sharply alkaline at first, then settle toward neutral as the exposed surface carbonates (CaOH reacting with atmospheric/dissolved CO2 to form CaCO3, which is far less soluble). The documented magnitude for a concrete water tank: fresh concrete pushes stored-water pH to roughly 10-11 initially, and it commonly takes two and sometimes three full empty-fill cycles before stored water settles to a safe pH near 9 and then into the 6-8 range in ordinary service. Rain Brothers, "Concrete Cistern pH," `https://www.rainbrothers.com/concrete-cistern-ph`. The WA Ecology data give the steady-state end of this trajectory for a carbonated concrete-tile surface: median runoff pH 7.2, higher than any other roof tested, from the concrete's calcium/magnesium matrix, `https://apps.ecology.wa.gov/publications/documents/1403033.pdf`. The general neutralization literature confirms concrete's interior pH is 12.5-13.9 and that acidic rain progressively neutralizes and carbonates the surface layer over time (ScienceDirect, "Neutralization evolution of concrete under acid rain and carbonation," `https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2238785423012899`).

Why it matters for treatment design: the early-life high-pH "cure leach" is real and transient. Design response is not a permanent neutralizer stage but a commissioning step — flush and cycle the roof/cistern (or hold and dump the first fills) before the water is put into potable service, exactly the practice item 59 below addresses for the cistern itself. A sealer or coating that isolates the concrete from the water (an NSF 61 coating, item 38) largely eliminates both the beneficial alkalinity buffering (item 60) and the nuisance early-life pH spike, flattening the trajectory to that of an inert surface; leaving the concrete bare preserves a mild, useful alkalinity buffer against acidic rain at the cost of the early-life break-in period. This is a genuine design tradeoff, not a settled single answer.
Lands in: Water Independence (cross-ref Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, and Radon).
SETTLED (magnitude and trajectory documented; the coating-vs-bare tradeoff is stated as an owner design choice, see items 59-60).

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Not calculable without a chosen parcel; the tool and method are confirmed. NOAA Atlas 14's Precipitation Frequency Data Server (PFDS), `https://hdsc.nws.noaa.gov/pfds/`, returns point precipitation-frequency estimates by latitude/longitude or place name for durations from 5 minutes to 60 days and return periods from 1 to 1,000 years; the controlling value for gutter/downspout sizing is the short-duration (5-minute or 15-minute), high-return-period (25- or 100-year) intensity in inches per hour. **4.** What gutter and downspout material, profile, and hanger spacing hold up best under Oregon's combination of high-volume winter rain, needle/leaf debris from conifers, occasional heavy wet snow load, and UV exposure, and which materials are safe for a potable-intent catchment train versus non-potable-only?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Seamless aluminum is the consensus best all-round gutter material for Oregon's climate: it resists rust and corrosion, does not embrittle and crack in freeze snaps the way vinyl does under UV and cold, and seamless installation removes the seam leak points; vinyl is specifically flagged as a poor Oregon choice because it becomes brittle from UV and cracks in cold (Gutter Empire, "Metal vs. Vinyl Gutters: What Works Best in Oregon's Climate?", `https://gutterempirellc.com/metal-vs-vinyl-gutters-oregon-climate/`). For conifer needle debris, a micro-mesh gutter guard on a rigid aluminum frame (rigid enough to carry wet-snow load without sagging) is the effective solution in needle-heavy Oregon settings; needles are the hard case that many guard styles fail (American Gutter Service, "Do Gutter Guards Work With Pine Needles?", `https://americangutterservice.com/gutter-guards-oregon-pine-needles/`). Hanger spacing and profile: hidden hangers at roughly 24-inch spacing (tightened to ~18 inches under heavy snow-load exposure) and K-style or half-round profile are standard practice; the manuscript should confirm hanger spacing against the local snow-load band rather than a flat number.

Potable-vs-nonpotable material safety: the TWDB manual is explicit that for a potable train, lead solder cannot be used on gutters/downspouts (older metal gutters sometimes used lead solder, which acidic rain dissolves) and copper flashing/components can discolor fixtures and add copper; safe potable-train conveyance materials named are half-round PVC, seamless aluminum, and galvanized steel without lead solder (`https://www.twdb.texas.gov/publications/brochures/conservation/doc/RainwaterHarvestingManual_3rdedition.pdf`, pp. 9). Aluminum and PVC are safe for potable-intent; lead-soldered or copper-containing conveyance is non-potable-only.

**5.** What screening options (basket strainer, leaf-eater, vortex/centrifugal filter) are available at the residential scale, what do they cost installed, and how much sediment load do they actually remove ahead of the first-flush diverter, backed by a manufacturer test result rather than a marketing claim?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** The residential ladder runs from simple gutter/downspout screens and basket strainers (a few dollars to tens of dollars, removing only coarse leaves/twigs), through downspout "leaf-eater"/rain-head deflector screens, to a vortex/centrifugal fine filter as the highest-performing pre-storage stage. The WISY vortex filter is the documented benchmark: the WFF-class vortex roof-washer separates debris larger than 280 microns and, per the US distributor's own product data, splits flow roughly 70 percent clean water into the cistern and 30 percent carrying dirt/debris out the bottom "trash" outlet during an average rain, with a self-cleaning vertical stainless screen; the volumetric design flow is about 2.78 L/s at average intensity. The 280-micron separation and 70/30 split are the manufacturer test-basis figures; note the vortex filter is a fine-screen separator, not a first-flush diverter, and the two work in series (the vortex removes particulates continuously; the first-flush diverter dumps the initial dirty volume, items 10-13).

**6.** For a conifer-heavy rural Oregon lot, what gutter-guard or screen maintenance schedule actually prevents needle-mat buildup across a wet season, and what does skipping it cost in first-flush volume and filter-clogging downstream?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** No single quantified maintenance-interval-versus-cost study was found; the researchable answer is the practice and the mechanism. On a conifer-heavy lot, micro-mesh guards drastically cut but do not eliminate needle accumulation, and the guard surface itself needs periodic clearing (needles mat on top of a mesh and shed water off the roof if left) — the documented recommendation is a seasonal sweep of the guard/mesh surface plus an inspection before and after the first heavy storms of the wet season, the same before-and-after-storm cadence the Land chapter's item 7 gives for culvert inlets (American Gutter Service, `https://americangutterservice.com/gutter-guards-oregon-pine-needles/`). The downstream cost of skipping it is mechanistic and real: a needle mat that partially blocks the gutter forces water to sheet past the catchment, cutting delivered yield; needle fines that do wash through raise the debris load hitting the first-flush diverter and the vortex/cartridge filters, shortening filter service life and (per item 25's UV-fouling logic) accelerating sleeve/pre-filter fouling on the treatment side. The manuscript should present this as a maintenance-discipline point (seasonal guard clearing plus storm-bracketed inspection) rather than a costed schedule, since no citable dollar figure exists.

**7.** Can the same roof surface feed both a fire-suppression cistern and a domestic-supply cistern from one catchment system, or does the fire-reserve volume need a physically separate catchment and conveyance path to avoid a single point of failure taking out both?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** One roof and one catchment/conveyance path can feed both a fire reserve and a domestic reserve; a physically separate catchment is not required and is rarely justified at single-family scale. The resilience concern (a single point of failure taking out both) is answered not by duplicating the roof but by the takeoff-elevation separation the plumbing chapter already establishes: the fire reserve is held in the lower portion of the tank (or a separate lower-elevation takeoff) so ordinary domestic draw physically cannot deplete it, and the fire draw reaches into that protected lower volume — see `answers/ch17_plumbing-supply.md` items 14 and 19, sourced to standpipe combined-service-tank practice (`https://up.codes/s/tanks-in-standpipe-system`). The genuine single-point-of-failure risks in a shared catchment are the shared gutter/downspout/first-flush path and the shared tank shell, not the roof surface itself; the mitigations are the maintenance discipline of items 6/13 (keep the conveyance clear) and, if the owner wants belt-and-suspenders redundancy for fire specifically, splitting storage across two tanks (item 20) so one tank out of service does not lose both reserves. NFPA 13/22 recognize captured rainwater as a fire-supply source (item 105), so a shared catchment feeding a fire reserve is a recognized configuration. A physically separate fire catchment becomes worth considering only where the fire reserve must be guaranteed independent of any domestic-system fault the owner cannot tolerate — a design choice, not a code requirement.
Lands in: Water Independence (cross-ref Resilience and Disaster Independence).
OWNER-DESIGN-CHOICE (shared catchment is workable and standard; physical separation is an optional redundancy the owner weighs).

**8.** What is the realistic combined loss (material coefficient, first-flush diversion, conveyance leakage and evaporation, filter backwash) between roof area and usable water actually reaching storage, as a single design percentage this project can use, rather than stacking several unverified individual factors?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Use a single combined collection-efficiency factor of 0.75 to 0.85 (0.80 is the defensible default) rather than multiplying individual loss terms. This is the figure the industry and the primary manual use: the TWDB *Texas Manual on Rainwater Harvesting* recommends 0.75 to 0.90 depending on how efficiently the system collects, and its own worked water-balance examples use 0.85 (`https://www.twdb.texas.gov/publications/brochures/conservation/doc/RainwaterHarvestingManual_3rdedition.pdf`, Chapter 4). Independent installer/engineering guidance converges on the same 75-90 percent band, with the losses it rolls up named explicitly as first-flush diversion, imperfect collection/material coefficient, filter waste, conveyance leaks and evaporation, and overflow (LiveAbout, "Design a Rainwater Harvesting System," `https://www.liveabout.com/design-a-rainwater-harvesting-system-in-6-steps-3157815`). For a potable-intent western-Oregon system running an active first-flush diverter plus vortex/cartridge filtration (more diversion loss than a bare non-potable barrel), the lower end of the band is prudent: this project should use 0.80 as the single design percentage, dropping to 0.75 if first-flush diversion is generous, rather than stacking separately guessed factors that would either double-count or under-count. The volumetric conversion to pair with it: 0.62 gallons per square foot of roof per inch of rain (TWDB), times roof area, times 0.80.

**9.** Does snow load on gutters and a first-flush diverter (as opposed to snow load on the roof itself, already covered in the structural chapter) require different hardware sizing or seasonal draining/winterizing at this project's elevation and snow-load band?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Yes at any elevation that sees hard freezes, and it is a hardware-and-winterizing question distinct from structural roof snow load. The two documented risks are (1) freeze damage to a standing-water first-flush diverter and to any water sitting in gutters/downspouts, and (2) wet-snow load sagging or tearing an under-supported gutter or gutter guard. The winterizing response: a first-flush standpipe diverter must be able to fully drain before a hard freeze, because a standpipe that cannot drain will break when the trapped water freezes (Rain Harvesting Supplies / permies cold-climate guidance, `https://permies.com/t/27911/Rainwater-catchment-areas-freeze`; BlueBarrel winterization, `https://www.bluebarrelsystems.com/blog/winterize-rain-barrel-system/`). Hardware sizing changes: gutter guards need a rigid aluminum frame to carry wet-snow load without sagging (item 4), hidden hangers tighten from ~24-inch to ~18-inch spacing in heavy-snow exposure, and snow stops/guards keep snow from sliding off before it melts and can be caught (item 103). At a low-elevation western-Oregon valley site the freeze exposure is intermittent and the design is a drainable diverter plus ordinary guards; at a higher snow-load-band foothill site the design adds tighter hanger spacing, rigid-frame guards, and a deliberate seasonal drain-down of the first-flush diverter. The exact band is parcel-elevation-specific and confirmed against the site's ground snow load (structural chapter) and county frost depth.

**10.** Which first-flush sizing rule (the low-pollution 1.25 gal/1,000 sq ft figure, the higher 10-20 gal/1,000 sq ft figure, or a demand-based method tied to actual roof condition and storm gap) is the right starting basis for a potable-intent system on this project, and where does that number come from in a citable source rather than a trade blog?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** For a potable-intent system on a conifer-heavy Oregon lot, start from the higher, debris-aware figure, not the low-pollution minimum. The citable primary source is the TWDB manual: its rule of thumb is to divert a minimum of 10 gallons per 1,000 square feet of collection surface, and it reports an Australian vendor study recommending 13 to 49 gallons per 1,000 sq ft; expressed per unit area, its recommended range is 1 to 2 gallons of first-flush diversion per 100 square feet (i.e., 10-20 gal/1,000 sq ft). TWDB manual, pp. 8, `https://www.twdb.texas.gov/publications/brochures/conservation/doc/RainwaterHarvestingManual_3rdedition.pdf`. The manual is explicit that there is no exact calculation — the right volume depends on the number of antecedent dry days, debris type/quantity, tree overhang, roof slope/smoothness, and rain intensity (it takes ~0.1 in/hr to wash a sloped roof, ~0.18 in/hr for a flat one) — so a demand/condition-based method is the honest design basis. For this project: use the 10-20 gal/1,000 sq ft band as the starting size, size toward the upper end (or provide a way to flush extra, item 13) because of heavy conifer debris and long dry-summer antecedent periods, and treat the low 1.25 gal/1,000 sq ft figure as applicable only to a clean, low-pollution roof this project does not have.

**11.** What is the most reliable, lowest-maintenance first-flush diverter mechanism for a system meant to run unattended through an Oregon wet season (standpipe with a bleed orifice, float-ball valve product, or a programmable/electronic diverter), and what is the failure mode and fix for each when the bleed orifice or valve seat fouls with sediment?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** The three mechanisms and their failure modes: (1) *Standpipe with a bleed orifice* — the simplest, no moving parts, non-electric; the standpipe fills first and drains slowly through a small orifice (a 0.25 mm orifice discharges ~80 mL/min, a 2.0 mm orifice ~845 mL/min) between storms. Failure mode: the orifice fouls with sediment and either stops draining (so the diverter stays full and diverts nothing on the next storm) or debris accumulates in the cleanout; fix is manual — pull the cleanout cap and clear it after events, which is why TWDB says it must be emptied and cleaned after each rainfall. (2) *Float-ball valve* — a floating ball seals the top seat when the chamber fills, trapping first-flush and routing the balance to the tank; failure mode is a fouled seat that fails to seal (first-flush leaks into the tank) or a ball stuck to a dirty seat; fix is cleaning the ball and seat. (3) *Programmable/electronic release valve* — lets the owner set how often the diverter empties, best water-quality control, but adds an electrical/timer dependency and a valve that can fail. TWDB manual, pp. 8-9, `https://www.twdb.texas.gov/publications/brochures/conservation/doc/RainwaterHarvestingManual_3rdedition.pdf`; NTO Tank "First Flush Diverter Explained," `https://www.ntotank.com/blog/first-flush-diverter-explained`; Rain Harvesting Supplies advanced release valve, `https://rainharvestingsupplies.com/advanced-release-valve-for-first-flush-diverter/`.

Recommendation for an unattended Oregon wet season consistent with this project's manual-override, no-single-point-of-electronic-failure priorities (echoed throughout ch17): a standpipe-with-bleed-orifice diverter is the most reliable and lowest-maintenance because it has no moving parts and no power dependency, with the accepted cost being a routine manual cleanout; size the orifice at the small end so it empties between storms but not mid-storm, and make the cleanout accessible. The float-ball adds a seat that can foul; the electronic diverter adds a failure mode the project's design philosophy specifically avoids.
Lands in: Water Independence.
OWNER-DESIGN-CHOICE (recommendation is the standpipe/bleed-orifice on reliability and philosophy grounds; the owner may prefer the electronic diverter's finer control).

**12.** Can an owner build a reliable first-flush standpipe diverter from ordinary PVC and fittings, and if so, what pipe diameter and length actually deliver the target diversion volume for this project's specific roof area, using the roughly 0.38-0.4 gal/ft (3-inch) and 0.6-0.7 gal/ft (4-inch) pipe-volume figures as a starting point to verify?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Yes — the standpipe first-flush diverter is explicitly a build-it-from-PVC design, and the pipe-volume figures to verify against are in the primary manual. TWDB gives the exact conversions: 3-inch PVC holds a gallon per 33 inches of length (≈0.36 gal/ft), 4-inch PVC holds a gallon per 18 inches (≈0.67 gal/ft), and 6-inch PVC catches a gallon in a little over 8 inches (≈1.5 gal/ft). TWDB manual, pp. 9, `https://www.twdb.texas.gov/publications/brochures/conservation/doc/RainwaterHarvestingManual_3rdedition.pdf`. The question's starting figures (~0.38-0.4 gal/ft for 3-inch, ~0.6-0.7 gal/ft for 4-inch) are confirmed close to the primary values (0.36 and 0.67). Sizing method: multiply this project's roof area (once finalized) by the item-10 target of 10-20 gal/1,000 sq ft to get the diversion volume in gallons, then divide by the chosen pipe's gal/ft to get standpipe length. Worked example for a 2,000 sq ft roof at 15 gal/1,000 sq ft = 30 gallons: in 4-inch PVC that is 30 ÷ 0.67 ≈ 45 feet of standpipe (impractically tall as one pipe, so use 6-inch PVC, 30 ÷ 1.5 = 20 feet, or gang several parallel standpipes, or step up to a purpose-made large-diameter chamber). The practical lesson the arithmetic reveals: for a real roof area the required diversion volume is large enough that a single small-diameter standpipe is too tall, so an owner build uses large-diameter (6-inch) pipe, multiple parallel standpipes, or a manufactured chamber — the DIY standpipe is reliable but its size has to be checked against the actual roof area, which is exactly what the pipe-volume figures let the owner do.

**13.** How does the first-flush volume target change between the wet season's first storm after a dry summer (heaviest accumulated roof debris) and a storm in the middle of a wet, frequent-rain stretch (roof already rinsed), and is a fixed diverter size the right answer or does the design need a way to flush more after a long dry spell?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** The first-flush target genuinely changes with antecedent dry days: TWDB names the number of dry days and accumulated debris as primary drivers of how much to divert, and the WA Ecology study found metals/contaminant concentrations in roof runoff positively correlated with the length of the antecedent dry period (more buildup over a longer dry spell) and inversely with total precipitation and rain intensity. TWDB, `https://www.twdb.texas.gov/publications/brochures/conservation/doc/RainwaterHarvestingManual_3rdedition.pdf`; WA Ecology, `https://apps.ecology.wa.gov/publications/documents/1403033.pdf`. So the first storm after a dry Oregon summer carries the heaviest load (a full summer of conifer needles, pollen, dust, bird/rodent droppings) and warrants far more diversion than a storm mid-wet-season on an already-rinsed roof. A single fixed diverter size cannot be optimal for both: sized for the dry-summer first flush it wastes water all winter; sized for the rinsed-roof case it under-diverts the dangerous first storm. The design answer is a way to flush more after a long dry spell — practically, either a manually adjustable/extendable standpipe (add capacity or hold the diverter closed longer for the first big autumn storm and empty it more aggressively), a deliberate manual "dump and rinse" of the roof/gutters before reconnecting the cistern at the start of the wet season, or a programmable electronic diverter (item 11) that empties on a schedule the owner lengthens after dry spells. For this project, the low-tech answer fits the design philosophy: size the fixed standpipe for ordinary wet-season storms and add a manual first-of-season roof/gutter flush plus a held-diversion of the first autumn storm, rather than oversizing the diverter year-round.
Lands in: Water Independence.
SETTLED (the seasonal variation is documented; a fixed size plus a manual seasonal flush is the recommended design).

**14.** Where does the diverted first-flush water go (a French drain, a rain garden, the ordinary site-drainage swale system already specified in the soil chapter), and does its contaminant load (roof grit, bird droppings, atmospheric deposition) require any particular disposal siting relative to the foundation, the septic drainfield, or a garden bed?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** First-flush water is routed to a planted area, rain garden, or the site's ordinary surface-drainage/swale system — TWDB explicitly says the flushed water "can be routed to a planted area" (`https://www.twdb.texas.gov/publications/brochures/conservation/doc/RainwaterHarvestingManual_3rdedition.pdf`, pp. 8). It is low-hazard clear-ish water with a modest contaminant load (roof grit, bird droppings, atmospheric dust), closer to ordinary roof runoff than to wastewater, so it does not need septic-grade disposal siting. The siting constraints that do apply are the general clear-water/foundation-drainage rules the soil chapter already sets: discharge it *away from the foundation* (not into foundation backfill, so it does not saturate the soil against the wall or defeat foundation drainage), and — because it carries bird-dropping bacteria and roof debris — keep the discharge point a reasonable distance from any vegetable garden bed intended for direct-contact edibles and away from the septic drainfield's own footprint so it does not add hydraulic load there. **23.** For a residential-scale cistern (a few thousand to a few tens of thousands of gallons), does ferrocement construction actually save labor, material cost, or schedule time over an owner-poured, formed concrete tank of the same volume, or is the tradeoff the other direction once the two-person hand-trowelling skill requirement is counted?

**26.** What is the real failure history of owner-built ferrocement tanks (cracking at the rod-to-mesh transition, shrinkage cracking from undercuring, corrosion of the armature where cover is too thin), and what inspection or repair method catches and fixes each before it becomes a structural failure?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** The documented failure modes and their fixes: (1) *Shrinkage cracking from undercuring* — the dominant owner-build failure; a thin ferrocement shell has high surface-to-volume ratio and cracks if it dries too fast, so the fix is prevention through moist curing (the UNHCR guide stresses proper mortar and cure, and the general water-tank literature notes new-tank effervescent white patches/efflorescence from the curing chemistry — see items 2/59). Caught by visual inspection for hairline surface cracks and by the hydrostatic leak test (item 33) before service. (2) *Corrosion of the armature where cover is too thin* — the reason galvanized mesh is specified (UNHCR: galvanized mesh preferred because it is less likely to rust) and why cover to the first mesh layer is a controlled design parameter; too-thin cover exposes the steel to water and rust-jacking cracks the shell from within. Caught by inspecting for rust staining bleeding through the shell and by verifying cover at build time. (3) *Cracking at the rod-to-mesh (skeletal-to-mesh) transition* — a stress-concentration point where the stiff skeletal bars meet the flexible mesh; managed in the UNHCR design by crediting and detailing the skeletal steel and adding stiffeners so the transition is not the weak point. UNHCR 2006, which draws on the evaluated performance of 20+ IFIC-supervised ferrocement tanks over 10-15 years, `https://mutualaiddisasterrelief.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/Large-Ferro-Cement-Water-Tank-Design-Parameters-and-Construction-Details.pdf`. The general inspection-and-repair method: a full-fill hydrostatic leak test before commissioning (item 33) to find cracks, weeping, or seepage; interior/exterior visual inspection at each cleaning for cracks, rust staining, and spalling; and repair of a found crack by chasing it out and re-plastering with the same rich mortar (or an NSF 61 crystalline treatment, item 38) before it opens into a structural leak path. No published failure-rate statistic for owner-built (as opposed to program-supervised) tanks was found; item 28 gives the program-scale service-life data.

**29.** If a cistern is integrated into the foundation footprint (a dedicated basement room or a below-slab vault used purely for water storage), what does that do to the foundation's structural design, waterproofing detailing, and the foundation drainage hierarchy already specified in the soil chapter, and does it change the excavation and formwork sequence in the build-sequence chapter?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Integrating a cistern into the foundation footprint is buildable and space-efficient but converts the foundation from a soil-retaining structure into a water-retaining one at that location, which changes every discipline the question names. Structural design: a water-storage vault carries full internal hydrostatic pressure when full *and* external soil/water pressure when empty, loaded in both directions, so the shared wall must be designed for the governing case (a wall designed only to retain soil is not automatically adequate to retain water, which pushes outward), and it should meet the stricter ACI 350 water-retaining crack-control and reinforcement rules (item 116) rather than the ACI 318 foundation-wall rules — this is a heavier reinforcement and mix specification than an ordinary foundation wall. Waterproofing: the shared wall now needs waterproofing on *both* faces for different reasons (item 34) — an NSF 61 potable liner on the inside retaining clean water, and the foundation's own below-grade waterproofing on the outside keeping groundwater out — and the potable requirement means the interior surface treatment must be NSF 61 listed, which an ordinary foundation waterproofing is not. **32.** What is the right owner-buildable keyway and waterstop detail between a cistern's floor pour and its wall pour, and between sequential wall-lift pours if the tank is too tall for one placement, to avoid the cold-joint leak path that is a recurring failure point in concrete water-retaining structures generally?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** The cold joint (the floor-to-wall junction, and any wall-lift-to-wall-lift junction) is the recurring leak path in concrete water-retaining structures, and the standard detail is a keyway plus a waterstop, ideally with continuous reinforcement crossing the joint. The keyway is a formed groove (or raised key) in the first pour that the second pour keys into, giving mechanical interlock and lengthening the water path; the waterstop is a continuous strip (PVC/thermoplastic, hydrophilic/bentonite, or a crystalline/hydrophilic product) embedded across the joint, half in each pour, to physically block water migration through the joint (ConSeal waterstop guidance, `https://conseal.com/technical-resources/application-specifications/waterstops/`; WaterproofMag, "Sealing Cold Joints with Waterstops," `https://www.waterproofmag.com/2017/04/sealing-cold-joints-with-waterstops/`; Kryton crack-inducing waterstop + keyway, `https://www.kryton.com/products/crack-inducing-waterstop/`). Owner-buildable detail: at the floor-to-wall joint, form a keyway in the floor slab (a beveled groove, often made with a keyway form strip or a length of beveled 2x4 pressed into the wet slab), set a hydrophilic or bentonite waterstop strip along the joint line before the wall pour (surface-mounted hydrophilic strips are the most owner-friendly — glued to the cured floor at the joint, they swell on contact with water to seal the joint), keep the wall's vertical reinforcement doweled continuously up out of the floor, and clean/roughen the joint surface before the wall pour for bond. Between sequential wall lifts, repeat the same waterstop-plus-continuous-vertical-steel detail at each horizontal construction joint and roughen the top of each lift. This is exactly the joint detail the Dancing Rabbit builder regretted omitting ("regretted not forming a keyway between floor and walls," item 30 source). Confirm the specific waterstop product is NSF 61 listed if it contacts stored potable water (item 38).

**33.** Rather than a compression cylinder test (the usual alternative-materials proof path for structural concrete on this project), what is the correct test-to-prove protocol for a water-retaining structure, a hydrostatic/leak test held for a set number of days at full fill, and what leakage rate is an acceptable pass versus a fail requiring rework?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** The correct proof test for a water-retaining structure is a hydrostatic (leakage/tightness) test: fill the tank to its design (overflow) level, let it stand a stabilization period so the concrete absorbs its initial water, then measure the drop in water level over a fixed test period, correcting for evaporation and rainfall. The controlling standard is ACI 350.1 (Tightness Testing of Environmental Engineering Concrete Structures); the general acceptance criterion in that family of standards is that the volume loss over a test period (commonly 24 hours after a stabilization/absorption period, sometimes extended to several days) not exceed a small fraction of the tank volume — the widely used pass threshold is on the order of 0.05 percent to 0.1 percent of tank volume lost per 24 hours (a tank that loses more than roughly a tenth of a percent per day is failing and needs the leak found and repaired), with visible flowing leaks or damp/weeping exterior surfaces an automatic fail regardless of the volumetric number. Because ACI 350.1 is a purchased standard, the exact percentage and stabilization period should be confirmed against it or against the engineer's specification before it is stated as a hard number in the manuscript. The owner-buildable version: fill the tank, mark the water level, wait the stabilization period (24-72 hours for the concrete to take up its initial absorption), then measure the level daily for several days with a separate evaporation reference (a floating pan or a covered witness container), and treat a stable level (within the small allowable loss) with no exterior weeping as a pass and any continued drop or visible seepage as a fail requiring the joint/crack to be found and repaired (item 32) before the tank goes into service. This replaces the compression-cylinder proof path for a water-retaining structure because the property being proven is water-tightness, not just compressive strength.

**34.** For a below-grade cistern, what waterproofing strategy (an NSF 61 interior coating alone, an exterior membrane, or both) actually protects against groundwater intrusion from the outside as well as retaining stored water from the inside, and does it need to coordinate with the foundation's own below-grade waterproofing detail already specified for the structural walls?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** A below-grade potable cistern faces water in both directions and generally needs a both-faces strategy, and it must coordinate with the foundation's below-grade waterproofing. Inside: an NSF 61-listed interior coating or liner (crystalline, cementitious/acrylic, or epoxy, item 38) both retains the stored water and, importantly, must be potable-contact-safe — this is the surface the drinking water touches, so it cannot be an ordinary waterproofing. Outside: an exterior waterproofing (membrane or crystalline treatment) keeps external groundwater from migrating *into* the tank through the wall when the tank is low or empty and protects the concrete/reinforcement from the surrounding soil moisture — the same function and often the same detail as the foundation's own below-grade waterproofing. The reason both are prudent for a below-grade tank specifically: an interior liner alone leaves the wall and reinforcement exposed to groundwater from outside (corrosion and freeze risk), while an exterior membrane alone leaves the potable-contact question unanswered and does not by itself guarantee the wall retains stored water — so a below-grade potable cistern uses an NSF 61 interior treatment plus exterior waterproofing, whereas an aboveground tank can rely on the interior treatment alone. Crystalline systems (e.g., Xypex, item 38) are attractive here because they integrally waterproof the concrete matrix itself (working from either face) and have NSF 61 listings, potentially satisfying both roles with one product line. Coordination with the foundation: where the cistern shares a wall with or abuts the foundation (item 29), the exterior waterproofing must be continuous and compatible with the foundation's below-grade waterproofing detail so there is no unwaterproofed seam at the junction, and the foundation drainage must still carry groundwater away from the tank's exterior.

**36.** What access-opening size, ladder or step detail, ventilation, and confined-space safety protocol does an owner need to safely enter a below-grade cistern for periodic cleaning or repair, and what is the real risk profile (oxygen displacement, structural collapse of an unsupported entry, entrapment) that a residential-scale tank still carries even though it is far smaller than a commercial confined space?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** A below-grade cistern is a permit-required confined space in the OSHA sense, and the risk is genuine at residential scale — the danger is not the tank's size but its atmosphere. The dominant hazard is oxygen displacement/deficiency: a closed water tank accumulates oxygen-deficient air from biological activity/off-gassing, and a space that smells clean can hold oxygen levels that incapacitate within seconds; secondary hazards are toxic gases (hydrogen sulfide from anaerobic activity, which settles low) and entrapment (OSHA 29 CFR 1910.146; OSHA tank-cleaning guidance, `https://www.osha.gov/storage-tanks/work-activities`; confined-space cleaning safety, `https://o6env.com/blog/confined-space-cleaning-safety-protocols-and-compliance-tips/`). The safe-entry protocol: never enter alone; test the atmosphere before entry in the correct sequence (oxygen first, then flammable gas, then toxics) and at multiple depths because gases stratify; force-ventilate the space with mechanical ventilation before and during entry to purge the deficient/toxic atmosphere; use a full-body harness with a retrieval line and a trained attendant stationed outside who does not enter (most confined-space deaths are would-be rescuers); and continuously monitor the atmosphere during the work. Access-opening and ladder detail: the access hatch must be large enough to admit a person wearing a harness and to allow retrieval of an incapacitated worker (a common practical minimum is roughly a 24-inch opening, confirmed against the retrieval equipment used), with a fixed ladder or step rungs cast into or bolted to the wall, and a lockable, mosquito-screened, opaque cover (items 41-42) when closed. The honest framing for the manuscript: a residential cistern is a real confined space that has killed people, and the owner must treat entry as a planned, ventilated, attended, atmosphere-tested operation, not a casual climb-in — or, better, design the tank so most cleaning can be done from outside (drain valve, pressure-wash through the hatch) to minimize entries.

**Answered (2026-07-14):** It is a real, measurable effect but not a fully reliable substitute for a controlled neutralizer, so it *reduces* but should not be assumed to *eliminate* the calcite stage without testing. The effect is documented: rainwater arrives acidic and low in alkalinity, and contact with a concrete tank's calcium-bearing surface neutralizes it upward — the WA Ecology concrete-tile runoff came off at pH 7.2, the highest of any surface, from the concrete matrix, and the concrete-cistern literature reports stored rainwater settling into the pH 6-8 "optimal drinking water" range after the early-life break-in, specifically because "the concrete then actually helps neutralize the water and balance the pH" (`https://apps.ecology.wa.gov/publications/documents/1403033.pdf`; `https://www.rainbrothers.com/concrete-cistern-ph`). So a bare concrete cistern does provide a genuine, mild, passive pH buffer that an inert (poly/steel/lined) tank does not. The reason it is not a guaranteed replacement for a calcite neutralizer: the buffering depends on contact time, tank turnover, water volume, and the state of the surface (a fully carbonated old surface leaches less than a fresh one, item 2), so the delivered pH is not precisely controlled the way a sized calcite bed's is, and it can be inadequate if turnover is fast or the surface is sealed. The honest design answer: rely on the concrete's passive buffering as a first-order benefit, but *test the delivered pH* (item 58) rather than assume it, and keep a calcite/neutralizer stage in the design as a controllable trim — often a smaller one than an inert-tank system would need, or on standby — sized to whatever residual correction the test shows is still required. Real and measurable, yes; reliable enough to eliminate the neutralizer blind, no — verify by test.

**64.** Building Art Ludwig's branched-drain design for this project's specific floor plan and slope, where does the graywater source sit relative to a downhill planting area, and does the site's actual topography support a gravity branched-drain layout, or does the layout force a pumped system instead?

**65.** What is the real-world failure and maintenance history of a laundry-to-landscape system (mulch basin overload, lint/fiber clogging, root intrusion into the outlet), and what design choices head those problems off before they start?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** The documented laundry-to-landscape (L2L) failure modes and their design fixes: (1) *Lint/fiber clogging* — an L2L system does not filter lint, and lint catches especially in a partially-closed ball valve or a too-small outlet; the fix is to not restrict the 1-inch line (a ball valve added at the end restricts effective pipe size across the whole zone and back-pressures the washer pump, causing premature pump wear), to inspect regularly during heavy use, and to keep outlets clearable (Greywater Action / L2L troubleshooting guide, `https://www.tcrcd.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/L2L-troubleshooting-guide.pdf`; Water Wise Supply, `https://www.waterwisesupply.com/education/greywater/laundry-landscape-greywater-system`). (2) *Mulch basin overload/ponding* — the basin must be sized so no graywater ponds on the surface; sizing depends mainly on soil texture (larger basins in slow-percolating clay loam), and undersized basins pond and run off; the fix is to size each mulch basin to accept the full discharge without ponding and to distribute across multiple outlets rather than one. (3) *Root intrusion into the outlet* — roots grow into and clog the outlets; the fix is to contain each emitter/outlet in an underground valve box (or an inverted flower pot) with an air gap between the pipe end and the soil so roots cannot grow up into the pipe, and to place the valve box over each outlet. (4) *Emitter boxes filling with dirt* — clear them out to restore flow. The design choices that head these off before they start: keep the full 1-inch line unrestricted with no downstream ball valve; size mulch basins to soil texture with margin; put every outlet in a valve box with an air gap; distribute across multiple outlets; and plan a routine inspection (Oregon DEQ's O&M manual, item 63, requires exactly this kind of maintenance description). L2L is low-maintenance if designed with these, and troublesome if the line is restricted or the basins are undersized.

**66.** How does year-round graywater reuse interact with Oregon's wet winters specifically: does a subsurface irrigation basin designed to accept graywater in a dry summer become saturated and non-functional through a wet Willamette Valley winter, and does the system need a winter bypass back to the septic system?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Yes — an Oregon wet winter is exactly the condition that makes a graywater irrigation basin non-functional seasonally, and a winter bypass back to the sanitary/septic path is the design answer (and is already contemplated by Oregon's rules). In a wet Willamette Valley winter the soil is at or near saturation and plants are dormant, so a subsurface mulch basin sized to infiltrate summer graywater cannot accept additional water — it ponds, backs up, or surfaces, and there is no irrigation benefit because the landscape needs no water. This is precisely why every Oregon DEQ graywater system must have a *diversion device* that lets the owner easily select where the graywater goes — to the reuse system or to the wastewater (sanitary sewer or onsite septic) system — and why the system "must connect to a sanitary sewer or onsite septic system" (Oregon DEQ Graywater Guide, extracted, `https://www.oregon.gov/deq/FilterPermitsDocs/GraywaterGuideHomeowners.pdf`; the O&M manual, item 63, requires describing how and when to send graywater to reuse versus the sanitary path, and draining irrigation lines before winter). So the design must include the winter bypass, and operating it is a documented seasonal task: divert graywater to the septic system through the wet months (when the soil is saturated and plants are dormant) and to the reuse basin through the dry summer (when the landscape needs it and the soil can accept it), draining the irrigation lines before the first hard freeze. This also means the septic system must still be sized to accept full flow (the drainfield gets no permanent design-flow credit for graywater diversion — item 68/Land item 11), because the graywater comes back to it every winter. The winter bypass is not optional in wet-winter Oregon; it is the mechanism that makes year-round graywater reuse workable.

**72.** What electrical load does a fan-ventilated composting or incinerating toilet actually draw continuously, and how does that load fit into the solar/battery sizing and the critical-versus-noncritical circuit planning already being researched for the energy-independence chapter?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** A composting toilet's continuous load is small; an incinerating toilet's is large and intermittent, and they belong in different circuit categories. For this project's off-grid resilience, the composting toilet's small continuous fan is the right fit (put it on critical circuits, size the small continuous load into the base load); an incinerating toilet's load argues against it for an off-grid house. Exact continuous wattage is unit-specific and should be read from the chosen unit's fan spec.

**73.** What real-world odor, fly, and finished-compost-handling problems do full-time composting-toilet households report after multiple years of use (not just the first-season honeymoon period), and what operating discipline actually prevents them?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** The documented multi-year problems and the discipline that prevents them: (1) *Odor* — the leading complaint when things go wrong; caused by an overloaded or over-wet pile, a stalled/underpowered exhaust fan, or too little bulking material; prevented by continuous fan ventilation that never stops (item 72's critical-circuit point), adding sufficient carbon bulking material (sawdust, coir, peat) after use, and not overloading a small self-contained unit beyond its rated capacity — the Sun-Mar overflow-and-smell reports trace to overload under full-time use (`https://letterstocreationists.wordpress.com/2015/03/14/comparison-of-composting-toilets-towards-a-global-commode/`; inspectapedia, `https://inspectapedia.com/septic/Composting_Toilets.php`). The operating discipline that actually prevents the problems: continuous ventilation, disciplined bulking-material addition, urine diversion or good liquid control, not overloading capacity, and a real curing-and-disposal routine — the same "documented O&M discipline" DEQ requires. Households that follow it report no odor/fly problems multi-year; those that treat the unit as maintenance-free hit exactly these problems after the first season.

**99.** How does a low-yield well interact with the standby-power and generator-sizing question already raised in the water-supply chapter: does a slow-filling buffer tank reduce the generator size actually needed during an outage, since the pump can run continuously at a lower draw rate rather than cycling on demand?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Yes — a slow-filling buffer tank meaningfully reduces the standby-power demand a low-yield well imposes during an outage, and it does so by decoupling the pump's electrical draw from the household's peak-demand pattern. Without a buffer, the well pump (and any booster) must start on demand and deliver at the household's instantaneous peak rate, so standby power must cover the pump's full starting/running load whenever a fixture opens. With a buffer tank filled through a flow-limiting valve at the well's low sustained yield (item 98), the well pump runs at a low, steady draw to trickle-fill the cistern, and the household draws from the cistern through a booster (or gravity, item 108) — so the well pump's electrical load is small, continuous, and schedulable rather than a large intermittent peak. This lets a generator or battery/solar system run the well pump's modest continuous fill load at a convenient time (e.g., during peak midday solar, the ch17 item 10 "soak the midday PV" logic), rather than sizing standby power for the pump's full on-demand peak. It does not eliminate the booster/pressure load (the household still needs pressurized delivery from the cistern), but it removes the well pump's peak from the standby-sizing calculation and converts it to a small, time-shiftable base load — which reduces the generator size needed and fits the solar/battery critical-vs-noncritical planning (cross-ref Solar, Battery, and Energy Independence, and ch17 item 2's constant-pressure/generator-friendliness discussion). So: yes, the slow-filling buffer reduces the standby-power/generator size the low-yield well would otherwise require, by turning the well pump's on-demand peak into a small continuous, time-shiftable load.

**111.** What specific structural, waterproofing, and evaporation-control lessons from the ab anbar tradition are directly transferable to a modern reinforced-concrete cistern in a seismic Oregon setting, separate from the sarooj mortar recipe itself, which is climate- and material-specific to arid Persia?

(2) *A roofed/domed top with a vented headspace* — ab anbars used a massive covering dome and one-to-six wind-catcher ventilation chambers (~1 meter tall) to ventilate the headspace while keeping the water covered, which is directly the item-27 (roofed/domed integral top) plus item-41 (screened vent for airflow) plus item-42 (light exclusion) design — cover the water for light/evaporation/contamination control while ventilating the headspace. (3) *Waterproof render on the interior* — the ab anbar's principle of a dedicated impermeable interior render (sarooj was the material, but the principle is an interior waterproof lining distinct from the structural wall) transfers directly as the modern NSF 61 interior coating/liner (items 34/38); the sarooj recipe itself (lime, ash, egg white, goat hair, clay) is arid-Persia-specific and not transferable, but the *concept* of a separate interior waterproofing layer is. **116.** For any concrete water cistern or tank under the project's water-independence goal, does ACI 350's stricter crack-control and shrinkage-reinforcement requirement change the mix design, reinforcement spacing, or joint/waterstop detailing compared to an ordinary ACI 318 foundation wall, and by how much does that raise cost?

**Answered (2026-07-14):** Yes — ACI 350 (Environmental Engineering Concrete Structures, the code for liquid-containing structures) imposes stricter requirements than ACI 318 across mix, reinforcement, and joints, specifically to keep the structure liquid-tight, and it does raise cost. The documented differences: (1) *Reinforcement* — ACI 350 requires more minimum temperature-and-shrinkage steel: 0.0024·b·h for Grade 60 versus ACI 318's 0.0018·b·h (roughly a third more minimum T&S steel), and it applies stricter crack-width limits and reinforcement-distribution rules aimed at preventing the fine through-cracks that leak, which ACI 318 need not concern itself with (Eng-Tips ACI 350 vs 318, `https://www.eng-tips.com/threads/minimum-t-amp-s-reinforcement-aci-350-01-vs-aci-318.436642/`; CTLGroup liquid-tight cracking control, `https://www.ctlgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Concrete-QA-Control-of-Conrete-cracking-for-Liquid-Tight-Environmental-Structures.pdf`). (2) *Load factors* — ACI 350 uses a lateral-liquid-pressure load factor of 1.7 versus ACI 318's 1.4, so the wall is designed for a higher factored liquid load. (3) *Joints/waterstop* — ACI 350's liquid-tightness focus mandates the keyway/waterstop cold-joint detailing (item 32) that an ordinary foundation wall does not need. (4) *Mix* — ACI 350 pushes toward a lower-permeability, crack-resistant mix (controlled w/c, shrinkage control), stricter than a general foundation mix. Cost impact: the increase comes from ~30 percent-plus more minimum shrinkage/temperature steel, tighter crack-control reinforcement, the waterstop and keyway detailing, the lower-permeability mix, and the hydrostatic leak test (item 33) — collectively a meaningful premium over an ACI 318 foundation wall of the same dimensions, though no single published percentage figure was located (it scales with tank size and the specific reinforcement the ACI 350 crack check demands). The manuscript should state that a water cistern is designed and detailed to ACI 350, not to the ACI 318 foundation-wall standard — more steel, a higher liquid load factor, waterstop/keyway joints, a lower-permeability mix, and a leak test — and that this is a real but justified cost premium for a liquid-tight, long-life (multi-century-goal, item 28) structure. ACI 350's full text is a purchased standard; the vs-318 differences above are from secondary engineering sources.
