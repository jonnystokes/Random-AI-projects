## Plumbing: DWV, Sewage, Venting, Slope, Distance, and Fixtures

**1.** Septic drainfield premature failure, the biomat / solids-carryover problem. The dominant real-world failure chain (skipped pumping leads to solids/grease reaching the field leads to biomat clogging the soil) and the prevention stack: effluent filter and its cleaning, pump interval, keeping vehicles/roots/compaction off the field, and what never goes down the drain.

**Answered (2026-07-13):** The failure chain is confirmed by Oregon DEQ's own maintenance guidance: when solids and scum are allowed to accumulate past a safe fraction of tank volume, incoming sewage no longer has enough retention time in the tank, so solids carry over into the drainfield. There they feed anaerobic microbes at the soil interface that produce a waterproof polysaccharide "biomat," which seals the soil pores and stops infiltration — failure is gradual, not sudden, and by the time sewage surfaces on the ground the drainfield itself typically needs replacement, not just a pump-out (DEQ, "Septic Tank Maintenance" fact sheet, https://www.oregon.gov/deq/FilterDocs/septictankmaintFS.pdf).

DEQ's own pumping guidance (based on Oregon State University Extension Circular No. 1343, April 2000): pump when sludge and scum occupy more than 35 percent of tank volume; a properly sized tank typically has 3 to 8 years of capacity. For the Oregon-required minimum 1,000-gallon tank serving a 4-bedroom house, DEQ's example is roughly every 3 years at 4 occupants and roughly every 6 years at 2 occupants; tanks installed before 1979 may be undersized and need more frequent pumping. Pumpers must hold a DEQ sewage disposal service license (same source).

Effluent filters are the mechanical backstop between the tank and the field: OAR 340-071-0100(58) defines an "Effluent Filter" as a device at the septic tank outlet designed to block suspended matter larger than 1/8 inch, and OAR 340-071-0220 requires one on new installations unless the agent approves an equivalent method (https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_340-071-0100; local copy `C:/Users/jon/Projects/theBook/revision6_code_library/oar/oar-340-071-full.html`, lines ~243 and ~4070-4078). A clogged, uncleaned filter does not prevent failure — it just moves the symptom forward: DEQ guidance says it should be cleaned at pump-out (not more often than necessary, since over-cleaning disrupts the tank's nitrification), which is why "clean the effluent filter" belongs on the same maintenance ticket as "pump the tank."

The general standards at OAR 340-071-0130 prohibit vehicular traffic, paving, filling, cutting, or other soil modification over the absorption area and its reserved replacement area (compaction and root intrusion are the two mechanisms DEQ guidance and practitioner sources such as SludgeHammer and Inspectapedia cite for physically damaging trench infiltration capacity independent of biomat: https://sludgehammer.net/blog/fixing-biomat-clogging-in-septic-drainfields/, https://inspectapedia.com/septic/Septic_Biomat_Formation.php). "What never goes down the drain" per DEQ and the same maintenance fact sheet: fats/oils/grease, garbage-disposal waste, and non-degradable solids (wipes, feminine products, cigarette butts) all shorten tank retention life and increase carryover risk.

**2.** Septic type selection on a hard site (Oregon DEQ terms). For high water table / shallow or slow-perc soil, walk the ladder: conventional gravity vs pressure-distribution vs sand filter vs ATU vs mound/capping fill, with siting, required reserve/replacement area, and the cost climb. What does the site/perc evaluation decide?

**Answered (2026-07-13):** Oregon's onsite system ladder, all under OAR chapter 340 division 71 (https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_chapter_340_division_53; local copy `oar-340-071-full.html`):

- **Standard subsurface (gravity) system** — OAR 340-071-0220. Requires adequate effective soil depth (Table 3: minimum 30 inches at low slope, increasing with slope) and adequate depth to the seasonal high water table; absorption trench length is set by Table 4 (soil texture group A/B/C vs. effective soil depth) and Table 5 (soil group vs. depth to temporary groundwater), e.g. 50 linear ft per 150 gpd in the best soil/depth combination up to 175 ft/150 gpd in the worst combination the standard system still qualifies for.
- **Pressurized distribution** — OAR 340-071-0275, used where gravity distribution cannot achieve even loading (flatter sites, larger systems, or where the standard system's soil/depth combination is marginal).
- **Conventional sand filter** — OAR 340-071-0290/-0295, a 2-foot-plus engineered sand bed fed by pressure distribution on an intermittent basis; used where native soil cannot adequately treat effluent before it reaches the water table (shallow soil, shallow groundwater).
- **Alternative Treatment Technology (ATT/ATU)** — proprietary aerobic treatment units, listed at https://www.oregon.gov/deq/residential/pages/onsite-att.aspx; used on small lots, shallow water table, steep slopes, or poor soil where a standard system cannot be sited at all, because ATTs produce a higher-quality effluent that allows a smaller or shallower absorption area.
- **Capping fill (mound)** — OAR 340-071-0265, an engineered soil cap over an absorption trench keyed at least 12 inches into natural soil; used where natural soil depth or a high water table rules out any in-ground option; construction is restricted to the June 1-October 1 dry window and the upper 18 inches of native soil must not be saturated or over-worked when built (local file lines ~4535-4579).

**Reserve/replacement area:** OAR 340-071-0130(12) and -0220 require that an equally-sized replacement area be reserved and protected from vehicular traffic, paving, filling, or cutting for the life of the system; a replacement area may be waived only where a city/county/sanitary district commits in writing to provide sewer service within five years (local file line ~2232).

**What the site/perc evaluation decides:** the state's site evaluation (test pits at least 75 ft apart within the proposed system and replacement area, OAR 340-071 test-pit procedure, local file line ~2178) measures effective soil depth, soil texture group, and depth to seasonal groundwater — those three numbers, read against Tables 3-5, mechanically determine which rung of the ladder a given site is eligible for; DEQ fee schedule (Table 9A, OAR 340-071-0140) prices a single-family site evaluation at $700 regardless of outcome. Cost climbs sharply up the ladder in permit fees alone (Table 9B: roughly $890 install permit for a standard/Type C system vs. $1,272-$1,566 for ATT/capping-fill/pressurized (Type D) vs. $1,566-$1,710 for sand filter (Type E)), before any difference in construction cost, which this Wave did not price (would need local installer quotes).

**3.** Creative wastewater load-cutters, graywater reuse and composting toilets, what Oregon allows. Oregon's graywater reuse permit tiers, whether an indoor composting toilet is accepted, and how each reduces septic load and extends field life on a marginal site.

**Answered (2026-07-13):** Oregon has two separate, stackable load-cutters:

**Graywater reuse (OAR chapter 340 division 53).** Recognizes three graywater qualities: Type 1 (no treatment beyond primary filtration, e.g. a grease trap; subsurface irrigation only, cannot be stored over 24 hours), Type 2 (primary + secondary treatment to BOD5 ≤10 mg/L and TSS ≤10 mg/L; allows drip irrigation, ponds, and storage), and Type 3 (adds disinfection; beyond DEQ's homeowner guide scope). Two permit tiers gate this (Table 4, DEQ "Reusing Graywater in Your Landscape" guide, https://www.oregon.gov/deq/FilterPermitsDocs/GraywaterGuideHomeowners.pdf): Tier 1 (permit 2401) for Type 1 only, under 300 gpd, subsurface irrigation, permit application only, $50 new/$40 annual (waived 4 of 5 years with an annual report); Tier 2 (permit 2402) for Type 1 or 2, under 1,200 gpd, subsurface/drip/ponds, requiring a system design plan and O&M manual, $534 new/$50 annual. Graywater legally excludes dishwasher, garbage-disposal, toilet, and cloth-diaper wash water, and cannot include water contaminated with hazardous chemicals.

**Composting toilets** are separately authorized under ORS 447.118 and OAR 918-770-0080: an owner may install one without prohibition if it meets NSF International Standard 41 and a plumbing permit is obtained; installation is limited to sites where a DEQ-approved graywater disposal path exists or graywater is piped to a public sewer. This creates what DEQ calls the "split waste method" (OAR 340-071-0320, local file line ~6162): black waste goes to the compost toilet, graywater goes to an onsite system, and — critically for a marginal site — DEQ's own composting-toilet fact sheet states the graywater-only absorption/drainfield facility may then be approved at as little as two-thirds the size of a full-flow system (https://www.oregon.gov/deq/FilterDocs/os-compostingtoilets.pdf), because removing blackwater removes roughly 90/50/60 percent of the nitrogen/phosphorus/potassium load respectively (same source, citing the Falmouth, MA eco-toilet program) and cuts overall bathroom water use about 25 percent.

Both systems can be run together seasonally: DEQ's composting-toilet FAQ explicitly describes using the graywater-reuse system in dry months and reverting to the septic/sewer system in wet months when the reuse system would otherwise be offline.

**4.** DWV venting you can't redo later in a masonry/concrete house. Wet venting, island/loop vents for a kitchen island, air-admittance valves where OPSC permits them, and a slab-embedded drain layout that has to be right the first time.

**Answered (2026-07-13):** Air admittance valves (AAVs) are allowed in Oregon under **Statewide Alternate Method No. 07-01** (Oregon BCD, issued 2007-12-14, last updated 2023-10-01, https://www.oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/Documents/sam-07-01-airadmittancevalves.pdf), which conditionally approves AAVs for detached one- and two-family dwellings (and their accessory structures) subject to: each valve serves a maximum of 3 fixtures, and only when those fixtures form a single grouping (bath, kitchen, or laundry area) on one floor level; the valve sits at least 4 inches above the fixture drain it vents; it stays within the vent's maximum developed length; it is accessible for inspection/maintenance/removal in a ventilated space; **at least one stack vent must still extend to open air** (AAVs protect only against negative-pressure siphoning of trap seals, not positive-pressure events); and AAVs may not serve sumps, chemical wastes, or supply/return air plenums. AAVs must meet NSF 14-2009 material standards and ASSE 1050/1051-2009 performance standards. This is exactly the mechanism used for an island sink where the classic loop-vent detail (a foot vent taken off the fixture vent below the floor, run to the nearest wall, then up through the roof) cannot be routed inside a poured wall — an AAV under an island cabinet is the practical Oregon-legal alternative to a loop vent.

Wet venting (using one fixture's drain to vent another in the same fixture group) is a standard UPC/OPSC method and, together with island-vent geometry, is set in OPSC Chapter 9 (Vents; https://up.codes/viewer/portland/upc-2021/chapter/9/vents); it is compatible with poured-wall construction as long as pipe sizing and the wet-vent stack's maximum fixture-unit load are locked into the rough-in drawings before concrete goes up, since — unlike wood framing — a missed or undersized wet vent cannot be fixed by cutting into a stud bay later.

**The controlling constraint for a masonry/concrete house is procedural, not technical:** every vent path, AAV location, and wet-vent connection has to be finalized on paper and verified against the rough-in drawings before the pour, because retrofitting any DWV vent path through a cured concrete or grouted CMU wall means core-drilling and compromising the wall's continuity — this is why the under-slab and in-wall DWV layout (see item 5) is treated as a single "get it right once" design package rather than typical rough-in that tolerates field changes.

**5.** Under-slab DWV, get it perfect before the pour. Trench and slope layout, sleeving through the slab, pressure/test-ball testing the buried system before concrete, protecting stub-ups, and sealing every slab penetration for both radon and water.

**Answered (2026-07-13):** The controlling test procedure is UPC (OPSC basis, 2021 UPC) **Section 712, DWV Systems Testing**: on completion of rough piping, the system is tested by water, by air (non-plastic pipe only), or by vacuum (plastic pipe); a water test fills the section to at least 10 feet of head above the highest fitting in that section (or the highest point in the system) and must hold for 15 minutes without loss; an air test pressurizes to 5 psi (or 10 inches of mercury) and must hold 15 minutes without added air; **plastic pipe may not be air-tested** — only water or vacuum-tested (https://up.codes/s/dwv-systems-testing; cross-checked against the same IRC/UPC-derived language via multiple building-department rough-in checklists, e.g. https://mybuildingpermit.com/sites/default/files/inline-files/15%20Plumbing%20Rough%20In.pdf). This test must pass, and be signed off at rough-in inspection, before the slab is poured over it — there is no code-compliant path to test or repair a leak after concrete cures over the pipe.

Practical pre-pour package that follows from this: lay out trench slope so every horizontal drain run is confirmed (not assumed) to meet the OPSC minimum slope for its pipe diameter; sleeve every pipe that must move independently of the slab (thermal/settlement isolation) or that crosses a footing/thickened-slab edge; cap and physically protect every stub-up (a temporary standpipe capped and braced, not just a taped fitting) against rebar work, foot traffic, and concrete placement crews; and treat every slab penetration as two separate seals — a water/vapor seal (per the slab's vapor retarder detail) and, where the local radon zone or design calls for it, a radon-sealing detail at the same penetration (this is a shared item with the envelope/radon chapter; do not assume the plumbing sub's caulk is sufficient for the vapor-retarder or radon seal unless it is specified as such in the envelope-chapter detail).

**6.** For this project's actual household size and fixture count, what is the realistic daily graywater volume from showers, bathroom sinks, and laundry, and does it fit inside the Tier 1 (under 300 gpd) ceiling, or does the design need to plan for Tier 2 (under 1,200 gpd) from the outset?

**Answered (2026-07-13):** The methodology is settled and must be run against this project's actual occupant count and fixture choices; no generic household figure should be used in its place. EPA's general benchmark cited in DEQ's own homeowner guide is that "a typical US residence produces approximately 90 to 100 gallons of graywater each day" (https://www.oregon.gov/deq/FilterPermitsDocs/GraywaterGuideHomeowners.pdf, p.3) — that whole-house figure already sits comfortably under the 300 gpd Tier 1 ceiling for an ordinary single-family household, **before** any fixtures are excluded from collection.

DEQ's guide gives the exact per-source formulas to size this project specifically: shower flow (gal/wk) = shower gpm × minutes/session × sessions/day × 7; washing-machine flow (gal/wk) = gallons/load × loads/week (DEQ's worked example: 4 loads/week at 30 gal/load = 120 gal/wk ≈ 17 gpd); bathroom-sink flow is harder to estimate and DEQ recommends direct observation over several days rather than a rule of thumb. Kitchen-sink graywater is legally includable but is flagged as the "dirtiest" source (grease, food solids, pathogens) and requires primary treatment (grease trap) if used at all.

**Whether this specific project stays under 300 gpd (Tier 1, cheap/simple) or needs to plan for Tier 2 (1,200 gpd ceiling, $534 new-permit fee plus a system design plan and O&M manual) depends on: (a) the final occupant count, (b) how many bedrooms/bathrooms the design lands at, and (c) which fixtures the owner actually chooses to plumb to the graywater line** (showers and laundry only, vs. also bathroom and kitchen sinks). Given the EPA per-residence benchmark, a typical single-family household diverting showers, bathroom sinks, and laundry only will land well under 300 gpd and can plan on Tier 1; a large household, a design that also diverts kitchen-sink water, or a design intending Type 2 storage/drip/pond reuse (which requires Tier 2 regardless of volume, per Table 4) should plan for Tier 2 from the outset. This project's specific bedroom/occupant count was not supplied to this research pass and should be run through the DEQ worksheet once finalized.

**7.** What does a Tier 2 (permit 2402) system design plan and operations/maintenance manual actually have to contain for DEQ approval, and is that documentation burden realistic for an owner-builder to prepare directly, or does it call for hiring a designer, and at what cost?

**Answered (2026-07-13):** Per the DEQ homeowner guide (https://www.oregon.gov/deq/FilterPermitsDocs/GraywaterGuideHomeowners.pdf, "6. Document your system"), a Tier 2 submittal requires two documents:

- **System design plan:** beneficial uses of the graywater; system location; the design flow (the maximum graywater volume the system is sized to handle); which fixtures are the graywater source; the distribution/reuse system design; a description of any treatment system used; and the name and contact information of the person responsible for the design.
- **Operation and maintenance manual:** a diagram showing the diversion device (3-way valve) and any filter/component locations; a description of when to route flow to reuse vs. sanitary; the usage assumptions/calculations behind the design flow; which products are and are not safe to send to the system; for Type 2 systems specifically, the monitoring and annual-reporting procedure; and a maintenance schedule (spring start-up, filter changes, flushing the surge tank and lines, winterizing/draining before freeze risk).

**Realism for an owner-builder:** DEQ explicitly frames this guide as written "to help Oregon homeowners obtain and comply with a DEQ permit... at single-family residences" and states the process is "accessible to the average homeowner," and confirms "if you own your property and you are installing a graywater system on your property yourself, you may do so" without a contractor license (only a hired installer must be Landscape Contractors' Board-licensed). For a **technology-based** Type 2 treatment product (a third-party-certified unit meeting the BOD5/TSS performance standard), the documentation burden is realistically owner-preparable using the DEQ guide's own worksheets and no annual water-quality monitoring is required. For a **performance-based** Type 2 system — most notably a constructed-wetland/plant-based treatment approach — DEQ's guide itself recommends "working with a designer familiar with wastewater treatment," and requires an annual sample analyzed by an accredited private lab (list at https://public.health.oregon.gov/LaboratoryServices/EnvironmentalLaboratoryAccreditation/Documents/acclab.pdf) submitted with the annual report. **What a designer would cost for this specific project is a fee-quote question for a local wastewater/onsite designer, not a published rate** — this Wave found no published Oregon fee schedule for private graywater-system design services.

**8.** Building Art Ludwig's branched-drain design for this project's specific floor plan and slope, where does the graywater source sit relative to a downhill planting area, and does the site's actual topography support a gravity branched-drain layout, or does the layout force a pumped system instead?

**Answered (2026-07-13):** This is not researchable in the abstract — it requires this project's actual floor plan, fixture locations, and a site topographic survey (elevation of each graywater source relative to candidate planting areas), none of which this research pass had access to. What is settled is the design method itself: Art Ludwig's branched-drain approach (source: Art Ludwig, "Create an Oasis with Greywater" and "Branched Drain Greywater Systems," publisher's summary at https://oasisdesign.net/greywater/laundry/) routes graywater by gravity alone through a branching network of 1.5-inch-minimum plastic pipe with no filters, pumps, valves, or surge tanks to foul with solids — every branch point uses a simple flow-splitter fitting, and the pipe is sized deliberately large and unobstructed so that hair, lint, and food solids pass through rather than clog a filter. This works only where the graywater source (a shower or laundry standpipe, typically inside the foundation wall) sits **above** the planting area it feeds by enough elevation to maintain fall over the whole branched run — Ludwig's own design rule is to preserve gravity fall at every branch, which is a site-specific geometry problem, not a fixed number.

**Where the site does not offer that fall** (a source below or level with the planting area, or a planting area too far away to maintain slope), the practical Oregon alternative is the laundry-to-landscape (L2L) pumped system, which uses the washing machine's own internal pump (see item 9) instead of relying on gravity, at the cost of the pump-longevity limits documented there (roughly 8 ft of lift, roughly 100 ft of run). Once this project's grading plan and floor plan are set, the specific choice between a branched-drain (gravity) and an L2L (pumped) layout, and exactly which fixture feeds which planting bed, is the owner's site-specific design decision to make with a topographic survey in hand.

**9.** What is the real-world failure and maintenance history of a laundry-to-landscape system (mulch basin overload, lint/fiber clogging, root intrusion into the outlet), and what design choices head those problems off before they start?

**Answered (2026-07-13):** A field troubleshooting guide from Sierra Watershed Progressive / Tehama County RCD (https://www.tcrcd.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/L2L-troubleshooting-guide.pdf) documents the recurring failure modes directly:

- **No water reaching the greywater pits:** three-way valve left set to sewer, breaks/leaks in the piping, or dips in the pipe run (a dip traps water and silt and becomes a clog point — the fix is proper pipe hanging with continuous fall, not sag).
- **Pump/machine damage:** the washing machine's own pump can only push water about 8 feet of vertical lift, and runs shouldn't exceed about 100 feet of horizontal distance — exceeding either "drastically reduces the life of [the] pump" and can blow door seals; this is a hard design constraint on where the mulch basins can be sited relative to the laundry room.
- **Scummy buildup in mulch shields:** caused either by non-greywater-safe soap/detergent (fix: use greywater-compatible products) or simple sediment accumulation requiring periodic cleaning of the mulch shield.
- **Overflowing basins:** caused by undersized/too-shallow basins (illegal ponding and a health hazard), by too few plants or low-water-need plants that fail to take up the design flow (L2L relies on plant uptake, not soil percolation alone, to consume the water), or by mulch that has saturated and composted down and needs replacing, or by root mats clogging the mulch shield — periodic mulch replacement and root-clearing at the shield are the prescribed maintenance.

Design choices that head these off, per the same source and Greywater Action (https://greywateraction.org/laundry-landscape/): correctly size basins for the design flow up front, plant enough water-hungry vegetation to match that flow, keep the pump's lift and run within its rated limits, use only greywater-safe cleaning products, and put mulch-shield inspection/mulch replacement on a recurring maintenance schedule rather than treating the system as "install and forget."

**10.** Is a small constructed wetland or reed-bed treatment step ever a realistic single-family addition on a rural Oregon lot with room to spare, and what would it add over a conventional Tier 2 graywater system in treatment quality, land area, and maintenance?

**Answered (2026-07-13):** Yes — Oregon DEQ's own homeowner guide treats a small constructed wetland as one of the two recognized "performance-based" treatment routes to Type 2 graywater quality (the other being a slow sand filter), and gives a literal step-by-step example: after primary treatment (e.g., a grease trap), graywater is routed into a small lined pond, or "even an old bathtub," planted with wetland species; the plants and their root-zone microbial community perform the secondary treatment (BOD5 ≤10 mg/L, TSS ≤10 mg/L) via phytoremediation (DEQ, Appendix C, "Type 2 Plant Treatment System Example," https://www.oregon.gov/deq/FilterPermitsDocs/GraywaterGuideHomeowners.pdf, pp. 16-17). DEQ names specific approved wetland species (broadleaf cattail, water sedge, slough sedge, creeping spikerush, American white waterlily, common duckweed) and specifically warns against planting state-listed noxious species that are otherwise common in wetland-treatment literature (reed canary grass, yellow flag iris, common reed of the Old-World subspecies, water hyacinth) — see Oregon's noxious weed list at https://www.oregon.gov/ODA/PLANT/WEEDS/lists.shtml.

**What it adds over a conventional (technology-based) Tier 2 system:** DEQ requires no ongoing water-quality monitoring for a technology-based (third-party-certified product) Type 2 system, but a performance-based system like a wetland must be sampled at least once per year by an accredited private lab and the results sent to DEQ with the annual report — so a wetland trades equipment cost for a recurring lab-testing obligation and, per the guide, "consider working with a designer familiar with wastewater treatment" during design. Land area is genuinely small-scale (DEQ's own illustrated example uses an old bathtub), so "room to spare" is not the limiting factor on a rural lot — the limiting factors are the annual monitoring requirement and the need for professional design input on a performance-based system, which a technology-based system avoids.

**11.** How does year-round graywater reuse interact with Oregon's wet winters specifically: does a subsurface irrigation basin designed to accept graywater in a dry summer become saturated and non-functional through a wet Willamette Valley winter, and does the system need a winter bypass back to the septic system?

**Answered (2026-07-13):** Yes, and this is not an edge case the owner has to improvise — it is DEQ's own stated operating rule. The homeowner guide states plainly: "you cannot discharge graywater into frozen or saturated soil," and gives the field test to check it (dig six inches down, squeeze a handful of soil — if water drips out, the soil is saturated) (https://www.oregon.gov/deq/FilterPermitsDocs/GraywaterGuideHomeowners.pdf, p.12). The same guide requires every system to include a diversion device (typically a 3-way valve) precisely so that "if you produce excess graywater, you must send it to the sewer or septic system" whenever the reuse site cannot safely accept it — DEQ's illustrated example shows the valve routing to reuse when open and to the sanitary system when closed. DEQ's separate composting-toilet fact sheet confirms this is the normal seasonal pattern in practice, describing graywater reuse systems as being used "during dry months" with the household reverting to "a septic system during wet months," and notes the reuse system is deliberately taken offline in wet-weather months (https://www.oregon.gov/deq/FilterDocs/os-compostingtoilets.pdf, p.2).

**Consequence for this project's design:** the graywater reuse system is never a replacement for full septic/drainfield capacity — the drainfield still has to be sized to accept 100 percent of household flow (see item 18), because the design assumes graywater reuse is switched off through the Willamette Valley's wet season and all flow reverts to the septic system at exactly the time of year when reuse would otherwise be most tempting (highest household water use is typically dry-season, but irrigation-season site saturation limits are a real summer risk too — DEQ's guide also caps site slope at 45 degrees and requires 4 feet of separation to groundwater "during the irrigation season," so a shallow-water-table site can be excluded outright, not just seasonally, from reuse).

**12.** What accessible, serviceable diverter-valve product (allowing a fast, tool-free switch between the graywater-reuse path and the ordinary sanitary path, already required generically by OAR 340-053-0100) actually exists on the market at the residential scale, and what does it cost?

**Answered (2026-07-13):** The residential graywater community's standard product is a repurposed pool/spa three-port diverter valve, not a plumbing-specific product: Jandy and Pentair three-way valves, sold through pool/spa suppliers, are the commonly cited choice for gravity-fed systems (https://oasisdesign.net/greywater/divertervalves/), available in 1.5-inch and 2-inch ABS/PVC sizes to match standard drain and laundry-standpipe pipe. Current published pricing: a Jandy "Never Lube" three-port valve runs about $49; a dedicated 1-inch brass three-way laundry diverter valve is available for about $59 (sources aggregated via https://oasisdesign.net/greywater/divertervalves/ and general retail listings; exact current price should be re-checked at time of purchase). These are simple hand-turned ball/gate valves, operable without tools, which is what lets a household make the "route to reuse vs. route to sewer/septic" decision in seconds as DEQ's operating guidance requires (item 11). Whichever specific valve is chosen, OAR 340-053-0100 requires it be "constructed and installed to meet the requirements of the Oregon [Plumbing] Specialty Code" (per the DEQ guide, p.11) — i.e., it must still pass OPSC review as a plumbing fitting, not just perform mechanically.

**17.** What water-rights and DEQ implications, if any, follow from discharging cistern overflow, first-flush diversion water, or treatment-train reject water (RO reject, softener brine, filter backwash) to the ground on this property, beyond the general clear-water and indirect-waste rules already established in the plumbing chapters?

**Answered (2026-07-13):** These are not a single category and should not be treated as one. **Cistern overflow and first-flush roof-runoff diversion water** are functionally stormwater — clean water that never contacted a treatment chemical — and are generally handled the same as ordinary roof/site drainage (grading to daylight, a dry well, or a rain garden), which does not typically trigger a DEQ wastewater permit or an OWRD water-right question on a single-family lot, though local grading/erosion-control rules still apply and should be confirmed with the county.

**RO reject water and water-softener regeneration brine are a materially different case.** They carry concentrated total dissolved solids (softener brine specifically carries elevated chloride, sodium, and potassium from the regeneration salt, plus whatever hardness minerals were stripped from the source water). The practical, code-compliant default used by most Oregon plumbing installers is to route softener regeneration discharge and RO reject into the sanitary drain line ahead of the septic tank (a small added hydraulic load, not a chemical-loading problem for the tank itself) rather than to a dedicated dry well or direct-to-grade discharge — but whether the local AHJ requires or merely permits this routing, and whether the added flow needs to be accounted in septic design-flow sizing, is a jurisdiction-specific plumbing-plan-review question this research cannot settle in the abstract.

## Electrical: Service, Panels, Grounding, and Load Planning

**1.** Concrete-encased electrode (Ufer), the vapor-barrier trap. Why rebar in a slab poured over poly cannot count as the CEE (broken earth contact), how to use the footing instead, the exact stub-up/connection detail cast before the pour, and the fallback when the whole foundation sits on foam/vapor barrier.

**Answered (2026-07-13):** NEC 250.52(A)(3) defines the concrete-encased electrode (Ufer ground) as at least 20 feet of either 1/2-inch (or larger) rebar, or bare #4 AWG copper conductor, encased by at least 2 inches of concrete that is **in direct contact with the earth**, located near the bottom of a foundation or footing. An added Informational Note (referenced across multiple code-analysis sources, e.g. https://expertce.com/learn-articles/ufer-ground-rules-nec-250-52/, https://legalclarity.org/concrete-encased-electrode-nec-250-52a3-requirements/) clarifies that where insulation, a vapor barrier, or a similar film separates the concrete from the earth, direct earth contact is lost and that concrete no longer qualifies as a CEE. This is exactly the trap in a modern slab: **a slab poured over a polyethylene vapor barrier (standard under any conditioned slab) cannot be used as the CEE**, because the poly is the isolating layer the Informational Note describes.

**The fix is to locate the CEE rebar in the footing/foundation stem wall, not the interior slab** — wherever the foundation concrete is genuinely earth-contact (a footing poured directly against native soil or against forms with no vapor barrier between concrete and soil), that portion qualifies, per the same section, provided the 20-foot/2-inch-cover minimums are met (source: https://www.texasinspector.com/files/UFERsandVaporBarriers.pdf, cited by the research-question lead). **The exact detail that must be cast before the pour:** identify a continuous 20-foot run of footing rebar (or add a dedicated bare #4 copper conductor along the footing) with 2 inches of concrete cover, and leave a bonding lug or a length of conductor stubbed up through the footing/stem wall into the location where the service equipment will land, so the electrician can bond the grounding electrode conductor (GEC) to it — this cannot be added after the pour without excavating.

**Fallback when the entire foundation sits on foam/vapor barrier (no earth-contact concrete exists anywhere):** per NEC 250.52(A)(4) through (A)(8), the next electrodes in the priority list are used instead — a ground ring, rod/pipe electrodes (with the standard practice of driving two rods rather than measuring for the 25-ohm single-rod exception), plate electrodes, or other local metal underground systems. A CEE, where it exists, does not need to be supplemented by another electrode; where none of the concrete-based options exist, one or more of these alternate electrodes must be installed instead.

**2.** Grounding, bonding, and surge protection for a steel-and-electronics house. Foundation electrode plus supplemental electrodes, bonding of any gas/CSST and metallic water, DC grounding for the solar/battery, and whole-house SPD at the panel to protect the well pump and electronics from rural lightning and utility surges.

**Answered (2026-07-13):** Four separate NEC requirements stack together here:

- **Foundation/supplemental electrodes:** per item 1, the CEE (where earth-contact footing concrete exists) is the primary electrode and does not need supplementing; where it does not exist, ground rods or another 250.52(A)(4)-(8) electrode substitute.
- **Gas piping (CSST) bonding:** NEC 250.104(B) requires a metal gas piping system likely to become energized be bonded to the grounding electrode system, with a bonding jumper sized per Table 250.122 based on the circuit most likely to energize the piping, and not smaller than 6 AWG copper (https://www.electricallicenserenewal.com/Electrical-Continuing-Education-Courses/NEC-Content.php?sectionID=3292.0; https://www.ncosfm.gov/electrical/250104b-bonding-gas-piping/open). CSST manufacturers' own installation instructions frequently call for a bonding jumper larger than the NEC's stated minimum, specifically because CSST's thin corrugated wall can be perforated by the arcing that occurs when it carries any part of a lightning or fault current — for a rural property with above-average lightning-surge exposure, follow the CSST manufacturer's (not just the NEC's) bonding-jumper size.
- **Metallic water piping:** bonded to the grounding electrode system under NEC 250.104(A), standard practice regardless of house type.
- **DC grounding for solar/battery:** governed by NEC Article 690 Part V. Modern residential PV using a transformerless (string or microinverter) architecture is typically a "functionally grounded" system per the 690.2 definition (an electrical reference to ground that is not solidly grounded, maintained by the inverter's internal ground-fault monitoring) rather than the older "solidly grounded" single-conductor-grounded design; ground-fault detection/interruption (GFDI) is required above 30V/8A thresholds (690.41; https://up.codes/s/pv-system-dc-circuit-grounding-and-protection; https://expertce.com/learn-articles/grounding-bonding-solar-pv-systems-nec/). Where PV/battery equipment is mounted on the building, its equipment grounding conductors bond back to the same premises grounding electrode system under NEC 690.47 rather than creating a separate DC ground — one grounding electrode system serves the whole house, AC and DC alike.
- **Whole-house SPD:** NEC 230.67 (2020 NEC onward) requires a surge-protective device on **every service supplying a dwelling unit**, either integral to or immediately adjacent to the service equipment (or at the next downstream distribution equipment); the 2023 NEC extended the same requirement to dormitories, hotel/motel guest rooms, and nursing-home patient sleeping rooms (https://www.eaton.com/us/en-us/markets/residential/nec-2023-updates/surge.html; https://citel.us/en/nec-2023-article-23067-dwelling-unit). This is now a mandatory item, not an optional upgrade, and directly answers the "protect the well pump and electronics from rural lightning and utility surges" half of the question — Type 1 or Type 2 SPD at the service panel is the code-required baseline; a rural property with a long overhead service run and known lightning exposure is exactly the case the rule was written for.

**3.** Service and panel sizing for an all-electric load. Running an NEC-220 load calc for heat pump plus HPWH plus induction range plus EV plus well pump, why 200 A often ends up tight, and the fixes: load-management/EVEMS to dodge a service upgrade, or a 320 A meter-main. What margin should a long-life house design in?

**Answered (2026-07-13):** NEC Article 220 governs the load calculation. Under the **Optional Method** (220.82, the calculation typically used for a single dwelling with electric heat), general loads (3 VA/sq ft of habitable area, plus 1,500 VA per small-appliance circuit and per laundry circuit) are combined with the nameplate VA of all fixed appliances (range, water heater, dryer, well pump, etc.), the largest of heating-or-cooling load (not both, since they don't run simultaneously) is added, and EV charging is added per 220.57 (a minimum of 7,200 VA or nameplate, whichever is larger) or, where an EMS limits current, per 220.70 using the EMS setpoint as the continuous load instead of the full nameplate. A demand factor is then applied: the first 10,000 VA of the combined total counts at 100 percent, the remainder at 40 percent (https://www.mikeholt.com/newsletters.php?action=display&letterID=2434; https://expertce.com/learn-articles/residential-load-calculation-nec-220/).

**Why 200 A gets tight:** stacking a heat pump (roughly 10-15 kW), an electric/heat-pump water heater (roughly 4.5 kW nameplate for a resistance HPWH backup element), an induction range (roughly 9.6 kW), a Level 2 EV charger (roughly 7.7-9.6 kW), a well pump, plus ordinary general loads pushes the un-demand-factored nameplate sum well past 200 A's 48,000 VA capacity (240V × 200A) before any demand-factor relief; after the 220.82 demand factor most such houses land under 200A on paper, but the margin is thin, and a second EV, a future shop, or an oversized heat pump can push it over.

**Two fixes**, both used in practice: (a) **Load management / EVEMS** — an Electric Vehicle Energy Management System (or a whole-panel EMS under NEC 220.70/705.13) monitors real-time panel capacity and throttles or delays EV charging (and can manage other flexible loads) so the calculated/actual peak never exceeds the service rating, avoiding a service upgrade entirely (https://blackboxinnovations.com/pages/what-is-an-energy-management-device; https://rve.ca/en/blog/what-is-an-electric-vehicle-energy-management-system-evems/). Oregon's own Statewide Alternate Method 09-01 (OESC Article 625, updated 2023-10-01, https://www.oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/Documents/sam-09-01-calcelectricalvehiclecharge.pdf) permits a demand-factor table for multi-charging-station installations (100 percent for 1-4 stations, stepping down to 50 percent above 41 stations) — this is written for shared/commercial charging arrays and gives essentially no relief for a single house with 1-2 EVs, so for a residential single- or two-EV design, an EMS-based load-management approach (220.70) is the practical lever, not the SAM 09-01 demand table. (b) **A 320 A meter-main** is a real, code-recognized middle option between 200A and 400A residential service, used specifically where an all-electric load calc is genuinely too large for 200A but doesn't warrant a full 400A service.

**Margin for a long-life house:** given the trajectory toward more electrification (a second EV, future heat-pump dryer, future shop tools, potential battery/backup expansion), sizing to the calculated load with no headroom is a foreseeable regret; the practical recommendation surfacing across these sources is to either size the service one step above the calculated need (i.e., choose 320A or a load-managed 200A design capable of adding capacity later) or ensure the panel itself has enough spare physical breaker space and busbar rating even if the service conductor size is deferred. No single numeric "percent margin" figure is code-mandated; this is a design judgment, not a citable rule.

**4.** Where solar/battery ties in, main vs sub-panel and the busbar rule. Line-side (supply-side) tap vs load-side breaker, the 120 percent busbar interconnection limit, whole-home backup vs a protected/critical-loads sub-panel, and provisioning spare panel space now.

**Answered (2026-07-13):** NEC Article 705 governs this. **Supply-side (line-side) connection** taps the inverter output into the service conductors ahead of the main breaker, governed by NEC 705.11 in the 2023 NEC, including specific tap/splice rules at 705.11(C); because this connection point is on the supply side of the service disconnect, it is **not subject to the 705.12(B) load-side busbar calculations at all** (https://solarbuildermag.com/news/nec-2020-705-11-load-and-supply-side-connections/). **Load-side connection** lands the PV/battery backfeed breaker on the panel's busbar downstream of the main breaker, and is where the **120 percent rule** applies: per 705.12(B)(3)(2), when the backfed breaker sits at the opposite end of the busbar from the main breaker, the sum of 125 percent of the inverter output-circuit current and the main breaker's rating must not exceed 120 percent of the busbar's ampacity (https://www.greenlancer.com/post/120-rule-for-solar; https://www.greentechrenewables.com/article/what-panelboard-sizing-120-rule). The 2023 NEC's new **705.13 Energy Management System (EMS) pathway** offers an alternative to the static 120 percent busbar math: an EMS that actively limits current can be used to justify an interconnection that would otherwise exceed the traditional busbar calculation.

**Whole-home backup vs. a critical-loads sub-panel** is fundamentally a battery-capacity and inverter-output sizing decision, not a code mandate either way — a critical-loads sub-panel (well pump, refrigeration, some lighting/outlets) lets a smaller battery/inverter ride through an outage for the loads that matter most, while whole-home backup requires enough continuous and surge capacity to run everything, including large loads like the heat pump and EV charger, and costs proportionally more in inverter and battery sizing. **Provisioning spare panel space now** (extra busbar positions, a larger panel can, or a sub-feed lug reserved) is the practical way to defer this decision without foreclosing either the supply-side-tap or load-side-with-120-percent-rule path later, since retrofitting a too-small panel after the fact often forces a full panel change rather than a simple breaker addition.

**5.** Backup interconnection safety, transfer switch, interlock, and the backfeed hazard. Manual transfer switch vs listed interlock kit vs whole-home ATS, why a "suicide cord" kills, and how a battery-inverter with a microgrid interconnect device changes the whole wiring picture.

**Answered (2026-07-13):** NEC 702.5 requires every optional standby (backup generator/battery) system to include a transfer switch or an equivalent listed means that positively prevents the backup source and the utility from being connected to the panel simultaneously (https://breakerhunters.com/blogs/news/generator-interlock-kits-vs-transfer-switches-2025-buyer-s-guide). Oregon's own consumer guidance confirms this directly at the state level: BCD's "Generator Connections" bulletin states "the Oregon Electrical Specialty Code requires the use of a transfer switch or other safety devices to prevent unsafe back feeding," that this work requires a permit, and that it should be done by a licensed electrical contractor (https://www.oregon.gov/bcd/Documents/generator-connection.pdf, issued April 2021). A **"suicide cord"** (a cord wired with male plugs on both ends, used to backfeed a generator into a wall receptacle without any transfer equipment) kills because it energizes the entire house wiring and, critically, can backfeed out through the utility service transformer onto de-energized "downed" utility lines outside the house at full line voltage — exactly the hazard the BCD bulletin calls out for utility linework crews as well as the homeowner, and it also removes any interlocking protection against the homeowner closing the main breaker onto an already-energized panel.

**Interlock kit vs. manual transfer switch vs. whole-home ATS:** an interlock kit is a UL-listed mechanical slide plate at the panel that makes it physically impossible to close both the main breaker and a dedicated generator breaker at the same time (a "break-before-make" mechanical safety); NEC 702.5 explicitly permits an interlock kit as transfer equipment provided it is listed for that exact panel model and installed per its listing instructions — generic/universal kits are not code-compliant everywhere (https://inspectapedia.com/electric/Backup-Generator-Interlock-Kits.php). Manual transfer switches and whole-home automatic transfer switches (ATS) are the higher-cost, higher-convenience alternatives (see item 6 for pricing).

**Microgrid interconnect devices (MID):** a battery-inverter system built around a listed MID (e.g., the class of products marketed for whole-home battery backup) integrates the transfer function into the inverter/battery system itself rather than requiring a separate mechanical transfer switch — the MID senses grid loss, opens the connection to the utility, and forms an isolated "microgrid" fed by the battery/inverter/generator, which is a materially different wiring topology than a simple backfeed-through-a-breaker generator setup and should be designed and labeled as such at the panel.

**7.** Energized electrical work: state precisely where the code-mandated two-person rule applies (above 600 V) and where it does not (ordinary residential branch circuits and panels), and separately state the practice-recommendation case for never doing residential panel work alone even though it is not code-mandated. Avoid implying a code requirement that does not exist at residential voltage.

**Answered (2026-07-13):** The two-person mandate is an OSHA rule, not an NEC rule, and it is scoped to utility-class work. **29 CFR 1910.269(l)(1)(i)** requires at least two qualified employees present for installation, removal, or repair of lines energized above 600 volts; for de-energized lines still exposed to contact with parts energized above 600 volts; and for equipment such as transformers and capacitors exposed to contact with parts energized above 600 volts (https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.269; https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/2001-08-27-1). Three narrow exceptions exist even at that voltage class: routine circuit switching where safe performance is demonstrated, work performed with live-line tools kept out of reach of energized parts, and emergency repairs to protect the public. **1910.269 governs electric power generation, transmission, and distribution — utility-class work — not ordinary building wiring**; this research found no OSHA or NEC provision requiring two people for work on a residential branch circuit or a standard residential service panel (which operates at 120/240V, far below the 600V threshold that triggers 1910.269). **Residential electrical work by a homeowner or a licensed residential electrician falls under a different, general framework (OSHA's Subpart S / general electrical safety-related-work-practices standards, not 1910.269), and no two-person mandate applies there.**

**The practice-recommendation case, separate from any code requirement:** professional electrical-safety literature broadly recommends never working alone on any energized panel, at any voltage, because a second person can immediately cut power, call for help, or begin CPR/first aid if the first person is shocked or otherwise incapacitated — an unwitnessed injury in a solo work session can be fatal simply because no one is present to respond in time. This is sound practice advice worth including in the manuscript, but it must be presented explicitly as a **recommendation**, not as an implied legal requirement, since no such requirement exists for residential-voltage work — conflating the two would misstate the code.
