29. What is the right order to lock the major site-plan elements (house footprint, septic primary and reserve, well and its setback, solar array's shade-free zone, driveway and turnaround, shop, block-yard, and every outbuilding) onto one master site diagram, and which of those elements is genuinely fixed first versus flexible around the others? [METHOD] [OWNER-DESIGN-CHOICE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Based on the regulatory and physical facts already established elsewhere in this chapter (items 1, 2, 3, 26-28 in `ch03_land-site-and-feasibility.md`), the elements sort into a clear rigidity order, from least negotiable to most negotiable, because each later element must design around the footprint the earlier ones already claimed:

1. **Septic primary and reserve area.** This is the most rigid element because it is not simply "sited with a setback," it is restricted to wherever the DEQ-licensed site evaluator finds soil that actually qualifies (OAR chapter 340, division 71; see item 1). A parcel may have only one or two patches of suitable soil. The reserve area (a second, equal footprint, mandatory under DEQ practice) must be locked at the same time as the primary system, not treated as a later add-on.
2. **Well location.** Locked second because it must simultaneously satisfy (a) wherever the driller's hydrogeological judgment says water is likely, and (b) fixed separation distances from the septic system already placed in step 1: 100 feet from the absorption/reserve area and 50 feet from the septic tank and other treatment units (OAR 340-071-0220 Table 1, items 1 and 2, quoted in full in the answer to item 30 below). Reversing the order (drilling the well first, then trying to fit septic around it) risks losing both good water and good soil.
3. **Hazard and buffer exclusion zones**, where applicable: floodplain, mapped wetland buffer (item 65 below), landslide-susceptibility zones (item 59 below). These are hard no-build zones, not negotiable setbacks, and must be subtracted from the buildable envelope before anything else is drawn.
4. **Driveway alignment, grade, and fire-apparatus turnaround.** Fixed early because the physical entry point (frontage, existing culvert crossing, achievable grade) is usually a fact about the parcel, not a choice, and because construction traffic cannot reach any other element (house pad, block-yard, well rig, septic installer) until the driveway exists (see item 3 in Part 1 on why the durable driveway is typically built first).
5. **House footprint.** Anchored inside whatever buildable area remains after steps 1-4, respecting the same DEQ Table 1 setback (10 feet from the absorption/reserve area, 5 feet from the septic tank, measured to the building's foundation line) and the OAR 690-210-0030 well setback (5 feet minimum from a permanent structure, though far more is typically kept for drilling-rig and pump-service access). Orientation for solar gain (item 58 below) is folded in here, since the house's south wall placement is what step 6 depends on.
6. **Solar array shade-free zone.** This is a zone reserved outward from the house (or a ground-mount array site) once the house orientation is fixed; it constrains what may be built or planted to the south afterward but is not itself a footprint that displaces other elements.
7. **Shop, block-yard, and remaining outbuildings.** The most flexible category. None of these carry a DEQ- or OWRD-mandated siting rule beyond the same generic building-to-septic and building-to-well setbacks that apply to any structure (item 30), so they fill the remaining buildable area last, arranged around whatever the fixed elements above left available. Where a future ADU is a live possibility, its own siting logic further narrows this remaining space (item 31 below).

This sequence is a synthesis of the setback and site-evaluation facts already sourced elsewhere in this chapter, not itself a single codified checklist; no Oregon agency or code publishes a mandatory site-plan sequencing order. The order above reflects which constraints are externally fixed (soil, water, hazard maps, code setbacks) versus which are the owner's to arrange, which is why this item carries both a METHOD and an OWNER-DESIGN-CHOICE tag: the sequence is a defensible method, but the final arrangement within it remains the owner's choice.
   Source: synthesis of OAR 340-071-0220 Table 1 and OAR 690-210-0030 (see item 30 below for full citations), cross-referenced with items 1-3 and 26-28 in `ch03_land-site-and-feasibility.md`, 2026-07-12.
   Lands in: Land, Site, and Feasibility (site-plan sequencing) and Developing the Site: Shop, Block-Yard, and Outbuildings chapter.

30. What real setback distances (from the septic system, from the well, from the property line, from the house) govern where a generator shed, a battery shed, a block-yard, or a barn can legally sit, distinct from the zoning setbacks already covered in the Land, Site, and Feasibility chapter for the house itself? [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Read directly from the downloaded DEQ table. OAR 340-071-0220 Table 1 (Minimum Separation Distances) lists, at item 11, "Foundation Lines of any Building, Including Garages and Out Buildings": 10 feet from the subsurface absorption area (including the replacement/reserve area) and 5 feet from the septic tank and other treatment units, effluent sewer, and distribution units. A generator shed, battery shed, or barn is a "building" or "out building" for this purpose, so this is the controlling DEQ-side setback for any of them. The same table's item 9 ("Property Lines") sets 10 feet from the absorption area and 5 feet from the septic tank as the property-line setback that the septic system itself must respect, which indirectly caps how close a structure on the neighboring side of that line could be sited relative to a septic system near the boundary. A block-yard is not itself a "building" with a foundation in the code's sense (it is typically an open, unroofed pad), so Table 1 item 11 does not numerically apply to it the way it applies to a shed or barn; the more relevant DEQ-side concern for a block-yard is keeping vehicle traffic and material loading off the absorption/reserve area's soil, which the item 11 setback effectively also protects by keeping structures and their associated activity 10 feet clear.

On the well side, OAR 690-210-0030 (Placement of Water Supply Wells) sets: not less than 5 feet from a permanent structure or the roof, eaves, or overhangs of a permanent structure; not less than 25 feet from a residential petroleum storage tank and 50 feet from a commercial one; not less than 500 feet from a hazardous-waste storage, disposal, or treatment facility absent written OWRD permission; and a recommended (not required) 20-foot separation from power poles for future drilling/service-rig access. The rule does not set a specific well-to-property-line distance; instead it directs the driller to apply "greater setback distances" where topography or local geology warrants. A generator shed or battery shed sited near a wellhead is legally permitted as close as 5 feet under this rule, though keeping meaningfully more clearance is prudent for drilling-rig or pump-service access, which the rule's own power-pole guidance (20 feet) implicitly signals as a more workable practical minimum even though it is not a hard requirement for buildings.

Property-line setbacks for these structures from the parcel boundary itself (as opposed to from the septic or well) are a zoning matter, not a DEQ or OWRD matter, and are set by the specific county's or city's zoning ordinance (typically a smaller accessory-structure setback than the primary dwelling's setback, but the exact figure is not uniform statewide). This must be confirmed with the specific target county's planning department at the time the parcel and structure locations are chosen.

31. How does a future accessory dwelling unit (ADU), already flagged as a possible future-flexibility item in the Land, Site, and Feasibility chapter, interact with the placement of the shop, the block-yard, and the other outbuildings, if the owner wants to keep an ADU genuinely possible without knowing its exact future footprint yet? [OWNER-DESIGN-CHOICE] [METHOD]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The interaction depends entirely on which zoning category the parcel carries, and this splits into two very different answers.

Oregon's statewide accessory-dwelling mandate, ORS 197.312, requires cities and counties to allow at least one ADU per detached single-family dwelling, but only within an urban growth boundary, in a zone that already allows detached single-family dwellings; it does not apply to Exclusive Farm Use (EFU) zones or to other rural land outside a UGB. For a rural EFU or forest-zoned parcel, a second dwelling of any kind is not an "ADU" question at all; it is a separate farm-dwelling or nonfarm-dwelling land-use approval question governed by ORS 215.283/215.284/215.705 and related sections, which is covered elsewhere in this chapter's numbered items on EFU and forest zoning (outside this pass's assigned range) and must be resolved as its own land-use approval, not assumed as a by-right future option.

For a Rural Residential (RR)-zoned parcel, Oregon Senate Bill 391 (2021) gives counties the option (not the obligation) to allow a rural ADU by adopting its provisions into local zoning code. Where a county has adopted SB 391, the baseline standards are specific and unusually constraining for site-planning purposes: the ADU is capped at 900 square feet of usable floor area, the parcel must be at least 2 acres in size with exactly one existing single-family dwelling on it, and, critically, the ADU must be located no farther than 100 feet from the existing dwelling. A county may add further restrictions (on outbuildings that support the ADU, on additional property-size conditions, or otherwise) but SB 391 does not let a county expand the ADU beyond what it authorizes; adoption is entirely at the county's discretion, and even where the county has not adopted it, nothing prevents a future adoption before the owner is ready to build a second unit.

This turns the item's question into a fairly narrow, practical answer for an RR-zoned parcel: because a future SB-391 ADU (if the county has or later adopts it) is legally confined to within 100 feet of the main house, the owner does not need to reserve a large, undefined chunk of the property "somewhere" for ADU flexibility. The only genuine planning move is to keep the ground within roughly 100 feet of the house's eventual location clear of the shop, the block-yard, and other outbuildings, and ideally to route a utility stub (power, water, septic connection point) toward that same zone during initial construction, since retrofitting utilities to a spot chosen years later is far more expensive than running a spare stub while trenches are already open. Everything outside that 100-foot radius is unconstrained by the ADU question and can host the shop, block-yard, and other outbuildings without conflict. Where the parcel is EFU or forest-zoned, this 100-foot-radius logic does not apply at all, and the owner should not plan around it; the second-dwelling question there is a distinct approval process that should be resolved (or ruled out) before any outbuilding layout is finalized around an assumed future ADU location.

   Lands in: Land, Site, and Feasibility (future flexibility) and Developing the Site: Shop, Block-Yard, and Outbuildings chapter.

33. What does an Accredited Land Consultant (ALC) actually cost the buyer (commission structure, whether buyer-side representation is free to the buyer as in ordinary residential purchases), and is the fee worth it for a single owner-builder purchase versus a self-directed search? [COST] [REAL-WORLD-EXPERIENCE]
   Leads: REALTORS Land Institute (rliland.com) find-a-consultant directory.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** An ALC is not a separate fee-charging credential; it is a professional designation awarded by the REALTORS Land Institute (RLI) to a real estate broker who has completed the Institute's 104-hour LANDU coursework and meets an experience threshold (at minimum two years in land brokerage, or three years of comparable experience in auction, leasing, development, or related land services, plus either five closed land transactions totaling $15 million or 25 separate land transactions). An ALC earns money the same way any other licensed land broker does: through a negotiated commission on the transaction, not through a separate consulting fee layered on top. There is no published, fixed "ALC surcharge."

Whether buyer-side representation is free to the buyer, as it traditionally has been in ordinary residential purchases, changed as of the 2024 National Association of Realtors settlement: seller payment of the buyer's agent commission is no longer an automatic default and is instead a separately negotiated item, memorialized in a written buyer-representation agreement signed before the agent shows property. Land-sale commissions generally run higher than typical residential-home commissions, in the range of 5-10% of price (reflecting the longer marketing timelines rural land often takes), and this total is what is available to be split between listing and buyer-side agents or negotiated directly with the buyer. So the honest answer is: an ALC's compensation is negotiated exactly like any other broker's, it may or may not be paid by the seller depending on the specific listing and the buyer's own representation agreement, and there is no data located in this pass quantifying an "ALC premium" over a non-designated broker's typical commission.

Whether the fee is "worth it" for a single owner-builder purchase is a judgment call the research cannot make for the owner: an ALC's specialized land training (soils, water rights, timber valuation, land-use zoning, 1031 exchanges) is precisely the skill set this project's parcel search needs (marginal septic sites, EFU/forest zoning navigation, water-rights nuance), which argues for using one; a self-directed search avoids any commission split going to a buyer's agent where the seller is not covering it, which argues against one if the buyer is confident doing the due-diligence work independently. This is presented as a genuine trade-off rather than a settled recommendation.

34. How does a buyer legally and effectively pull county assessor mailing-address data to contact absentee or inherited-land owners directly, and does Oregon or any target county restrict bulk use of assessor data for this purpose? [METHOD] [TOOLS]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Oregon's public records law is intent-blind: any person has the right to inspect a public record that is not specifically exempt from disclosure, and the requester's purpose (including a commercial marketing purpose such as a land-acquisition mailing campaign) is not grounds for denial. County assessor tax-roll data, including owner name and mailing address, is a public record maintained under the county assessor's office (with the Register of Deeds/County Clerk holding the related recorded-deed history), so a buyer can request it directly from the specific target county's assessor office, either as a walk-in/records-request or, in many counties, through an existing bulk-data-export or subscription service the assessor's office already offers to title companies, real-estate data vendors, and other bulk users. Fees are legally allowed, covering the county's actual cost of records retrieval and any bulk-export format the county provides; the fee schedule varies by county and is not standardized statewide.

No specific Oregon statute or state-level rule was located in this pass restricting bulk commercial use of assessor mailing-address data for a purpose like unsolicited direct-mail land-acquisition outreach. This does not rule out an individual county imposing its own administrative conditions on a bulk-export request (for example, requiring a specific request format, charging a higher fee tier for bulk versus single-parcel lookups, or limiting the frequency of large exports), which would need to be confirmed directly with each specific target county's assessor office, since this was not verified county-by-county in this pass. In practice, most direct-mail land-investing guides describe pulling this data either directly from the county (often already digitized and downloadable from the county GIS/assessor web portal at no cost for non-bulk lookups) or through a third-party data-aggregation service (e.g., a county-records-based skip-tracing or list-building tool) that has already licensed or scraped the county's public data, which sidesteps the need to negotiate a bulk-export agreement with the county directly.

43. What redemption-period rules, if any, apply to sheriff sales on vacant land in Oregon specifically, as opposed to occupied residential foreclosures? [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The controlling distinction in Oregon is not vacant-versus-occupied; it is judicial-versus-nonjudicial foreclosure, and this matters more than the item's own framing assumes. A "sheriff's sale" in the strict sense (an execution sale conducted by the county sheriff, following either a judicial foreclosure of a mortgage or trust deed, or a money judgment generally) is a different, less commonly used track, and it does carry a statutory redemption period under ORS 18.960-18.983: a judgment debtor may redeem property sold at a sheriff's execution sale within 180 days after the date of sale, and a lien claimant may redeem within 60 days after the sale, by paying the full bid amount plus the purchaser's costs, taxes, assessments, and any superior-lien payments, with required notice to the sheriff no more than 30 days and no less than 2 days before the redemption. Directly on point for this item: ORS 18.964's redemption-period text does not distinguish occupied residential property from vacant land: the 180-day (judgment debtor) and 60-day (lien claimant) periods apply uniformly to any property sold at a sheriff's execution sale, vacant or occupied.

57. What specific tools (PVWatts, a solar pathfinder, a phone app using the camera and compass) give a reliable early solar-access reading for a candidate building site before committing to a full site survey? [TOOLS] [METHOD]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Two complementary, genuinely free-or-low-cost tools cover this need, and they are confirmed to serve different, non-overlapping purposes rather than being redundant with each other. NREL's PVWatts Calculator (pvwatts.nrel.gov) is a free online tool that uses historic weather and climate data for the nearest available weather station to estimate a solar array's energy production and value for any specified location, system size, and orientation; it answers "how much energy would a system of this size and orientation produce here," drawing on regional climate data, but it does not itself assess site-specific shading from trees, terrain, or nearby structures on a particular building site.

The Solar Pathfinder is a physical, non-electronic instrument (a reflective dome placed over a printed sun-path diagram) that has been the standard analog solar-site-analysis tool since the late 1970s; because it works by reflection rather than by observing an actual shadow, it can be used at any time of day, in any season, and in any weather (cloudy or clear), and it directly shows exactly which portions of the site's sky are obstructed by trees, terrain, or buildings at every hour and month of the year, answering the question PVWatts cannot: how much of the theoretically available sun at this location does this specific spot actually receive once real, on-the-ground obstructions are accounted for. The two tools are explicitly designed to be used together: combining the Solar Pathfinder's site-specific shading data with PVWatts's location-based weather/climate data produces a substantially more accurate site-specific solar-access estimate than either tool alone. A phone-app equivalent exists (software using the phone's camera and compass/GPS to approximate the same sun-path-and-obstruction analysis the physical Solar Pathfinder performs) and several are available, though this pass did not independently verify a specific current app's accuracy or continued availability; the Solar Pathfinder company itself also sells companion "PV Assistant" and "Thermal Assistant" software to process readings taken with its physical device.

For a candidate parcel's pre-survey screening specifically, the practical sequence is: use PVWatts first (no site visit required, gives the location's baseline solar resource), then bring a Solar Pathfinder or an equivalent phone app to the actual site visit to check for shading before an offer, reserving a full professional solar-site survey for after the purchase decision, once the specific house and array siting is being finalized.
