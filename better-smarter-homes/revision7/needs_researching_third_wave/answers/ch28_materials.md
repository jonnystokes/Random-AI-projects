### Aggregates, Sand, and Local Sourcing

**1. What aggregate gradation and manufactured-sand spec does dry-cast block production need, and can an owner crush/screen local rock in rural Oregon to hit it (sieve sizes, fines percentage, dust content)?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** ASTM C33/C33M (Standard Specification for Concrete Aggregates) sets the controlling fine-aggregate gradation: sieves No. 4 (4.75 mm), No. 8 (2.36 mm), No. 16 (1.18 mm), No. 30 (0.6 mm), No. 50 (0.3 mm), No. 100 (0.15 mm), and No. 200 (0.075 mm), with no more than 45% retained between any two consecutive sieves and a fineness modulus required between 2.3 and 3.1 (test-to-test variation no more than 0.20) (ASTM C33 summary, https://blog.certifiedmtp.com/understanding-astm-c33-standards-for-concrete-aggregates/; https://infinitalab.com/astm/astm-c33/; full standard text, https://dl.azmanco.com/standards/ASTM-C/ASTM-C-Series-Full/C/C33.pdf, checked 2026-07-12). Material finer than the No. 200 sieve (silt/clay-size fines) is normally capped at 3% for natural sand, but ASTM C33 explicitly relaxes this for manufactured sand: "if the material finer than the No. 200 sieve consists of the dust of fracture, essentially free of clay or shale, these limits are permitted to be increased to 5 and 7%" — a specific, favorable allowance for crusher-run material as long as the fines are crusher dust, not clay contamination. A separate acceptance route cited for manufactured fine aggregate: less than 4% by mass finer than 2 micrometers, with a methylene-blue adsorption value under 5 mg/g, is generally considered suitable. For dry-cast block specifically, NCMA/NPCA guidance (already captured in `ch12_blocks_part1.md` item 1) favors smaller, more rounded coarse aggregate (pea gravel is the NPCA-cited default) because larger angular aggregate accelerates press/mold wear, with cementitious material set near 470 lb/cubic yard and w/cm roughly 0.30-0.38.

Can an owner crush/screen local rock to hit this in rural Oregon: yes as a documented practice — the Wave 1 owner-fabrication research already found "most Oregon pits sell ASTM-graded products by name" and that a one-time sieve analysis ($150-300 at a local materials lab) confirms compliance (`done_researching/research_questions_phase2_owner_fabrication_and_equipment.md`, line 250). But owner-crushed rock is not automatically in-spec: crushing generates excess fines and dust that must be screened or washed down to the ASTM C33 bands above, and the crusher-dust fines allowance (5-7%) only applies if the fines are genuinely dust-of-fracture rather than incidental clay from the parent rock — which the source rock must be checked for. The practical path: crush/screen to a trial gradation, run a sieve analysis (ASTM C136) at a local lab (item 40 below names candidate Oregon labs), and adjust screening until the curve falls inside the No. 4-No. 200 bands before committing to volume production.

SETTLED for the ASTM C33 gradation numbers and the manufactured-sand fines allowance; the owner-crushing feasibility is documented general practice but needs a project-specific sieve-analysis confirmation once a source rock is chosen, which is a test the owner must run rather than a number this research can supply.

**2. What soil composition makes good cement-stabilized rammed earth (the target sand/silt/clay/gravel ratios, plasticity index, and Atterberg limits), and how does an owner test a local soil against those bands cheaply?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** For cement-stabilized rammed earth (CSRE), the commonly cited target gradation is sand content above 50% with combined clay-and-silt content below 30%; a related soil-concrete-style target rearranges native soil to roughly 5-10% fines, 55-71% sand, and 24-40% gravel (Auroville Earth Institute raw-materials guidance, https://dev.earth-auroville.com/raw-materials/; rammed-earth soil-property literature summarized via https://www.researchgate.net/publication/245308374_Soil_Property_Criteria_for_Rammed_Earth_Stabilization and https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S095006182200383X, checked 2026-07-12). Total clay content is usually kept in the 5-30% range because higher clay drives shrinkage/swelling that fights the cement stabilization. Atterberg limits: liquid limit below roughly 35-40% and plasticity index below roughly 10-30% are the cited target bands (same sources); with cement stabilization, the allowable clay/fines fraction can be pushed toward the low end of that range (8-10% in some high-stabilization-rate cases) because cement itself, not clay, is doing more of the binding work.

Cheap owner field tests, in ascending rigor: the **ribbon test** (work a moistened ball of soil between thumb and forefinger to extrude a ribbon; a long, smooth, glossy ribbon indicates high clay content, a short crumbly one indicates a sandier soil) needs no equipment beyond water and hands (GrowIt BuildIt, https://growitbuildit.com/testing-soil-texture-by-hand-soil-ribbon-test/, checked 2026-07-12). The **mason-jar sedimentation test** (fill a jar roughly 1/3 with dry, clod-free soil, top with water, shake, then mark the settled layers at 1-2 minutes [sand], 1 hour [silt], and 24+ hours or until clear (may take several days) [clay]) gives an approximate sand/silt/clay percentage breakdown for the cost of a jar and a few days of waiting (Clemson HGIC jar test, https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/soil-texture-analysis-the-jar-test/; GrowIt BuildIt mason-jar test, https://growitbuildit.com/mason-jar-soil-test-clay-sand-silt/; earthbag-building-specific version, https://www.earthbagbuilding.com/faqs/jartest.htm, checked 2026-07-12). Neither test substitutes for a lab Atterberg-limits determination (ASTM D4318) if the owner needs a defensible plasticity-index number for a building-official conversation, but both are legitimate, well-documented first screens an owner can run for free before paying for lab confirmation.

SETTLED for the gradation/Atterberg target bands and the field-test methods; a lab Atterberg-limits test remains the settled route to a defensible number for permitting purposes.

**3. How does soil clay mineralogy (kaolinite vs illite vs expansive montmorillonite) change cement-stabilized rammed-earth strength, shrinkage cracking, and cement demand, and how does an owner identify which clay he has?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** A peer-reviewed controlled study (the exact "leads" study named in the question) found that clay mineralogy changes CSRE compressive strength independent of the amount of clay present: montmorillonite lowered compressive strength the most, beidellite lowered it to a lesser extent, while kaolinite slightly increased compressive strength relative to a clay-free control (MDPI, "The Effect of Soil Mineral Composition on the Compressive Strength of Cement Stabilized Rammed Earth," https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1944/13/2/324; same study, PMC, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7013938/, checked 2026-07-12). The mechanism is water behavior: montmorillonite's expanding-lattice structure and interlayer cations give it very high water absorption and swelling, so soils with even small amounts of smectite (the montmorillonite/beidellite family) swell intensively, while illite- or kaolinite-bearing soils do not swell the same way — swelling drives the shrinkage cracking on drying that most damages CSRE, and it also increases cement demand because more of the cement's early hydration goes to fighting clay-water interaction rather than binding sand/gravel particles. The study's overall conclusion: it is not correct to treat "clay" as uniformly bad for CSRE — a kaolinite-bearing soil can outperform a cement-only sand mix — but even small changes in mineral composition measurably change the outcome, so mineralogy, not just clay percentage, needs to be known before finalizing a mix.

Identifying which clay an owner has without a lab: this pass did not find a validated cheap field method that reliably distinguishes kaolinite from illite from montmorillonite (X-ray diffraction is the actual laboratory method, and was not found offered as a low-cost field substitute). The best available cheap proxy is behavioral, not mineralogical: a soil that swells and shrinks visibly with wetting/drying cycles, sticks heavily when wet, and shows a high plasticity index in the ribbon/jar tests (item 2) is behaving like a montmorillonite-bearing (expansive) soil regardless of exact mineral identity, and that behavioral signal is what should drive caution (extra cement, a lower total clay fraction, or rejecting that soil source) even without a definitive mineralogical ID. A soil-mechanics lab that runs Atterberg limits can typically also flag "high plasticity" (CH in the Unified Soil Classification System) soils, which correlates with expansive clay content, giving the owner an actionable warning sign from the same test recommended in item 2 without needing a separate XRD test.

SETTLED for the mineralogy-vs-strength/shrinkage findings; NOT-RESEARCHABLE-PRO for a validated cheap field method to positively identify the specific clay mineral (this requires a soil-mechanics or geology lab's XRD test if a definitive ID rather than a behavioral proxy is needed).

**4. What soil pre-processing does compressed earth block production need (pulverizing clods, screening, and removing oversize), and what equipment does an owner need to feed a press continuously?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** CRATerre-EAG's manual (the standard reference for CEB production) sets out three sequential steps: **pulverizing** breaks up clay lumps/clods to obtain a homogeneous mix; **screening** removes stones and organic material and is described as indispensable whenever texture is wrong (oversize particles or too much organic matter) or pulverizing was inadequate, with raw soil typically screened on-site to approximately 1/2 inch for block-making use; then **mixing/stabilization** (soil combined with sand, cement, or another stabilizer) precedes **feeding and compression** into the press (CRATerre-EAG, "Compressed Earth Blocks: Manual of Production," https://rexresearch1.com/HouseConstructionLibrary/CompressedEarthBlockManualProduction.pdf; process summary, https://propagelleprojects.blogspot.com/2012/10/compressed-earth-block-ceb-soil.html, checked 2026-07-12). At a scale beyond hand-sieving, purpose-built equipment exists for a continuous feed: a soil **pulverizer** using two counter-rotating cylinders is documented at roughly 9 m³/h output; the CATAPULTER Model UNI 400 sieve machine, powered by either a 7 hp gasoline engine or a 5.5 kW electric motor and weighing about 220 kg, pulverizes lumps and removes particles over 20 mm at roughly 3 m³/h; and a planetary mixer (electric or diesel powered, roughly 200-liter practical capacity) handles the final soil/stabilizer blending stage before the press (equipment specifications per the Auroville Earth Institute CSEB page, https://dev.earth-auroville.com/compressed-stabilised-earth-block/, checked 2026-07-12). The DYNATERRE 03 system packages a pulverizer, a vibrating sifter, and a binder proportioner together as an integrated soil-prep line, which is the closest documented match to "continuously feeding a press" rather than batch-processing soil by hand. At owner-builder (non-commercial) scale, a lower-cost substitute exists and is already documented in this project's own research: a rotary hand-crank or small electric soil sifter/sieve (roughly 12-15 inch drum, sold for garden/compost use, $100-300 class) can perform the screening step at a batch size matched to a manual or small hydraulic press, though it will not sustain the 3-9 m³/h throughput the purpose-built machines above provide.

SETTLED for the CRATerre-EAG process steps and the named equipment classes/throughput figures; the owner-builder substitute-equipment sizing is a reasonable inference from documented small-scale sifters rather than a source that tested them specifically for CEB soil prep.

**6. Could an owner calcine local kaolin clay into metakaolin himself (kiln temperature, soak time, particle size) to make his own geopolymer precursor, and is the result reactive enough?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Yes, in principle, and the temperature/time window is well documented. Kaolinite dehydroxylates (converts to amorphous, reactive metakaolin) starting around 530-570°C for disordered kaolinite and 570-630°C for ordered kaolinite; one study cites 650°C for 90 minutes as producing near-complete dehydroxylation, while 650°C for 3 hours is documented as sufficient even for poorer-quality kaolin with low alunite content (calcination-temperature review, https://www.researchgate.net/post/Please_would_anyone_know_the_best_calcining_temperature_and_time_for_the_production_of_calcined_kaolin_from_the_parent_kaolin_clay; FEECO calcination overview, https://feeco.com/the-calcination-of-kaolin-clay/, checked 2026-07-12). For geopolymer-precursor use specifically, the optimal window widens to roughly 600-800°C, giving good reactivity; going above about 900°C forms mullite, a low-reactivity phase that defeats the purpose, and exceeding roughly 1,100°C risks forming cristobalite, a crystalline silica phase that is a recognized carcinogen if the resulting dust is inhaled — a genuine safety limit, not just a quality one (MDPI review on calcined-kaolin reactivity regulation, https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9717/12/10/2268, checked 2026-07-12). Commercial calcination is done in rotary kilns or vertical kilns; an owner-scale substitute would need a controllable-temperature kiln (a small gas or electric kiln capable of a sustained, monitored 600-800°C, with some means of turning/agitating the clay for even heat exposure) rather than an open fire, since uneven heating leaves under- or over-calcined material mixed together.

Is the result "reactive enough": this pass did not find a specific study that tested an owner-scale, small-kiln-calcined local Oregon kaolin against ASTM C618's strength-activity-index acceptance criterion (see item 7 below for that test's numbers). The chemistry says yes, a correctly time/temperature-controlled calcination of genuine kaolinite clay will produce reactive metakaolin — but reactivity in practice also depends on the parent clay's actual kaolinite purity (versus illite/quartz/other contaminant minerals) and on particle fineness after grinding, neither of which this pass can confirm for any specific Oregon clay deposit without item 7's strength-activity-index test being run on that specific material. This is a real "test to prove it" item, not a settled yes/no.

SETTLED for the kiln temperature/time targets and the safety ceiling; NOT-RESEARCHABLE-PRO / test-required for whether a specific owner's local clay, calcined at owner scale, actually meets a usable reactivity threshold — that requires running the ASTM C618 strength-activity-index test (item 7) on the owner's own calcined product.

**7. What natural pozzolans exist in Oregon (volcanic ash, pumice, diatomaceous earth, or owner-calcined clay), and how does an owner test a candidate pozzolan for reactivity (a strength-activity index)?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Oregon's volcanic geology gives it a real natural-pozzolan resource base: volcanic ash, pumice, and diatomite (fossil diatomaceous-earth deposits) all occur in the state, with diatomite specifically documented in central Oregon, though historically these deposits were mined for industrial diatomaceous-earth products (filtration media, absorbents) rather than as a cement pozzolan, so use as a cement replacement "would depend on local demand and competition from volcanic ash pozzolans in the region" (Burgex Mining Consultants, "Diatomite as a Natural Pozzolan for Cement and Concrete," https://www.burgex.com/2025/05/20/diatomite-as-a-natural-pozzolan-for-cement-and-concrete/, checked 2026-07-12). The strength-activity-index test is ASTM C618's own acceptance criterion: replace 20% of the portland cement in a reference mix with the candidate pozzolan, and compare the 7-day and 28-day compressive strength of the replacement mix against the unreplaced control; a minimum strength-activity index of 70% at both 7 and 28 days is the pass/fail threshold. Documented performance for the pozzolan classes named in the question: volcanic ash/pumice blends tested at 30% replacement achieved strength-activity indices in the 67-100% range (some formulations meeting, some falling short of, the ASTM threshold); diatomaceous-earth admixtures achieved a notably high 92.9-113.6% strength-activity index, evidencing strong pozzolanic activity; and the commonly cited optimum replacement level is around 20% for volcanic materials and around 10% by weight for diatomaceous earth for maximum long-term strength gain (strength-activity-index literature, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666549224000252; volcanic-ash/pumice study, https://www.academia.edu/48415559/Volcanic_ash_and_pumice_as_cement_additives_pozzolanic_alkali_silica_reaction_and_autoclave_expansion_characteristics; diatomaceous-earth study, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9573052/, checked 2026-07-12).

The practical owner-builder path: identify a candidate pozzolan source (a local pumice quarry, a volcanic-ash deposit, or an owner-calcined clay per items 6 and 34), then have a materials-testing lab run the ASTM C618 strength-activity-index protocol (a cast-and-break test, not a chemistry-only assay) before committing to that source at volume — this is exactly the "test to prove it" pattern this project's research repeatedly favors over trusting a generic literature number for an untested local material.

SETTLED for the Oregon deposit types and the ASTM C618 test method/threshold; the specific reactivity of any one candidate Oregon deposit remains untested and is a genuine test-to-prove-it item, not settled by this research.

**8. What compressive-strength penalty comes from recycled concrete aggregate, and how is the mix adjusted (extra cement, lower w/c, aggregate pre-soak) when the owner crushes his own?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The commonly cited penalty for 100% recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) replacement of virgin coarse aggregate is roughly 20-25% lower 28-day compressive strength than the equivalent conventional-aggregate mix; up to 25% RCA replacement of virgin crushed stone is documented as not significantly affecting concrete strength, meaning the penalty is concentrated at high-percentage or full RCA replacement, not at modest blending ratios (RCA strength-penalty literature, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/324990748_Compressive_strength_improvement_for_recycled_concrete_aggregate; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/313317860_The_Effects_of_Recycled_Aggregates_on_Compressive_Strength_of_Concrete, checked 2026-07-12). Three documented mix-adjustment strategies mitigate the penalty: **pre-soaking/pre-wetting** the RCA before batching is reported to produce the highest compressive strength among the strategies tested, because the pre-absorbed water performs an internal-curing function during hydration, which also improves freeze-thaw performance; **cement-mortar pretreatment** of the RCA particles (essentially re-coating the recycled aggregate in a thin cement slurry before use) is separately documented to significantly improve compressive strength; and standard mix-design levers — increasing total cement content and lowering the water-cement ratio — are confirmed as relevant adjustment variables in the same studies, consistent with how a lower-quality (higher-absorption, more micro-cracked) aggregate is compensated for in any concrete mix design (PMC review on RCA pretreatment, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10222201/, checked 2026-07-12).

For an owner crushing his own demolition concrete on site: the practical sequence is (1) pre-soak the crushed RCA to near-saturated-surface-dry condition before batching rather than using it bone-dry or highly variable in moisture, (2) budget for extra cement and/or a lower target w/c versus a virgin-aggregate mix of the same nominal strength class, and (3) treat any structural (not just fill/base-course) use of owner-crushed RCA as needing its own trial-batch compression-cylinder verification (ASTM C39, already the standard field-testing protocol this project's owner-mixed-concrete research already establishes) rather than assuming a literature penalty number applies directly to that specific crushed material.

SETTLED for the strength-penalty range and the three mitigation strategies; NOT-RESEARCHABLE-PRO / test-required for the exact adjustment (how much extra cement, what w/c) a specific owner-crushed RCA batch needs, which requires trial-batch cylinder testing rather than a fixed number.

**9. Which waste-stream fillers are structurally viable versus decorative-only (crushed glass, crushed brick, quarry fines, wood/rice-hull ash), and how does each affect strength and durability?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** **Crushed glass** is the clearest decorative-only-by-default case: post-consumer glass aggregate causes alkali-silica reaction (ASR) — the "strong alkali-silica reaction between the cement paste and the glass aggregate" is well documented as the reason broader structural adoption has not happened (ASR-and-glass literature, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/245308382_Investigating_the_Alkali_Silica_Reaction_of_Recycled_Glass_Aggregates_in_Concrete_Materials; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali%E2%80%93silica_reaction, checked 2026-07-12). Particle size changes the risk materially: glass ground to U.S. sieve No. 50 or finer produces ASTM C1260 mortar-bar expansions under 0.1% — actually less expansion than a glass-free reference bar — meaning finely ground glass used as a partial cement/pozzolan-like filler is a genuinely different (and much lower-risk) material than coarse crushed-glass aggregate; a coarser crushed-glass aggregate used structurally without ASR mitigation (a low-alkali cement, a proven SCM blend, or carbonation curing, all separately documented as mitigation routes) is the decorative/non-structural case this question is asking about. **Crushed brick** shows a real but dose-dependent effect: brick powder used as a fine-aggregate/filler substitute at 5% and 12.5% dosages measurably changes flow, rheology, and both strength and durability of self-compacting concrete in the cited study, meaning brick fines are a genuine partial-structural filler at controlled, modest dosage rather than purely decorative, but not a wholesale sand replacement without a mix-specific strength check (Sādhanā/Springer study, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12046-023-02278-x, checked 2026-07-12). **Quarry fines** (the fine byproduct of crushed-stone aggregate production, either dry screenings or washed pond screenings) are an established, structurally recognized material already covered by the same ASTM C33 manufactured-fines allowance discussed in item 1 — this is the most "structurally viable, not just decorative" of the four named fillers, precisely because it is chemically identical to the parent aggregate rather than a foreign waste stream (quarry-fines literature, https://library.ctr.utexas.edu/digitized/iacreports/1992_specrsrch_natlstoneassoc.pdf, checked 2026-07-12). **Rice-hull ash** (RHA) functions as a genuine pozzolanic filler, not merely decorative, capable of replacing or extending portland cement, though this pass did not find a specific strength-activity-index figure for RHA parallel to item 7's volcanic-ash/diatomite numbers; RHA's practical relevance to this Oregon project is limited by rice-hull availability (Oregon is not a significant rice-growing region), making it a less locally sourceable option than the others (rice-hull-ash literature, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6584775/, checked 2026-07-12).

SETTLED for crushed glass (decorative-only at coarse gradation without ASR mitigation; a genuinely different, lower-risk material when ground fine) and for quarry fines (structurally viable, ASTM-recognized); SETTLED-WITH-CAVEAT for crushed brick (structurally usable at documented modest dosage, needs a project-specific strength check at higher dosage) and rice-hull ash (pozzolanically real but not a locally abundant Oregon material).

**10. What is the honest per-unit cost of each candidate owner-made block material (cement/binder, aggregate, activator, energy, mold amortization, labor) versus simply buying standard CMU or ready-mix?**

### Mold and Machine Materials, Release Agents, and Import Sourcing

**12. Which release agents work for each mold material (steel, HDPE, rubber/urethane, wood), and how do cheap DIY options (diesel, used motor oil, vegetable/canola oil, form oil, soap) compare to commercial products for release quality, surface staining, dust, and effect on early curing?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** For **rubber/urethane molds specifically**, the finding is unambiguous and matches this question's own "Leads" note: petroleum-based oils and DIY substitutes chemically attack polyurethane rubber, causing it to swell, degrade, and eventually tear — this is documented as a real, cumulative material-damage mechanism, not just a theoretical concern (Volatile Free Inc., https://volatilefree.com/do-i-need-to-use-a-release-agent-with-urethane-rubber/, checked 2026-07-12). The purpose-built alternative confirming the lead's own figure: GlobMarble's G-1 concentrated concrete release agent is a water-soluble, non-petroleum-based formula used at roughly 30:1 dilution, specifically formulated to extend polyurethane mold life without causing the rubber to soften, shrink, or swell (https://globmarble.com/concrete-release-agent-g-1.html, checked 2026-07-12); silicone-based releases (e.g., Volatile Free's MR-515) are a documented second commercial option for non-porous mold surfaces (https://volatilefree.com/do-i-need-to-use-a-release-agent-with-urethane-rubber/, checked 2026-07-12). Vegetable/petroleum-jelly-class DIY substitutes are separately confirmed to "destroy the rubber mold over time and may stain concrete" — the DIY route that works acceptably for wood or steel forms is documented as actively wrong for rubber/urethane molds specifically.

For **wood and steel/melamine molds**, the picture is different and more forgiving: canola oil is documented as working "fine" for plywood/2x2 formwork casting concrete pavers, and vegetable oils containing a small amount of tall oil are cited as superior form-release agents generally, with canola preferred over plain vegetable oil (contractor-forum and BuildingGreen sourcing, https://www.remodelormove.com/what-kind-of-oil-do-you-use-on-concrete-forms/; https://www.buildinggreen.com/product-review/green-release-agent-concrete-forms, checked 2026-07-12). Diesel fuel is documented as an in-use professional practice for wood/steel forms, but used motor oil is specifically and explicitly discouraged even for wood/steel: residual sulfuric acid in used oil can damage both the form and the concrete surface, and used oil commonly carries heavy-metal and PCB contamination that becomes an environmental-disposal and site-contamination concern, not just a quality concern (same sourcing). Cooking sprays (e.g., Pam) are documented as working well as a low-cost, low-toxicity substitute for wood/steel forms.

Net comparison across the four mold materials the question names: **steel and wood/melamine** tolerate diesel, vegetable/canola oil, or cooking spray without serious downside (used motor oil is the one DIY option to avoid on any material, for contamination reasons independent of the mold material itself); **HDPE** was not separately documented in this pass beyond the general principle that a purpose-built release (or a mild soap/water solution, commonly used for HDPE precast forms per general precast literature) is standard; **rubber/urethane** is the one mold material where a petroleum-based DIY release is a documented, quantifiable mistake (accelerated mold degradation and swelling), making the ~30:1 water-soluble commercial product (or a silicone release) the genuinely different and correct choice rather than an equally-valid cheaper alternative.

**15. Which machine classes need 3-phase power, what is their total connected kW and peak (vibration-motor inrush) draw, and can a rural single-phase service, a phase converter, or a generator realistically run them?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Industrial block machines (the semi-automatic and fully-automatic tiers in item 13) are documented as standardly built for 3-phase power, "which is common in industrial areas" — implying single-phase compatibility is the exception, not the default, for these machine classes (Bess Machine generator-sizing guidance, https://bessmachine.com/blog/how-to-choose-the-right-generator-or-grid-for-your-concrete-block-making-machine/, checked 2026-07-12). This project's own prior "Leads" note (already on record) gives a vibration-system draw of roughly 15 kW in short bursts and a fully-automatic machine's total connected load around 51 kW. Generator/genset sizing guidance found in this pass confirms the same order-of-magnitude sizing logic: size the genset to at least 2x the machine's total connected kW to cover vibration-motor starting surge (a simplified rule-of-thumb figure), noting that motor starting current for compressor/vibration-class loads can reach up to 6x the running load in the worst case, and that a genset's usable continuous output should be planned at only 70-85% of its rated capacity rather than 100%, both of which push the practical generator size well above the machine's steady-state running kW alone (industrialmonitordirect.com generator-sizing guidance, https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/sizing-generators-for-motor-starting-kw-vs-kva, checked 2026-07-12). Voltage stability is flagged as a separate, real concern: unstable generator or rural-grid voltage can damage the machine's motors and controls, and a voltage stabilizer/surge protector is recommended good practice regardless of power source (same source).

Can a rural single-phase service, a phase converter, or a generator realistically run these machines: a correctly-sized generator (roughly 2x the machine's connected kW, per the rule of thumb above, i.e. on the order of 100+ kVA for a fully-automatic 51 kW machine) is a documented, standard solution — this is exactly the sizing math the industry's own supplier guidance describes, not a workaround. A rotary or static phase converter is the standard device for running 3-phase equipment from a single-phase rural service, but this pass did not find a source specifically validating a phase converter against a block machine's vibration-motor inrush surge (as opposed to steady-state 3-phase motor loads generally); phase converters are well-established for many 3-phase industrial tools, but the vibration-motor's inrush characteristic is exactly the kind of load that most stresses an undersized converter, so this should be confirmed with the specific converter manufacturer against the specific machine's nameplate starting-current figure before committing, rather than assumed to work generically. For this project's own realistic scale (~100 units/day, per item 13's finding that even the smallest imported machine tiers are over-scaled for that target), the manual/hand-vibrating tier avoids this power question almost entirely, which is itself a relevant finding: the power-infrastructure burden of 3-phase machine tiers is another documented reason (alongside the throughput over-scaling in item 13) that a manual or lightly-powered rig may be the better-matched choice for this specific project.

**22. How do you store and batch sand/aggregate for a consistent daily block mix (bunker/bin design, keeping stockpiles covered), and how do stockpile moisture swings force a correction to the water dose (volumetric versus weigh batching)?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Stockpile moisture is documented as genuinely variable day-to-day and even hour-to-hour, driven by sun, wind, rain, and humidity exposure, and this variation is confirmed as the direct cause of batch-to-batch inconsistency in the water-cement ratio, aggregate-cement ratio, mix yield, and even mix color if uncorrected (Agg-Net moisture-control guidance, https://www.agg-net.com/resources/articles/concrete/moisture-control-in-concrete-production, checked 2026-07-12). The documented mitigation at the stockpile-management level is straightforward and matches general concrete-industry practice already on record in this project's Wave 1 research (`done_researching/research_questions_phase2_owner_fabrication_and_equipment.md`, aggregate-storage section, already establishing covered-tarp or paved-pad storage): reducing sun/wind/rain exposure at the stockpile itself measurably reduces day-to-day moisture swings before they ever reach the mixer, meaning a covered bin or bunker (a roofed three-sided bay, or at minimum a tarped pile on a paved or well-drained pad) is the first and most effective correction — cheaper than compensating for large swings at the batching stage. The only fully accurate way to know a stockpile's actual moisture content, per the same source, is direct lab-style sampling: weigh a sample, dry it (oven-dry or a moisture-meter probe), and weigh again to calculate the evaporated water fraction (ASTM C566 is the standard method, already cited in this project's Wave 1 research). Field-practical substitutes exist for daily production use short of a full lab test each time: aggregate-moisture probes (in-line or hand-held) are documented as the modern in-plant solution for continuous moisture correction at the batching stage, feeding directly into an automatic water-dose adjustment on larger commercial plants (Hydronix moisture-sensor guidance, https://www.hydronix.com/resources/blogs/control-slump-in-concrete-production-using-moisture-sensors/, checked 2026-07-12) — a genuinely commercial-scale solution likely beyond a single-house owner-builder's budget, but the same underlying principle (measure moisture, subtract it from the mix-water dose) applies at hand-tested scale using ASTM C566's oven-dry-and-reweigh method on a representative sample pulled each production day.

Volumetric versus weigh batching: this project's own Wave 1 research already establishes that owner-mixed structural concrete is code-permitted under ASTM C685 (volumetric batching and continuous mixing) and ASTM C387 (packaged dry-mix), with the underlying principle that a mix design's stated water figure is total water including aggregate moisture, so the water actually added must be the design figure minus the aggregate's measured moisture content (`done_researching/research_questions_phase2_owner_fabrication_and_equipment.md`, "Aggregate moisture" subsection, line 313, already on record). Weigh batching (measuring aggregate by weight rather than volume) is inherently more precise for moisture correction because a weight-based moisture-content percentage translates directly into a weight of water to subtract, whereas volumetric batching must additionally account for bulking (moist sand occupies measurably more volume than the same dry weight of sand, a separate, well-documented aggregate-technology effect) — meaning volumetric batching without a bulking correction can under-correct or over-correct even after a moisture percentage is known. For a small, owner-scale daily block-production operation, the practical recommendation synthesized from these sources is: weigh-batch where practical (a simple platform scale is inexpensive relative to the mix-consistency benefit), pull and oven-dry (or microwave-dry, a documented quick field substitute for ASTM C566's full protocol) a stockpile sample each production day or after any weather change, and adjust the water dose by the measured moisture weight before each day's first batch.

**30. Does using recycled concrete aggregate (crushed demolition concrete) in any part of this project introduce its own ASR risk profile, separate from virgin-aggregate testing?**
