**1. What concrete mix design (cement content, slump, aggregate size) actually produces a crisp board-form grain transfer without honeycombing, and how does that mix interact with this project's own long-life mix-design targets (low w/c ratio, SCM content)?**
Lands in: Aesthetics and Architectural Expression.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Trade practice for board-formed work is a "5-sack" mix (roughly 470 lb cement per cubic yard, about 3,500 psi design strength) placed at a 5-inch slump rather than the typical 4-inch structural slump, specifically because the higher-flowing paste patterns the wood grain and reduces honeycombing at the form face ([JLC, "Tips for Building With Board-Form Concrete"](https://www.jlconline.com/how-to/interiors/tips-for-building-with-board-form-concrete_o); [BuildShow Network, "Board-form concrete – How to know what's good"](https://buildshownetwork.com/contents/boardform-concrete-how-to-know-whats-good)). The rich cement paste at the surface is what takes the wood's texture; a lean, harsh mix leaves the board pattern faint and increases the chance of bugholes at the grain lines.

This is reconcilable with a low-w/c, high-SCM long-life mix (per Structural System and Concrete Design section 6.13's w/c 0.45 max, 20-30% fly ash target) without abandoning either target, because slump and w/c ratio are not the same variable. A superplasticizer (high-range water reducer) raises slump without adding water, so the 5-inch flowable slump the board-form face needs is reached while the w/c ratio stays at or under 0.45 (JLC, same source, notes plasticizers are the standard additive for this reason). Fly ash at 20-30% replacement also tends to improve paste flowability and reduce bleed, which works in the same direction as a crisp grain transfer, but fly ash mixes set and gain early strength more slowly, which matters for form-stripping time on a board-form job (stripping too early tears the grain; stripping too late makes the boards harder to remove cleanly). This project's mix designer (per Structural System and Concrete Design) should specify the target slump increase via superplasticizer dosage, not added water, and add 1-2 days to the planned strip schedule to account for the fly ash cure curve. No specific w/c-vs-grain-quality test data crossing both variables was found in this research pass; a jobsite mockup panel (item 6) poured with the project's actual mix design is the way to confirm grain transfer before the real pour.

**2. Which wood species and board preparation (rough-sawn vs. surfaced, cedar vs. Douglas fir vs. pine) are actually available and affordable in Oregon for a board-form job, and what grain result does each give?**
Lands in: Aesthetics and Architectural Expression.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Douglas fir gives the strongest, most distinct grain transfer of commonly available softwoods: "of all the lumber species tested, Douglas fir leaves the most beautiful grain pattern... without having to sandblast or otherwise treat each board" ([Life of an Architect, "Board Formed Concrete"](https://www.lifeofanarchitect.com/board-formed-concrete/)). Cedar also transfers grain well, while pines such as ponderosa show comparatively little grain definition (same source). Rough-sawn (not surfaced/planed) boards are the standard choice, because the saw-textured face is what telegraphs into the concrete; dressed lumber gives a flatter, less distinct result.

Oregon is Douglas-fir country: rough-sawn Douglas fir dimension lumber is a standard regional sawmill product, unlike cedar or specialty species, which cost more and may require special order. No specific 2026 per-board-foot price from a named Oregon yard was verified in this pass; the builder confirms current rough-sawn Douglas fir pricing and lead time with a local Oregon lumber yard (a regional sawmill supplier, not a big-box retailer, typically stocks rough-sawn dimension lumber) before ordering board-form quantities.

**5. What form-tie type, spacing, and finishing (fiberglass vs. steel, snapped flush vs. patched) avoids rust staining and reads well in a board-formed wall over decades?**
Lands in: Aesthetics and Architectural Expression.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Fiberglass form ties, not steel, are the documented solution for a decades-stable board-formed surface. Steel ties, "even if supplied with a waterstop, will eventually rust with constant exposure to water," and a steel tie cut flush is conspicuous against the concrete once the cut end begins to oxidize ([Steel Dog, "Board Form Concrete Application"](https://steel-dog.com/board-form-concrete-application); citing industry practice). Fiberglass ties never rust, are gray and blend into the concrete color, are roughly five times stronger than steel by weight, and are cut flush with a grinder with no break-back labor, plugging, or patching required afterward ([Steel Dog, "Fiberglass Ties"](https://steel-dog.com/products/fiberglass-ties); [RJD Industries, SuperTie System](https://rjdindustries.com/products/supertie-system/)). Ties are laid out in a uniform vertical and horizontal grid coordinated with the board module so the tie pattern reads as a deliberate part of the composition rather than a random puncture pattern (item 4's mock-up principle applies here too). No specific tie-spacing dimension (e.g., "24 inches on center") tied to this project's actual wall thickness and form design was found in this pass; that spacing is an engineering value the form design (not this chapter) sets based on concrete pressure and form-panel strength.

**7. What causes color blotching specifically in board-formed work (as opposed to plain smooth-form work), and what release agent, board-sealing, or curing practice prevents it?**
Lands in: Aesthetics and Architectural Expression.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Two documented mechanisms cause blotching, and both apply with extra force to board-formed work because the wood face absorbs release agent unevenly: (1) release-agent absorption into the form face wears unevenly and cannot be renewed, leaving a blotchy surface, and switching release-agent type or brand mid-job changes concrete color ([CIP #23, "Discoloration," concreteanswers.org](https://www.concreteanswers.org/CIPs/CIP23.htm)); for architectural concrete, the fix is a single, consistent 100-percent non-staining, pigment-free release agent used on every form for the entire job (same source). (2) Uneven moisture evaporation, curing-compound overlap, and calcium-carbonate ("efflorescence-like") deposits during cure cause blotching independent of the form face; plastic-sheet curing that directly contacts the concrete causes streaking wherever the sheet touches unevenly (same source). For board-formed work specifically, the wood's own sugar content and moisture content vary board to board, which is a documented factor in surface color variation ("changing sand source... the wood species used and its sugar content... affects concrete color," per the mockup-panel research above); using boards from a single species, a single cut batch, and pre-sealing or pre-wetting the boards uniformly before the pour is the practical control measure. The manufacturer's release-agent application instructions and a single-source, single-lot board order are the two controllable levers; expect color to even out over the first 60-90 days of cure regardless (concreteanswers.org, same source).

**15. What is the actual step-by-step surface-retarder-and-wash procedure timing (hours after placement to wash, water pressure, distance) for a vertical wall form versus a horizontal slab, since almost all documented examples found are slabs?**
Lands in: Aesthetics and Architectural Expression.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Vertical-wall retarders are a distinct product class from horizontal-slab retarders: a vertical retarder (e.g., Euclid Chemical's Formula F, or a product like "Exposee V") is thick enough to be painted, rolled, or sprayed directly onto the form face before the pour, and dries to a semi-hard coating, unlike a horizontal retarder applied to the top surface of fresh concrete after placement ([Concrete Decor, "Using Surface Retarders on Vertical Concrete"](https://www.concretedecor.net/departments/walls-hardscaping/rocking-out-using-surface-retarders-on-vertical-concrete/), title and topic confirmed via search; direct fetch of the article was blocked by the publisher's server, so this is a secondary citation of the search-indexed content). Timing: strip and wash within 6-24 hours after placement depending on temperature and humidity, confirmed by an approved mockup panel; under normal ambient cure, a retarder keeps the surface paste workable for up to about 72 hours, while accelerated (steam) curing narrows the window to about 12 hours; one named commercial product (Top-Stop Vertical Retarder) specifies 12-14 hours of cure before stripping and washing (search-result synthesis, same topic). Washing method: a garden hose with a clean broom, or a pressure washer, working a small area at a time, stopping if the reveal depth exceeds target and letting the concrete cure further before continuing. Full cure before any sealer or coating is 28 days, with wet-curing maintained through that period (same source). Because the direct article could not be fetched for full procedural detail (water pressure in PSI, wand distance), the builder should obtain the specific product's technical data sheet (Euclid Chemical or equivalent) before specifying this on the project.

**16. How does seeding a decorative aggregate onto a vertical form face (rather than a horizontal slab) actually work, or does exposed aggregate on a vertical wall require a different technique (retarder-in-the-form-liner, not seeding) entirely?**
Lands in: Aesthetics and Architectural Expression.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Seeding (broadcasting loose aggregate onto the top of a freshly placed horizontal slab and floating it in) is a slab-specific technique that depends on gravity holding the seeded stone in place while it is worked into the surface; this mechanism does not transfer to a vertical form face, where gravity would simply drop any seeded aggregate off the form before the concrete set. The vertical-wall approach documented across manufacturer literature (Sika LITHOCAST, Euclid, and the vertical-retarder sources at item 15) is retarder-in-the-form: the retardant is applied to the form face itself before the pour, so the aggregate already present in the mix design is exposed at the surface once the retarded surface paste is washed away after stripping, rather than a separate decorative aggregate being seeded on afterward. This means for a vertical wall, the exposed-aggregate look is controlled by the mix design's own coarse-aggregate selection (item 14's aggregate choice becomes a structural-mix decision, not an applied topping), and by the retarder's depth of effect, not by a seeding step. Patented "surface-seeded" vertical processes exist (e.g., US Patent 7,614,820, "Non-slick surface-seeded aggregate concrete and method of forming," found in this search pass) but represent proprietary or historical methods, not a documented mainstream field technique for a house-scale wall; the retarder-in-the-form method above is the practical, sourced route for this project.

Lands in: Aesthetics and Architectural Expression.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Primary efflorescence occurs when excess water present during the material's original mixing and curing hydrates the cement and releases calcium hydroxide, which appears as a white powdery surface deposit typically within the first 72 hours after the material is placed or the building material is put into service; it is a one-time, temporary condition tied to the original construction moisture, and once removed, does not recur if that is genuinely its cause ([RadonSeal, "Efflorescence on Concrete, Brick, and Pavers"](https://www.radonseal.com/concrete-sealers/articles/efflorescence-causes-remedies-prevention.htm); general efflorescence sourcing synthesized across multiple sources including [Groundworks glossary](https://www.groundworks.com/glossary/efflorescence/) and [HowStuffWorks](https://home.howstuffworks.com/home-improvement/construction/materials/efflorescence1.htm)). Secondary efflorescence appears on mature, already-cured material and is caused by water penetrating from an external source after construction (rain, groundwater, or a plumbing leak) dissolving salts from the surrounding soil or from within the masonry itself and depositing them on the surface as the water evaporates; unlike primary efflorescence, it recurs indefinitely until the underlying moisture-intrusion source is found and fixed. The practical, sourced way an owner distinguishes the two years after construction: primary efflorescence is a documented, one-time event tied to the original construction period; if a white deposit appears years after construction, reappears after being cleaned off, or is localized at a specific point (a crack, a joint, a grade-contact area) rather than distributed evenly across a surface that dried out long ago, it is secondary efflorescence and indicates an active, ongoing moisture-intrusion path that needs to be traced and corrected, not simply cleaned.
