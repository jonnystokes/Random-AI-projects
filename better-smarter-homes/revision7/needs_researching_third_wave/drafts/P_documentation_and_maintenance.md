## Part 1: Gathered Understanding

### A. The Build Record And As-Builts: What To Record And How

**The timing principle.** Industry guidance is consistent on one point: the record captured during the work is reliable, and the record reconstructed afterward from memory is not. The most-cited single failure mode in as-built documentation practice is capturing rough-in only at project close-out instead of at the moment of work, because once a wall or slab is closed, a photograph is the only surviving record of what is inside it. The specific highest-value moment named repeatedly: a 30-second photo burst or short video pass of every rough-in run immediately before the drywall or the pour closes it, done as a habitual part of every site walkthrough rather than as a special end-of-phase event.

**What "before it begins" adds.** Professional as-built practice photographs existing conditions before work starts as well as after, and treats the as-built record as the documented difference between the two. For an owner-builder on a new build this maps to: photograph the excavation and the undisturbed grade before any trenching, and photograph the bare form or the bare wall cavity before any embed goes in, so the "before" state is provable, not assumed.

**Methods actually used, cheapest to most capable:**
- Phone photo burst with a scale reference (a tape measure or a marked rod laid in frame) in every shot, named and dated immediately, not batch-renamed later.
- Phone apps that auto-embed GPS coordinates and timestamps on capture (a named product category: CompanyCam is built specifically around this workflow for trade contractors, reportedly starting around $79/month for a small crew as of 2026; this and any other product name in this file is an unverified lead, not a recommendation). The auto-embedded location and time removes the manual-naming failure point.
- A narrated video walkthrough (already in the existing chapter for pre-insulation and pre-drywall); the addition here is to narrate dimensions out loud while filming ("this run is 14 inches left of the window centerline, 8 inches deep") so the video is self-describing without a separate caption pass.
- A 360-degree camera pass of every room shell before cover, which captures context a directional photo misses (what else was in the frame, the relationship between two nearby rough-ins) at a one-time equipment cost rather than a recurring one.
- Smartphone and tablet LiDAR (iPad Pro-class hardware) for a rough 3D model of a space; reported accurate to roughly plus or minus 1-2 percent of measured dimensions, good enough for space planning and a visual reference layer over the photo archive, not accurate enough to replace a taped or scanned dimension for construction-grade reuse.
- Professional photogrammetry or laser scanning, for the specific runs or conditions where a millimeter-accurate three-dimensional record earns its cost: reported residential combined cost (scanning plus CAD/BIM deliverable) of roughly $2,500-$9,000 total (2026), or $0.20-$0.70 per square foot for the scanning pass alone, with drone photogrammetry cheaper than ground-based laser scanning for exterior and roof geometry specifically. This is a targeted tool for a handful of the highest-value, hardest-to-reconstruct conditions (the finished foundation footprint, a complex roof geometry, the in-slab radiant and conduit layout immediately before the pour), not a whole-house default.

**Organizing the record once captured.** The commercial operations-and-maintenance (O&M) manual industry organizes the handover binder by a standard numbering scheme (the Construction Specifications Institute's MasterFormat divisions), so a facilities manager who has seen one O&M manual can navigate any other. The existing chapter's system-folder structure (site, foundation, structure, envelope, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, energy, smart home, administration) already does the same job in plain language; the transferable idea from commercial practice is Division 1: a front section listing every trade or supplier who touched the house, their contact information, and what scope each one performed, so a future service call starts with a name and a number instead of a search.

**The handover-document concept.** Commercial and residential-developer practice increasingly delivers a "digital homeowner portal" or "digital homeowner manual" at handover, alongside the physical package (as-built drawings, O&M manuals for every piece of equipment, and a warranty and supplier contact list). Some new-home quality codes now require a formal pre-completion inspection plus a homeowner user guide before legal ownership transfers. The transferable structure for a self-built house: a short, plain-language "how this house works" layer sitting on top of the full technical binder, distinct from it, aimed at a reader (a family member, a future buyer, a house-sitter) who needs to run the house today, not audit its construction.

### B. Mapping And Labeling Buried And Embedded Systems

The existing chapter names "owner-drawn as-builts (trench routes with depths and photos, septic and well coordinates, in-slab radiant and conduit maps)" as a single bullet and does not specify method. Real methods, cheapest to most durable:

**Photographed dimensions from fixed benchmarks.** Before any trench or slab is covered, photograph the run with its horizontal and vertical distance to at least two permanent reference points that will still exist decades later (a foundation corner, a fixed structural column, a property-line survey monument), not to a temporary object (a stake, a piece of forming lumber) that will be gone within the year. This is the zero-cost version of what a professional as-built survey does with instruments.

**Detectable marking tape and tracer wire.** For any non-metallic buried pipe or conduit, the trade standard (from the Common Ground Alliance and utility-marking practice) is to lay a continuous strip of detectable marking tape roughly 12 inches above the line and at least 12 inches below finished grade, so a future excavator's metal detector or pipe locator finds it before a machine finds it. Tape color follows the American Public Works Association code (yellow for gas/fuel, blue for potable water, green for sewer/drain, red for electrical, orange for communications). Plain foil-core tape degrades in soil over years; tape built around a continuous jacketed tracer wire is reported more durable than foil-only tape. Separately, direct-burial tracer wire (commonly 14 AWG copper, already required on some Oregon water-service installations per the existing plumbing research) can be brought to a labeled, accessible test point at grade so a future locator does not need to dig to attach a clamp.

**Electronic marker balls.** A passive, battery-free, no-power ball (roughly 4 inches in diameter, buried directly over or alongside a utility) that a technician's locator wand can find later by its unique electromagnetic signature; standard range detection is roughly 4-5 feet deep, with an extended-range product reaching roughly 8 feet. A newer product class carries programmable identification data (installation date, asset type, even GPS coordinates) readable by the same class of locator, which converts a buried marker from "something is here" into "here is what and when." These are a real, inexpensive, no-maintenance option (unverified per-unit cost; a research question below) for every buried tee, splice, valve, or direction change that the trench photographs alone will not pinpoint decades later after the landscape has changed.

**Embedded RFID and NFC tags cast directly into concrete.** An industrial practice from precast concrete manufacturing: epoxy- or ceramic-housed passive RFID tags rated to survive concrete's high pH (above 13) and steam-curing temperatures up to roughly 120°C, read at a range of roughly 10-15 cm even through rebar-reinforced sections, each carrying a unique ID linked to a database record. The precast industry uses this for factory-to-installation lifecycle tracking; the transferable, currently unverified idea for an owner-built house is casting a small number of these at a handful of high-value, hard-to-locate points (a critical splice, a below-grade valve body, a structural connection needing later verification) as a durable, powerless, in-place pointer to the digital record, in addition to (not instead of) the surface-level marking above.

**Ground-penetrating radar (GPR) as after-the-fact verification.** GPR locates rebar and embedded objects in existing concrete to a reported accuracy of roughly plus or minus a quarter inch laterally, penetrating roughly 18-24 inches, and is standard practice before any future coring, cutting, or anchor drilling into a concrete element so the crew does not hit the reinforcement or a conduit. This is the tool that checks the as-built record decades later, not a substitute for keeping one; a good build-time record makes the eventual GPR pass faster and cheaper (a smaller area to scan) rather than a blind full-wall search.

**811 / Call Before You Dig, and its limit.** Oregon's one-call utility-locate system (like every state's) locates utility-owned lines up to the property's service point; it does not locate the owner's own private site lines (the well line, the septic lines, interior distribution, any owner-run conduit) because those were never registered with a utility locate service. The owner's own as-built record is the only thing that finds those, which is the practical argument for building the record at trenching time rather than relying on any external service later.

**Physical markers at grade for buried access points.** Septic and well service providers, and the Oregon septic as-built rule itself (an as-built plan is a required deliverable at system installation per the existing septic research), point to marking the septic tank lid location with a stake, flag, or a riser brought to grade specifically so future service calls, landscaping, and vehicle traffic do not bury or damage the access point; the same logic applies to any buried valve box, cleanout, or splice point the owner wants found without excavation.

### C. The Maintenance Regime: Component Life, Schedules, And The Reserve-Study Method

The existing chapter's replacement-horizon table and generic percentage-based maintenance reserve are sound as far as they go; the facility-management industry runs a more specific, named methodology worth adapting.

**The Reserve Study method.** Multifamily and community associations (regulated in most states, with the Community Associations Institute and the Association of Professional Reserve Analysts setting practice standards) fund long-term component replacement through a "reserve study": a component-by-component list, each with an estimated useful life, a remaining useful life at the study date, and a current replacement cost, rolled up into a funding plan. Professional reserve-study cost data is drawn from thousands of prior studies plus published cost-estimating references (RS Means and Marshall & Swift are the two most commonly named, at a zip-code level of specificity); this file did not verify professional per-project cost for a reserve study itself. A "DIY Reserve Study Kit" software product exists for self-administration at a reported one-time cost near $499 (2026, unverified), built around the same component list, useful-life, remaining-life, and cost fields; public-domain life-cycle-cost-analysis tools have also been applied at single-family-home scale (a documented example used a 1,400-square-foot house). The transferable idea for this project: run the existing replacement-horizon table as an actual funded component list (each line gets a remaining-life countdown and an annual set-aside, not just a horizon year), and treat it as a document that is recalculated on a schedule, the way a real reserve study is, rather than written once at move-in and left static.

**Facility Condition Assessment as the complementary practice.** Distinct from a reserve study (which is financial planning against known components), a Facility Condition Assessment is a physical inspection producing a prioritized deficiency and repair list; commercial building owners typically commission one on a multi-year cycle (a specific interval was not confirmed in this pass; a research question below) as the inspection-side counterpart to the reserve study's financial-side output. The parallel for this house: the annual owner walk-through (already in the existing calendar) is the low-cost continuous version, and a periodic outside-eye inspection (an engineer, a home inspector, or a tradesperson walk-through every several years) is the FCA-equivalent check against the owner's own findings.

### D. Repointing, Recoating, Reroofing, And Sealant Cycles

None of these three cycles appear in the existing chapter's replacement-horizon table.

**Masonry repointing.** Reported service-life figures: mortar roughly 25-50 years, fired brick 100-plus years. Recommended inspection interval: every 5-10 years by eye; recommended repointing interval: every 20-30 years unless visible damage appears sooner. The specific visual triggers cited across masonry-restoration sources: mortar eroded back more than about a quarter inch from the face, mortar crumbling or soft enough to scrape out by hand, hairline cracking in the joint, visible voids, or cracking that runs through the masonry unit itself rather than only the joint (a unit-cracking pattern signals a structural or movement problem, not an ordinary repointing need). ASTM E2260 is the standard guide for repointing (tuckpointing) historic masonry; the National Park Service's Preservation Brief 2 is the most-cited practice document for the method. The single most consequential principle for a century-scale house: the mortar should be softer and weaker than the masonry units, not stronger. A correctly proportioned lime or lime-blend mortar is "sacrificial": it erodes and cracks before the brick or block does, and it is the mortar joint that gets cut out and replaced every generation, not the masonry unit. Repointing a historic or intentionally soft-mortar wall with modern high-strength portland mortar is a widely documented failure mode: the harder repair mortar does not flex with the wall's normal thermal and moisture movement, so the masonry unit itself cracks or spalls at the joint instead of the mortar absorbing the movement, converting a cheap, routine repair into unit-level damage.

**Roof membrane recoating.** Reported general recoat interval for flat and low-slope membrane roofs: roughly every 3-10 years depending on system and climate exposure, with acrylic elastomeric liquid-applied coatings specifically reported at a 5-15 year recoat cycle depending on foot traffic and UV exposure. This is distinct from full reroofing (membrane replacement), and distinct again from the protected-membrane roof (PMR) assembly the existing envelope chapter already specifies, where the membrane sits under insulation and ballast rather than exposed; a PMR membrane is not recoated on this cycle at all because it is not exposed to UV or foot traffic, and its maintenance event is a full ballast-and-insulation-off membrane inspection and replacement only at true end of life, on a much longer cycle than an exposed membrane.

**Flashing as the dominant roof failure point.** Multiple roofing-industry sources converge on flashing, not field membrane or shingle material, as the majority cause of roof leaks (a commonly repeated figure is that roughly 80-90 percent of roof leaks originate at a flashing detail). The specific highest-risk locations named repeatedly: chimney flashing (which typically stacks step flashing, counter-flashing, and a saddle behind the chimney as three separately fallible layers), valley flashing (which channels the largest concentrated water volume on the roof), and pipe or vent penetrations (where a rubber or plastic boot cracks with UV and thermal cycling well before the surrounding roof material fails). This directly supports and sharpens the existing chapter's seasonal roof-and-flashing check: the check is worth concentrating specifically at these three detail types rather than spread evenly across the whole roof plane.

**Sealant and caulk cycles.** Reported lifespans: silicone sealant roughly 10-30 years in general, with UV resistance cited as its specific strength for exterior, sun-exposed joints; polyurethane sealant roughly 10-20 years under good conditions but as short as 5-10 years under sustained high-UV exposure, because polyurethane's organic base does not hold up to direct sunlight as well as silicone (polyurethane is generally the better choice where the joint will be painted, since silicone does not accept paint). The practical design rule this supports: specify silicone at high-UV, unpainted exterior joints (south- and west-facing reveals, unpainted trim-to-concrete joints) and reserve polyurethane for joints that will be painted or are shaded. Reported failure causes that shorten any sealant's real-world life well below its rated range: poor surface preparation, wrong sealant chemistry for the substrate, and sustained moisture or extreme temperature cycling, any of which can cut a nominally 10-plus-year product down to 1-5 years in practice.

### E. Monitoring And Sensors As Preventive Maintenance

This section treats monitoring as an ownership-phase maintenance program, not a device category; the smart-home chapter states the local-first architecture principle, and this section states which sensor classes exist for which failure mode, how they are chosen, and what real-world limits each has.

**Water leak detection: two different sensing strategies, not one.** Point sensors (a small probe or pad placed at a known risk spot: under a water heater, under a sink, next to a washing machine supply line) only catch a leak that happens exactly where the sensor sits. Whole-house flow-based sensors (a single sensor at the main supply line) catch a leak anywhere downstream by watching the flow pattern, at the cost of not immediately saying which fixture failed. The two leading flow-based approaches are ultrasonic (a non-invasive clamp installed on the outside of existing pipe, no cutting or plumbing permit implied by the sensor alone) and an in-line mechanical or ultrasonic flow meter spliced into the supply line (which is new plumbing work requiring a licensed plumber, as the existing smart-home chapter already states for a powered in-line shutoff valve). An independent comparison study (Utah State University, cited across multiple retail sources but not independently verified in this pass) reportedly ranked a specific ultrasonic whole-house product ahead of several mechanical-turbine competitors for accuracy. Named product classes found in this research, every one an unverified lead: whole-house flow-and-shutoff systems (reported features: drip-level detection down to roughly 1 drop per minute on one product, a several-week learning period before the system knows normal household flow patterns), point-sensor-plus-motorized-retrofit kits that physically turn an existing shutoff valve's handle rather than requiring a new in-line valve (a reported low-cost example near $270, 2026, unverified), and combined leak-and-freeze point sensors that add an ambient-temperature sensor to the same battery-powered unit. The practical design conclusion: a whole-house flow sensor at the main plus point sensors at the two or three highest-consequence locations (water heater, any washing-machine connection, any below-grade mechanical room) covers both failure patterns.

**Freeze alarms.** The same point-sensor hardware that does leak detection commonly reports ambient temperature as a second function, so a "leak and freeze" combined sensor at a vulnerable location (an unheated mechanical room, a pump house, a crawlspace) serves both purposes from one battery-powered device. The freeze-alarm product category historically existed as a stand-alone device built around a phone-dialer function specifically for seasonal or vacation properties left unattended in winter; the same need applies to any part of this house's mechanical infrastructure that sits outside the conditioned envelope (a well pump house, an outbuilding).

**Well and cistern level monitoring.** Two sensor technologies dominate: a submersible pressure transducer (rated for operating pressure up to roughly 200 psia, equivalent to about 500 feet of water column, which covers essentially any residential well depth), and a non-contact ultrasonic sensor mounted at the top of the well or tank, which needs a reasonably wide-diameter, obstruction-free air column above the water surface to read reliably. The practical selection rule found in this research: ultrasonic works well in a wide cistern or tank with clear headspace; a narrow drilled well favors the submersible pressure transducer because there is no room for an ultrasonic beam to work reliably. Either sensor answers the same preventive-maintenance question this project's water-independence goal depends on: is the well drawing down faster than it recovers, and is the cistern about to run dry or overflow, before either becomes an emergency.

**Rural telemetry without cellular or home Wi-Fi coverage.** A pump house, wellhead, or outbuilding on a rural parcel is often outside the range of the house's own Wi-Fi and may sit in a cellular dead zone. LoRaWAN (a long-range, low-power wireless protocol) is documented reaching roughly 10-15 kilometers in open rural line-of-sight from a single gateway, with sensor battery life commonly reported at 5 or more years on standard lithium cells because of the protocol's low duty cycle, and it operates on solar or battery power independent of grid or cellular infrastructure. A specific documented use case: transmitting a remote pump house's temperature or water-level reading back to the main house over a LoRaWAN link where no cellular signal reaches the pump house at all. Where a site has no reliable landline or cellular internet option at all, satellite internet (specifically the low-earth-orbit class of service) is documented delivering median download speeds in roughly the 65-220 Mbps range with better than 99.9 percent reported uptime in ordinary conditions, sufficient for remote camera or sensor telemetry, with acknowledged degradation under heavy snow accumulation on the dish or severe storm conditions. Combined, these two technologies solve the specific rural problem the existing smart-home chapter's local-first architecture does not address by itself: how a sensor physically too far from the house's own network still gets its reading back to the controller or the owner's phone.

**Structural movement monitoring.** Crack meters and tell-tale gauges (two overlapping marked plates spanning a crack, reusable, needing no power, reported around $15-50 retail per unit, 2025-2026) let an owner read whether a specific crack is stable or actively widening over a period of months, on a simple quarterly or seasonal reading schedule logged by hand or photo. This is the same instrument draft I documents from the concrete-durability angle; the maintenance-program angle added here is the reading protocol itself: a fixed schedule, a simple log (date, reading, photo), and a defined escalation trigger (a specific rate of widening, not just "it moved") that converts a raw reading into a decision to call a structural engineer. Beyond individual cracks, tiltmeters (wired electrolevel units feeding a datalogger, or newer wireless MEMS-based self-contained units) measure whole-element inclination and can flag differential settlement or retaining-wall movement before it becomes visible cracking; these are a professional-tier instrument for a specific suspect element (a long retaining wall, a foundation on marginal soil) rather than a whole-house default, given cost and the need for a baseline reading taken by someone qualified to interpret it.

**Battery, inverter, and solar system telemetry.** For an off-grid or grid-tied-with-battery system, a central monitoring unit (the reference architecture found in this research is a Victron Cerbo GX or equivalent communication hub) aggregates the inverter, charge controller, and battery monitor onto one interface, with both a local display and a cloud portal (Victron's is called VRM) for remote viewing of state of charge, solar production, load, generator run status, and tank or temperature sensor readings, plus automatic generator start-stop logic tied to battery state. The maintenance-preventive value: a sudden, otherwise-unexplained drop in reported battery capacity or an unusual charge/discharge pattern is an early warning of cell degradation or a wiring fault, readable remotely, months before a battery bank fails outright. The local-first principle from the smart-home chapter applies here too: the local display and local logging should not depend on the cloud portal remaining available, given the vendor-sunset case history below.

**Septic high-level alarm.** A float switch inside the tank (or, for a pumped or alternative-treatment system, inside the pump or dosing chamber) rises with the liquid level and trips an alarm box, typically wall-mounted inside the house on a standard household outlet, with an audible horn and a warning light; the alarm is tested by manually lifting the float or by a built-in test button. For an alternative-treatment system already under the perpetual service contract the existing chapter documents, the alarm is typically part of the contracted system's own control panel; the underlying float-switch mechanism is simple, inexpensive, and worth understanding on its own so the owner can verify it is actually working rather than trusting an unverified indicator light.

**Humidity and moisture as continuous data, not a one-time check.** A logging humidity and temperature sensor left in a crawlspace or attic space (distinct from the existing chapter's one-time "maintenance-schedule observation" framing) turns a condition that normally goes unnoticed until visible mold or rot appears into a trend an owner can catch early, at low hardware cost, with no wiring beyond battery power and a wireless link to the same controller handling the other sensors in this section.

### F. Inspection Routines For Concrete, Masonry, And Envelope Over Decades

Draft I documents the concrete-corrosion science in depth; this section is the cross-material maintenance-program layer: what routine, at what interval, with what tool, logged where.

**Three tiers of inspection, by cost and skill.** A useful way to organize a decades-scale inspection program, consistent with what draft I found for concrete specifically and extended here to masonry and envelope: a free annual visual walk (the owner, no special equipment), a cheap instrumented layer (tell-tale gauges, a moisture meter, a basic borescope: bought once, read repeatedly, tens to low hundreds of dollars), and a periodic professional diagnostic layer (thermal imaging survey, GPR, half-cell testing: hired, and only as often as the visual and instrumented layers flag a reason to look closer, not on a fixed calendar).

**Concrete-specific routine (cross-referencing draft I, not repeating its material science).** Beyond the annual crack survey the existing chapter already specifies: a tap test or chain-drag pass across a suspect slab or wall area (dragging a length of chain, or tapping systematically with a hammer, and listening for a hollow versus solid sound) is standard bridge-deck inspection practice for finding subsurface delamination before it is visible at the surface, and it is directly performable by an owner with no special equipment. Rust staining on an exposed surface is a visible warning sign that corrosion has already started, often before any crack or spall is visible, because corrosion products can migrate through the pore structure to the surface ahead of mechanical damage.

**Masonry-specific routine.** The repointing-trigger inspection (joint depth, crumbling, hairline cracking, described in section D) is itself the masonry inspection routine; the addition at the whole-wall level is watching efflorescence patterns for recurrence at the same location after cleaning, since a spot that keeps re-efflorescing after being cleaned is flagging an ongoing water path that needs tracing to its source, not a cosmetic problem that a wash resolves permanently.

**Envelope-specific routine.** Infrared thermography (a handheld thermal-imaging camera) is recommended at roughly an annual interval by home-inspection industry sources, and detects moisture patterns, missing or settled insulation, and air-leakage paths that are invisible to the eye, by reading surface-temperature differences caused by the thermal or moisture anomaly underneath. A pin-type or pinless moisture meter provides a spot-check numeric reading at any wood trim, sheathing, or framing member accessible without demolition, useful anywhere a stain or a thermal-camera anomaly needs a number attached to it. A flexible borescope lets an inspector look inside a closed wall or roof cavity through a small access hole; the creative, currently unverified idea worth carrying forward from this research: design in one or more small, capped, deliberately placed inspection ports at construction time (in a location chosen for representativeness, not concealment) specifically so a future borescope check does not require drilling a new, uncertain hole into a finished wall decades later.

### G. Upgrade Paths Designed In

The existing chapter's future-upgrade provisions list (spare conduit, panel capacity, solar/battery zones, network rack, shutoffs, roof penetrations, accessibility) is already substantial; this section adds the concrete-specific technique and sizing detail behind it.

**The utility chase as an alternative to fully embedded conduit.** Commercial and masonry construction practice defines a "chase" as a continuous, deliberately left recess (vertical or horizontal) built to hold and later access pipes, conduits, and cabling, as distinct from conduit cast solid into the pour. A commonly cited minimum chase width for holding multiple systems with room to work is roughly 18-24 inches, and any masonry directly above a chase or recess wider than about 12 inches needs its own lintel support, which is a structural decision made at design time, not something added later. The tradeoff against the existing chapter's default (conduit embedded directly in the pour): a chase costs more design complexity and wall volume up front, but anything routed through it can be pulled, replaced, or added to without coring or demolition, which a fully embedded conduit cannot offer once it is undersized or its planned use changes. This is a genuinely different technique from what the existing chapter specifies, worth considering for one or two specific walls with the highest probability of future change (the network and mechanical wall is the obvious candidate) rather than the whole house.

**Conduit sizing rules of thumb from commercial low-voltage design.** For future capacity: roughly 1 inch is treated as a practical minimum for a single future branch-circuit or data corridor, with 1.25-2 inches or larger recommended for a feeder run, a multi-pair data trunk, or a future EV or solar feeder. Design guidance also recommends avoiding multiple tight 90-degree bends in a long conduit run (favoring gentler sweeps and intermediate pull boxes instead), respecting standard conduit fill limits, and leaving a pull string in every empty conduit at the time it is poured or buried, so a future cable pull does not start with fishing an empty pipe blind. For a concrete house specifically, this sizing decision is a one-time, non-recoverable choice in a way it is not for wood framing: an undersized empty conduit cast in a slab or wall cannot be enlarged afterward, only paralleled by coring a second hole, so the standard commercial advice to oversize the empty pathway (since the pathway, not the wire, is the expensive and hard-to-add part) applies with more force here than in a house that can be opened up later.

**Structural upgrade provisions.** Commercial phased-construction practice leaves projecting, capped reinforcing dowels at a wall or slab edge specifically so a later addition can tie new concrete into the existing structure with a proper load path, instead of relying on a surface bond alone. This is a genuine structural-engineering decision (bar size, embedment length, and location all depend on the anticipated future load), not a do-it-yourself detail, and is flagged in Part 2 as a test-to-prove or engineering-consultation question rather than asserted as guidance here.

### H. Managing Manuals, Warranties, And Vendor Sunset

**The documented vendor-shutdown case history.** Several dated, named, real cases establish the pattern the existing chapter's vendor-sunset section already warns against in the abstract:
- Insteon (a major DIY smart-home hardware brand) shut down its cloud servers with no advance warning in April 2022 after its parent company became unable to meet its financial obligations; customer hubs and apps stopped functioning even though the underlying hardware was undamaged.
- Revolv, a smart-home hub company acquired by Google/Nest, had its servers shut down by the new owner, which reportedly rendered every customer's Revolv hub completely inoperable regardless of the hardware's condition, despite customers having paid for the hardware outright.
- Wink, a smart-home hub brand, announced in 2020 that continued basic functionality (automations, remote access) would require a new monthly subscription; customers who declined lost features they had already paid for as part of the original purchase.
- Multiple retail-branded smart-home cloud services (Lowe's-owned Iris, Best Buy-owned Insignia, and Staples' Connect hub) were each shut down by their retail parent company, stranding the connected hardware in the same way.

The shared root cause across every case: the device's actual intelligence lived in a vendor-controlled cloud service, not in the hardware itself, so a purely commercial decision by the vendor (insolvency, acquisition, or a business-model change) could disable already-purchased, undamaged hardware with no recourse for the owner. This is the concrete, dated evidence behind the existing chapter's and the smart-home chapter's local-first and open-protocol preference; it is evidence worth citing directly rather than only stating the abstract principle.

**Right-to-repair and parts-availability law.** The European Union's 2019 ecodesign rules require manufacturers of specific appliance categories (including washing machines) to make defined spare parts available to professional repairers for a period after the last unit of a model is sold, commonly cited at 10 years. In the United States, state-level right-to-repair laws are an active and changing area; Colorado enacted a broad right-to-repair law in 2024 covering most digital electronic equipment, with an effective date of January 1, 2026. Oregon's own right-to-repair statute status for the equipment classes relevant to this house (appliances, HVAC controls, solar and battery electronics) was not confirmed in this pass and is a direct research question below.

**Owner-side defenses beyond the smart-home chapter's device-selection advice.** Two practices found in this research apply to every proprietary control board or electronic module in the house, not only the smart-home layer: buying a spare of a critical wear part or control board at the time of original installation, while the exact model is still available, rather than trying to source a match years after the manufacturer has moved on to a different design (a documented general pattern: manufacturers periodically redesign the internal parts of a product line for cost, regulatory, or technology reasons, and eventually discontinue support for the old parts entirely, which is the mechanism, not a one-time event); and deliberately preferring components with broad aftermarket and multi-manufacturer support over sole-source proprietary designs at the specification stage, which is the selection-level version of the same defense.

**Standardization as its own maintenance strategy.** Reducing the number of distinct valve types, fitting standards, and connection styles used across the house (for example, standardizing on ball valves as the default shutoff type throughout, and one connection standard per pipe material) is a documented practice for shrinking the spare-parts list a household or facility needs to stock and the troubleshooting knowledge a future owner needs to hold, independent of any single component's own service life.

### I. Passing The House To Future Occupants

**The stranger-buyer problem.** Owner-built and highly customized homes carry a specific, documented risk that a production-builder home does not: no manufacturer or builder warranty transfers with the sale, so a buyer with no relationship to the person who built the house is relying entirely on inspection and disclosure to understand what they are getting, and the same is true in reverse for a future owner encountering unfamiliar systems (a radiant-slab layout, an alternative septic system, an owner-designed water-independence setup) with no one to call who actually built them. The existing chapter's audience-extract system (buyer, tenant, contractor, responder, inspector) already addresses the sale transaction; the gap this research identifies is the "quick start" layer for someone who is not buying the house through a transaction at all, but inheriting it, house-sitting it, or otherwise stepping in without the sale-disclosure process as the forcing function.

**The house manual / quick-start layer.** Short-term-rental and new-construction handover practice has converged on a specific document type distinct from the full technical or O&M binder: a short, plain-language "how this house works" guide (where the shutoffs are, how to run each major system day to day, who to call for what) meant to be read start to finish by someone with no background on the house, as opposed to the technical binder, which is meant to be searched by someone who already knows what they are looking for. The style guide's existing rule that a dense technical section needs a plain orientation block is the same idea applied at the whole-house level: the technical binder needs a short, plain front door.

**The letter of instruction, adapted from estate planning.** Estate-planning practice recommends a "letter of instruction": an informal document, not legally binding and not a substitute for a will, that supplements the formal estate plan with practical information the executor or family needs, commonly including instructions for property maintenance routines, seasonal tasks, and vendor or service-provider contacts for each property owned, reviewed and updated periodically (annual review is a commonly suggested cadence). This maps directly onto passing a self-built house to family: a document distinct from both the technical binder and any legal will, written specifically to the person who will next be responsible for the house's upkeep, naming who serviced what, on what schedule, and why a given non-obvious decision was made the way it was.

**A real-world model for multi-generational property record-keeping.** State "Century Farm" and "Heritage Farm" recognition programs (run by state agriculture departments in several states, including a long-running Iowa program dating to 1976) formally recognize families who have held the same land for 100 years or more, and require documentary proof (typically a complete chain of deeds) to qualify. This is evidence, outside the construction industry entirely, that ordinary families do successfully maintain property records across multiple generations when there is a clear, simple record type (here, the deed chain) and a reason to keep it intact; the transferable lesson is keeping the record format simple and singular enough that a non-specialist heir, two or three generations removed from the builder, can still follow it.

### J. Long-Term Digital-Record Durability

The existing chapter's digital-record content is the 3-2-1 backup rule (three copies, two media types, one off-site) plus encryption and password-manager advice. That rule defends against loss by a single event (fire, theft, hardware failure); it does not address a different, slower problem: a file format or storage medium becoming unreadable decades later even though every copy is intact. This is new ground for the existing chapter.

**Format obsolescence versus redundancy: two different problems.** Digital-preservation practice (from national archives and research-library literature) distinguishes format migration (periodically converting files to currently readable formats before the old format or the software that reads it disappears) from simple backup redundancy; a perfectly redundant set of three copies of a file in a format nothing can open anymore is still a loss. The specific institutional practice worth adapting: treat the archive as something reviewed and, where needed, migrated on a recurring schedule (a specific interval was not found in this pass and is a research question below), not a "store once" event.

**Archival-appropriate formats.** The National Archives and Records Administration distinguishes formats acceptable for transfer from formats actually suitable for long-term preservation, and explicitly warns that a format being convenient to hand over is not the same as a format being safe to store for decades. Formats repeatedly named as durable choices in this research: PDF/A (an ISO-standardized, self-contained archival subset of PDF that embeds its own fonts and disallows external dependencies, unlike an ordinary PDF), plain text and CSV for tabular or textual data, TIFF for images, and open, published CAD and building-model exchange formats (DXF, IFC) rather than a single CAD vendor's proprietary file format, on the reasoning that an open, published format can in principle be read by new software written against its public specification even if the original software that created the file is gone, where a proprietary format depends on one company continuing to support it.

**Media durability, roughly ranked.** Consumer hard drives and solid-state drives are commonly cited as reliable for roughly 5-10 years, not longer, meaning they need periodic replacement and re-copy regardless of format questions. Magnetic tape is cited at a few decades under proper storage. Archival-grade optical media (the M-DISC product category) is marketed with multi-century to millennium-scale lifespan claims; this research did not find independent long-term verification of that claim (the technology has not existed long enough for direct proof) and it is flagged as an unverified manufacturer claim, not a fact. At the extreme, genuinely century-plus-proven end of physical (not magnetic or optical-disc) archival technology: micro-etched nickel disks (the specific product referenced in Long Now Foundation and Rosetta Project archival work, branded HD-Rosetta) physically etch text as microscopic images onto a durable metal disk readable with nothing more than an optical microscope, are reported to withstand temperatures up to roughly 500°C, are immune to electromagnetic effects entirely because they store no charge or magnetic state, and are marketed with lifespan estimates in the thousands to tens of thousands of years. This is a niche, low-capacity, currently unpriced-in-this-research technology, not a whole-archive solution, but it is a real, existing option worth naming for the single most irreplaceable core record set (the deed, the permit numbers, the structural drawings) precisely because this project names a multi-century structural goal explicitly.

**A physical, low-technology hybrid.** Printing the most critical documents (permits, the approved plan set, the deed, a summary of the as-built record) onto acid-free archival paper is a documented, low-cost, no-format-obsolescence-risk complement to the digital archive: acid-free paper is reported stable for a century or more under ordinary storage, and the highest-quality cotton-rag archival papers are reported capable of remaining stable for multiple centuries, against roughly 20-50 years for ordinary acidic paper before it visibly degrades. This does not replace the digital archive (a paper copy cannot hold the full photo and video record), but it is a genuine belt-and-suspenders answer for the single-page, highest-stakes documents specifically, and it needs no future software or hardware to remain readable.

**Durable on-site physical labeling.** For equipment tags, panel labels, or any physical marker meant to remain legible outdoors or in a mechanical space for decades, laser-engraved stainless steel is reported more durable against weather, chemical exposure, and mechanical wear than a printed or adhesive label, at a cost and scan-contrast tradeoff against laser-engraved aluminum (aluminum is cheaper and shows an engraved QR code with better contrast; stainless steel is tougher and more chemical-resistant). Either can carry both human-readable text and a scannable QR code pointing to the fuller digital record, giving a future technician standing at the physical equipment a direct link to its documentation without needing to already know where the binder is.

**The archive's own succession.** The existing chapter already recommends a password manager and a "where everything is" note. The specific mechanism that makes that survive its author: modern password managers increasingly ship a built-in emergency-access or inheritance feature (examples found in this research, all unverified leads: a downloadable one-time "emergency kit" document meant to be stored with a will or in a safe-deposit box; a named-contact system with a waiting period during which the account owner can decline an access request before it is automatically granted; a designated emergency recipient with either an immediate or a delayed handoff). Naming a specific mechanism, not just "leave a note," is the difference between an archive that is theoretically accessible and one a specific named person can actually open when the time comes.
