## Part 1: Gathered Understanding

### 1. What Mass And A Monolithic Pour Make Possible

A short framing list, stated as properties, not as a sales pitch for concrete:

- Compressive strength without added material lets a mass wall or vault carry loads in pure compression, including corbelled or catenary forms that need no tension reinforcement at all (the Nubian and Auroville vault techniques below).
- A fluid state before set lets concrete take the shape of its formwork exactly once, so curved, sculptural, and doubly curved geometry costs formwork and labor, not material chemistry, unlike a modular unit system.
- Volumetric heat capacity (concrete stores roughly 0.2-0.25 BTU per pound per degree F, unverified figure pending a materials-science source) combined with density gives a wall or slab thermal-storage capacity and sound-transmission mass at the same time, from the same pour.
- Density gives fire resistance, radiation and impact resistance, and acoustic mass together; a wall built for one of these purposes usually delivers the others without added cost.
- Permanence cuts the other way: a feature cast in now is difficult or destructive to add later (already the project's structural standard for embeds and penetrations, stated in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement chapter). Every idea in this draft is a decision to make before a pour, not after one.

### 2. Earth-Sheltering And Berming

**Forms.** The standard three-way classification, credited to architect Malcolm Wells ("the father of modern earth-sheltered architecture," per multiple secondary sources):

- **Elevational (single-slope):** one full building face exposed, usually south-facing, the other walls and sometimes the roof bermed or covered. Simplest for daylight, natural egress, and passive solar gain because only one face needs glazing and egress openings.
- **Penetrational (full berm):** every wall bermed except door and window penetrations; the building sits fully within a mound or into a hillside with only openings breaking the earth cover.
- **Atrium (courtyard):** rooms arranged around a central sunken courtyard that is the building's light, air, and entry source; the building can be fully below original grade with no exposed perimeter wall at all.

**Structural loading.** Earth cover adds dead load beyond ordinary roof loads. One source (a builder's account of an owner-built earth-covered house called Earthwood) cites roughly 10 psf of added dead load per inch of saturated soil cover and a combined design load near 185 psf for that specific structure; unverified, needs an engineering source rather than a builder's own account. Saturated soil after rain or snowmelt is heavier than dry soil, so the roof design load must use the wet-soil condition, not the dry weight. This load stacks on top of, not instead of, the snow and live loads already tabulated in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter's roof-structure section.

**Waterproofing.** The membrane on an earth-sheltered or bermed wall or roof will never be inspected or replaced without full excavation, which raises the acceptable-failure-rate bar above the below-grade waterproofing systems already specified in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement and Envelope chapters for an ordinary basement. Systems found in this research: hot-applied (or cold, peel-and-stick) rubberized asphalt sheet or fluid membrane; bentonite-clay composite panels (bentonite bonded to HDPE sheet with a fabric cover, which swells on water contact and self-seals around fasteners and minor penetrations); PVC and EPDM sheet; cementitious coatings. Multiple sources repeat the same operating principle: drainage, not the membrane, is the primary defense, because a membrane under permanent hydrostatic or saturated-soil pressure eventually leaks, while a membrane that never has to hold back standing water rarely does.

**Drainage** is therefore the design lever that matters most: perimeter drains, a free-draining backfill material, and a positive gravity or pumped outlet, layered the same way the Foundation chapter already specifies for an ordinary basement, but sized for a larger bermed area and, on a full-berm or atrium design, for water arriving from more than one side.

**Seismic.** A bermed wall is a retaining wall and a building wall in the same element, carrying static lateral soil pressure plus, in Oregon Seismic Design Category D0/D1 (the Willamette Valley baseline the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter already states), a seismic increment on top of it. The commonly cited method for that seismic increment is the Mononobe-Okabe pseudo-static analysis, an extension of classical (Coulomb) earth-pressure theory; multiple geotechnical sources note there is no single consensus method across all wall and soil types, and safety factors against sliding and overturning commonly drop from about 1.5 (static) to about 1.1 when seismic load is included. This is squarely inside the "definitely requires PE" territory the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter already sets for retaining walls over 4 feet and for any building classified as irregular.

**Egress.** Every sleeping room needs an emergency escape and rescue opening (5.7 sq ft net clear, per the dimensions already specified in the Envelope and Interiors chapters). A fully bermed wall cannot carry that opening without a window well, and multiple sources flag this as the actual design-limiting constraint on penetrational and full-atrium designs: bedrooms tend to migrate to the one exposed (elevational) face, or the plan needs deep, code-sized window wells on every bermed sleeping-room wall.

**Radon.** A 1980s-era USGS study of earth-sheltered buildings in Colorado measured radon in the roughly 1-9 pCi/L range in most structures, with one poorly ventilated earth-sheltered storage bunker reaching about 39 pCi/L, higher than typical conventional construction; unverified applicability to Oregon soil and to the project's already-specified active-capable passive radon system. More below-grade surface area (walls and sometimes roof, not just a slab) is more soil contact, which is the mechanism a mitigation design has to address.

**Real-world problems documented by owners and builders:** condensation and persistent mustiness in older earth-sheltered houses (an issue several personal accounts attribute to 1970s-80s construction without today's vapor-open interior assemblies and mechanical dehumidification, both of which the HVAC chapter already specifies generally); difficulty obtaining financing and insurance because comparable-sales appraisal (the standard mortgage-underwriting method) cannot find enough similar recently sold properties nearby, an obstacle documented in detail for monolithic dome homes below and reported anecdotally for earth-sheltered homes as well; and a widely repeated construction-cost premium figure of roughly 10-20 percent over conventional (wood-frame) construction, against which some regional builders report costs comparable to ordinary construction once labor and material substitutions are counted. None of these cost figures are sourced to more than aggregator or advocacy pages; they need verification, and the wood-frame baseline they compare against does not match this project's baseline, which is already concrete and masonry.

**Insulation placement for a berm** is a design choice distinct from the ordinary below-grade wall insulation already specified in the Envelope chapter. The Passive Annual Heat Storage design tradition (next section) uses a horizontal "umbrella" of insulation extending laterally outward from the wall near the surface, intended to keep a large mass of surrounding soil dry and thermally isolated from seasonal surface swings rather than insulating the wall itself.

**Not concrete, but a documented precedent:** Mike Oehler's Post/Shoring/Polyethylene (PSP) method, documented in "The $50 and Up Underground House Book," is a wood-post-and-plastic-sheeting earth-sheltering system, not a concrete method; only its site-selection and drainage-first design principles (uphill entry, drainage planned before excavation, an earth roof over a structural frame) transfer to a concrete-and-masonry design. Paul Wheaton's "wofati" (an acronym for Woodland Oehler Freaky-cheap Annualized Thermal Inertia) extends the same wood-post idea with a heavier earth roof; again, a site-planning precedent, not a structural method for this project.

### 3. Passive Annual Heat Storage, Annualized Geo-Solar, And Cast-In Thermal Mass Storage

**PAHS/AGS.** John Hait (Rocky Mountain Research Center) published the Passive Annual Heat Storage concept in 1983: solar heat entering through glazing is conducted through the floor and walls into a large mass of soil kept dry and thermally isolated by an "umbrella" of insulation extending roughly 20 feet outward from the structure at shallow depth, so the soil mass becomes a seasonal (annual, not daily) heat store. A related, separately branded variant, Annualized GeoSolar (AGS), developed by Don Stephens, uses a dedicated solar air collector to actively charge the same kind of soil mass rather than relying only on the building's own glazing. Reported performance claims (one case account) describe an interior swing of only a few degrees across the year, floating between roughly 70°F in winter and 76°F in summer, with earth temperature at depth rising from about 45°F to 64°F; unverified, self-reported, single-case data.

**Building-science skepticism**, specifically raised by researcher Robert Riversong in an online technical discussion, includes: the large volume of concrete and insulation the system requires carries high embodied energy and life-cycle environmental cost; soil moisture content, thermal conductivity, and drainage strongly affect the result and are difficult to model in advance (one geotechnical commenter called the needed transient thermal analysis "not an easy job"); the system has to be engineered correctly at the start because altering a buried heat-storage mass afterward is cost-prohibitive; and independently monitored, multi-year performance data is scarce, with most published accounts either proponent-authored or informal.

**The Oregon-specific problem.** A peer-reviewed study interpreting monitored field data from four passive-solar sunspaces in Eugene, Oregon (cited in the literature as challenging "conventional wisdom") found that standard thermal-mass design rules need substantial revision for a rainy, cloudy climate, and that the data favored smaller thermal-mass quantities with reduced ground contact than conventional passive-solar guides specify. Separately, general passive-solar literature notes that over 43 million Americans live where winter days are predominantly overcast, that roughly half of winter days are cloudy in the median large American city, and that a week of cloudy January weather will deplete any thermal-mass store, requiring real backup heat regardless of climate. Because PAHS and AGS both depend on solar gain (through glazing or a dedicated collector) to charge the storage mass, Oregon's marine west-coast winter cloud cover is a first-order design constraint on whether either technique performs as claimed at this project's actual site, not a minor caveat.

**Water thermal storage.** Steve Baer's "Drumwall" (Zomeworks, used at his own Corrales, New Mexico house in the early 1970s) stacks 55-gallon steel drums, painted black, behind south glazing, with an insulating night shutter (Baer's related "Skylid" system automated a similar function for roof glazing). Water is reported to store 10-20 percent more heat than an equivalent volume of masonry (a commonly repeated but unverified figure). Fiberglass water-storage tubes are sold today as a manufactured equivalent to steel drums for Trombe and water-wall applications. This project's own water-independence goal (a rainwater cistern) raises a genuinely open combination question: whether a structural or semi-structural cistern, cast into or against the building, can do double duty as thermal mass, a combination no source in this research pass specifically evaluated.

**Rock-bed thermal storage.** A 1970s-80s "Solar Room" design tradition used forced-air charging of a subfloor or side rock bed: rock sized roughly 3/4 to 1-1/2 inch, air flow in the tens of CFM in cited research examples, charging time shortened by higher flow rate and smaller rock size. This is an active (fan-and-duct), not passive, system, and needs the same accessibility-for-maintenance consideration as any buried system: a rock bed sealed permanently inside a slab cannot be inspected or have rock replaced.

**Trombe walls.** A glazed, unvented or vented concrete or masonry wall, typically 8-16 inches thick (20-41 cm; some sources report vented designs up to 400 mm), sited to receive direct winter sun, with vents near top and bottom of the wall in a ventilated design (roughly an 8 percent vent-to-wall-area ratio in one cited optimization) and glazing covering roughly 37 percent of the facade in another cited sizing study. A Trombe wall's design places its mass on the unconditioned (exterior, glazed) side of the wall's own insulation, which is the opposite placement from the whole-building envelope strategy this project already adopted (continuous exterior insulation, mass on the conditioned side, stated in the Envelope chapter); reconciling a Trombe-wall section with the rest of the envelope is a real detailing problem, not just a sizing exercise.

**Phase-change materials (PCM).** Materials (commonly paraffin-based or salt-hydrate) that absorb and release heat at a phase transition, giving a thin material high volumetric heat-storage density without concrete's bulk. Reviewed literature raises unresolved problems for building use: leakage from the encapsulation, reduced mechanical strength when PCM is mixed directly into concrete or mortar, and fire hazard; commercial building-integrated PCM products exist (wallboard, plaster additives) but the technology reads as still maturing rather than a proven long-life material fit for a multi-century structural component.

### 4. Green And Living Roofs On A Concrete Deck

**Categories and load.** Extensive (shallow substrate, 2-6 inches, low-maintenance ground cover such as sedum) reportedly adds roughly 15-35 psf dead load and can go on timber, steel, or concrete decks. Intensive (deep substrate, commonly cited from 8 inches up to 24-36+ inches, walkable, capable of shrubs, trees, and food crops) reportedly adds roughly 35 psf up to 100-120+ psf and, per multiple sources, should only be built on a concrete deck because of the added weight; one source states roughly 7-8 lb per square foot per inch of soil depth alone, before plants or retained water. Every figure above is from cost-aggregator or product-marketing pages, not a structural or FLL-standard source, and needs verification before use. The project's own roof structure already defaults to a concrete deck with a protected membrane roof (PMR), so the deck-material precondition for either green-roof type is already met; the open question is added structural capacity and cost, not deck compatibility.

**Assembly and root barrier.** A green roof's typical layer order (waterproofing membrane, root barrier, drainage layer, filter fabric, growing medium, vegetation) sits directly on top of the same membrane-on-deck arrangement the Structural System And Concrete Design and Envelope chapters already specify for a bare-ballast PMR roof; in effect, the growing medium and root barrier replace the gravel ballast and its filter fabric. Root barriers are physical (HDPE, polypropylene, or TPO sheet, sometimes with a copper-hydroxide chemical root inhibitant added) or a waterproofing membrane pre-certified as root-resistant under the German FLL testing standard, which is reported to eliminate the need for a separate root-barrier layer entirely. The drainage layer is typically a geocomposite (a drainage core bonded to filter fabric) engineered to shed excess water quickly, because green-roof plants that tolerate an unirrigated Oregon dry summer will not survive standing water in a wet Oregon winter.

**Cost (aggregator-sourced, unverified, year as reported by source):** extensive systems roughly $10-25 per square foot installed; intensive systems roughly $20-50+ per square foot; structural reinforcement, where required beyond the baseline deck, reported to add roughly $5-15 per square foot; ongoing maintenance reported at roughly $0.75-2 per square foot per year for extensive and $1.50-4 for intensive systems.

**Problems documented:** a leak under a green roof is hidden by growing medium and root mat and is far harder to locate than a leak under exposed gravel ballast, which several waterproofing-industry sources give as the reason electronic leak-detection systems (a low-voltage grid embedded at or under the membrane, tested before and periodically after the growing medium is placed) are marketed specifically for green-roof and plaza-deck work; root penetration at flashings, drains, and penetrations, where a root barrier is thin or poorly lapped; and drain or scupper clogging by soil and root growth, which can cause ponding and add unplanned load.

**Food production.** An intensive roof with 12-24 inches or more of engineered growing medium can carry a genuine vegetable garden or raised beds, at the top end of the load range above; this is a materially heavier structural commitment than an extensive sedum roof and should be a deliberate early design decision (per the project's own Structural System And Concrete Design chapter, adding capacity after the fact is not how a concrete structure is designed).

**Wildfire-hardening overlap.** The Envelope chapter's wildfire-hardening section (ORSC R327, where a jurisdiction adopts it) already requires a Class B or better roof covering with no combustible vents; a well-irrigated or gravel-heavy green roof surface is a plausible complement to that requirement, though this research pass found no source directly addressing whether a vegetated roof itself satisfies or interacts with R327's roof-covering classification.

### 5. Cast-In Features

**Benches, planters, tables, niches, shelving.** These can be formed integrally with a structural wall or slab pour, or added later as a secondary pour doweled and keyed into the cured structural element. Cantilevered ("floating") benches and shelves use two or three concealed cantilever supports in the formwork rather than visible legs. A commonly cited rule of thumb for a cantilevered concrete element generally (not specific to furniture-scale pieces) is roughly a 2:1 ratio of anchored backspan to cantilever length; unverified for a light interior bench versus a structural balcony, and the line where an owner-built cantilever needs an engineer of record's sign-off is itself an open question. Exterior planters attached to or cast against a structural wall need their own drainage (weep holes, an internal drainage layer) and, ideally, isolation from the adjoining structural wall's waterproofing so irrigation or rainwater in the planter does not wick into the wall assembly.

**Utility chases.** The Foundation chapter's existing rule (every sleeve, penetration, and embed planned before the pour, because nothing can be added to cured concrete without coring or demolition) argues for over-providing chase capacity now rather than accepting a fixed, exactly-sized conduit run. General trade and HUD-published guidance on future-proofing wiring recommends leaving spare, empty conduit runs and a centralized, accessible pull-box or utility chase at construction time, specifically because retrofitting a chase later is far more disruptive than installing a slightly oversized one during the pour. For a structure aiming at a multi-century structural life while its service systems (wiring, plumbing, data) will be replaced many times over that life, an accessible cast-in chase or corridor, not just a sleeve sized to the day-one wire count, is a genuine design-freedom idea specific to this project's stated goals.

**Cold/root cellar.** General root-cellar guidance (multiple homesteading and DIY sources) targets 32-40°F and 85-95 percent relative humidity, achieved by keeping the cellar's earth-contact wall(s) and floor uninsulated on the earth side (to stay coupled to stable ground temperature) while insulating and air-sealing the wall(s) and ceiling that separate the cellar from any heated space, plus two vents (roughly 3-4 inch diameter, one high and one low) to manage humidity and ethylene gas from stored produce. A masonry or concrete cellar can use a simple arched or vaulted roof (a shape concrete and masonry can form without an engineered flat-slab reinforcement scheme) if a small enough span is involved. Because a cellar of this kind sits outside the conditioned envelope and, in many designs, outside the coverage of the passive radon system specified for the conditioned slab in the Envelope chapter, it may need its own soil-gas consideration.

**Integrated safe room.** FEMA P-361 states the performance criteria (near-absolute protection from tornado and hurricane wind and windborne debris); ICC 500 (Standard for the Design and Construction of Storm Shelters) is the referenced building-code-style standard that specifies how to build to that performance level, including structural design loads (wind, missile impact, and, for an in-ground unit, hydrostatic and flood loads) under ACI 318 or ACI 332 for concrete elements, and a slab-on-grade minimum of 3.5 inches with reinforcement in two perpendicular directions. Because this project's walls are already reinforced concrete or masonry, the incremental cost of a code-qualifying safe room is likely concentrated in the door (a rated, tested assembly), any vents (missile-impact-tested), and any windows (none, typically), not in the wall material itself; unverified how much this reduces the reported general safe-room cost range of roughly $3,700-$30,000 (aggregator-sourced, wide range by size, above-ground versus below-ground, and location within the house).

**Fire-separation walls for a production zone.** This project plans owner-fabrication of concrete block, which implies a production, curing, and storage area adjoining or near the living space. A reinforced concrete or grouted CMU wall, already the project's default structural material, is inherently a strong fire-separation assembly without the added gypsum-board thermal barrier a wood-framed wall would need; the specific fire-resistance-rating credit for an unlined mass wall of a given thickness is an open, code-specific question, not yet answered in this research pass.

### 6. Curved, Sculptural, Domed, Vaulted, And Thin-Shell Forms; Cantilevers; Heavy Point Loads

**Curved masonry walls with ordinary units.** CMHA (the concrete masonry industry association) publishes a minimum-radius design method for curved block walls using standard units: the achievable radius is a function of unit length, joint width, and how much coursing offset (the "stick and string" layout method lays out the curve directly on site) the design accepts; a commonly cited rule is offsetting vertical joints between courses by at least one-quarter of the unit length for both structural integrity and appearance. Pre-molded radius blocks exist for ICF (insulated concrete form) systems in stock radii from roughly 5 to 10 feet, with custom radius forms available to order; documented ICF projects have used radii from about 4 feet up to 70 feet along natural site contours.

**Ferrocement.** A wire-mesh (or expanded metal) and steel-rod armature over which thin coats of cement mortar are hand-troweled, historically used for boat hulls, water tanks, garden structures, and small shells; described repeatedly as inexpensive and well suited to an individual builder because the armature can be bent to almost any curved or doubly curved shape without specialized formwork. Basic technique: wet the mesh before mortaring to improve bond and slow drying, work the first mortar coat into the mesh by hand, apply additional thinner coats once the first has set. The real question for this project is where ferrocement's structural and durability limits sit relative to the project's reinforced-concrete and masonry systems, and whether it is a legitimate loadbearing residential technique in this jurisdiction or a decorative/storage/outbuilding-only technique.

**Thin shells and the hyperbolic paraboloid (hypar).** Félix Candela's shells were buildable with straight wooden formwork boards because a hyperbolic paraboloid, though it reads as a curved surface, is mathematically a ruled surface generated entirely by straight lines; this is the specific insight that made his doubly curved concrete roofs constructible without curved formwork. Heinz Isler, separately, developed physical form-finding methods (a hanging, soaked fabric membrane, inverted, gives a pure-compression shape; a membrane under air or water pressure gives another) rather than relying on mathematical calculation, and considered the inverted hanging-cloth method his most reliable technique. Both are specialized structural-engineering disciplines in practice today, not owner-builder techniques as commonly taught, though the underlying formwork principle (straight boards can generate a curved, structurally efficient hypar surface) is a real lead for a small-scale application such as a carport or porch roof.

**Fabric formwork.** Research (including work associated with the CAST program at the University of Manitoba) documents using a stressed textile or film membrane instead of rigid plywood as the form face, for columns, beams, and doubly curved wall or bench elements, with methods including pneumatic (air-pressurized) molds, hanging-fabric molds, and prestressed-fabric molds; cited benefits include reduced formwork material and the ability to vary a cast element's cross-section to match its actual structural demand. This remains largely a research and specialty-precast technique; no source in this pass identified an owner-builder-scale commercial fabric-formwork kit.

**Catenary and free-spanning vaults built without formwork.** The Nubian vault technique (Nile valley origin) corbels sun-dried mud brick courses inward at roughly 60-70 degrees from horizontal, each course overhanging the one below by 2-3 cm, tracing an inverted catenary curve (the shape a hanging chain makes, which under load reversal carries force in pure compression) and needing no wooden centering form. The Auroville Earth Institute (India) modernized the technique using compressed stabilized earth block (CSEB, roughly 5 percent cement-stabilized, pressed in a manual Auram press) and a "free-spanning" method that allows horizontal, not just vertical, coursing, laid with a cement-stabilized joint reported as thin as 1-2 mm; their stated research goal is increasing span and decreasing shell thickness. This overlaps directly with the project's own owner-made-block ambitions (covered in the Owner-Made Blocks And Alternative Concretes research area) and raises a genuine question of whether a small, non-habitable structure on this project (a cellar roof, a well house, a garden structure) could use a formwork-free vaulted roof built from the same press and blocks planned for the walls.

**Monolithic domes.** Built by inflating a PVC "Airform," spraying polyurethane foam insulation on its interior face, attaching steel reinforcing bar in a hoop-and-vertical pattern, and applying shotcrete in layers until the design thickness is reached; the Airform stays in place as permanent weather protection and insulation is trapped between it and the shotcrete shell. Reported residential cost is roughly $100-250 per square foot, with total project cost commonly cited between roughly $164,000 and $296,000 (unverified, source-dependent, wide range), and the dome shell plus foundation reported at roughly 40-50 percent of total project cost. Documented performance claims include resistance to hurricane- and tornado-force wind (laboratory testing at Texas Tech University reportedly demonstrated survival of simulated winds exceeding 300 mph) and fire resistance; multiple monolithic domes reportedly qualify as FEMA P-361 safe rooms outright because of the shell's mass and continuity. Financing and insurance are reported as the harder problem, not construction: lenders rely on comparable-sales appraisal (three similar homes sold nearby within about 12 months), which is close to impossible to satisfy for a dome, and this reportedly blocks conventional mortgage financing regardless of the structure's actual quality; this obstacle is specific to a mortgaged, appraised transaction; whether it applies the same way to an owner-built, owner-occupied, unmortgaged project is unconfirmed in this research pass. Airform shotcrete construction is a licensed, specialized technique (Monolithic Dome Institute network); whether it is realistic for a mostly-solo owner-builder to self-perform, versus needing a hired specialty crew, is an open, cost-relevant question.

**Cantilevers and heavy point loads.** General structural guidance repeats a roughly 2:1 backspan-to-cantilever-length rule of thumb and notes that cantilevered concrete elements are governed by deflection and vibration serviceability (a commonly cited limit near span/250) as much as by raw strength; higher-strength concrete and a thicker section both extend the practical cantilever length. Beyond a covered entry, a balcony, or a floating stair, the same principle covers heavy, concentrated point loads a concrete floor or roof handles without special framing: a masonry heater or heavy woodstove, a home foundry or kiln, a stone countertop island, heavy fixed shop machinery, or a rooftop-mounted hot tub. Some of these are already inside the floor live-load table in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter (habitable-room and stair loads); a genuinely concentrated point load (a kiln, a press, a machine tool) is a separate point-load check the structural engineer of record needs regardless of the floor's tabulated uniform load rating.

**Acoustic mass.** Reported sound-transmission-class (STC) figures put 4-8 inch poured concrete or concrete block walls in roughly the 40s-to-50s range, against roughly STC 33 for an ordinary two-layer-drywall wood-stud wall and roughly 41-45 for an upgraded (double-layer, insulated, sealed) wood-stud wall; unverified source authority (acoustic-product marketing pages, not a primary testing-standard source). Where this project already builds a wall in concrete or masonry for structural reasons, the acoustic performance is close to a free byproduct; a genuinely useful design move is choosing which interior partitions (a media or music room, a primary suite away from a shop or mechanical room) get built as structural-grade mass rather than a lightweight partition specifically to capture this.

### 7. Board-Formed And Exposed-Aggregate Texture: Structural And Forming Coordination Points

The brief for this draft reserves the visual and aesthetic case for board-forming, exposed aggregate, and integral color for the Aesthetics And Architectural Expression area; the notes below are limited to the structural, waterproofing, and mix-design coordination points a forming or finishing choice creates, which belong here because they affect whether the technique is safe and durable to use, not how it looks.

**Board-formed concrete.** Wood boards used as the form face imprint their grain, knots, and joints into the cured concrete. Reported cost runs roughly $30-50 per square foot or $60-280 per linear foot, and contractors reportedly bid board-form work at roughly double an ordinary smooth-form job because of the redo risk if the boards shift, bow, or leak paste during the pour. The coordination question this project needs answered, not found in this pass: whether a board-formed wall face can still carry a fully adhered exterior air-control or waterproofing membrane (already a continuity requirement in the Envelope chapter) without the board texture compromising adhesion, or whether a board-formed face needs to be reserved for interior or protected locations only.

**Exposed aggregate.** Removing the surface cement paste, by seeding aggregate into the still-wet top surface, by a surface retarder followed by washing or light brushing (the most common contractor method, because it avoids abrasive removal), or by mechanical abrasion (sandblasting, grinding), reduces the depth of concrete covering the outermost material. No source in this pass quantified how much cover is typically removed by each method, which means the interaction with the project's specified reinforcement cover (already 1.5 to 3 inches depending on exposure, per the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter) is presently an open coordination question between a finishing technique and a structural specification, and should be flagged to the structural engineer of record rather than assumed to already be absorbed by the standard cover values.

**Integral color.** ASTM C979/C979M is the governing pigment specification, with a maximum dosage reported at roughly 10 percent of cement mass; no source in this pass quantified strength or workability impact at or near that dosage ceiling for a structural mix, which is a mix-design question (a trial batch, a water-reducer adjustment) distinct from the color-selection question that belongs to the Aesthetics area.

### 8. Vernacular And Historical Precedent Worldwide

- **Pacific Northwest indigenous pit houses (kekuli / earth lodges):** semi-subterranean dwellings, roughly 8-10 meters in diameter and about 1.5 meters excavated depth in documented interior-Pacific-Northwest and British Columbia examples, built around four main posts (typically cedar) with an earth-and-grass or earth-and-bark roof, sited near major rivers (Columbia, Fraser) and occupied seasonally, in winter. The structural method (timber post and earth roof) does not transfer to a concrete structure, but the site-selection logic (river proximity, seasonal thermal strategy, community siting) is a legitimate, regionally specific precedent for this project's own site planning.
- **Troglodyte (cave and rock-cut) dwellings worldwide:** documented long-inhabited examples in Cappadocia (Turkey), Matmata and other sites (Tunisia), Granada (Spain), and sunken courtyard dwellings in Henan and Shaanxi provinces (China, unverified exact naming from this pass); repeatedly described as thermally stable year-round because of earth mass, and several examples use a central sunken courtyard for light and air exactly matching the "atrium" earth-sheltering classification above at a genuinely vernacular, centuries-old scale.
- These are precedent and site-planning leads, not construction methods this project would replicate directly; the connecting thread to this draft's structural focus is the atrium and elevational forms these vernacular traditions validate at scale and over time, which the modern earth-sheltering literature (Malcolm Wells, the University of Minnesota Underground Space Center) formalized for contemporary construction.
