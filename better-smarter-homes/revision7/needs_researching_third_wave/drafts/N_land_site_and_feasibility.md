# Draft N: Land, Site, And Feasibility, Beyond The Existing Filter (Wave 3)

Scope note: the existing chapter (`g1_ch_03_land-site-and-feasibility.md`) already contains a strong pre-purchase severity-sort filter covering climate loads, zoning confirmation steps, title and recorded-restriction categories, utility and access verification, septic and water feasibility cost ranges, topography and drainage, and distance/logistics. This draft does not repeat that filter. It goes beyond it into finding land, evaluating a parcel creatively for this specific build, the mechanics of the deal, Oregon land-use nuance the existing chapter only touches lightly, creative acquisition paths, and real pitfalls owners hit.

Dedupe performed against: draft D and E (confirmed E14 septic-ladder, E18 well-siting/yield, E61 driveway/access budget, E63 water-independence layered redundancy already cover that ground at the system-design level; this draft stays at the pre-purchase screening level and cross-references those items by topic instead of repeating them), draft J (Oregon's water-rights tangle, exempt-use detail, well rehabilitation; this draft treats water only as a parcel-screening question and points to the Water Independence chapter for the deep dive), and draft K (Developing the Site: shop, block-yard, outbuildings, driveway build, retaining walls, fencing; this draft treats buildable area only as a pre-purchase acreage-and-layout screening question, not the post-purchase construction of those elements).

## Part 1: Gathered Understanding

### 1. Finding Rural Oregon Land

**Listed sources.** The major national land-listing aggregators active in Oregon: LandWatch, Land And Farm, Lands of America, LandSearch, Land.com, LandHub, LandFlip, and Landmodo (which specializes in owner-financed listings). Regional and specialty brokerages: Oregon Farm and Home Brokers (rural and luxury, large marketing reach), Whitney Land Company (dryland wheat, irrigated ground, cattle ranches, recreational/timber-adjacent parcels, concentrated in eastern Oregon), Mossy Oak Properties (recreational and hunting-oriented rural land), and county-level independent brokers who are not on the national aggregators at all. Ordinary residential MLS (through a licensed Oregon principal broker) also carries rural listings, especially parcels with an existing structure; vacant land is more likely to sit outside MLS on land-specific platforms.

**Off-market and pocket listings.** A pocket listing is a property a broker markets privately (personal network, word of mouth) without placing it on MLS or public aggregators. Rural land brokers who work a single county repeatedly often know of parcels before or instead of a public listing, because a seller (a farm family dividing an estate, a retiring rancher) prefers a quiet sale. Direct-mail and "driving for dollars" tactics, common in general real-estate investing, apply cleanly to vacant land: drive target areas, note vacant or under-used parcels, pull the owner's mailing address from the county assessor's parcel record (public data in every Oregon county), and send a direct letter. Vacant rural land disproportionately has absentee or inherited owners (a parcel held in an estate, a family member who moved away) who are more open to an unsolicited approach than an occupied-home owner. Apps built for this workflow (DealMachine, PropertyRadar, and similar) combine route planning, owner skip-tracing, and mail-merge; they are general real-estate-investing tools, not land-specific, but work the same way for vacant parcels.

**Auctions and tax sales.** Oregon counties acquire tax-foreclosed property in fee title (Oregon does not sell tax lien certificates the way some states do) and periodically auction it. Process pattern across counties (Lane, Linn, Washington, Yamhill, Douglas, and others publish their own procedures): notice published weekly for four weeks before the sale; many counties use a sealed-bid format, one bid per property, best and highest offer; winning bidder pays roughly 20 percent within 24 hours and the balance within about 5 business days; property conveyed by quitclaim deed, "as is, where is," with no title warranty, so buyer-side title investigation is the buyer's own responsibility. Properties that do not sell at public auction, or that are valued under roughly $15,000 or judged unsuitable for a dwelling, often move to a rolling private-sale list rather than waiting for the next auction cycle. Separate from county tax sales: general foreclosure auction platforms (Auction.com, Xome, and similar) list some vacant land alongside houses; sheriff sales (court-ordered foreclosure auctions) carry redemption-period risk in some circumstances and carry the same as-is, unverified-title exposure as tax sales.

**Government and institutional inventory.** USDA Farm Service Agency maintains a public resale/inventory site (properties.sc.egov.usda.gov) for farm and ranch real estate the agency holds through its lending programs, with priority consideration for beginning farmers and ranchers in some listings. The Bureau of Land Management periodically disposes of parcels identified as suitable for sale in an approved BLM land-use plan; this is a narrow and infrequent path (most BLM land is retained, not sold) but real disposal parcels do occur, publicized through BLM's lands-and-realty program pages. State surplus-property auctions (oregon.gov/das/surplus) occasionally include land parcels alongside vehicles and equipment, though this is uncommon.

**Timber and farm-deferral parcels as acquisition targets.** Large timber holders (Weyerhaeuser, Hancock/Manulife, and others) primarily trade in bulk institutional transactions, not owner-builder-scale parcels, but they periodically divest smaller edge parcels, and local timber-company land offices sometimes sell cutover or non-strategic acreage directly. A parcel currently enrolled in farm-use or forestland special-assessment (a property-tax deferral program tied to continued agricultural or timber use) can be a genuine acquisition opportunity because the current low-productivity-use requirement holds the price down relative to unrestricted rural residential land, but disqualifying the parcel from that program at purchase (by partitioning it, or by using it for a non-qualifying purpose) triggers a rollback/additional tax (see section 8 below); that cost has to be priced into the negotiation, and the contract has to state clearly who pays it.

**Land agents and brokers.** The Accredited Land Consultant (ALC) designation, awarded through the REALTORS Land Institute (a National Association of Realtors affiliate), marks brokers who specialize in land transactions across five categories: farms and ranches, undeveloped tracts, transitional/development land, subdivision and lot wholesaling, and site selection/parcel assembly. An ALC-designated agent working rural Oregon typically has direct relationships with surveyors, water-rights examiners, foresters, and other landowners who are not actively listing, which matters most for off-market and creative deals. The RLI website (rliland.com) has a find-a-consultant directory.

### 2. Evaluating A Parcel Creatively For This Build

**Access, beyond the existing legal/physical check.** The existing chapter correctly separates legal access (a recorded easement or public-road frontage) from physical access (a road that carries a loaded concrete truck in winter). Two additional access issues recur on rural Oregon parcels: (1) a "paper road," a road shown on the county plat or GIS as a public right-of-way that was platted decades ago but never built, graded, or maintained; GIS access lines are not proof of a drivable road, and the county road department can confirm whether a mapped road is maintained, vacated, or merely platted; (2) landlocked parcels that rely on an easement-by-necessity claim rather than a recorded easement. Oregon courts treat statutory and implied easements by necessity as disfavored and hard to establish: the claimant must show no existing legal access and no other legal way to obtain it, and an implied-necessity easement terminates once an alternative access exists. A parcel with no recorded easement and no road frontage is a dealbreaker-band item, not a negotiable risk, unless a recorded easement is obtained before closing.

**Water and septic feasibility, at the screening level.** Beyond the existing chapter's cost-range treatment: check whether the parcel sits inside one of Oregon's 22 designated groundwater administrative areas (critical groundwater areas, limited/classified areas, or areas withdrawn from further appropriation); inside a critical groundwater area, the state can restrict or prohibit new wells, including new exempt-use domestic wells, which can convert an otherwise-buildable parcel into a dealbreaker. The domestic (household) groundwater exemption allows up to 15,000 gallons per day without a water-right permit, plus separate exemptions for stock watering and up to half an acre of noncommercial lawn/garden irrigation; the exemption is per-property, so multiple wells on one parcel still share the same statutory cap. Well interference (two or more wells drawing from overlapping cones of depression, reducing yield for all of them) is a documented real problem in specific Oregon groundwater basins (parts of the Deschutes basin and southern Oregon have had reported dry-well events tied to nearby heavy withdrawal); checking OWRD's well-log database for the density and depth of nearby wells is a real due-diligence step before relying on a shallow or low-yield well. The septic and well cost material in the existing chapter is the right level of detail for a feasibility pass; the system-design depth (which septic type fits which soil, well-yield interpretation, buffering a low-yield well) lands in the septic and water-independence material elsewhere, not here.

**Power distance and the off-grid crossover.** The existing chapter gives grid-extension cost ranges ($15-$30/ft overhead, $20-$50/ft underground, 2026). The creative option worth adding explicitly: past a certain extension cost, self-generation (solar plus battery, sized for the house's real load) becomes cheaper than paying for grid extension, especially for a project that already plans solar and battery for resilience reasons. General guidance found (not Oregon-specific, treat as a planning heuristic, not a rule): once a written grid-extension quote exceeds roughly $10,000-$20,000, it approaches or exceeds the installed cost of an off-grid solar-plus-battery system sized to replace it, and the calculation should compare total extension cost against total off-grid system cost, not against a monthly electric bill alone. This project already plans solar, battery, and energy independence in depth elsewhere; the land-evaluation-level question is simply whether a specific parcel's grid-extension quote is high enough to make full off-grid a live option rather than a fallback.

**Solar exposure, slope, and aspect.** A site evaluation for this project benefits from the same passive-solar site logic used in passive-solar design generally: unobstructed southern exposure, with the south wall receiving direct sun for a meaningful part of the day through the heating season, and no ridge, hill, or tree line close enough to the south to block low winter-sun angles. A south-facing slope gains more usable winter solar exposure than flat ground at the same latitude; a north-facing slope loses it. This overlaps the existing chapter's topography section (which already flags slope orientation, cold-air pooling, and wind exposure); the addition here is treating solar orientation as an explicit, separately scored site-evaluation criterion alongside those factors, not folded into "topography" generally, because for a house planning solar-electric independence, roof and array orientation depend on the same south-exposure facts as passive heat gain.

**Wildfire, landslide, and flood hazard screening.** Oregon repealed its statewide wildfire hazard map and the associated mandatory building-code trigger via SB 83 (signed July 24, 2025); the 2025 legislature replaced the mandatory framework with a voluntary model defensible-space code that individual cities, counties, and fire districts may adopt on their own timeline (the existing chapter's Code block already states this). What that means for land evaluation specifically: the absence of a state hazard designation does not mean the fire risk is absent, only that the regulatory trigger is gone; a buyer still needs to independently assess fuel load, slope, aspect, and defensible-space distance to structures on any parcel in forested or brush terrain, because insurance underwriting (below) responds to the physical risk regardless of the legal designation. DOGAMI's Statewide Landslide Information Database for Oregon (SLIDO), an interactive map at oregon.gov/dogami/slido, compiles identified landslides and landslide-susceptibility layers; it is not exhaustive (many landslides are not yet mapped) but is a free first screen for a parcel on a slope. FEMA flood-zone status is checked free through a zone-determination letter referencing the property against the National Flood Insurance Program's Flood Insurance Rate Maps; a full elevation certificate (needed for some insurance or siting decisions) costs roughly $170-$2,000, averaging around $600, and requires a licensed surveyor.

**Buildable area for house, shop, and block-yard, as a pre-purchase screening question.** This project needs more usable flat-to-moderate ground than an ordinary house lot: the house footprint, a septic primary area, a septic reserve area (protected, not usable for anything else), a well and its setbacks, the driveway and a truck turnaround, a staging area for a concrete pour, and, specific to this project, a shop building, a block-yard and production pad (for owner-made block, with its own truck and material-staging needs), and material storage. Before buying, sketch all of those on one site plan using the parcel's actual contours and setbacks, not just the house. A parcel that is nominally large in acreage can have very little of it that is both level enough and far enough from setbacks, wetlands, and hazard buffers to hold all of that. Wetland buffers alone can consume much of a small parcel: mapped-wetland buffers commonly range 25-200+ feet depending on wetland class and local ordinance, so a 10-acre parcel with several acres of wetland can be effectively a much smaller buildable parcel once the buffer is subtracted. The National Wetlands Inventory (fws.gov, free) gives a first screen, but it is not a jurisdictional determination; a site-specific wetland delineation by a qualified consultant, if the initial screen shows anything questionable, runs roughly $2,500-$5,000 for a small parcel and $15,000-$20,000 or more for a larger or complex one.

### 3. The Deal: Pricing, Contingencies, Financing, Partitions

**Pricing rural Oregon land.** Price varies enormously by region, zoning, water rights, and access, more than most residential comps. Ranges found (all unverified leads, treat as planning orientation, not appraisal): statewide rural average cited around $3,900-$5,895 per acre (2026 planning figures from land-marketing sites, not an appraisal source); dryland wheat ground in Umatilla/Morrow/Sherman counties around $1,500-$3,500/acre; irrigated cropland with water rights along the Columbia and Umatilla rivers around $4,000-$8,000/acre; general agricultural land in Crook County cited around $2,000-$10,000/acre depending on soil and water; a cited rural Klamath Falls figure around $15,000/acre against urban Portland-area land running into the hundreds of thousands per acre. The pattern that matters for this project: water rights, soil class, and proximity to a population center move price far more than raw acreage does, so comparing a candidate parcel's price per acre against a same-zoning, same-county, same-water-status comp is more useful than a statewide average.

**Contingencies that protect the buyer.** For raw land specifically (harder to evaluate than an existing house), a due-diligence/inspection period of 30-90 days is typical, with 30-60 days common for a straightforward rural parcel; more complex parcels (contested access, unclear water rights, forestland dwelling qualification) justify asking for longer. Recommended contract language makes satisfaction of inspection reports a matter of the buyer's "sole and absolute discretion," rather than requiring the buyer to prove a defect, which avoids a dispute over whether a finding is serious enough to justify walking. A tiered due-diligence structure is a real, usable pattern: a short initial feasibility window (10-15 days) during which the buyer can terminate for any reason and recover earnest money, followed by specific contingency periods for financing, title, survey, septic/well evaluation, and any other named condition. Ending the inspection period requires a written withdrawal referencing the specific contingency clause to protect the earnest money; a buyer who wants to renegotiate price after an adverse finding does so before that written withdrawal, while still inside the contingency period.

**Seller financing and land contracts.** A land sale contract (sometimes called a contract for deed) lets the buyer take possession and equitable title immediately while making installment payments, with the seller retaining legal title until the balance is paid; this is a real and common path for rural Oregon land, especially from an individual seller (a retiring farmer, an estate) who does not need the sale proceeds immediately and prefers the interest income. Reported market terms (unverified, general planning figures, not Oregon-specific verified data): interest rates commonly 8-12 percent, down payments commonly 10-25 percent of price. Oregon law gives the buyer specific statutory protection against forfeiture: a seller cannot declare forfeiture without serving the statutory notice of default, and the buyer then has a right to cure, generally 60-120 days, by paying the arrears, costs, and capped attorney fees before losing the contract. Notarization of a land sale contract is not legally required in Oregon but is standard practice to avoid later disputes over authenticity.

**Partitions and lot splits.** Oregon distinguishes a partition (creating three or fewer new parcels) from a subdivision (four or more new lots); nearly every county requires at least a two-step local process (preliminary approval, then final plat approval) even for a minor partition, and county approval is required even where a county has not adopted its own specific partition ordinance. A property-line adjustment (relocating or consolidating a shared boundary between abutting parcels without creating a new parcel) is a materially simpler and cheaper process than a partition, and is the right tool for adjacent-parcel assembly (see section 5) rather than a partition. Both partitions and property-line adjustments require a new survey and a recorded plat or deed language with the precise legal description; county review standards, hearing requirements, and fees vary by county. A critical, easy-to-miss cost: partitioning land currently enrolled in farm-use or forestland special assessment is itself a disqualifying event that triggers the deferred-tax rollback (see section 8) at the time the new plat records, before the partition benefits the buyer at all.

**Earnest money and inspection-period tactics.** Earnest money size is negotiable and functions partly as a signal of buyer seriousness to the seller; a larger deposit paired with strong financial documentation strengthens an offer against a competing buyer without raising the purchase price itself. Because raw land is harder to evaluate quickly than a house, a buyer who wants a shorter path to a firm offer (useful in a competitive or off-market situation) can commit to the tiered structure above: a brief no-explanation-needed window to keep the earnest money moving, buying time to actually order title work, a septic evaluation, and a survey, without losing the parcel to a faster buyer.

### 4. Oregon Land-Use And Zoning Nuance

**Exclusive Farm Use (EFU): the farm-dwelling path.** A primary farm dwelling on a 20-or-more-acre EFU parcel is available where the farm operation grosses at least $2,500 per year from crops, livestock, or forest products, and the operation is not smaller than the county's average farm or woodlot at that income level. On a parcel smaller than that 20-acre/$2,500 threshold, the bar is much higher: at least $20,000 in gross farm income in two of the prior three years, or the land planted in perennials capable of producing $20,000 a year once mature. A relative's dwelling (a second home for a family member helping run the farm) is possible on the same lot as the operator's dwelling, under a defined-relationship test. No new dwelling of any of these types may be established in conjunction with a marijuana crop.

**EFU: the nonfarm-dwelling and lot-of-record paths.** Where the farm-income test cannot be met, ORS 215.284 provides a narrower nonfarm-dwelling path, gated on when the parcel was created and its soil class: in the Willamette Valley, the parcel must have existed before January 1, 1993, and be predominantly Class IV-VIII soils (not prime/unique or Class I-II when irrigated); outside the Willamette Valley, the same 1993 parcel-existence date applies with a "generally unsuitable for farm crops, livestock, or merchantable tree species" standard instead of a specific soil-class test; a Willamette Valley large-parcel variant allows a new 20-acre-minimum parcel where at least 95 percent of the soil is Class VI-VIII and at least 95 percent is incapable of producing 50 cubic feet per acre per year of timber. Separately, a lot-of-record dwelling path exists for a parcel lawfully created and separately held before zoning restricted it, generally requiring acquisition by the current owner (or by inheritance from a qualifying prior owner) before January 1, 1985, with no intervening merger into a larger tract. These are dense, county-administered tests; the existing chapter correctly flags EFU as "restrictive, treat with care," and this section supplies the actual numeric gates behind that caution for the research-and-write phase to verify against current statute text and confirm with the specific county planner.

**Forest zoning: the two dwelling paths.** A large-tract forestland dwelling (ORS 215.740) requires a contiguous tract of at least 160 acres in western Oregon or 240 acres in eastern Oregon; a public road or waterway crossing the tract does not reduce the counted acreage; non-contiguous tracts in the same or an adjacent county can be combined to reach 200 acres (western) or 320 acres (eastern), but only if the owner records an irrevocable deed restriction giving up all future dwelling rights on the combined tracts. A small-tract (template) forestland dwelling (ORS 215.750) uses a template test instead of a flat acreage minimum: a 160-acre square (or a 1-mile-by-quarter-mile rectangle) is drawn centered on the subject tract, and the count of other lots and existing dwellings inside that template must meet a threshold that scales inversely with the land's timber productivity (measured in cubic feet per acre per year); lower-productivity land needs fewer surrounding parcels and dwellings to qualify, higher-productivity land needs more. Reported thresholds (unverified, need statute confirmation at research time): in western Oregon, 3 or more other lots plus 3 or more existing dwellings for the lowest productivity band, rising to 7-plus and then 11-plus lots (each still with a 3-dwelling floor) as productivity rises; eastern Oregon uses lower productivity breakpoints for the same tiered structure. The subject tract must have been lawfully established, must not have had a dwelling on it as of January 1, 2019, and a property-line adjustment made after January 1, 2019, cannot be used to manufacture qualification.

**Rural Residential and Mixed Use Agricultural (MUA).** The existing chapter already ranks Rural Residential (RR-2/5/10/20, the number being the minimum lot size in acres) as often the cleanest zoning target, and MUA-10/MUA-20 as often workable, county-dependent. State-level floor rules found worth adding: counties cannot create new parcels smaller than 2 acres within existing rural-residential zones, and cannot rezone in a way that reduces an existing rural-residential minimum lot size below its current level; for a brand-new rural-residential "exception area" (land taken out of resource zoning specifically to allow it), the state minimum parcel size is 10 acres, though clustering provisions can allow smaller individual lots within a larger planned area. MUA zoning specifics (permitted uses, whether a home occupation involving equipment like a block press is allowed, outbuilding limits) vary meaningfully county to county and need a county-by-county confirmation call, matching the existing chapter's instruction to verify zoning directly and in writing.

**Non-resource lands, a narrow and mostly closed path.** Counties may designate rural land that fails the statutory definition of farm or forest land as "non-resource land," a category with generally more flexible dwelling rules and smaller minimum-parcel sizes than EFU or forest zoning. In practice this path is narrow: it requires the county to affirmatively designate the land against Statewide Planning Goals 5, 6, 7, and 14 (natural resources, water quality/hazards, and rural character), and a multi-county pilot effort (Douglas, Jackson, and Josephine counties) to expand this designation did not reach consensus and largely stalled. A buyer should treat "non-resource land" as an occasional found opportunity on an already-designated parcel, not a strategy to pursue by asking a county to newly designate a candidate parcel.

**Measure 49 and Measure 56, two different things worth not conflating.** Measure 49 (2007) modified the earlier Measure 37 (2004) regulatory-takings compensation scheme: a landowner with a validated Measure 37 claim can develop up to a defined number of home sites (the count depends on the value-reduction tier originally claimed), each home site allowing the parcel to be divided and a dwelling built. Unlike the original Measure 37 waiver, a Measure 49 home-site authorization is transferable to a subsequent owner, which means a parcel for sale today can carry real, usable dwelling rights from a decades-old claim, discoverable through DLCD's Measure 49 claim database and analyzer tool. The catch for a buyer: a subsequent owner (someone who did not hold the original claim) faces a completion deadline, reported as roughly 10 years from acquiring the approval, to actually build and finish the authorized dwelling, unlike the original claimant, who had no deadline; this deadline needs verification against the current OAR text at research time, because it directly affects whether a Measure 49 parcel is a genuine opportunity or an expiring one. Measure 56 (approved by voters, reported here as either 1998 or 1999 depending on source, needing date verification) is unrelated to dwelling rights: it requires local government to mail individual notice to affected landowners before a zoning change that would limit or prohibit a previously allowed use, and it connects to property tax because a rezoning is one of the specific events (alongside new construction, subdivision, and disqualification from special assessment) that lets assessed value jump by more than the normal 3 percent annual cap under Oregon's Measure 50 system. The task brief's shorthand "Measure 49/56 legacy rights" bundles two different mechanisms; the research and writing phase should treat them separately, since one is a dwelling-right benefit that can transfer to a buyer and the other is a tax-notice procedural safeguard.

### 5. Creative Acquisition

**Co-purchase.** Buying land jointly with siblings, extended family, or friends is usually structured as tenancy in common (allows unequal ownership shares, useful when contributions differ) rather than joint tenancy (equal shares, right of survivorship). The real risk, documented repeatedly in general real-estate-law sources, is that any co-owner can file a partition action at essentially any time, for any reason or no stated reason, and a court will often order a forced sale if the property cannot be physically divided; reported general partition-litigation costs run into the tens of thousands of dollars and many months, though these figures are from general U.S. sources, not Oregon-specific, and need Oregon-specific verification. The documented fix is a written co-ownership agreement executed before or at purchase, addressing decision-making, expense-splitting, a buyout mechanism and its pricing method, and what happens if one co-owner wants out or stops paying; a few hours of attorney time on that agreement is the standard advice against a partition action later.

**Family land.** Land already owned by a family member (inherited, or held for decades) can be acquired by purchase at a negotiated or below-market price, by gift, or by a life-estate/remainder arrangement (the older owner keeps use for life, the builder holds the remainder interest). Each path has different tax consequences (gift-tax reporting, cost-basis carryover on a gift versus a stepped-up basis on inheritance) that belong with a CPA, not this book, but the land-evaluation-level point is that family land often skips the open-market search and pricing process entirely, while still requiring the same zoning, access, water, and septic feasibility verification as any other parcel; family familiarity is not a substitute for the feasibility filter. Where the ownership structure involves any entity (a family LLC holding the land, for instance) the owner-builder posture questions in the legal chapter apply and should be checked before relying on the homeowner exemption path.

**Lease-to-own.** A lease with a purchase option (the tenant leases the land, with an agreed option to buy at a set or formula price within a set period, often with a portion of rent credited toward the eventual purchase) is a real, if less common, path to rural land, more often found through a direct relationship with a landowner (a farmer easing into retirement, a family estate not ready to sell outright) than through a public listing. It lets a buyer occupy and evaluate a parcel over time, including seasonal water and access conditions, before committing capital, but it defers the zoning and dwelling-approval risk: an owner-builder still needs the dwelling-approval path (EFU, forest, or otherwise) resolved before treating the option period as construction time, and needs to confirm whether construction is even allowed on leased land before the option is exercised.

**Adjacent-parcel assembly.** Buying a second, adjoining parcel, either at the same time as the primary purchase or later, to reach a size or configuration threshold (more buildable area, more separation for a shop and block-yard, or a forest-dwelling acreage threshold under ORS 215.740's non-contiguous-tract combination rule) is a real strategy. The mechanical tool for combining or reshaping adjoining parcels without creating a new lot is the property-line adjustment (see section 3), materially simpler than a partition; combining tracts to reach a forest-dwelling acreage threshold specifically requires the irrevocable deed restriction described in section 4, a permanent tradeoff (giving up future dwelling rights on the combined acreage) that has to be weighed against the value of qualifying for the one dwelling now.

### 6. Red Flags And Real Pitfalls

**Severed rights, beyond the existing chapter's list.** The existing chapter already lists severed mineral, timber, water, and other rights as verification items. The pitfall worth adding: a severed right does not need to be exercised to affect the parcel's value or usability; a severed mineral estate gives the mineral owner a legal right of reasonable surface access to extract, which can conflict with a house, driveway, or septic system sited without accounting for it, even if no extraction has happened in decades and none is currently planned.

**Access that looks fine on a map and is not.** Confirmed above: a platted "paper road" is not a built road, and an easement-by-necessity claim is a difficult, disfavored legal fallback, not a substitute for a recorded easement. A buyer relying on either should treat the parcel as a dealbreaker-band item until a recorded, physically confirmed easement or public-road frontage exists.

**Well interference and declining local aquifers.** Documented real cases exist in Oregon (southern Oregon's Midland-Rogue basin area, and central Oregon's Deschutes basin) of domestic wells going dry as nearby heavy groundwater use expanded; a state critical-groundwater-area designation is the state's tool for responding to this, but it typically arrives after the problem is already visible in a basin, which means a parcel outside a currently designated critical area is not guaranteed to stay that way. Checking the density, depth, and age of neighboring wells through OWRD's well-log database, and asking the current owner and neighbors directly about well performance in late summer, are real, low-cost checks before relying on a well.

**Failed or marginal septic soil.** Already the existing chapter's central dealbreaker item; the pitfall worth flagging here is the disqualification/rollback-tax interaction when a septic evaluation forces a house-site relocation on farm- or forest-deferred land: moving the buildable envelope to find passable soil can push the disturbed area, or the dwelling itself, outside what the deferral program allows without disqualifying the remainder of the parcel, which needs to be checked with the county assessor before finalizing a site plan on deferred land.

**Farm- or forestland special-assessment rollback tax as a surprise closing cost.** Land enrolled in farm-use or forestland special assessment carries a potential additional tax liability: if the land is disqualified (by use change, by underproduction, by recording a subdivision or partition plat, or by a transfer to a use that does not qualify), the county calculates the difference between what was actually paid and what would have been paid without the special assessment, going back a period reported as up to 5 years for farm-use land and up to 5-10 years for forestland (sources disagreed on the forestland figure; this needs a direct statute check, likely ORS 308A.703 and ORS 308A.724, before it appears in the manuscript). This liability does not accrue interest but is due as a lump sum at disqualification, and disqualification is commonly triggered by the very acts a buyer plans to take (partitioning off a home site, building a non-farm dwelling, ending active farm or timber use). The purchase contract needs to state explicitly who bears this cost, because it can run into real money on land that has been under deferral for decades.

**Wildfire insurance availability and cost, independent of the code question.** Even where a jurisdiction has not adopted the voluntary wildfire building-hardening code, insurance underwriting responds to the physical wildfire risk on its own terms. Reported figures (unverified, general market commentary, needing current verification): standard-zone homeowner premiums cited around $2,500 a year against $4,000-$6,000 or more in a high-risk zone, or reliance on the Oregon FAIR Plan (the state's insurer of last resort) at higher cost and reduced coverage when standard carriers decline to write a policy at all; premium increases statewide reportedly exceeding 27 percent since 2020. A parcel that is cheap because of its wildfire exposure can carry a permanent, recurring cost the purchase price does not reflect, and that cost can also depress resale value and buyer-pool size years later, independent of anything the code requires at permit time.

**Financing friction on unusual zoning or raw land.** Conventional mortgage lenders are generally reluctant to lend against raw land, EFU or forest-zoned parcels, or non-resource-designated land, compared to an ordinary residential lot; raw-land loans typically require larger down payments and carry higher rates than a home mortgage, and some zoning categories are essentially unfinanceable through a conventional lender regardless of down payment. This affects both the purchase (cash or seller financing may be the practical path on some zoning) and eventual resale (a smaller pool of cash or seller-financed buyers). This overlaps the personal-finance material elsewhere in the book and should be cross-referenced there rather than duplicated in depth here.

**Environmental legacy issues.** Former orchard land in parts of Oregon (the Hood River and Rogue Valleys in particular) can carry residual soil arsenic and lead from historic lead-arsenate pesticide use, a real and documented issue, not a hypothetical; a soil test is the only way to know before committing a garden, food-storage, or excavation plan to a former-orchard parcel. Oregon DEQ's environmental cleanup tracking (formerly the ECSI database, migrated to the "Your DEQ Online" system as of 2024) lets a buyer check whether a specific parcel or a nearby parcel has a known or suspected hazardous-substance history (old fuel tanks, illegal dumping, prior industrial or agricultural chemical use); appearing in the system does not prove contamination, but it is a free first screen worth running on any parcel with an unclear prior-use history.

**Seasonal bias in the site visit.** Already implied in the existing chapter's "walk the site after rain" instruction; worth stating as an explicit pitfall: a single dry-season visit systematically misses seasonal springs, high water tables, standing water, and difficult winter road conditions, all of which are common causes of a parcel turning out to be far more expensive to build on than it looked at first viewing. A second visit in the wettest part of the local rain year, not just "after a rain," is the real fix.

**Boundary and fence-line uncertainty.** An old, informal fence line that does not match the recorded boundary is a common rural-Oregon condition, not a rare defect, especially on parcels that have been in one family for generations without a recent survey; Oregon recognizes adverse possession claims under specific statutory conditions, so a long-standing fence in the wrong place is a real, if usually low-probability, title risk worth a boundary survey before relying on a visible fence as the property line.

**Internet as a solved problem, if budgeted.** Not a pitfall so much as a corrected assumption: satellite internet (Starlink and comparable low-earth-orbit services) now reaches most of rural Oregon, including forested and mountainous terrain that legacy fixed-wireless and DSL never reached, at reported download speeds in the 50-200+ Mbps range; a remote parcel's internet question is now more often a monthly-cost and line-of-sight-to-sky question than a genuine dealbreaker, which changes the weight the existing chapter's distance/logistics severity sort should give to internet access specifically, separate from physical road distance.

### 8. Proposed Additions To The Chapter's Section Outline

The existing chapter's structure (feasibility filter, climate/design loads, zoning, title/restrictions, utilities/access, septic/water, topography/drainage, distance/logistics, purchase-decision review) stays as the spine. Candidate new sections or subsections this research suggests, placed to extend rather than replace:

- A new early section, "Finding The Parcel," before the feasibility filter: listing sources, off-market tactics, auctions and tax sales, land agents, government/institutional inventory, and timber/farm-deferral parcels as acquisition targets.
- Within or after the existing zoning section, a deeper "Dwelling-Approval Paths On Resource Land" subsection carrying the specific EFU farm-dwelling, EFU nonfarm-dwelling, lot-of-record, and forest-dwelling (large-tract and template) numeric tests, since the existing chapter currently only ranks these zones qualitatively.
- A new "The Deal" section covering pricing approach, contingency structuring, seller financing/land contracts, and partitions/lot-line adjustments, distinct from the existing title/restrictions section, which covers what is already recorded rather than how the purchase itself is structured.
- A new "Creative Acquisition" section or subsection: co-purchase structuring, family land, lease-to-own, adjacent-parcel assembly.
- A short "Measure 49 And Measure 56" clarification, likely inside the title/restrictions section, distinguishing the two by mechanism.
- A short "Farm And Forest Deferral: The Disqualification Cost" subsection, likely inside the title/restrictions or the deal section, since this is a real, frequently-missed closing-cost item specific to buying deferred-status land.
- Expansion of the existing topography/drainage section to explicitly add solar exposure/aspect and wildfire/landslide/flood hazard-mapping tools (DOGAMI SLIDO, FEMA, DEQ environmental-cleanup lookup) as named, separate screening steps rather than folding them into general topography language.
- A short "Buildable Area For The Whole Project" subsection addressing house, septic (primary and reserve), well, driveway/turnaround, shop, and block-yard together on one sketch, cross-referenced to Developing The Site for what happens on that area after purchase.
