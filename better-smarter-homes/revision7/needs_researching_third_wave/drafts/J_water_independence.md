## Part 1: Gathered Understanding

### 1. Roof Catchment, Conveyance, And Screening

**Runoff coefficient by roof material.** Reported ranges across multiple sources: smooth pre-painted or Galvalume standing-seam metal, 0.90-0.95. Asphalt composition shingle, 0.70-0.80 (sheds petroleum residue and granule grit; discouraged for potable collection by several sources, including the American Rainwater Catchment Systems Association). Concrete tile or a bare concrete roof deck, coefficient similar to metal for volume but with a materially different water chemistry (below). Ceramic tile reported as low in leachable pollutants in comparative studies. Membrane roofing (TPO, EPDM) varies by product; some EPDM formulations use additives not rated for potable contact, so a membrane roof intended as catchment needs a potable-rated product confirmed with the manufacturer, not assumed. Green roofs are discouraged for catchment: they filter volume and add organic matter, tannin color, and nutrient load to the runoff. Using the wrong coefficient is reported to over- or under-size a system 15-30 percent.

**A note specific to this house.** A flat or low-slope concrete roof (an option this house's structural chapter allows) is itself a catchment surface. Multiple water-quality studies report bare or concrete-tile roof runoff at a distinctly higher pH than metal-roof runoff (means around 7.2-7.6 versus rainwater's native 4.8-5.9), from lime leaching as mildly acidic rain contacts the alkaline concrete surface. That is the opposite chemistry problem from the low-pH corrosion issue already flagged for metal-roof rainwater and for some well water (draft E item E5): a bare concrete roof's first years of runoff may run alkaline rather than acidic, which changes which corrosion-control step is needed, and a coating or sealer on the concrete roof surface changes the leaching behavior again. This interaction is unverified in specifics and roof-age-dependent (leaching drops as the surface carbonates over years); it needs its own research pass rather than an assumption borrowed from the metal-roof literature.

**Conveyance sizing.** Gutter and downspout sizing keyed to short-duration, high-return-period rainfall intensity, not the annual average. One source states the 5-minute, 100-year rainfall intensity for Portland is roughly 2.5 inches per hour, and recommends erring toward oversized gutters and downspouts in high-intensity regions; the source figure is unverified against a primary NOAA Atlas 14 pull for the actual parcel. Reported downspout capacity rules of thumb: a 2x3-inch downspout serves roughly 600 square feet of roof at 1 inch/hour; a 3x4-inch downspout serves roughly 1,200 square feet; spacing guidance of one downspout per 20-30 linear feet of gutter or 600-800 square feet of roof area. Oregon's coast and west-slope rainfall (60-100+ inches per year per the land-and-feasibility chapter) argues for conveyance sized above the IPC 1-inch/hour baseline, not at it.

**Screening.** Leaf and debris exclusion sits ahead of the first-flush diverter: gutter guards or leaf screens at the gutter, then a dedicated inlet screen or vortex/centrifugal filter (a device that spins debris out of the flow before it enters the tank, used at the Bullitt Center system described below) at the tank inlet. A basket or mesh strainer at the tank inlet is the minimum; a self-cleaning vortex filter is a step up that also reduces the sediment load the first-flush and downstream cartridge stages have to handle.

### 2. First-Flush Diversion

**Sizing.** Reported rules of thumb diverge by source and by end use: a low-pollution, non-potable rule of roughly 1.25 gallons per 1,000 square feet of roof; a higher-pollution or potable-intent rule in the range of 10-20 gallons per 1,000 square feet; a commonly cited general rule of 1-2 gallons diverted per 100 square feet of roof (10-20 gallons per 1,000 square feet). The spread is wide enough that the number used should be tied to intended end use (irrigation-only versus potable), roof condition (tree cover, bird activity, industrial exposure), and the storm gap (a roof that has sat dry for weeks accumulates more to flush than one rained on two days earlier). None of these figures is a code minimum; they are practitioner rules of thumb from rainwater-harvesting trade sources, unverified against any Oregon-specific standard.

**Mechanism types.** A standpipe or "roof-washer" design: the diverted volume fills a vertical pipe below the downspout tee, and once full, subsequent flow floats a ball onto a seat (or simply overflows the standpipe) and routes to the tank; the standpipe drains slowly through a small orifice or a leaking valve between storms so it resets. A dedicated first-flush valve product (a factory-built diverter with a float ball) is the low-maintenance version of the same idea. Pipe-volume figures reported for standpipe sizing: roughly 0.38-0.4 gallons per foot of 3-inch pipe, roughly 0.6-0.7 gallons per foot of 4-inch pipe (both approximate, since actual pipe ID varies by schedule).

**Maintenance failure mode.** A first-flush diverter with a slow-drain orifice that clogs with sediment stops resetting between storms and starts passing the "dirty" first flush straight into the tank; this is a recurring problem-and-solution item in DIY rainwater forums and argues for a diverter that is easy to open and flush by hand each season.

### 3. Cistern Sizing For Dry-Season Autonomy

**Two sizing logics.** Supply-based sizing asks how much water the roof and rainfall pattern can deliver; demand-based sizing asks how much storage covers the household's use through the longest expected dry stretch. A workable design runs both and takes the constraint that binds. The basic demand-based formula reported across multiple sources: daily use in gallons times the length of the drought or dry season in days equals the minimum cistern size, with a safety margin added.

**Oregon's dry-season shape.** Western Oregon's rainfall is heavily winter-loaded. Reported precipitation figures for Salem (unverified pull from a secondary weather-data aggregator, not a direct NOAA Climate Normals query): January roughly 6.1 inches, December roughly 7.2 inches, July roughly 0.4 inch. That means a cistern sized on the annual total is the wrong number; the sizing question is how much stored volume carries the household from the last significant spring rain through the first significant fall rain, roughly a four-to-five-month dry stretch in the Willamette Valley (longer east of the Cascades, shorter and less pronounced on the immediate coast). A full NOAA Climate Normals pull (monthly, 1991-2020 or 2006-2020 normals) for the specific parcel's nearest station is the correct basis for a real design; the figures above are planning-order-of-magnitude only.

**The gallons-per-inch-per-square-foot conversion.** One inch of rain on one square foot of catchment yields 0.623 gallons (a geometry fact: 1 ft² x 1/12 ft depth x 7.48 gal/ft³ = 0.623 gal), before any runoff-coefficient or first-flush loss is subtracted. A 2,000-square-foot roof catching 40 inches of annual rain has a theoretical yield near 49,800 gallons before losses; applying a 0.85 combined coefficient (material loss plus first-flush loss plus conveyance loss) brings that to roughly 42,000 gallons theoretically available, which is a supply ceiling, not a stored-and-usable figure, since most of it falls in the wet season and a realistic cistern cannot hold months of winter surplus.

**Mandated sizing as an external benchmark.** Jurisdictions where rainwater is the primary or sole domestic source publish sizing mandates that provide a comparison benchmark, even though the mandates themselves do not apply in Oregon. The U.S. Virgin Islands building code (unverified secondary citation, not the primary code text) reportedly requires cistern capacity of roughly 10 gallons per square foot of roof for a one-story dwelling and roughly 15 gallons per square foot for two stories, where no other potable connection exists; an estimated 90 percent of USVI homes run on active cisterns. Bermuda has required a cistern on every new dwelling since a 1949 law, sized by roof area, and mandates the roof surface itself (traditionally limewashed stepped limestone) be constructed to shed clean water. These are full-dependency mandates for climates with a different rainfall pattern than Oregon's, so the ratios do not transfer directly, but the design logic (size to roof area and to the length of dependency, not to an arbitrary round number) transfers.

**A worked concept, unverified numbers.** One source frames the balance directly: if summer demand across four months is estimated at some volume and the roof is expected to deliver a much smaller volume of usable rain across that same window, the cistern needs to already hold the difference at the start of the dry season, which is a materially different design question than "how big a tank."

### 4. Storage Construction: Ferrocement

**What it is.** A thin shell of cement mortar (sand, cement, water, no coarse aggregate) trowelled over a reinforcing armature of closely spaced small-diameter rod or heavy wire (roughly 4 mm and up, reported spacing on the order of 100-200 mm, i.e., 4-8 inches) with one or more layers of fine woven or welded mesh (commonly called chicken mesh, wire diameter roughly 0.5-1 mm) wrapped over the rod grid. It is a distinct construction technique from a poured, formed concrete wall, and is the dominant technique in decades of rural water-tank programs across Sri Lanka, Thailand, Kenya, Brazil, Mexico, India, and other regions, including formal NGO and UN manuals (CuveWaters, UNHCR, PACE).

**Method, as reported across several manuals.** Build or weld the rod cage to the tank shape (commonly cylindrical, which is structurally efficient for hoop stress from water pressure). Wrap mesh layers over the cage, tied off. Plaster the mortar from one side while a second person holds a float or backing board on the opposite side, so the mortar is compacted into the mesh rather than pushed through it; this is typically a two-person, hand-trowelled operation, though shotcrete/mortar-spray equipment is used on larger commercial builds. Apply in multiple thin coats rather than one thick one, to control shrinkage cracking. Cure damp (wet burlap, plastic sheeting, or repeated wetting) for a minimum of two weeks, longer where practical; inadequate curing is repeatedly cited as the top cause of shrinkage cracking and eventual leaks.

**Where it fits this project.** Ferrocement is a wall-thinness and skilled-hand-trowel technique, distinct from the project's structural concrete work; it would be a deliberately different skill and material system layered onto the same jobsite, most relevant if a freestanding or partially buried tank shape (cylindrical, domed) is wanted rather than a tank integrated into the foundation footprint. It shares a knowledge base with the plaster/render and mortar work already researched for masonry craft; the two research threads should cross-reference rather than duplicate.

### 5. Storage Construction: Poured Concrete, Cast-In-Mass, And Below-Grade

**Owner-built poured tanks are documented, with real failure modes.** A first-person account from an ecovillage build (Dancing Rabbit, Missouri; a single project's report, not a general standard) describes an 8-by-8-by-12-foot underground reinforced-concrete cistern built into a cottage foundation, two chambers with a cleaning/isolation partition. Reported specifics: #6 rebar on roughly a 1-foot grid; a 4-inch floor slab with reinforcing mesh; a cement-based waterproofing product with a latex additive (brand-named "Damtite" in the source) troweled onto the interior. Reported problems: 1/2-inch plywood forms were not stiff enough and the walls bulged, producing wavy, out-of-tolerance walls; actual concrete volume ran roughly 50 percent over the estimate because of that bulging and because the excavation's bottom came in smaller than its top; a keyway between the floor pour and the wall pour was not formed in the first pour and had to be worked around; a crossover pipe between the two chambers was omitted during construction and had to be drilled in afterward. The account's own recommendation for a repeat build: use at minimum 3/4-inch plywood or conventional 16-inch-on-center stud-framed forms, preform the keyway, and install any crossover or through-wall piping before the pour, not after.

**Slipform is a second historical owner-builder method.** Round tanks built by slipform (a short wooden form section raised in lifts as concrete is placed, a technique documented in early-20th-century farm-homestead construction manuals) produce a continuous cylindrical wall roughly 10 inches thick in the reported historical examples, with a separately cast lid.

**Shotcrete is a third option, with a caveat.** Shotcrete (sprayed concrete) over an excavated hole and rebar cage is reported as workable but inherently porous, requiring a separate impervious interior lining (a cementitious parge coat or a swimming-pool-style liner) rather than relying on the shotcrete shell alone for watertightness.

**Precast is a fourth option, purchased rather than built.** Precast concrete cisterns and septic-adjacent precast products (from firms serving the onsite-wastewater trade) are delivered and set by crane; this trades field-labor risk for delivery/access constraints and a fixed size menu; site access limits already discussed in the land-and-feasibility chapter's staging section bear on the choice directly.

**Below-grade or foundation-integrated storage, as a design category.** A tank built into or under the structure (a basement room used purely as a cistern, or a tank cast as part of the foundation footprint) shares excavation, formwork, and concrete-placement work already happening for the structural foundation, and gains passive temperature stability and freeze protection from burial depth. It also means the tank's structural design, its access/inspection openings, its overflow path, and its waterproofing all have to coordinate with the structural foundation design and the foundation waterproofing and drainage layers already specified in the soil and foundation chapters, rather than standing as an independent structure. A tank of any real size (the code-research already on file flags roughly 1,000 gallons as a rough threshold past which a stamped design and environmental-health sign-off should be expected) is a structural and code item, not just a plumbing item, and belongs in the structural design process from the start rather than as a late addition.

**Historic and vernacular precedent, as design ideas rather than a code source.** The Persian and wider Middle Eastern ab anbar is a roofed, below-grade cistern lined historically with a waterproof lime-and-ash mortar (sarooj), fed by a qanat or rainwater, sized and shaped partly to resist earth pressure and earthquake, and topped in some regional variants with a windcatcher tower that draws air through the headspace for evaporative cooling and to discourage stagnation. Roman and Byzantine large cisterns (including cut-stone and vaulted-brick construction) demonstrate multi-column vaulted covered storage at a scale beyond residential need, but the covered, dark, temperature-stable storage principle is the same one this project would apply at a residential scale. These are noted as creative-idea precedent, not as sources of any code value.

### 6. Storage Materials: Liners, Coatings, Tank Classes, Seismic, Freeze, And Pest Control

**NSF/ANSI 61 potable-contact materials.** Beyond the general certification requirement already in the water-supply chapter, specific named product classes reported in the coatings trade: crystalline waterproofing (a coating that grows crystals into the concrete's capillary pores, one branded example Xypex HD-150, applied to bare concrete), cementitious/acrylic coatings (one branded example Masterseal, another Vandex Super), and 100-percent-solids epoxy linings rated for potable immersion service (branded examples include Tnemec Series 22/FC22 Epoxoline and Sherwin-Williams Sherplate 600). All are reported as NSF/ANSI 61 listed for potable tank service; product-specific technical data sheets and current listing status need direct verification at design time, since certifications and formulations change.

**Tank class costs, reported ranges, 2026 dollars, unverified and wide.** Polyethylene tanks: roughly $800-$6,000. Steel or corrugated steel (liner-protected): roughly $2,000-$12,000. Concrete (precast or formed): roughly $1,100-$6,800 for the tank itself. Fiberglass: roughly $5,000-$9,000, reported as the highest per-gallon cost class. A full installed system (aboveground): roughly $2,000-$12,000; underground: roughly $6,500-$24,000. A separate source gives a blended installed-cost rule of thumb of $2-$6 per gallon of capacity, rising for concrete/custom-liner tanks and advanced filtration. All figures are trade-source estimates, not verified quotes, and should be treated as planning-order-of-magnitude only, consistent with how the manuscript already treats other purchased-system cost ranges.

**Seismic design for a large tank.** Prestressed and reinforced concrete water tank design standards (AWWA D110 for wire-and-strand-wound prestressed tanks, AWWA D115 for tendon-prestressed tanks) reference ASCE 7 seismic provisions and ACI 350.3 (seismic design of liquid-containing concrete structures) for the sloshing and hydrodynamic-pressure loads a large water mass imposes during an earthquake, on top of ordinary structural loads. This is squarely relevant in Oregon's Cascadia-subduction seismic design categories (already established as D0-D2 across most of western Oregon in the land-and-feasibility chapter) for any tank of real size, and is a distinct engineering question from the tank's watertightness.

**Freeze protection.** Full burial below the local frost depth is reported as the most reliable cold-climate answer, since buried mass stays above freezing once below frost depth; a tank set on an insulated pad or a gravel base (rather than directly on native soil) is recommended to slow heat loss into the ground beneath the tank, which otherwise can thaw unevenly and destabilize the tank's bearing. Partially buried or aboveground tanks in a cold climate need supply and overflow piping buried below frost depth at minimum, with additional wind-break or insulation wrap protection for the exposed tank shell itself.

**Mosquito and algae control.** Reported as one integrated design problem rather than two: an opaque (light-excluding) tank shell or interior surface starves algae of the light it needs, and a fully sealed lid with screened vents and a screened or trapped overflow denies mosquitoes egress to lay eggs; both failure modes point to the same fix (a light-tight, insect-tight enclosure), which argues for treating tank sealing as a single design requirement covering both problems rather than as separate biological and chemical controls.

**Fire-suppression dual use.** NFPA 1142 (Standard on Water Supplies for Suburban and Rural Fire Fighting) sets minimum stored-water-volume and delivery-rate guidance for rural properties outside hydrant-served areas, and is a live design question for any rural Oregon parcel with wildfire exposure (already flagged as a code item in the land-and-feasibility chapter's hazard-zone section). A residential fire-suppression cistern's typical components (a manhole, a fire-department suction connection, a vent, and a fill path) are described for underground concrete cisterns built specifically for this purpose, and dry-hydrant guidance in the same standard limits static lift to roughly 10 feet. NFPA 13 (the sprinkler standard) is reported to recognize captured rainwater as an acceptable source on the same footing as a pond or lake. Sizing a domestic-supply cistern to also cover a fire-reserve volume (a dedicated lower section of tank volume that a float or standpipe keeps off-limits to the domestic pump) is a real, if unverified in Oregon-specific application, way to get two uses from one structure.

### 7. Treatment Trains: Rainwater To Potable

**A full worked precedent.** The Bullitt Center (Seattle, a commercial building, not residential, but the fullest documented modern rainwater-to-potable build) collects roof water through a parapet-roof gutter system into a 56,000-gallon poured concrete cistern in the basement, passing the incoming flow through a vortex filter first to strip large particulate before storage. From storage, the treatment sequence reported is a 5-micron cartridge filter, then two 0.5-micron carbon filters in series, then ultraviolet disinfection, then a small chlorine dose added at the end for residual protection in the distribution piping; treated water is held in three 500-gallon potable-storage tanks downstream of treatment, separate from the raw-water cistern. The building reports operating at "net positive water" (producing more usable water on-site than it draws from any outside source) for over a decade, under the Living Building Challenge's Net Positive Water requirement, alongside a constructed wetland for its own wastewater. This is a scale and program well beyond a single house, but the train order (coarse filter at the tank inlet, storage, then step-down cartridge filtration, carbon, UV, and a small terminal chlorine residual) is a directly applicable model, distinct in a few details from the well-water train already fully specified in the water-supply chapter: rainwater generally arrives with far less dissolved iron/manganese/hardness than groundwater, so the train skips the oxidation and softening stages that dominate the well-water train, and leans more heavily on particulate filtration and disinfection.

**UV specifics for a rainwater train.** Multiple sources converge on the same 40 mJ/cm² NSF/ANSI 55 Class A dose already established for the well-water train, and add a rainwater-specific caveat: the water reaching the UV stage needs turbidity below roughly 5 NTU for the UV dose to actually penetrate and disinfect, which is the functional reason the cartridge and carbon stages sit ahead of UV in every reported design, not just a generic filtration-before-disinfection rule.

**A note on blending two source chemistries.** Where this house runs a well and a rainwater system side by side (even if never physically cross-connected), the two raw waters likely arrive with different pH, hardness, and mineral content, meaning the corrosion-control and scaling answer for one source is not automatically correct for the other. A single whole-house point-of-entry correction (for example, a calcite neutralizer sized to the well water's specific low pH) may over- or under-correct rainwater fed to different fixtures or a different distribution zone, which argues for either a shared blending/equalization step before any single point-of-entry treatment, or two independently corrected distribution zones; this interaction is not addressed by the well-only treatment-train material already in the water-supply chapter and is unverified as to which approach is standard practice.

### 8. Treatment Trains: Well-Water Depth Beyond The Existing Chapter

**Radon in water.** Two point-of-entry (whole-house) treatment classes are reported: packed-tower or spray aeration, which strips dissolved radon gas into an exhausted airstream at a reported 95-99 percent removal, and granular activated carbon (GAC) adsorption, cheaper to install but with a reported caution that radioactivity accumulates on the filter media over time, creating a handling and disposal consideration for the spent media. This is a deeper build-out of the "radon-in-water in granitic terrain" tier-2 test item already named in the water-supply chapter's testing section, which does not currently specify a treatment response.

**Iron and sulfur bacteria.** Distinguished from dissolved iron/manganese chemistry (already covered in the water-supply chapter's oxidation and filtration stages): iron and sulfur bacteria are living organisms that build a slime layer using dissolved iron or sulfur compounds as an energy source, the sulfur-reducing strains specifically responsible for "rotten egg" hydrogen sulfide odor (iron bacteria alone are reported not to cause that odor). Shock chlorination is the first-line response, but sources note the bacterial slime layer itself shields the organisms from standard chlorine concentrations, with one source recommending a higher shock concentration specifically for iron/sulfur bacteria (roughly 500 ppm) than the standard coliform shock protocol (roughly 200 ppm, matching the 50-250 mg/L range already specified in the water-supply chapter for a different purpose, storage-tank disinfection); mechanical scrubbing of the casing and pump column ahead of chlorination is reported as often necessary for a persistent case, which is beyond an owner-builder's routine scope and points toward a well-service contractor call.

**Off-grid and no-power backup treatment.** Two low-tech methods recur across international development and homestead-preparedness sources as a backup layer behind an electric UV or pump-fed cartridge train, relevant to this project's stated goal of continued function during a grid outage: the biosand filter (a concrete or plastic container filled with a specific sand/gravel media stack, operated by intermittent daily pours rather than continuous flow, relying on a biological layer, the schmutzdecke, that forms on the sand surface; reported removal rates in the 90-99 percent range for bacteria and above 99 percent for protozoa such as Giardia and Cryptosporidium, requiring no electricity or replacement cartridges, championed and standardized internationally by CAWST); and solar water disinfection (SODIS: clear PET bottles filled with low-turbidity water and laid in full sun, reported as effective after roughly 6 hours of strong sun or up to 2 days under heavy cloud, WHO-recognized for household and emergency use but explicitly limited to small volumes and to biological contamination, not chemical). Neither replaces the primary treatment train; both are candidates as a documented manual fallback method for the resilience case where the primary electric-powered train is unavailable.

### 9. Greywater Reuse: Oregon's Tier Structure And Real Systems

**The tier structure, with figures beyond what the DWV chapter currently states.** Oregon DEQ's graywater program runs three tiers. Tier 1 (general permit 2401): flow under 300 gallons per day, Type 1 graywater only (untreated or physically screened to remove solids/fats/oils/grease), subsurface irrigation or compost use only, application-only permitting with no design plan required, the simplest tier. Tier 2 (general permit 2402): flow under 1,200 gallons per day, Type 1 or Type 2 graywater (Type 2 adds secondary treatment and oxidation), a more involved application requiring a system design plan and an operations/maintenance manual. Tier 3: an individual permit for any system exceeding 1,200 gallons per day or falling outside the general-permit categories, including Type 3 graywater (secondary treatment, oxidation, and disinfection, the tier required for any indoor reuse such as toilet flushing). The laundry-to-landscape exemption (clothes-washer effluent only, no pump, no storage, no piping run under a structure) needs no DEQ permit at all, only ordinary plumbing-permit awareness; this is the lowest-friction entry point for graywater reuse on this project and is already flagged that way in the water-supply chapter's cross-reference, but the full tier ladder above it is new detail.

**Real system types beyond the code categories.** Art Ludwig's laundry-to-landscape design (a widely replicated, well-documented DIY system: the washer's own internal pump routes discharge through 1-inch tubing to multiple shallow mulch basins around plantings, no separate pump or filtration needed because the washer already pressurizes its own discharge) is the most common owner-built entry point into greywater reuse in North America. His branched-drain design (a gravity-fed network of pipe fittings, engineered to split flow evenly between multiple mulch-basin outlets without moving parts, filtration, or a surge tank) serves shower and sink sources on a sloped lot where the planting area sits downhill of the fixture, and is a documented, mature, non-proprietary design class with its own failure modes (a basin that receives too much flow relative to its soil infiltration rate goes anaerobic and surfaces; root intrusion into distribution pipe; grease or lint clogging an undersized filter). A constructed wetland or reed-bed treatment step (a shallow, lined, gravel-and-plant bed that graywater or septic effluent flows through before final dispersal, used at a commercial scale at the Bullitt Center precedent above) is a higher-tier, higher-maintenance option, named here even though it is a bigger undertaking than a single-family Tier 1 or Tier 2 system typically needs.

**Composting and low-water toilet brand landscape and septic-load effect.** Self-contained units (the whole composting chamber under the seat) versus remote/central units (the seat is a low-flush or dry fixture, with the composting chamber in a basement or separate room below, serving one or more fixtures) are the two basic architectures. Reported brand examples: Sun-Mar, Envirolet, and Biolet as self-contained brands in a roughly $1,500-$3,000 class (unverified, 2026 planning figure); Separett as a Swedish urine-diverting dry-toilet brand, marketed on a cleaner, more residential-fixture-like appearance; Clivus Multrum as the most established remote/central composting system, designed for decades of service and large volumes, used in commercial and institutional green buildings as well as houses. All routes still require an approved leachate or graywater disposal path under Oregon rules (already correctly stated in the DWV chapter), because a composting toilet removes the blackwater stream, not the wastewater system as a whole. Septic-load reduction from removing toilet flow entirely is a large fraction of total household wastewater by design-flow convention (a water closet is typically the single largest fixture-unit and design-flow contributor in residential sizing tables), which is the mechanism, beyond the DWV chapter's existing composting-toilet code citations, by which a composting or urine-diverting toilet choice can shrink the drainfield size a marginal septic site needs.

### 10. Oregon's Water-Rights Tangle

**Prior appropriation, plainly.** Oregon groundwater and surface water are both allocated on a first-in-time, first-in-right basis administered by the Oregon Water Resources Department (OWRD): an earlier-dated water right is satisfied ahead of a later one when supply runs short, and most new water uses require a permitted water right unless the use falls into a statutory exemption.

**The domestic exemption, with the exact statute figures.** ORS 537.545 lists exempt groundwater uses, several with their own volume caps rather than one blanket number: single or group domestic purposes up to 15,000 gallons a day; watering a noncommercial lawn or garden up to one-half acre with no stated gallon cap; a single industrial or commercial purpose up to 5,000 gallons a day; stockwatering, uncapped in the general case but capped at 12,000 gallons a day specifically for a new confined animal feeding operation; down-hole heat exchange, with no volume limit stated in the exemption itself (see the open-loop geothermal item below for why this specific category needs careful reading rather than assumption); and a handful of narrower categories (school-ground irrigation inside a critical groundwater area, reuse of previously appropriated industrial water). Recording an exempt use costs a $300 fee, and a well constructor is required to file a location map with OWRD within 30 days of completing a well.

**A 2025-2026 statute change, unverified in final detail.** House Bill 3372 (2025 session), reported effective January 1, 2026, adds a new exemption for irrigating a commercial garden (crops grown for sale, not just personal use) up to one-half acre and up to 3,000 gallons a day, which previously required a full water-right permit; combined with other exempt commercial/industrial use, the stacked cap is reported at 5,000 gallons a day total (leaving 2,000 gallons a day of headroom for other exempt commercial use once the garden exemption is used at its 3,000-gallon ceiling). Two sources gave inconsistent detail on how the bill treats cannabis/hemp (one describing a cannabis exclusion, another describing a specific allowance for licensed industrial hemp growers under ORS 571.281, which is a legally distinct category from marijuana licensing); this detail needs to be read directly from the enrolled bill text, not inferred from secondary summaries. A carve-out reportedly delays the new commercial-garden exemption until 2028 specifically in the Lower Umatilla Basin Groundwater Management Area, citing nitrate contamination in that basin's groundwater. None of this changes the 15,000-gallon domestic exemption that would cover this house's ordinary household use, but it is directly relevant if any homestead-scale market garden, nursery, or similar income use is part of the property plan.

**Critical and restricted groundwater areas.** Oregon has designated critical groundwater areas where new groundwater rights (and in some cases even exempt uses) are limited or barred by administrative rule, reported examples including several areas inside the Umatilla Basin, the Cow Valley area in the Malheur Basin, the Dalles area in the Hood Basin, and the Cooper-Bull Mountain area in the Willamette Basin; the Harney Basin in southeast Oregon was reported newly designated a critical groundwater area in December 2025; the Deschutes Basin runs under a mitigation program (new groundwater pumping must be offset, rather than a flat prohibition) rather than a critical-area designation. Any parcel under serious consideration in a basin with active groundwater controversy (the four named above, or any basin OWRD flags as over-allocated) needs this checked directly with OWRD before relying on an exempt domestic well, since a designated area can narrow or remove even the exempt-use path depending on the specific administrative rule for that area; this is squarely a site-feasibility item as much as a water-independence item.

**Rainwater collection legality, confirmed and compared.** Already correctly stated in the water-supply chapter: rooftop rainwater collected and used on-site is exempt from OWRD water-right permitting under ORS 537.141, while collection that diverts or impounds a natural surface flow (a stream, a swale carrying someone else's water) is not exempt and needs a water right. Washington State's parallel rule (unverified secondary summary) is reported as similar in spirit but narrower in one detail: Washington reportedly limits the on-site-use exemption to catchment from an existing structure that has some other primary purpose, which would matter if a purpose-built, non-habitable catchment structure (a large separate collection roof with no other function) were ever considered; whether Oregon's ORS 537.141 carries or lacks that same structure-purpose limitation is unverified and needs a direct read of the statute text rather than an assumption of parity with Washington.

**Open-loop geothermal water rights, flagged as attorney/permit-time grade.** A ground-source heat pump using an open loop (water is drawn from a well, passed through a heat exchanger, and either reinjected into a second well or discharged) is reported to sit under two separate regulatory tracks in Oregon: an OWRD water-right appropriation question (drawing groundwater for the heat-exchange use), and, for the reinjection side, a federal Underground Injection Control (UIC) question under the EPA's Class V well category, specifically the "Heat Pump/Air Conditioning Return Flow Well" subclass, which in Oregon may be administered by DEQ (unverified which state agency holds delegated authority for this specific UIC subclass; this needs direct confirmation, not assumption). A closed-loop system (a sealed pipe loop with no water withdrawal or injection) is reported to avoid both questions and is treated as materially simpler for permitting, which argues for closed-loop as the default unless open-loop's higher heat-transfer efficiency justifies the added permitting complexity for a specific site. Separately, ORS 537.545(1)(e) exempts "down-hole heat exchange" from water-right permitting with no stated volume cap, but a down-hole heat exchanger is a specific, narrower technology (a closed heat-exchange element lowered into an existing well or borehole, with no groundwater actually withdrawn) and it is unverified whether that exemption's language would be read by OWRD to cover a true open-loop system that does withdraw and reinject groundwater; treating the two as interchangeable without a direct answer from OWRD would be a guess, not a citation, and should be flagged that way to whoever designs the heating system.

**Combining a well and a rainwater system on one property.** Not itself a water-rights question (each source is independently exempt or permitted on its own terms) but a cross-connection and backflow-prevention question, already partly covered in the water-supply chapter (air gap as the gold standard at any rainwater-to-potable makeup connection, reduced-pressure assembly as the acceptable alternative). Australia's mature dual-source residential plumbing practice, where a rainwater tank commonly supplements or substitutes for municipal mains inside the same house, offers a useful design-pattern comparison even though its code (AS/NZS 3500.1) is a different jurisdiction: Australian guidance ties the required backflow device to a hazard rating that depends on tank placement, an aboveground tank rated low-hazard and satisfied by a simple dual-check valve, a buried or in-ground tank rated high-hazard and requiring a testable reduced-pressure device, and purpose-built rain-to-mains changeover valves (branded examples reported: WISY's VARIO-class controllers, and various "Hydro Switch" products) that use a float switch in the tank to automatically call for mains (or, in this house's case, well) makeup water only when the rainwater level drops below a set point, keeping the two sources from ever pressurizing the same line simultaneously. Whether Oregon's OPSC applies an equivalent hazard-tiered logic (buried tank treated as higher-hazard than aboveground) is unverified; confirming it directly matters, since the answer changes which backflow device class this house's design actually needs at the well/rainwater interface.

### 11. Low-Yield Well Buffering And Well Rehabilitation

**The buffering logic, extended beyond the water-supply chapter's brief mention.** The water-supply chapter already establishes that a well below roughly 5 GPM sustained yield calls for a storage-and-booster build rather than direct pressurization. The buffering math itself (how large a cistern a given shortfall actually needs) is not yet stated anywhere in the manuscript: the design question is not the well's instantaneous GPM but its gallons-per-day yield against the household's gallons-per-day peak demand, with the cistern sized to absorb the gap during the hours of heaviest use and refill during the hours of lightest use, which is a different and smaller number than sizing for a multi-day outage.

**Well rehabilitation, as an alternative or supplement to buffering.** Hydrofracturing (injecting water under high pressure down the well to open and extend existing bedrock fractures, applicable to bedrock wells, not a sand/gravel aquifer well) is reported to increase yield by a wide and site-dependent margin, with one source citing an average yield increase around 200 percent across a study sample, unverified as to how that figure was derived or how representative it is of Oregon geology specifically. Airlift development (surging air and water through the well to remove fine sediment and open the surrounding formation) and mechanical or chemical redevelopment (acid or surfactant treatment to dissolve scale and disperse biological or mineral fouling, paired with physical scrubbing of the screen and casing) are reported as lower-intervention options tried before hydrofracturing or a deeper/replacement well. None of these is a guaranteed fix; a driller's professional judgment on the specific formation is the deciding input, not a general rule.

**Automatic source-switching as the buffering system's control layer.** The same float-switch-and-solenoid-valve logic described above for well/rainwater blending (the WISY-class controller) applies equally to a low-yield-well-plus-storage-tank design without a second source at all: the controller can hold the storage tank topped up from the slow well during low-demand hours and prevent the booster pump from ever calling on the well faster than its sustained yield, which is the mechanical version of the "match pump GPM at or below sustained yield" instruction already in the water-supply chapter, applied at the control-system level rather than only at the pump-selection level.

### 12. Alternative And Supplementary Sources

**Spring development.** Where a parcel has a natural spring or a hillside seep, a spring box (a small watertight collection structure, commonly concrete, set into the hillside or over the seepage point) is a documented, low-cost alternative or supplement to a drilled well, widely covered in agricultural-extension and homestead literature. Reported method: intercept the seep with a buried cutoff wall and a gravel collection bed, route the collected water into a watertight box with a sealed, insect- and animal-proof lid, and provide both an overflow pipe and a service outlet, with the collection area's surface graded or capped (commonly with an impermeable layer such as clay or plastic sheeting) so surface runoff cannot enter and contaminate the captured groundwater. A spring box on a hillside, once developed, can often deliver by gravity alone with no pump, which is a meaningful resilience property matching this project's stated goal of function during a power outage; how OWRD and Oregon's construction and water-quality rules treat a spring source (versus a drilled well) for permitting, water rights, and potability testing is not yet researched here and is a real gap.

**Snowmelt roof collection.** At a higher-elevation Oregon site (the land-and-feasibility chapter's snow-load table already reaches well above sea-level valley conditions), a roof sheds meltwater across a longer season than it sheds rain, and the same catchment and first-flush hardware used for rain applies to meltwater with the added complication of freeze/thaw cycling in the gutters and first-flush components themselves; this is a real seasonal-yield extension, named here as a design idea and unverified as to how much it actually adds to annual yield at any specific elevation.
