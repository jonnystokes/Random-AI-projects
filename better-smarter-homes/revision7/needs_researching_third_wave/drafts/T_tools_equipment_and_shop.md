## 1. Earthwork and Excavation Equipment

- **Mini excavator.** Rental found in the range of $150-$250/day for a 1-2 ton class machine and $400-$500/day for a 6-8 ton class machine (2026, multiple rental-comparison sources). Weekly rates commonly run to roughly $500-$1,700, monthly $1,200-$4,500; renting by the week is reported to save roughly 60 percent versus paying the daily rate for seven days, because most yards charge the equivalent of 3-4 daily rates for a full week. Delivery and pickup typically bill $75-$200 each way. Budget an extra 20-30 percent above the base rate for fuel, delivery, insurance, and attachments.
- **Skid steer versus mini excavator.** Skid steers move and grade material (soil, gravel, snow) and load trucks; mini excavators dig below grade (trenches, footings, pools) and work in tight spaces using 360-degree rotation without repositioning. Mini excavators run on tracks, giving lower ground pressure and better flotation over mud and soft soil than a wheeled skid steer. One forum consensus for an owner who can only own one machine: buy the skid steer (it gets far more use across a build) and rent the excavator for the specific weeks that need it.
- **Mini skid steer / compact utility loader (Toro Dingo class).** Narrow (often under 36 inches), tracked or wheeled, useful where a full-size skid steer cannot fit or would compact ground too heavily; one model runs a ground pressure as low as 3.4 psi. Rental found around $249-$329 for an 8-hour day, or roughly $295/day, $1,180/week, $2,250/month (2026, vendor listings). Useful for a residential lot where trench work, material moving, and auger work happen in confined side yards.
- **Skid-steer / compact-loader attachments.** Skid steers accept over 40 attachment classes. Purchase costs found (2026): buckets roughly $750-$1,000, pallet forks roughly $500-$1,000, augers roughly $2,000-$2,500, trencher attachments roughly $4,000-$6,000. A trencher attachment is reported to cut trenches roughly four times faster than digging the same trench with a bucket, with more consistent results and less operator skill required. Augers cover fence-post holes, tree planting, and footing holes, which matters directly for this project's fencing and outbuilding work (cross-reference Developing the Site).
- **Trencher (dedicated machine, not an attachment).** Walk-behind rental $100-$300/day (compact class $150-$250/day; mid-size $250-$400/day); ride-on rental $300-$600/day; a blended average across classes reported at roughly $280/day (2026). Many yards set a 4-hour minimum. Delivery runs roughly $85-$175 for a walk-behind unit and $160-$400 for a ride-on unit needing a trailer.
- **Battery-electric earthmoving equipment.** A real, commercially available 2026 category, not a concept: New Holland's E25X (2.5 metric ton, 32.2 kWh lithium-ion pack, 4-8 hour runtime) and E15X (1.5 metric ton), Volt's EV-X2 (2-ton, 28 kW, 400A CATL battery, 6-10 hour runtime, 6-7 hour recharge on a 240V outlet), Tadano's TB20e, and Volvo's ECR25 Electric. Off-grid charging options exist: solar charging stations (Beam), mobile charging units (Spark Charge), and plug-and-play off-road quick-charging stations (Hitachi KTEG Powertree). Relevant to this project's all-electric, solar-plus-battery framing, though likely a purchase (not rental-market-available yet at small scale) and a cost question against a single-house budget.
- **Used equipment auctions.** IronPlanet, GovPlanet, and Ritchie Bros. run online and on-site auctions carrying excavators, skid steers, backhoes, concrete mixers, trenchers, drills, and air compressors, sold with no warranty and buyer-inspection risk. A real sourcing channel for an owner willing to travel to inspect or bid sight-unseen on a known model.
- **Small-engine fuel.** Ethanol content above 10 percent is reported to damage small engines (corrosion of metal parts, breakdown of rubber and plastic components, hard starting, shortened engine life), and two-stroke engines are harder-hit than four-stroke because their simpler, non-computer-controlled combustion cannot compensate. Ethanol-free fuel or a fuel stabilizer, and draining or stabilizing fuel that will sit unused more than about a month, are the reported mitigations. Directly relevant here: this project's many small gas engines (compactors, cutoff saws, mixers, generators, trimmers) will sit idle between phases for weeks or months at a time across a multi-year build.

## 3. Concrete Mixing, Placement, and Finishing Tools

The foundation and concrete-placement chapter already tables mixer types, capacities, and 2026 costs in its mixing-equipment section, and it already lists the pour-day finishing-tool checklist. This section adds selection depth, tool-level cost, and the maintenance layer.

- **Internal (poker) vibrators.** Head diameters run 25-150mm; smaller heads suit congested reinforcement and narrow forms, larger heads suit mass pours. Purchase cost found: roughly $200-$1,000 (2026), varying by brand, head size, and power source (electric, gas, pneumatic, or hydraulic flex-shaft). The foundation and concrete-placement chapter already states the 5-15 second dwell-time guidance.
- **External (form) vibrators.** Clamp or bolt to the outside of a form and are used where internal vibration cannot reach (tall walls, congested cages). Purchase cost found: roughly $150-$2,000 (2026), a wider range than internal units because of mounting-hardware variation.
- **Walk-behind power trowel.** Gas units in the 36-48 inch class, 5.5-13 hp, 4-blade rotor, reported to finish up to 1,000 square feet per hour. A specific day-rental rate was not found in this pass (a gap to fill; riding trowels were found ranging $560-$5,040, but that figure's time basis, day versus week, was not confirmed and needs a direct rental-yard quote). For a house-scale slab (garage, basement, or the radiant topping slab), a walk-behind unit is the relevant class; a riding trowel is a commercial-scale tool unlikely to earn its keep on this build.
- **Vibratory (power) screed.** Rental found at roughly $102-$135/day, $375-$405/week, $938-$1,013/month in one regional market (2026); a second source cited a $127 self-pickup day rate against a $552 day rate with delivery, an inconsistency across sources that needs a direct Oregon-yard quote before it goes in the manuscript. A vibratory screed strikes off and consolidates a slab in one pass, an upgrade over a hand-pulled straightedge for anything larger than a small footing or patio slab.
- **Laser screed.** A large-slab commercial tool (warehouse floors, big flatwork); pricing found by square footage ($0.10-$0.12/sq ft with a roughly $600 minimum, plus delivery) rather than by day, which signals it is priced for jobs far larger than a house footprint. Named here for completeness and then ruled out for this project's scale, not presented as a live option.
- **Bull float.** A 4-foot magnesium bull float with bracket and handle purchased new runs roughly $100-$125 (2026); rental runs roughly $25-$40/day.
- **Darby (hand float).** A 14-inch magnesium darby runs roughly $18 (2026); wood-bodied versions cost less.
- **Edger and groover.** Named and their function stated in the foundation and concrete-placement chapter's placement sequence, but no cost was found in this pass; both are inexpensive hand tools (a gap to close with a direct retailer check, expected low, likely under $30 each).
- **Trowels (steel finishing trowel).** Steel blades for a hard troweled finish, used in one or more passes with increasing pressure as the foundation and concrete-placement chapter already states; magnesium floats and steel trowels are different tools for different stages (magnesium works the surface while it is still open; steel densifies it as it closes), a distinction stated plainly here since a first-time owner-builder can otherwise buy the wrong one.
- **Concrete wheelbarrow.** Steel-tray wheelbarrows are reported as the construction-site standard for mixing and hauling concrete and mortar because the tray resists the load without flexing; poly-tray wheelbarrows are lighter and cheaper but rated lower (one source cites roughly 300 lb capacity) and are better suited to light garden use than to wet concrete or mortar. Single-wheel barrows maneuver better in tight forms and narrow paths; dual-wheel (two-wheel) barrows resist tipping on slopes and carry heavier loads more stably. Specific purchase costs were not pinned down in this pass (a gap).
- **Concrete floor grinder (surface prep).** Relevant only if the topping slab or a garage/shop slab is ground or polished as a finish (cross-reference Interiors). Grinding service cost found at roughly $1-$4/square foot; owner-operated planetary grinders (lighter, more maneuverable, multiple simultaneous pads) are the residential-scale class, distinct from ride-on units built for warehouse floors.
- **Mixer maintenance (cross-reference the foundation and concrete-placement chapter's mixer table, which does not cover upkeep).** Rinsing and scrubbing a mixer drum after every batch is the standard advice found; a high-pressure wash (2,500+ psi) is called out as the most effective method. Stubborn buildup is treated with a concrete-dissolving agent, diluted vinegar, or a monthly acid wash. A release agent applied to the drum before loading is reported to reduce adhesion and ease cleanup. Consequence of skipping this: dried buildup throws the drum out of rotational balance, stresses the bearings and drive, and degrades mix quality; a well-maintained steel drum is reported to last 10-15 years against under 5 years for a poorly maintained one, a real stake for a project that plans to run its own mixer for years across many small batches (block production, footings, mortar).

## 4. Concrete Pumps

The foundation and concrete-placement chapter's pour-day checklist already names "pump truck or chute setup planned and rehearsed" without pricing or comparing pump types; that is filled in here.

- **Line pump versus boom pump.** A line pump pushes concrete through flexible hose laid out by hand, suited to smaller, tighter-access pours (footings, slabs, block fill) where a boom cannot reach or is not justified. A boom pump uses a hydraulic articulated arm to place concrete at height or distance without hand-moving hose, suited to foundation walls, elevated slabs, or a site where the truck cannot get close.
- **Cost found (2026, multiple regional markets, not Oregon-specific and needing a local quote before use in the manuscript):** line pump roughly $125-$225/hour, or $700-$1,900/day depending on market; boom pump roughly $200-$350+/hour, or $1,400-$4,500+/day depending on boom size and market. Nearly every provider sets a minimum time block (typically 3-4 hours) regardless of actual pour duration, with minimum charges of roughly $600-$1,600. On top of time-based billing, a per-yardage fee (roughly $3-$10/cubic yard pumped) is common, along with a no-washout fee (roughly $250-$450), extra pipeline footage charged per foot beyond an included length (roughly $1.50-$3.00/ft), and a fuel surcharge (examples found: 7, 8, and 12 percent).
- **Operator note.** The foundation and concrete-placement chapter's placement table already flags that high pump pressure can affect the mix (water squeezed out under pressure) and that the pump operator's experience with the specified mix should be confirmed; this is a hire-with-operator service, not owner-operated equipment, which simplifies the buy-versus-rent question here to a straightforward rent-with-operator decision for any pour where the truck chute cannot reach.

## 5. Masonry Hand Tools and Layout Aids

This is the deepest gap against the existing manuscript: the foundation and concrete-placement chapter does not touch masonry hand tools, and draft C (mortar, grout, jointing) carries only one general question (Q49) about the minimum tool kit, without naming or costing individual tools. This section is written to feed both the Masonry Craft chapter and this draft's own proposed chapter.

- **Brick trowel (mason's trowel).** Point-nosed, rectangular blade that curves slightly upward at the front, used for spreading mortar ("buttering") and general laying of brick, block, and stone.
- **Pointing trowel.** A small brick trowel used for filling narrow cavities and repairing crumbling joints.
- **Margin trowel.** Flat-nosed, narrow (roughly 2-5 inches wide), used to work mortar into tight spaces and corners a brick trowel cannot reach, and to clean excess mortar from joints.
- **Jointers (joint-tooling irons).** Shaped to match the intended joint profile (concave, V, raked, weathered, flush); the profile choice affects both appearance and water-shedding performance at the joint, which is a genuine technical point, not a cosmetic one, a point that belongs in the Masonry Craft chapter's joint-tooling section.
- **Story pole.** A straight pole (wood or metal) marked with the course heights for the wall being built, used to keep coursing consistent without measuring every course by hand. Commercial systems exist (Story-Rite is one named brand) with a base that adjusts for plumb and presets block coursing height; Bon Tool's Model C guide pole (9 ft by 2 in) carries permanently etched coursing scales for both modular and standard brick spacing. A story pole is inexpensive to shop-build (a straight length of lumber and a tape measure) versus buying a manufactured guide system, a real buy-versus-build choice for a solo builder.
- **Line blocks, line pins, and line twigs.** Line pins (steel or aluminum) anchor the mason's line at each end of a wall or lead. Line twigs (small T-shaped pieces) hold the line at intermediate points between courses. Line blocks (wood, plastic, or metal, larger than pins) add stability on long or complex runs. Together these are the low-cost system that keeps a coursing line straight and taut without a helper physically holding it, which matters directly for a mostly-solo builder (cross-reference the pacing and solo-work material in the Body and Mind draft).
- **Brick hammer.** One end for a controlled splitting blow on brick or stone, the other typically a standard hammer face; paired historically with a brick set (a wide-bladed chisel) for a cleaner split than the hammer alone.
- **Masonry/mortar hawk and mortar board.** A hand-held hawk (a flat board with a handle underneath) holds a working supply of mortar close to the point of use; a mortar board (a larger flat surface, sometimes on a stand) holds the mixed batch at working height so the mason is not bending to a wheelbarrow or tub for every trowel load, a real fatigue-reduction point (cross-reference Body and Mind for the ergonomics case; this draft's job is naming the tool and its role in the workflow).
- **Traditional/vernacular hand tools.** The mason's core personal kit is reported consistently across sources as trowels and jointers, a plumb rule or spirit level, a brick hammer and brick set, a steel square, a mason's line, and a tape or folding rule; most modern equipment is described as an evolved version of these same tools rather than something categorically different. Regional and vernacular tool names vary by place (one example found: a spade called a "phavada" and a mortar pan called a "ghamela" in South Asian building practice), a reminder that hand-tool masonry is a worldwide, low-tech-compatible craft and not one that depends on any single country's manufactured-tool market, relevant to this project's independence goal. Historic stone-masonry tools (a two-person crosscut/felling saw, a frame saw with a blade held in tension inside a wooden frame) apply if a stone-veneer or stone-accent option is ever considered, though that is a materials-chapter question, not this chapter's.
- **Masonry/block/brick table saws.** Stationary wet saws with blades from roughly 14 to 36 inches, gas or electric powered, used for repeated, precise block or brick cuts (corners, half-units, custom returns) at a fixed station rather than freehand with a cutoff saw. A dedicated brick paver saw with a roughly 10-inch blade cuts to about 3.75 inches deep, sized for pavers and thin units rather than full CMU.
- **Guillotine/lever block splitters.** A lever-operated splitter for a repeated cut pattern (commonly rock-faced or split-face veneer units); one purchase figure found is roughly $1,500 (2026). A real problem-solution finding surfaced in this research: at least one owner reported buying a splitter for roughly that price and rarely using it, because the actual cut volume on a typical project did not justify a dedicated machine over a saw or a rental. This is a genuine buy-versus-rent cautionary example for the manuscript; the splitter is not an obvious buy.
- **Gas cutoff saw (handheld, abrasive or diamond wheel).** The Stihl TS420 class (66.7 cc, 14-inch wheel, roughly 4.9-inch cutting depth) was repeatedly named as the reference point other cutoff saws are compared against, described by users as compact, lightweight, and reliable, with anti-vibration and dual-stage air filtration. No retail price was found in this pass (dealer-set pricing, not published); a gap to close with a direct dealer or rental-yard quote.

## 6. Cutting Tools: Saws, Blades, and Core Drills

- **Walk-behind concrete saw (14-20 inch class).** Rental found at roughly $90-$175/day, $255-$600/week, $650-$1,800/4-week (2026). Many yards price the saw "tool only" and bill the diamond blade separately, either as a flat rental fee or a wear/consumption charge.
- **Early-entry (green-concrete) saw.** Cuts control joints in fresh concrete before it fully cures, reducing random shrinkage cracking versus waiting for a full-cure cut. Rental found at roughly $125-$200/day, $450-$750/week, $1,100-$2,200/4-week (2026).
- **Diamond blade rental and purchase.** Rental found at roughly $30-$60/day for a 14-inch blade and $45-$100/day for an 18-inch blade. Purchase costs found: segmented blades (general-purpose, rough cuts) roughly $20-$90; DIY/entry-level roughly $20-$50; contractor-grade 12-16 inch roughly $80-$200; premium professional blades $200 and up. The relevant cost metric is "cost per inch-foot of cut," not sticker price: taller diamond segments (one example, 15mm) hold up to 50 percent more usable diamond than a standard segment, meaning roughly 50 percent fewer blade changes for the same linear footage cut. Blade bond hardness is matched to the material cut (concrete, block, brick, or stone each wear a blade differently); using the wrong bond either glazes over (stops cutting effectively) or wears out fast.
- **Core drill.** Handheld electric units cut up to roughly 6 inches in diameter; rig-mounted (stand-mounted) units cut up to roughly 10 inches and are easier to control for a deep or precise hole (sleeve penetrations, anchor holes, mechanical/electrical penetrations through a foundation wall). Rental figures found were inconsistent in stated time basis (roughly $190-$250 per unit at several yards, day basis not always confirmed) and need a direct quote before use in the manuscript.
- **Rotary hammer (SDS-plus versus SDS-max).** SDS-plus units run roughly $150-$230 (Bosch Bulldog-class roughly $167, a 20V cordless DeWalt roughly $230 on sale); SDS-max units run roughly $420-$1,500 (Bosch RH540M-class roughly $420, DeWalt roughly $590, Hilti TE-60-class roughly $1,500). SDS-plus has a practical diameter ceiling around 26mm in standard-strength concrete (lower in high-strength concrete); using an SDS-plus hammer above its rated energy class is reported to roughly double bit wear and halve the drilling rate, a real, specific problem-and-solution point (buy or rent the right class for the hole size, rather than pushing an undersized hammer).

## 7. Rebar Tools

- **Manual rebar cutter-bender.** Purchase cost found roughly $200-$400 across several brands (examples found: $200, $235, $300, $350, $400); some 5/8-inch-capacity models found around $199-$225.
- **Hydraulic rebar cutter.** Handheld manual-hydraulic units (10-ton class) found around $50-$100; 16-ton class around $65-$130; larger manual-hydraulic units up to roughly $980. Electric-hydraulic units run from roughly $265-$350 at the small end to roughly $1,100-$1,300 for larger machines.
- **Selection point.** A manual bender-cutter suits the bar sizes and volumes of a residential foundation and reinforced-masonry wall system; a powered (electric-hydraulic) unit's added cost pays back mainly at higher bar diameters or where a large quantity of bends and cuts must happen in a short field window, a genuine buy-versus-rent-versus-upgrade question sized to this project's actual bar schedule (cross-reference the structural chapter's reinforcement detailing for typical bar sizes used).

## 8. Fastening and Anchoring Into Concrete and Masonry

- **Powder-actuated tools.** Fire a fastener directly into concrete or steel using a gunpowder-based charge, faster than drill-and-anchor for high-volume light fastening (sill plates, furring, wall plates). Purchase costs found (2026): a semi-automatic single-shot tool (Hilti DX 2 class) roughly $241; a fully automatic magazine-fed tool (Hilti DX 5 class, Bluetooth-connected to a fleet-management app) roughly $1,848; a competing brand's top single-shot model (Ramset XT540 class) roughly $538. Some manufacturers (Hilti named specifically) offer a monthly fleet-management fee covering the tool plus service and repair, an alternative to outright purchase for a single-project owner-builder if the tool sees heavy sustained use.
- **Epoxy anchoring (rebar dowels, bolts, railings into cured concrete).** Dispensed with a caulking-style gun; a pro-grade gun with a 12:1 mechanical advantage ratio is recommended because of the force needed to push two-part epoxy through a static mixer nozzle. Some epoxy products (one example: AnchorFast) package as a standard dual-cartridge that fits an ordinary caulk gun, avoiding a dedicated (and more expensive) applicator gun; pneumatic and powered injector tools exist and are described as expensive, justified mainly at higher volume. General installation guidance found: hole diameter roughly 3-4 times the bar diameter, hole depth roughly 8-12 times the bar diameter (a general figure, not sourced to a specific product's ICC-ES report, and not to be used as a design value without verification against the actual product's published data and the structural engineer's specification). Product examples found with cure-time claims: a 24-hour full-cure and a faster roughly 10-hour full-cure two-part epoxy, both claimed to reach 10,000 psi (vendor claims, unverified, not engineering values).
- **Mechanical (screw and expansion) anchors.** Concrete screws (the Tapcon-style product category) and expansion/wedge anchors are the lighter-duty and lower-cost alternative to epoxy for many fastening tasks; this pass did not research specific products, sizes, or costs in depth, a gap that needs a dedicated question, since this is likely to be one of the most frequently used fastener types across the whole build (attaching plates, brackets, and fixtures to finished concrete and masonry).
- **Rotary hammer as the shared platform.** The rotary-hammer research above (section 6) is the same tool used to drill anchor holes for epoxy and mechanical anchors, not a separate purchase; stated plainly here so the manuscript does not present fastening and cutting/drilling as needing two different tool investments.

## 9. Measuring, Layout, and Control

- **Basic laser level.** An owner-builder-oriented estimate found around $550 (2026) for a general-purpose self-leveling laser, distinct from a rotary/grade laser (below). A line laser of this class has limited outdoor range in daylight, reportedly as little as about 10 feet without a detector, which matters directly for exterior layout work (it is an indoor- and short-range tool, not a substitute for a rotary laser outdoors).
- **Rotary (grade) laser level.** Rotates a laser plane 360 degrees and, paired with a receiver/detector, reads outdoors at long range for grading, foundation layout, and slab screeding. Package pricing found (receiver, tripod, and grade rod included): roughly $2,070-$2,120 for one named mid/pro model, roughly $2,670-$2,790 for a comparable Leica model; budget rotary units (a named budget brand, 2,000 ft rated range) cost substantially less, aimed at smaller sites. Reported accuracy for higher-end units: roughly plus-or-minus 1/16 to 1/8 inch at 100 feet.
- **Total station.** Full professional units were quoted as high as roughly $130,000; more realistic small-contractor/owner-scale budgets found in the $8,000-$10,000 range, with robotic (self-tracking) versions around $25,000, or a lease around $300+/month with a buyout option after about five years. Reported accuracy down to about 3mm, better than a laser level or GNSS receiver. A real, repeated finding across sources: many contractors who bought a total station ended up using it mainly as a simple site level, underutilizing its actual capability, which argues against this purchase for a single-house owner-build where a mid-range rotary laser plus the owner-exempt layout work already covered in the Build Sequence chapter (measuring from found monuments) likely covers the real need.
- **Traditional low-cost layout tools.** A line level (a small level that clips onto a taut string) and a 4-foot mason's level remain in active use as the cheapest layout tools for short runs and individual courses; specific current costs were not pinned down in this pass (a gap that needs only a quick retailer check, not a deep research question).
- **Practical framing for this project.** The real decision is not laser-versus-total-station in the abstract; it is which single instrument (or small set) covers foundation layout, slab and footing grading, and masonry coursing control for one house, once, without renting three different tools for three different weeks. That framing question belongs in Part 2 below.

## 10. Lifting and Material Handling

Draft M already researches this territory in depth from the body-mechanics angle (vacuum block/slab lifters, concrete block clamps, the MULE lift-assist device, back-support exoskeletons, carts and dollies, motorized wheelbarrows/power buggies, DIY and purchased gantry cranes, block-and-tackle rigging) with real 2026 cost figures already gathered there. This draft does not re-research those figures. It adds two things M's body-focused framing does not: (1) two pieces of equipment sized above hand-lifting aids, and (2) the tool-inventory framing question of which lifting aids belong in the owned kit versus the rental-for-the-phase kit.

- **Telehandler (rough-terrain telescoping forklift).** National average rental found at $665/day, $1,644/week, $3,592/month (2026, aggregated data). Compact units (roughly 5,000-6,000 lb capacity) start around $350/day; heavy units (15,000-20,000 lb) run $1,000-$1,500/day. Monthly rental works out to roughly $120/day effective, an 82 percent discount off the daily rate, a strong argument for renting by the month rather than the day or week during any phase (a multi-week masonry campaign, a truss and roof-structure set) that genuinely needs sustained lift capacity.
- **Scaffolding.** Frame/section scaffold rents for roughly $7-$22/day per section or $40-$150/week; assembled tower systems run roughly $75-$200/day at 15 feet and $150-$350/day at 20 feet. Reported regional swing: metro-area rates run 20-30 percent above rural rates, which may cut the other way for a rural Oregon site far from a metro yard (delivery distance versus base rate is the real local variable to check).
- **Framing question for the merge into a full tool table.** Draft M's own question M24 already asks for a buy-versus-rent table across the lifting aids it researched, landing in the Body and Mind chapter. ## 11. Block-Making Surrounding Hand and Shop Tools

Draft B thoroughly covers the block-press and mold machinery itself. The surrounding hand and shop tools it does not detail:

- **Tamping tools.** For hand-tamped or lever-press block production, the tamper head design (flat versus shaped, weight, handle length) affects both compaction consistency and the operator's fatigue across a production day (cross-reference Body and Mind, which already flags manual-press effort as a body-mechanics issue at draft M's question M31).
- **Handling carts and pallet jacks for green block.** Draft M's own question M28 flags this as shallow-researched: standard warehouse-floor dollies are not suited to the uneven, often muddy ground of a rural block yard. A cart or hand truck rated for outdoor, uneven-ground use is a distinct product category from a shop dolly, and not yet sourced to a specific product in this pass.
- **Curing racks and covers.** Green block needs support (open-slat wood or steel racking that lets air reach all faces) and moisture control during cure (plastic sheeting, damp burlap, or a curing blanket) to hit strength on the fast-turnaround schedule the owner-made-blocks chapter targets. Rack material, spacing, and reuse across a roughly 100-unit-per-day production run is a real design question distinct from the mold and press themselves.
- **Pallets.** Wood, plastic, or steel pallets under each cast unit during demold and early cure each trade off cost, weight (relevant to a solo operator moving many pallets by hand or cart), durability against repeated wetting, and reusability across a production run measured in the thousands of units.
- **Mold-cleaning tools.** Wire brushes, scrapers, and a pressure washer keep steel or wood molds free of cement buildup that would otherwise degrade dimensional tolerance and surface finish on later casts; mold-release application (spray bottle versus brush) is a related, low-cost but production-rate-relevant tool choice.

## 12. Power, Air, Water, and Fuel at the Tool Level

- **Generator sizing for tools (distinct from whole-house backup power, which belongs to Solar, Battery, and Energy Independence and to Resilience).** A rule of thumb found: a solo operator running one power tool at a time can get by on roughly 3,600 watts; a two-to-three-person crew running multiple tools, a compressor, and task lighting needs roughly 6,000-8,000 watts as a practical minimum. Sizing method: sum the running watts of everything expected to run at once, add the highest single motor's startup surge on top (not averaged in), then add a 20-30 percent margin.
- **Air compressor.** Named in passing in the epoxy-anchor research (pneumatic rotary hammers and pneumatic injector tools exist as a professional-grade option) but not separately researched in this pass; a gap, since a compressor also serves pneumatic nailers, blow-down/cleanout of forms and cores before pour, and general shop use, and its sizing and cost deserve their own question.
- **Water at the tool level.** Distinct from the concrete-washout containment the foundation and concrete-placement chapter already specifies (a lined pit or a portable washout container): wet-cutting water for silica dust control (required or strongly preferred by the OSHA Table 1 controls named in section 13 below) and a general tool-wash station both need a water supply and a place for the runoff to go that is not simply "wherever it lands" on a site that also has its own water-independence and greywater goals (cross-reference Water Independence).

## 13. Safety Gear and PPE

Draft M already treats PPE from the body-systems angle (why silica hurts lungs, why noise damages hearing, why kneeling damages joints) in its sections on lungs, hearing/eyes/vibration, and skin. This section treats the same territory from the equipment, task-compliance, and cost angle, which is this chapter's assigned ground per the task brief.

- **OSHA respirable-crystalline-silica rule, Table 1 (29 CFR 1926.1153).** Names 18 specific construction tasks (including concrete and masonry sawing, grinding, drilling, and jackhammering) and states, per task, the required engineering control (commonly water-fed wet cutting or a vacuum dust-collection shroud) and the respirator requirement, keyed to task, duration, and indoor-versus-outdoor setting. A contractor or owner-builder who follows Table 1's controls exactly for a given task is not required to also perform air monitoring or separately demonstrate compliance with the permissible exposure limit; deviating from Table 1 means measuring actual exposure instead. The permissible exposure limit itself is 50 micrograms per cubic meter of air, an 8-hour time-weighted average.
- **Task-specific findings.** Wet cutting outdoors for 4 hours or less needs no respirator at all, provided water flows continuously at the blade. Indoor cutting or grinding always carries stricter controls. Tuckpointing is called out as the one task requiring a respirator at all times, even with dust collection running, which matters directly to the mortar-repointing and repair content already planned for the Masonry Craft chapter (cross-reference).
- **Respirator classes and assigned protection factors.** An APF of 10 corresponds to an N95 filtering-facepiece respirator (or equivalent); an APF of 25 corresponds to a half-face elastomeric respirator, the minimum called for on higher-exposure tasks. N95 filters pass up to about 5 percent of the most-penetrating particle size; P100 filters pass about 0.03 percent, roughly 167 times fewer particles than N95 under the same conditions, which is why P100 (not N95) is the recommended standard specifically for concrete cutting, grinding, and enclosed-space masonry sawing. A half-face elastomeric respirator with P100 cartridges (3M 6300 class) was found priced around $14-$26 for the mask body plus filters (2026).
- **Knee protection.** Two product families: strap-on knee pads (mobile) and standalone kneeling boards (stationary, the worker moves on and off). For concrete and masonry specifically, a hard outer shell resists puncture and abrasion from aggregate better than a soft shell; gel cushioning distributes pressure better for long static kneeling while closed-cell foam holds up longer under repeated impact; waterproofing matters on its own because wet mix that soaks through a pad defeats the pad's purpose and exposes skin to an alkaline burn risk.
- **Hand protection.** Cut-resistant gloves are called out specifically for rebar handling and cutting. Separately, waterproof, alkali-resistant gloves are called out for handling wet concrete and mortar, whose high pH is a real skin-burn hazard over repeated or prolonged contact.
- **Hearing protection.** Required where exposure exceeds 85 decibels over an 8-hour time-weighted average; jackhammers and saws are named specifically as the tools that cross this line on a typical residential job.

## 14. Buy-Versus-Rent Economics and Multi-Year Amortization

- **General utilization threshold.** A commonly cited rule: if a piece of equipment is used more than roughly 65 percent of available working days, ownership tends to win financially; below that, renting tends to win. A simple break-even calculation: divide the net annual cost of ownership (purchase price plus financing, maintenance, insurance, and storage, minus resale value, annualized) by the daily rental rate to find the number of rental-days at which owning and renting cost the same.
- **Folk rule found repeatedly:** if a tool is needed about once a week, own it; if it is needed about once a year, rent it. Hand tools and most corded/cordless power tools are treated as automatic buys under this rule because their cost is low relative to the value delivered across a whole build.
- **This project's specific multi-year wrinkle.** The Build Sequence chapter already states that a solo owner-builder takes roughly two to four times as long as a contractor crew for the same scope, meaning this build runs multiple years rather than a contractor's typical months. That cuts two ways for the buy-versus-rent question, and both need to reach the manuscript: (1) a purchased tool has far more calendar time to earn back its price against the rental alternative, pushing more items toward "buy" than a short conventional build would; but (2) a tool bought early and used intermittently across years sits idle for long stretches between uses, during which small-engine fuel degrades (section 1), battery packs self-discharge and lose capacity, steel rusts (section 16), and a given model may fall out of production or parts support, all of which argue for staged purchase timed to each phase rather than buying the full kit on day one.
- **Draft E's seed list (item E67).** Buy list proposed there: rotary hammer, laser level/transit, mixer, plate compactor, quality PPE. Rent-only list proposed there: concrete pump, excavator, boom/scissor lift. This draft's research above supports extending both lists considerably (for example: plate compactor, wheelbarrow, basic laser level, and the core masonry hand-tool kit toward buy; telehandler, laser screed, and total station toward rent-or-skip) and turning it into a single table covering every category in this draft, phased to the build sequence.
- **Hidden costs of ownership**, named generally in the equipment-fleet research (depreciation, maintenance parts and labor, insurance, storage, and financing) apply at a much smaller scale to a one-house build than to a contractor's fleet, but the categories themselves (not the large-fleet dollar figures found, which do not transfer to this project's scale) are the right checklist for sizing this project's own tool budget.

## 15. Sourcing: New, Used, Auction, Rental, and Shared

- **Used equipment auctions.** IronPlanet, GovPlanet, and Ritchie Bros. (the latter having combined with IronPlanet) run public online and on-site auctions of used excavators, skid steers, mixers, compressors, trenchers, and drills, with no warranty and buyer-inspection risk; a real channel for the larger machine purchases (mixer, compactor, possibly a compact loader) rather than for small hand and power tools.
- **Tool libraries and equipment co-ops.** More than 80 tool-lending libraries are reported operating across the US and Canada (a network directory exists at toollibraries.org). Rural and farm-specific equipment-sharing cooperatives exist as a distinct, older model (one named example: "Tool Legit" in North Carolina), typically starting as informal neighbor equipment-swapping before formalizing into a shared inventory with a booking system. Relevant to a rural Oregon parcel where the nearest commercial rental yard may be a real drive (cross-reference the Land, Site, and Feasibility chapter's distance-and-logistics material): an informal or formal equipment-share arrangement with nearby landowners is a genuine, real-world cost- and trip-saving option: a creative-but-real idea, not a hypothetical one.
- **Cordless battery-platform lock-in.** Milwaukee's M18 platform and DeWalt's 20V MAX platform are each reported to span 250-300+ tools; batteries and chargers are not cross-compatible between brands. Standardizing on one platform early avoids duplicate battery and charger purchases repeated across a multi-year build as new cordless tools are added phase by phase, a genuine practical decision point for this specific project's timeline. Two 2026-specific developments found here: DeWalt introduced a separate high-voltage "PowerShift" platform (distinct from 20V MAX/FLEXVOLT) aimed at heavy-duty concrete-adjacent tools (a cordless demolition hammer and a cordless cutoff saw were named); Milwaukee introduced a large PACKOUT-compatible portable power supply (roughly 7200W/6.0kWh) built from its battery ecosystem, which is at least a curious overlap with this project's own battery-storage and off-grid framing, carried into a question below.
- **New retail versus rental yard versus manufacturer fleet programs.** Named in the powder-actuated-tool research: at least one manufacturer (Hilti) offers a monthly fleet-management fee covering a tool plus its service and repair, an alternative for even a single project if a given tool sees heavy, sustained use across many months.

## 16. Tool Maintenance, Repair, and Consumables

- **Hand-tool cleaning and rust prevention.** Rinsing a trowel or float under water and scrubbing with a stiff brush after every use is the baseline; a 15-minute warm soapy soak loosens stubborn buildup before a second scrub; a commercial concrete-remover product handles heavy buildup; rubbing a tool against a rough concrete surface abrades off hardened residue in the field without a dedicated cleaning product. Rust prevention found: a light wipe with a light oil (WD-40, mineral oil, or light machine oil) after cleaning and full drying; a classic trick found in multiple sources is a bucket of clean, dry sand mixed with a small amount of motor or mineral oil, into which the metal working end of a trowel is plunged after use, both cleaning off residue and leaving a light protective oil film. Storage matters on its own: concrete floors wick ground moisture upward and create a locally humid microclimate at floor level (the same mechanism behind the unheated-shop condensation problem draft K already raises for the building itself), so tools stored directly on a concrete shop floor rust faster than tools hung on a wall or shelved above floor level.
- **Diamond blade wear and consumables budgeting.** Already covered in section 6 above (cost-per-inch-foot as the real metric); the budgeting implication for this project is that blade and consumable costs (blades, core-drill bits, sanding/grinding discs, respirator cartridges, PAT fastener loads) are a recurring line item across a multi-year build, not a one-time purchase, and deserve their own budget category distinct from the tool purchases themselves.
- **Small gas engine care across long idle periods.** Already covered in section 1 (ethanol fuel damage, stabilizer use, draining fuel before storage over about a month); this is a recurring maintenance task across this project's specific multi-year, intermittent-use pattern, not a one-time setup step, since the compactor, cutoff saw, mixer, and any gas-powered pump or generator will each sit idle between phases repeatedly.
- **Battery-pack storage across long idle periods.** Not directly covered in the sources found this pass; a real gap. Lithium-ion packs are broadly known to lose capacity when stored fully charged, fully depleted, or in temperature extremes over long periods, which is a genuine risk for a multi-year build where a given cordless tool might not come out of the case for months between relevant phases; this deserves its own research question rather than an assumed answer.
- **Repair, parts availability, and right-to-repair as a purchase factor.** Not directly researched in this pass beyond the general observation (echoed in the DIY-tool-regret research) that some tools are reported to age out of manufacturer parts support faster than others. A dedicated question follows: for a build expected to run several years, does the brand's parts and service availability (replacement brushes, bearings, batteries, and blades staying available for the life of the project) belong in the purchase decision alongside sticker price, especially for the small number of tools this project will lean on hardest (mixer, rotary hammer, cutoff saw)?

## 17. The Workshop and Tool Storage System

Draft K already sizes and costs the shop building itself (floor area, door count and sizing, floor material, condensation control, three-phase power, and the hoop-house and shipping-container alternatives), and K's own question K15 already asks about a general storage system for tools, fasteners, and consumables. This section's job is the fit-out layer inside whatever building K's research lands on, which K does not detail.

- **Pegboard and slotted wall-track systems.** A standard, low-cost wall-storage method (named vendors found: Wall Control, OmniWall, and generic pegboard kits) that keeps hand tools visible and off the floor (tying directly into the rust-prevention point in section 16). Modular slotted-rail systems let hooks, shelves, and bins be repositioned as the tool inventory changes phase to phase across a multi-year build, an advantage over fixed shelving for a shop whose contents will look very different during earthwork than during finish carpentry.
- **Shadow-board labeling.** A lean-manufacturing-derived method: outlining each tool's silhouette on the board (paint or marker) so a missing tool is visible at a glance, both a time-saver (finding tools faster) and a light theft/loss deterrent (an empty outline is noticed immediately, versus a general tool chaos where a missing item goes unnoticed for weeks).
- **What a generic shop-storage answer misses for this project specifically.** This build's tool inventory spans an unusually wide range in size and character within one shop: earthmoving-attachment storage (heavy, bulky, outdoor-tolerant), a masonry hand-tool wall (light, needs rust protection), a fastener and consumables section (small parts, needs bins and labeling, restocked repeatedly across years), a battery-charging station (needs dedicated outlets and ventilation for multiple simultaneous chargers across at least one, possibly two, cordless platforms), and a tool-maintenance and sharpening area (bench space, a vise, sharpening stones or a bench grinder for chisels and trowel edges). None of these five zones is addressed by K's general building-sizing research, and each is a real design decision for the shop's interior layout.

## 18. Security and Insurance

- **Builder's risk insurance and tools.** Builder's risk insurance is reported to cover theft, fire, windstorm, and vandalism affecting materials and the structure under construction, but contractor- and owner-owned tools and equipment are commonly excluded from the standard policy and need either a specific endorsement or a separate inland-marine policy to be covered against theft or damage. A further exclusion found: builder's risk does not cover theft by an employee (an insider), which would need separate crime coverage; directly relevant given the Build Sequence chapter's material on when paying a helper makes the owner an employer.
- **Jobsite theft, scale of the problem.** US construction-equipment theft losses are reported in the $300 million-$1 billion per year range, with over 11,000 reported incidents annually and an average per-incident cost around $6,000; roughly 20 percent of jobsite theft is reported to involve an insider. Power tools, generators, and heavy machinery are named as the most commonly targeted items, alongside bulk material theft (copper wiring, lumber, appliances).
- **Rural-site-specific risk and mitigation.** General security sources note that low foot traffic and slower law-enforcement response make rural and remote sites a higher theft risk than urban ones, echoing the same point draft K already raises for the block-yard specifically. Mitigations found: perimeter fencing (a commonly cited standard is six feet), site lighting, mobile solar-powered surveillance cameras (explicitly noted as viable on remote or off-grid sites without utility power, a direct fit for this project), GPS or Bluetooth asset trackers on higher-value tools and equipment, and simply returning tools to secured storage after each use rather than leaving them staged outdoors overnight.
