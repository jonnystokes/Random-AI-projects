## Part 1: Gathered Understanding

### 1. The Roof As A System: Three Independent Layers That Must Agree

A roof on this house is three separable problems that the chapter should treat as three separable problems, then coordinate:

- **Structure**: what holds the roof up and carries snow, wind uplift, seismic, and dead load to the walls. Already specified in Structural System and Concrete Design (the three-path table: concrete deck plus protected membrane roof, concrete deck plus sloped false roof, or wood truss on bond beam).
- **Weather surface**: the material that actually sheds water and has a design life shorter than the structure (30 to 150+ years depending on material, against the structural shell's multi-century target). This chapter's job is to lay out every weather-surface option in enough real detail to choose among them, and to make replacement of the weather surface a planned, low-disruption event rather than a structural intervention.
- **Junctions**: parapets, eaves, penetrations, and the roof-to-wall transition, where the structure's control layers (air, water, vapor, thermal) must connect to the wall's control layers without a gap. Multiple independent industry sources (NRCA, IIBEC, the Pacific Northwest EIFS litigation history below) identify this as the highest-failure-rate location on any building envelope, not just an aesthetic seam.

The three problems interact: the structural path chosen constrains which weather surfaces are physically possible (a concrete deck can carry gravel ballast or growing medium; a wood truss roof at 3:12-6:12 pitch cannot), and the junction detailing depends on which weather surface meets which wall assembly (a parapet is normal on a flat concrete deck and rare on a pitched truss roof, per the envelope chapter's own note).

### 2. Roof Structure Options For A Mass-Walled House

The structural and envelope chapters already state the three-path table (concrete deck plus PMR, concrete deck plus sloped false roof, wood truss on bond beam) with design-life figures, the drainage spec (1/4 inch per foot minimum slope, two drains minimum, overflow sized for the 100-year storm), and the solar-integration note (cast-in anchors, ballasted racks). This chapter's job is not to re-derive that table; it is to add the weather-surface depth, the junction depth, the drainage depth, and the green-roof depth that make the table usable. Two structural-side items surfaced in this research pass that were not in the existing table and may be worth flagging back to Structural System and Concrete Design rather than duplicated here:

- **Ponding instability is a structural stiffness check, not only a slope spec.** The envelope chapter lists "ASCE 7 ponding-instability check" as a bullet; the actual check (below, Drainage section) depends on the stiffness of the primary and secondary framing members and the deck, not just the nominal slope, and a flexible bay can still pond and progressively deflect even with correct as-built slope if a drain clogs. This is a structural engineering input the roof chapter should explain in plain terms so the owner understands why the engineer needs the drain layout before finishing the structural design, not after.
- **Coastal and special wind zones may govern parapet height and edge design**, not just wall design. Reported figures (unverified, need primary-source confirmation): Oregon's ocean-exposure areas fall in wind Exposure Category D, with basic design wind speeds reported around 125-145 mph depending on risk category, and Exposure D parapet height requirements reported at 8 inches more than Exposure C with a 12-inch floor. A structural engineer sets this, but the chapter should flag that a coastal or ridge-top parcel changes the coping and edge-metal wind design, not just the wall design.

### 3. Weather Surfaces And Service Life

#### 3.1 Single-ply membranes (TPO, EPDM, PVC)

The three dominant single-ply membrane families for a flat or low-slope roof, all compatible with the PMR (protected membrane roof / inverted roof) assembly already specified:

- **EPDM (ethylene propylene diene monomer, a synthetic rubber sheet)**: reported service life 25-30+ years with maintenance; UV and ozone resistant from its carbon-black composition, which lets it stay flexible for decades; performs well in cold climates; seams are adhered or taped, historically the weaker point versus heat-welded thermoplastics. Reported installed cost $4-8 per square foot (2026, aggregator sources, unverified).
- **TPO (thermoplastic polyolefin)**: reported service life 20-30 years, shorter proven track record than PVC or EPDM because it is a newer product family; heat-welded seams; white reflective surface option; some industry commentary (Midwest Roofing Contractors Association, per one source) flags TPO heat and UV degradation risk depending on formulation and thickness; reported cost $5-8.50 per square foot. Membrane thickness (mil rating, commonly 45-80 mil) reportedly matters more to actual service life than the TPO-vs-PVC choice itself; an 80-mil TPO can carry a warranty comparable to a PVC product.
- **PVC (polyvinyl chloride)**: reported service life 25-30+ years, some sources citing 35+; heat-welded seams reported as the strongest seam type in the industry; best chemical and grease resistance of the three, relevant only if a commercial-type exhaust or fuel exposure is on the roof (not expected on this house); reported cost $6-10 per square foot, generally the most expensive of the three.

Practical framing for a long-life house: seam integrity and membrane thickness matter more than brand-family choice; a heat-welded thermoplastic (TPO or PVC) at a heavier mil rating, fully adhered, is a defensible long-life choice; EPDM is the most field-proven for pure weather resistance but historically has the weaker seam technology (adhesive or tape rather than heat weld). Under a PMR assembly all three are equally protected from UV and foot traffic by the ballast above them, which reduces the practical difference between them for this house's specific assembly (their exposed-roof service-life differences matter far more on an unprotected exposed membrane roof, which this house does not plan to use).

#### 3.2 Modified bitumen (SBS and APP)

Two chemically distinct modified-bitumen types, both asphalt-based sheet membranes reinforced with polyester or fiberglass mat:

- **SBS (styrene-butadiene-styrene)**: rubber-modified, more flexible and elastic, especially in cold weather; can be installed torch-applied, hot-mopped, or cold-adhesive (solvent-free cold adhesives exist for low-VOC application); reported service life 20-30+ years.
- **APP (atactic polypropylene)**: plastic-modified, more rigid, better UV and heat resistance in hot climates; torch-applied only (not compatible with asphalt adhesive or hot-mopping).
- Cold-adhesive SBS application (no open flame) is the relevant method for an owner-builder or a crew wary of torch work near a wood-framed transition or near the block-yard's combustible storage; ARMA (Asphalt Roofing Manufacturers Association) publishes cold-adhesive application guidance.
- Reported installed cost $4-8.50 per square foot (2026, aggregator sources, unverified).
- Maintenance-relevant fact: modified bitumen is repairable and recoatable in place (patches and reflective coatings both extend service life), which fits this house's replace-the-wearing-layer-not-the-structure philosophy as well as single-ply does.

#### 3.3 Standing-seam metal roofing

The specified surface for the sloped paths (concrete deck plus sloped false roof, and the wood truss path), already named in the manuscript at 24-gauge minimum with a Kynar (PVDF) finish.

- **Steel or aluminum, 24-gauge, Kynar 500/Hylar 5000 (PVDF resin) finish**: reported service life 40-70 years, some sources citing up to 60+ years for unpainted 55% Al-Zn coated steel. Kynar finish itself reportedly carries a 30-40 year fade-and-chalk warranty and retains 80-90% of initial solar reflectance over 20 years. Concealed-clip systems (the panel hangs from hidden clips rather than exposed fasteners) reportedly achieve 160-200 mph wind-uplift ratings and are the correct choice over exposed-fastener panels for a long-life, low-maintenance roof (exposed fasteners back out and their gaskets fail over decades; concealed clips have no gasket to fail). Reported failure point: a correctly installed standing-seam roof essentially never leaks at the seam itself; it leaks at penetrations, so the chapter's penetration detailing matters as much as the panel choice.
- **Panel width and gauge against oil-canning** (a visible waviness in the flat pan between seams, an aesthetic rather than performance defect): reduced by heavier gauge (24 over 26), narrower panels (12-16 inch over 18-20 inch), and striations or minor-rib embossing.
- **Copper**: reported service life 100+ years, develops a self-healing protective patina (a green oxide layer) that improves with age rather than degrading; reported installed cost roughly $15-35 per square foot (2026, wide range across sources), the most expensive common metal roofing choice.
- **Zinc**: reported service life 60-100+ years, some sources citing up to 150; also self-healing (scratches in the protective oxide layer reportedly re-form over time); reported installed cost roughly $12-18 per square foot, cheaper than copper with a comparable service-life claim. VMZinc is the commonly cited manufacturer brand (unverified as the only option; a lead, not a fact).
- For this house's stated multi-century structural horizon, copper or zinc standing seam is a genuine alternative to painted steel worth presenting as an option, not just a luxury note: both materials are reported to outlast a Kynar-coated steel panel's finish life by 30-100+ years, at a real cost premium that the chapter should quantify once verified.
- **Underlayment**: metal roofs need a high-temperature-rated underlayment, not ordinary felt or standard synthetic underlayment, because the panel's thermal cycling (reported deck-surface range 240-265°F in sun) degrades ordinary asphalt-saturated felt. High-temp self-adhered ice-and-water-shield products (rated to roughly 260°F, 120-day exposure, per manufacturer literature) or high-temp synthetic underlayments are the correct spec; this is a real, specific product-category gap the current manuscript roof sections do not mention.

#### 3.4 Slate (natural and synthetic)

- **Natural slate**: reported service life 75-150+ years, some sources citing up to 200 years for the best-quality stone; no material warranty exists because it is a quarried stone product, not a manufactured one (the installer's workmanship warranty is the only warranty available). Reported installed cost $15-40 per square foot (2026, wide range, thickness- and source-dependent); structural reinforcement for the roof framing to carry slate's weight reportedly adds $1,000-10,000 depending on the existing structure. On this house's concrete-deck-plus-sloped-false-roof path, the concrete deck already exists as the base structure, so slate's weight question becomes a question for the sloped sleeper framing above the deck, not the primary structure.
- **Synthetic/composite slate** (recycled rubber, plastic, or polymer-composite tiles molded to resemble slate): reported service life 40-50 years, notably shorter than natural slate; but with real manufacturer material warranties (50-year limited warranties reported from named brands DaVinci, Brava, EcoStar, unverified as complete list), Class 4 impact rating (the highest hail-impact rating), and installed weight comparable to or less than asphalt shingles, needing no special structural reinforcement. This is a genuine long-life-adjacent option that trades roughly half of natural slate's service life for a fraction of its weight, a real warranty, and easier owner-builder handling.
- Slate (natural or synthetic) only applies to the sloped paths, not the flat PMR deck.

#### 3.5 Tile (concrete and clay)

- **Concrete tile**: reported weight 8-12 lb per square foot; reported wind rating in excess of 150 mph with proper fastening; reported service life 50+ years (some manufacturer claims to 100 years for the tile itself, though underlayment beneath it typically needs replacement on a 20-30 year cycle, which is a real maintenance-access problem: reroofing under tile means lifting and resetting the tile).
- **Clay tile**: reported weight 6-20 lb per square foot depending on profile; reported wind resistance also in excess of 125 mph with proper attachment; historically very long-lived (centuries in some vernacular Mediterranean and Latin American traditions) but the underlayment-replacement problem is the same as concrete tile.
- **Seismic caveat, directly relevant to Oregon's seismic design categories**: heavier tile roofing was observed to correlate with more severe damage in the 1995 Kobe earthquake (a widely cited but not rigorously controlled observation); code response in high-seismic jurisdictions is to require mechanical fastening of every tile rather than mortar-set-only installation in Seismic Design Category D and above, which is squarely this house's territory per the structural chapter's own SDC discussion. A tile roof on this house's structure needs its added dead load and its mechanical-fastening requirement priced into the same engineered design that already governs the concrete walls.
- Tile, like slate, only applies to the sloped paths.

### 4. Drainage

#### 4.1 Slope, tapered insulation, and crickets

- Tapered insulation boards (rigid insulation manufactured or field-cut in a wedge profile) create the roof's slope on a structurally flat concrete deck, standard practice for a PMR assembly; standard tapers reported at 1/8, 1/4, and 1/2 inch per foot.
- A cricket (a small ridge or saddle-shaped tapered-insulation build-up) diverts water around a roof penetration, curb, or parapet-adjacent low spot toward a drain; the general design rule reported is that a cricket's slope should be roughly twice the slope of the adjacent roof field, so water does not pool against the upslope side of any obstruction.
- The tapered system is lowest at every drain, scupper, and edge condition and slopes upward away from them, which means the drain layout has to be finalized before the tapered-insulation shop drawings are cut, which in turn means the drain layout has to be finalized before the structural deck's cast-in sleeves and blockouts are placed. This is a sequencing dependency worth stating plainly in the chapter: drain layout drives tapered-insulation design drives concrete-deck penetration layout, in that order, and doing it out of order means cutting or patching cured concrete.

#### 4.2 Primary and overflow drains, scuppers

- Reported code pattern (IPC-family plumbing codes; exact Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code section numbers need verification): where roof drains are used, overflow (secondary) drains of the same size as the primary drains, or overflow scuppers sized at three times the primary drain size, are required.
- Reported scupper geometry: minimum 4-inch opening height; width tied to the roof drain size that would otherwise serve the same area (via a sizing table); overflow-scupper inlet flow line set low, reported at a maximum of about 2 inches above the low point of the adjacent roof, so it engages quickly if the primary system fails.
- Secondary (overflow) systems are sized for the same design storm as the primary system, using the 100-year, short-duration (commonly 15-minute or 1-hour) rainfall intensity for the site, and the code rule reported is that the primary system's flow is not credited when sizing the secondary system: the secondary system alone must handle the full design storm, on the assumption the primary is completely blocked.
- Oregon-specific rainfall-intensity data point (unverified, needs a direct NOAA Atlas 14 pull for the actual parcel, not a secondary aggregator): one source reports the 5-minute, 100-year rainfall intensity for the Portland area at roughly 2.5 inches per hour. Western Oregon's high annual rainfall (60-100+ inches per year on the coast and west slopes, already noted in the land and feasibility chapter) argues for sizing drainage above the code minimum rather than exactly at it.

#### 4.3 Ponding instability

- Ponding instability is a progressive-deflection failure mode: water pools in a low spot, the extra weight deflects the structure slightly more, the lower point holds more water, and the cycle can continue to structural failure if the framing lacks enough stiffness to resist it. IIBEC and structural-engineering sources describe roof collapses from ponding instability as among the more frequent structural failure types for low-slope roofs generally.
- Three factors govern it: the roof's actual as-built slope, the structural stiffness of the deck and its primary and secondary framing members, and the drainage design (drain count, placement, and how quickly a clog can be detected and cleared).
- ASCE 7 requires susceptible roof bays to be checked by structural analysis for adequate stiffness against progressive deflection, and current design practice folds a "ponding head" allowance into the rain load calculation itself rather than treating ponding as a separate afterthought check. This is a structural engineering calculation, not something an owner-builder computes, but the chapter should explain in plain language why a flat concrete deck's structural design is not finished until the drainage layout and the ponding check both close out, and why a clogged drain is a structural event, not just a maintenance nuisance, on this kind of roof.

#### 4.4 Internal drains vs. scuppers vs. edge gutters

Three drainage-discharge strategies, usable in combination on one roof:

- **Internal (interior) roof drains**: water enters a drain body set into the deck and travels down inside the building through vertical leaders (pipes) to daylight or to a stormwater system. Cleanest look (no exterior downspouts), but the leader piping runs through conditioned or semi-conditioned space, which means a leak inside an interior leader is a hidden, hard-to-find failure, and in a cold-elevation site, an interior leader routed through an unheated chase can freeze.
- **Scuppers through a parapet**: water exits through a wall opening at the roof edge, typically discharging to an exterior leader head and downspout, or free-falling (with a splash guard) at a low building. Keeps all the leader piping outside the thermal envelope, simplifies leak detection (a scupper leak shows immediately at the exterior wall face), but interrupts the parapet's waterproofing and structural continuity at every scupper location, which is itself a junction-detailing problem (see section 6).
- **Edge gutters** (no parapet, a conventional eave condition): the simplest and most easily inspected and maintained system, standard on the sloped truss and sloped-false-roof paths, not applicable to a true flat parapeted deck.
- A real design choice for this house: the concrete-deck-plus-PMR path defaults to a parapet with internal drains or scuppers; whether internal drains (hidden, freeze-risk, harder to inspect) or scuppers (exposed, easier to inspect, more junction penetrations) fit this project's stated preference for owner-inspectable, low-hidden-failure systems is a real decision the chapter should lay out rather than assume.

#### 4.5 Freeze considerations at elevation

The structural chapter already flags "cold-climate sites (mountain elevations, high desert)" as a real site condition for this project. An interior roof drain leader routed through an unconditioned chase or a scupper's exterior leader on a site with sustained sub-freezing weeks is a real freeze-and-burst risk; heat trace cable on the leader, or routing leaders entirely through conditioned space, or defaulting to scuppers with exterior leaders sized generously enough to tolerate some ice, are the three mitigation directions found in general commercial-roofing practice literature. Verification against a low-slope-roof-specific cold-climate source is still needed.

### 5. Overhangs, Gutters, And Protecting The Walls

#### 5.1 Overhang depth and wall-wetting research

- Peer-reviewed wind-driven-rain research (field measurement and CFD studies, both general building-science literature and Building Science Corporation's own digests) consistently finds that roof overhangs measurably reduce wind-driven rain deposition on the upper portion of a facade, with the effect driven mainly by wind speed rather than rainfall intensity itself.
- A frequently cited historical building-science finding: as overhang width decreased across a surveyed building set, the percentage of walls that had experienced moisture damage increased, i.e., overhang depth and wall moisture-damage rate are inversely related in the surveyed data (source: Building Science Corporation digest material, general citation, needs a direct pull of the original digest for the exact figures).
- One Building Science Corporation figure reports that even a 12-inch overhang on a peaked roof captures a large share of the reduction available from any overhang at all, meaning the benefit curve is front-loaded: going from zero overhang to a modest overhang matters more than going from a modest overhang to a very deep one. This is directly useful for balancing overhang depth against the aesthetic and structural cantilever questions the Aesthetics and Architectural Expression research already raised (its own overhang-depth question cites an 18-to-36-inch range for wet climates; this chapter's job is the water-protection mechanism behind that range, and Aesthetics' job is the visual and entry-composition decision built on it).
- For a mass-walled house specifically: reducing direct wall wetting reduces the inward vapor drive and the freeze-thaw cycling load on the exterior masonry or concrete face, which is a durability question squarely inside both this chapter's and Designing for Centuries: Durability Engineering's scope; the two should cross-reference rather than each deriving the mechanism independently.

#### 5.2 Gutters: sizing, material, and protection

- **K-style gutters** (the common U.S. profile with a decorative front face) hold more water per linear foot than half-round gutters of the same nominal size; reported figures: a 5-inch K-style holds roughly 1.2 gallons per foot versus roughly 0.7 gallons per foot for a 5-inch half-round, meaning a half-round system should be sized at least one size larger to carry the same flow.
- **Downspout sizing rule of thumb**: reported at one downspout per roughly 40 feet of gutter run as a floor, with more specific figures elsewhere (a 2x3-inch downspout serving roughly 600 square feet of roof, a 3x4-inch downspout serving roughly 1,200 square feet, both at a 1-inch-per-hour design rainfall); Oregon's higher rainfall intensity argues for sizing above these generic figures, and Water Independence's own rainwater-catchment research already flags conveyance sizing keyed to short-duration, high-return-period intensity rather than the annual average, a point that applies equally to gutters used purely for wall protection with no catchment intent.
- **Material choice and service life**: aluminum (the most common, lightweight, does not rust, wide color range, reported 20-30 year service life with regular cleaning) versus copper and zinc (reported 50-100+ year service life, self-patinating, far higher material cost, "outlast the building" language appears in trade sources for half-round copper and zinc specifically). Given this house's stated long-life goal, the gutter material choice is a real instance of the same replaceable-wearing-layer-vs-permanent-component decision the roof membrane choice already represents, at a much lower cost of entry (gutters are a small fraction of total roof cost) to buy the multi-century-adjacent material.
- **Leaf and debris protection**: relevant on a forested rural Oregon parcel; gutter guards or leaf screens reduce clogging-driven overflow, which is itself a wall-wetting mechanism (a clogged gutter overflows directly down the wall face it was installed to protect). This is a direct problem-solution pairing: gutters without maintenance access or protection can make wall wetting worse than no gutter at all if they clog and are not cleaned.

#### 5.3 Downspout placement and discharge

Downspout discharge location is a foundation-drainage question as much as a roof-drainage question: dumping roof water at the base of the foundation wall defeats the purpose of the gutter system by concentrating water exactly where the foundation-drainage and waterproofing systems (already specified in the foundation chapter) are working hardest. Splash blocks, buried solid-pipe extensions routed away from the foundation, or tie-in to a dedicated stormwater/dry-well/rainwater-catchment system (Water Independence's territory) are the three real options; this chapter's job is to state the roof-side requirement (get the water off the roof and away from the wall face) and point to Water Independence and the foundation-drainage material by topic for what happens to the water after the downspout.

### 6. Roof-Wall Junctions: The Highest-Failure Detail

This is the section the chapter exists to add. Both existing chapters gesture at kick-out flashing and parapet continuity in one or two sentences each; neither explains the mechanism or the failure history.

#### 6.1 Parapets and coping

- A parapet is a wall extending above the roof surface; coping is the cap (metal, stone, or concrete) that sheds water off the top of the parapet and protects the wall assembly's exposed top edge.
- **Coping metal gauge by exposed face dimension** (reported, ANSI-referenced, unverified exact table): up to 4 inches of exposed face, 24 gauge; over 8 to 10 inches, 22 gauge; over 10 to 16 inches, 20 gauge. Most tested coping assemblies use a 4-to-6-inch vertical face; beyond 6 inches, oil-canning (visible waviness) becomes a real cosmetic issue.
- **Coping must move independently of the wall below it.** Coping is detailed with its own expansion joints, using a compressible but extensible joint material, and everywhere the wall itself has an expansion joint, the coping needs a matching joint at the same location. A continuous, rigid coping run over a wall that is designed to move at a control joint is a predictable future failure (a split coping seam, or a coping that pulls its fasteners loose).
- **Wind uplift**: coping and edge metal systems are tested and rated under ANSI/SPRI/FM 4435/ES-1 (a wind-resistance test standard specifically for edge-metal systems securing the perimeter of a low-slope membrane roof); reported wind warranties on tested coping systems run as high as 220 mph. The roof edge and corners see the highest wind pressures on the whole roof, so under-designing the coping is a whole-roof risk, not a cosmetic one: if wind gets under the coping or the edge metal, the failure can progress inward across the membrane.
- **NRCA's own technical guidance identifies flashing-termination failure as a leading cause of parapet-related interior water damage**, which is the connective tissue between this subsection and the barrier-continuity subsection below: a parapet fails less often because the coping metal itself fails and more often because the membrane termination underneath the coping was not detailed to stay continuous through thermal movement and time.

#### 6.2 Kick-out flashing: the real-world failure history

This is worth the chapter treating as a named, historically documented failure, not a generic flashing note, because the Pacific Northwest is the specific region where this failure became a legal and industry watershed.

- **What it is**: a metal diverter installed at the bottom termination of a roof-to-wall intersection (where a sloped roof plane runs into a vertical wall and stops, such as at the low end of a step-flashed valley or a shed-roof-against-wall condition), positioned to kick water that would otherwise run behind the siding or cladding out and away, into the gutter instead.
- **Installation specifics** (IRC-referenced, reported, needs verification against the current adopted edition): step flashing extends at least 4 inches up the wall and at least 4 inches along the roof deck; the kick-out itself is commonly detailed at roughly a 110-degree angle to actually throw water clear of the wall rather than merely redirect it along the wall; where metal, 0.019-inch (26-gauge-equivalent) galvanized steel minimum; the flashing's upper leg tucks behind the wall's water-resistive barrier so that any water reaching the WRB drains down onto the flashing and out, not behind it.
- **The Pacific Northwest EIFS and synthetic-stucco failure history is the canonical real-world case for why this detail matters.** Reported history: the first EIFS class-action lawsuit was filed in North Carolina in 1995; national media investigation followed in 1999; one attorney reportedly won roughly $150 million in claims against EIFS manufacturers; the pattern repeated in the Pacific Northwest specifically because of the region's sustained rainfall. Missing kick-out flashing at roof-wall intersections was repeatedly identified as the single most severe and most common shortcoming found in failed EIFS houses: water concentrated by the roof plane, with no diverter, ran directly behind the cladding at the wall-roof intersection and rotted the wall structure from the inside, sometimes destroying entire wall sections, traced back to (as multiple sources put it) a missing flashing piece costing on the order of $10. Portland-area construction-defect literature describes the same defect pattern (missing kick-out flashing, missing WRB, omitted flashing at critical transitions) as a large and recurring category of water-intrusion construction defect in the Portland metro building boom.
- **Direct relevance to this house**: the structural shell is concrete and masonry, which does not rot, but every place this house's roof plane meets a vertical wall plane at anything other than a full-width parapet condition (a shed-roofed appendage, a lower roof against a taller wall, a dormer-like condition, an entry canopy meeting the main wall) recreates the exact geometry the kick-out-flashing failure history is about, and the covering behind which water would travel (the rainscreen cladding over the exterior insulation, the finish material at the top of the below-grade wall, or interior finishes if water gets far enough) still fails even though the structural wall itself does not rot. The insulation, the air-control layer, and the finish materials at that joint are exactly as vulnerable on a concrete house as on a wood-framed one; only the failure's ultimate structural consequence differs.

#### 6.3 Air and water barrier continuity across the roof-wall transition

- The envelope chapter's own "pencil test" (each control layer must trace as one unbroken line around the entire building section) already names the roof-to-wall transition as one of the specific locations the trace has to survive; this chapter's job is the actual lapping sequence and material-compatibility detail that makes the trace real at this specific joint, not just named as a requirement.
- **Shingle-lap sequencing**: at a roof-to-wall transition, the roof's water-resistive layer (membrane or underlayment) must lap over or under the wall's WRB in the direction water actually flows, so gravity carries water from upper layer to lower layer without an exposed reverse lap anywhere in the sequence; getting this backward at a transition is a common real-world defect even when both individual systems (the roof membrane, the wall WRB) are correctly installed on their own plane.
- **Material compatibility across the transition is a real, product-specific problem, not an assumption.** A self-adhered bituminous membrane (common on roofs and at wall penetrations) is not automatically compatible with every fluid-applied wall air/water barrier; manufacturers publish transition-membrane products (silicone-based transition membranes, purpose-made compatible tapes) specifically to bridge between a fluid-applied wall system and an adjacent roof membrane or dissimilar wall system, and using an incompatible generic tape or sealant at this joint is a documented cause of early adhesion failure. The chapter should treat "confirm transition-membrane compatibility between the specified roof membrane and the specified wall WRB/air barrier, in writing, from both manufacturers" as a design step, not an installation afterthought.
- **This is exactly the joint where two different trades (the roofer and the waterproofing/siding crew) meet**, which is a scheduling and responsibility problem as much as a materials problem: a real, recurring failure mode reported across general low-slope-roofing literature is a roof-wall transition where each trade assumed the other trade's scope included the final lap, and neither installed it. The build-sequence chapter's coordination material and this chapter's technical detail should cross-reference on this point.

#### 6.4 Thermal bridging at the parapet

- A parapet is, structurally, an extension of the wall that sticks up through the roof's insulation plane, which makes it a natural thermal bridge (a path of higher heat conductivity than the surrounding assembly) unless deliberately detailed otherwise; taller parapets are reported to act as more effective heat-loss paths precisely because more of the parapet's surface area sits outside the building's insulated volume.
- **The fix is continuous insulation wrapped up the inside face of the parapet, across the top (under the coping), and down the outside face where feasible**, so the insulation plane does not stop at the roof deck and restart at the wall face with a gap at the parapet itself; energy-code language (2024 IECC and ASHRAE 90.1 pathways, per general industry sources, not yet confirmed against the Oregon-adopted energy code) reportedly requires either this wrapped continuity or a minimum thermal break (commonly cited around R-3) through the parapet structure.
- This connects directly to the Oregon energy-code UA-tradeoff problem the envelope chapter already documents for a concrete roof (no prescriptive table row exists; the compliance path runs through a manual U-factor calculation): a parapet's thermal bridge is exactly the kind of detail that erodes the UA-tradeoff margin the envelope chapter describes buying back through wall and window performance, so the chapter should flag the parapet detail as a real, quantifiable input to that same energy-compliance calculation, not a separate concern.

#### 6.5 Condensation inside the parapet cavity

- The envelope chapter already names "condensation inside the parapet cavity" as a parapet failure mode to detail against, without explaining the mechanism. The mechanism: a parapet cavity (the void behind the coping, inside the parapet wall, above the roof membrane's upturn) sits outside the conditioned building volume; if warm, moist interior or roof-cavity air reaches a cold surface inside that cavity (the underside of an unwrapped coping, an uninsulated parapet backup wall), it condenses, and that moisture cannot be seen or easily inspected without opening the assembly.
- The same continuous-insulation-wrap fix that solves the thermal-bridging problem (section 6.4) is also the condensation fix: keeping every surface inside the parapet cavity above the interior dew point by keeping it thermally connected to the conditioned side of the assembly, rather than letting it float at outdoor temperature. Where full continuity is not achievable (a masonry parapet backup wall with no room for a wrap), a dedicated thermal break under the coping is the fallback, consistent with the energy-code language in 6.4.

#### 6.6 Eave conditions (non-parapet)

On the sloped paths (concrete deck plus sloped false roof, and wood truss on bond beam), the roof-wall junction is an eave, not a parapet, and the envelope chapter already notes parapets are uncommon on the wood-truss assembly. The eave's own failure points: the drip edge (must extend past the fascia and gutter correctly to keep water off the fascia board and out of the soffit), the soffit vent continuity (relevant only to a vented-assembly choice), and the same air-barrier continuity problem as the parapet case, just in a different geometry (the wall's air barrier has to connect to the attic or roof-deck air barrier at the top plate or bond beam, not up and over a parapet). This condition is lower-risk than the parapet/kick-out cases above precisely because it is the most common residential detail in the region and the most standardized in local trade practice; the chapter's eave material can be comparatively brief, with a cross-reference to the parapet section for the underlying continuity principle.

### 7. The Green And Living Roof Buildup

Draft G ("What Concrete Uniquely Enables") already researched this ground in real depth: extensive-vs-intensive load figures, the FLL-certified root-resistant membrane option, the layer order (membrane, root barrier, drainage layer, filter fabric, growing medium, vegetation), leak-detection systems for buried membranes, and documented problems (hidden leaks, root penetration at flashings, drain clogging by soil and roots). This chapter should point to that material by topic rather than restate it. What follows is complementary material G's pass did not cover: Oregon-specific program detail, dry-summer plant selection, wind uplift on loose-laid systems, and a vernacular alternative.

- **Portland's Ecoroof program is a real, Oregon-specific green-roof design benchmark**, even though this house's rural parcel is not subject to a city green-roof mandate. Reported requirements for Portland's mandatory-tier buildings: minimum 4 inches of growing media; at least 50% evergreen vegetation; 90% vegetation coverage achieved within 2 years of planting; the roof required to reduce annual stormwater runoff volume by 50%; a structural engineer's certification required for approval. Portland's Bureau of Environmental Services also reportedly publishes a specific recommended plant list for the region's climate, which is a real, usable, climate-matched design resource distinct from generic green-roof plant guidance. A worthwhile research thread: whether the county the parcel sits in has any stormwater credit or incentive that a green roof (or any other on-site retention) could capture, independent of Portland's own program.
- **Dry-summer plant selection.** Willamette Valley and most of western Oregon share the Mediterranean-climate pattern (wet winter, dry summer) that the extensive-green-roof research literature treats as its own design case, distinct from a temperate climate with summer rain. Sedum species (a genus using CAM photosynthesis, which lets the plant close its stomata during the day and minimize water loss) dominate unirrigated extensive-roof plant lists specifically because of this drought tolerance; species-level research from Mediterranean-climate trials (Sedum sediforme, Drosanthemum floribundum, Lampranthus spectabilis in one cited trial) found some species significantly outperform others in shallow, unirrigated substrate, which argues for a climate-matched species list rather than a generic "sedum blend" purchase.
- **Wind uplift on loose-laid vegetative and ballasted systems is a real, standard-governed design question**, not just a structural dead-load question: ANSI/SPRI RP-14 (Wind Design Standard for Vegetative Roofing Systems) and ANSI/SPRI RP-4 (the equivalent standard for aggregate- and paver-ballasted single-ply systems) both exist specifically because loose growing medium or loose ballast can itself become windborne debris in a high-wind event if underdesigned, distinct from the membrane-uplift question. On a coastal or exposed Oregon site, this is a real design input for a green roof or a plain ballasted PMR roof alike, not an incidental detail.
- **A vernacular alternative worth naming for a small, secondary structure rather than the main house roof**: the traditional Scandinavian sod/turf roof used stacked birch bark (reported roughly a 30-year service life per layer before renewal) as its waterproofing layer beneath the turf, on a sloped timber roof, with modern equivalents substituting a rubber or synthetic membrane for the bark while keeping the same soil-and-turf buildup above. This is not a fit for the main house's concrete-deck PMR system, which already has a proven, code-recognized waterproofing and drainage buildup, but it is a real, low-cost, low-technology option worth having on record for a well house, root cellar, or other small outbuilding roof where a rustic, self-sourced buildup is more in keeping with the structure than an engineered membrane roof.

### 8. Penetrations And Rooftop Equipment

#### 8.1 Solar mounts

Three real mounting strategies, not interchangeable, and the right one depends on the weather surface underneath:

- **Ballasted, non-penetrating racks** on a flat PMR deck: the rack sits on the finished roof surface, held down by dead weight (concrete blocks or a similar ballast) rather than fasteners through the membrane. No roof penetrations at all, which preserves the membrane's warranty and service life exactly as the envelope chapter already notes; the added dead load (rack plus ballast plus panels plus snow) is a real, quantifiable structural input that has to enter the roof's live/dead load design, not an afterthought bolted on after occupancy. Wind uplift on a ballasted PV array follows the same design-standard family (ANSI/SPRI RP-4-adjacent wind engineering) as ballasted membrane roofing generally.
- **Penetrating, standoff-mounted racks**: fasteners go through the roof surface into the structure, each penetration needing its own flashed boot or curb detail; more common on the sloped truss and standing-seam paths, and on a standing-seam metal roof, this is where the specific product category below (clamp-based mounts) usually replaces true penetrating fasteners.
- **Clamp-based, non-penetrating mounts on standing-seam metal** (the S-5! product family is the commonly cited example, unverified as the only option): a clamp grips the standing seam itself, mechanically, with no hole through the panel at all, preserving the panel's warranty exactly as a ballasted rack preserves a membrane's warranty on a flat roof. Reported holding capacities vary widely by clamp model and seam profile (roughly 300 to 4,000+ lb depending on load direction and model), and the same clamp product family is also used for snow-guard and safety-anchor attachment on standing-seam roofs, which is a real efficiency: one attachment technology serving solar, snow retention, and fall-protection anchoring on the same roof.
- The chapter should lay these three out as a direct decision table keyed to which weather surface is chosen (ballasted for PMR; clamp-based for standing seam; true penetrating only where the other two are not applicable), rather than treating "solar mount" as one generic category, and should cross-reference Solar, Battery, and Energy Independence for the electrical and array-sizing side of the same decision.

#### 8.2 Vents and stacks

Every plumbing vent, exhaust vent, or other small penetration through the roof plane needs a flashing boot matched to the specific membrane or panel material (an EPDM boot on an EPDM roof, a TPO-compatible boot on a TPO roof, and so on); using a generic or mismatched boot material is a documented, avoidable leak source. On the PMR path, every penetration boot sits under the same protective ballast and insulation layer as the field membrane, which is a real advantage: the boot gets the same UV and impact protection the membrane does, unlike an exposed membrane roof where the boot is often the first thing to fail because it is thinner and more stressed than the field sheet around it.

#### 8.3 Curbs

Any larger rooftop equipment (an HVAC condenser or heat-pump outdoor unit if roof-mounted, a skylight, an access hatch) sits on a raised curb rather than flush on the deck, specifically so its flashing can achieve real vertical height above the roof's waterline. NRCA guidance reportedly recommends flashing heights of at least 8 inches above the finished roof surface generally, and 12-16 inches in high-precipitation regions, which describes most of western Oregon; curb flashing follows the same vertical-flashing-plus-counterflashing logic as the parapet detail in section 6.1. Matching the curb's flashing material to the field membrane, exactly as with vent boots, is the same real, avoidable-failure-if-skipped point.

#### 8.4 Walkway pads and maintenance access

A flat PMR or ballasted roof that will be walked on regularly for PV maintenance, drain clearing, or equipment service benefits from dedicated walkway pads (puncture-resistant mats, loose-laid, adhered, or heat-welded, matched to the membrane type) along the actual travel path from the roof hatch or access point to the equipment. The real mechanism this protects against is point loading: a person's weight concentrated through a boot heel or a dropped tool bag stresses the membrane locally far more than the same weight spread over a panel or ballast field, and repeated foot traffic without a defined, protected path is a documented cause of premature membrane wear concentrated along the informal route people actually walk. This is a low-cost, easy-to-install detail (loose-laid pads) that directly extends the service life of the expensive layer underneath it, worth a specific mention rather than folding into general roof maintenance advice.

### 9. Wind, Snow, And Seismic Loading Specific To This Project

- **Coastal and exposed-site wind**: Oregon's ocean-exposure areas are reported in wind Exposure Category D, with basic design wind speeds in the 125-145 mph range depending on risk category (unverified, needs primary-source ORSC/OSSC confirmation for the actual parcel), and reported parapet-height minimums increase by roughly 8 inches over Exposure C conditions, with a 12-inch floor. Corner and edge zones of any roof see the highest wind pressure on the whole roof, which is exactly where the coping and edge-metal detailing in section 6.1 matters most.
- **Localized wind speed-up**: general Pacific Northwest wind-engineering literature flags "special wind regions" (gorges, ridgelines, exposed passes) where actual wind speeds can exceed the mapped basic wind speed for the surrounding area; a site in the Columbia Gorge or on an exposed ridge needs this checked specifically, not assumed from the county-level map.
- **Snow guards on the standing-seam path**: a metal roof sheds snow as a sheet rather than holding it (unlike shingle or tile roofs), which is a real hazard to anything below the eave (a walkway, an entry, a parked vehicle, a person) and to the gutter system itself (a sliding snow sheet can tear a gutter off). Snow-guard spacing depends on roof pitch, roof length, and ground snow load, with manufacturer spacing tables typically valid up to about 45 psf ground snow load and requiring engineered layout above that; multiple staggered rows, not a single row at the eave, are the standard approach for any roof long enough to accumulate a full sheet of sliding snow. This is directly relevant wherever this house's site has meaningful snow load, and is a real, cheap, install-time-only detail (retrofitting snow guards after the fact means working on a finished, occupied roof).
- **Seismic and tile**: already covered in section 3.5; the mechanical-fastening requirement in Seismic Design Category D and above is squarely this project's territory per the structural chapter's own SDC discussion.

### 10. Biological Growth: Moss And Algae (Oregon-Specific)

- Western Oregon's combination of sustained cool-season moisture, frequent rain, and heavy tree canopy (shade) is reported as close to ideal moss- and algae-growth conditions, and moss on roofs is reported as one of the most common roofing complaints in the region specifically.
- **Zinc and copper strips near the ridge** are a widely marketed prevention method: rain contacts the metal strip and carries metal ions down the roof surface, which reportedly interferes with moss and algae cell structure over time. Reported effective life for a zinc strip is roughly 5-8 years before it needs renewal; copper is reported to last somewhat longer.
- **A real, sourced counterpoint exists and should not be omitted**: Oregon State University's own extension guidance (a primary regional source, not a vendor) is reported to state that metallic zinc or copper strip products are not effective at eliminating moss, because moss does not grow on the strip itself and the metal-ion runoff is diluted and washed past most of the roof surface rather than treating it. The chapter should present both the vendor claim and the OSU counterpoint honestly, as a genuinely contested point rather than picking one side, and should verify the actual OSU Extension moss guidance directly (it is a freely published resource) before writing the manuscript section.
- Roof material choice interacts with moss risk: a smooth, low-friction surface (metal) sheds spores and organic debris more readily than a rough or porous surface (concrete tile, asphalt shingle, an unballasted membrane with exposed granulation); this is a real, material-specific reason (beyond service life and cost) to prefer standing-seam metal on the sloped paths of a heavily treed rural parcel, worth stating as a design factor in its own right.
- Regular mechanical cleaning (soft-wash, not high-pressure, which can strip granules or damage seams and coatings) and keeping overhanging tree limbs cut back are the two non-product interventions repeatedly cited alongside or instead of metal strips.

### 11. Maintenance, Inspection, And Safety

- **Inspection frequency**: NRCA reportedly recommends at least twice-yearly roof inspection (spring and fall) for a low-slope membrane roof, with monthly inspection in the first year after any roofing work, and inspection after any major weather event.
- **Ponding as a maintenance trigger, not just a design defect**: standing water remaining more than roughly 48 hours after rainfall is flagged in commercial-roofing maintenance literature as a threshold requiring investigation (a clogged drain, settled ballast, or a genuine structural deflection), because prolonged ponding is reported to reduce membrane service life by as much as 50% through sustained thermal cycling and biological growth at the ponded area.
- **Recoating and restoration as a real, lower-cost alternative to full replacement**: single-ply and modified-bitumen membranes in generally sound condition (no widespread cracking, thinning, or loss of flexibility) are reported to commonly qualify for a recoat or restoration treatment around year 15-20 of service, which can defer full replacement; this is directly relevant to this house's stated preference for maintaining and extending components rather than defaulting to replacement.
- **General low-slope roof service-life data point, worth stating as context**: industry sources report an average low-slope membrane roof service life around 17 years under ordinary (not diligent) maintenance, against 30+ years achievable with correct design, installation, and maintenance discipline, and routine inspection plus preventive repair is reported to potentially double effective service life while cutting annual maintenance cost by up to half. This is a strong, concrete argument for building a real inspection habit into this house's maintenance documentation (Documentation, Maintenance, and Future Upgrades territory) rather than treating the roof as install-and-forget.
- **Owner-builder roof safety**: any owner-builder performing their own roof work, maintenance, or PV servicing on a low-slope or metal roof needs real fall protection, not informal caution. Standing-seam-specific roof anchors exist that clamp to the seam itself (the same clamp technology as the solar-mount clamps in section 8.1) without penetrating the panel, rated for a minimum 5,000 lb static load per general fall-protection standards; a full-body harness connected to an anchor by a lanyard or self-retracting lifeline is the minimum system for any low-slope or metal-roof work above a fall hazard, and warning-line systems (marking a safe zone at least 6 feet from any roof edge) are the relevant control for a flat PMR deck. This is real, load-bearing content for the chapter's practical/Applied register and for The Owner-Builder's Body and Mind, which should cross-reference each other on this point rather than each deriving fall-protection basics independently.

### 12. Creative And Vernacular Roof Ideas Worldwide

- **The Scandinavian turf roof** (section 7) is the strongest worldwide vernacular precedent found for this chapter specifically (as distinct from the general green-roof material draft G already covers), because its historic waterproofing material (birch bark) is itself a real, low-technology, owner-sourceable alternative to a manufactured membrane, worth naming for a small secondary structure even if not used on the main house.
- **Thin-shell, vaulted, and domed concrete roof forms** (Nubian-vault-style corbelled masonry vaults, CSEB free-spanning vaults, ferrocement shells, hyperbolic-paraboloid thin shells, monolithic Airform-and-shotcrete domes) are already researched in depth under What Concrete Uniquely Enables: Designing With Mass; this chapter's own, distinct angle on the same forms, not yet covered there, is the roof-specific waterproofing and drainage question once a curved or domed form is chosen: a dome or vault sheds water very differently from a flat or single-pitched plane (there is no straightforward "parapet" or "eave" edge condition on a true dome), and the waterproofing detailing at a dome's base ring or a vault's end walls is a genuinely open, roof-system-specific question this chapter should own even though the structural form itself belongs to the other chapter.
- **DIY standing-seam installation as an owner-builder skill question**: snap-lock standing-seam panels (the panel's male and female edges lock together over a clip by hand, with only a hand crimper needed for the end details) are a materially different, much more owner-builder-accessible installation method than traditional field-seamed standing seam (which needs a powered mechanical seamer to close the seam along the full panel length). This is a real, concrete decision point for a mostly-solo owner-builder deciding whether to self-perform the roof: snap-lock trades a small amount of long-term performance margin (a mechanically field-seamed seam is generally considered the more robust, higher-wind-rated joint) for a roof an individual can plausibly install without renting or operating a power seamer.
- **Owner-fabricated ferrocement roof elements**: the same ferrocement technique already researched in depth for cistern construction under Water Independence (a wire-mesh or expanded-metal armature hand-troweled with cement mortar) is a documented technique for small curved roof shells (garden structures, a well house, a small outbuilding) in vernacular and self-build literature worldwide; this is a genuine cross-reference rather than a separate research thread, since the skill, tools, and material are identical to the cistern-construction case.
