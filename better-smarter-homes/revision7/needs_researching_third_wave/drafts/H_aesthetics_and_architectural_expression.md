## Part 1: Gathered Understanding

### 1. Concrete Surface Expression

**Board-formed concrete.** The wood grain and board layout of the formwork transfers in reverse onto the cured concrete face. Reported working practice from trade sources (Fine Homebuilding/JLC, Life of an Architect):

- Douglas fir and cedar both transfer strong grain; ponderosa pine and some other softwoods transfer less pattern. Rough-sawn boards give crisper, more visible grain than surfaced (planed) boards. Board width and orientation (horizontal vs. vertical courses) set the visual scale and can read taller/narrower or wider/calmer.
- A concrete mix reported for board-form work: roughly a 5-sack mix (high cement content relative to standard residential mixes) at about a 5-inch slump, to pattern the wood grain without honeycombing. Cross-reference this against the mix-design targets already in Structural System and Concrete Design (section 6.13) before writing any specific number; this is a trade-source figure, not a verified spec.
- Form ties: fiberglass ties (gray, blend into the surface, snap flush) are reported as preferred over steel ties, which can rust-stain the face over time.
- A silicone bead at board-to-board joints controls paste bleed-through and keeps board lines crisp. Over-vibrating the pour is reported to bring paste to the surface unevenly and blur the grain pattern; the goal is a high paste percentage at the surface without over-working it.
- A full-size freestanding mockup panel, built and stripped before the real pour, is the reported standard way to catch problems (grain too faint, tie pattern wrong, color blotching) before they are permanent on the house.
- Corners and window returns are the reported hardest detail: the grain direction and board joint pattern have to be planned through the corner, not solved after the fact.

**Form liners (manufactured textured formwork).** Elastomeric-urethane, ABS, or HIPS plastic liners that impose a repeatable texture (stone, fractured stone, wood-grain, geometric/rhythmic, masonry-coursing patterns) onto cast concrete. Manufacturers found: Fitzgerald Formliners, Architectural Polymers, Spec Formliners, Scott System, Greenstreak, Sika. Catalogs commonly list 75+ standard patterns in panels around 4x8 or 4x10 feet. Reusable elastomeric liners are rated for roughly 100 uses by one manufacturer. Reported cost: liner material alone roughly $12 to $120+ per square foot depending on pattern depth/complexity and reuse count; one data point of $22-24/sqft liner plus $12/sqft framework labor (year and region unstated; needs a current quote). This is a manufactured-product route, distinct from board-forming, and is the route to a repeatable stone or masonry-coursing texture without carving individual forms by hand.

**Exposed aggregate.** Two distinct techniques found:

1. **Surface retarder plus wash.** A chemical retarder is applied to the form face (so it contacts the concrete at strip time) or to the fresh slab surface. It delays cement-paste hardening at that surface only, to a depth set by the retarder's strength/concentration. After roughly 12-24 hours, the retarded paste is washed off (pressure washer, moderated to avoid gouging), exposing the aggregate underneath without touching the aggregate itself. Reported as the most uniform, least labor-intensive route, and safer than acid etching or abrasive blasting.
2. **Seeding.** Selected aggregate (a specific color/size stone chosen for the look) is broadcast onto the fresh concrete surface and troweled in, then exposed later by the same retarder-and-wash step or by light abrasive work. This is the route to a face aggregate different from the aggregate in the structural mix, i.e., choosing a decorative stone for the visible skin only.

Exposed aggregate is a real option for the outer face of a board-formed or form-lined wall, for a garden/site-wall accent, or for a wear floor, and it is a documented use for owner-made dry-cast block face mixes (a decorative face-mix layer with different aggregate placed at the mold face before or with the structural mix; needs its own research to confirm feasibility for a solo block-press operation).

**Mechanical texturing (post-cure).**

- **Bush-hammering.** A tool with a serrated, hardened-steel face (rows of points), manual or powered, that impacts the cured concrete or stone surface, breaking off the top paste layer and exposing the aggregate underneath as a rough, pitted, pocked texture. Reported as flexible between light cosmetic texturing and aggressive surface removal, usable wet or dry, low-dust compared to blasting. One older patent-era source claims bush-hammering historically cost 4-5x sandblasting; this figure is dated and unverified and needs a current Oregon contractor quote.
- **Sandblasting.** Compressed air propels abrasive media at the surface, removing the paste layer to expose aggregate or to strip coatings/contamination. Reported as faster and more uniform over large areas than hand bush-hammering, but dust-heavy and requiring containment and PPE.
- Both techniques work on cured concrete, masonry, and (for bush-hammering specifically) natural stone, so both are candidates for texturing owner-made block faces after demolding and curing, not only for texturing a cast-in-place wall.

**Integral color (pigment mixed into the concrete or mortar).**

- Iron oxide pigments are the standard exterior choice: UV-stable and alkali-stable, meaning the color does not fade or leach under sun and cement chemistry over years. Carbon black pigments are reported UV- and alkali-unstable outdoors (can fade or leach) and are not recommended for an exterior application; this duplicates and cross-references sibling C's mortar-pigment finding (its question 18), which covers the same chemistry for mortar specifically.
- Manufacturer dosage guidance (Davis Colors, one named source): roughly 1 to 5 lb of pigment per 94-lb sack of cement for standard color depth, scaling up for darker colors; specific rates found: light gray about 2.5-3 lb/sack, dark gray about 5-6 lb/sack, graphite/near-black about 10-12 lb/sack (powder vs. liquid dose rates differ slightly). A maximum dosage around 10 percent of cement weight is cited as an upper bound (higher dosing can affect strength and set time).
- Pigment cost figures found were inconsistent across sources: one source states $0.60-0.82 added cost per cubic foot of mix; another cites roughly $16.50 per cubic yard at a 1 percent loading; a third gives retail iron oxide pigment at $3.50-6.00 per kg, which would add roughly $61-105 per cubic meter at a 5 percent dosage. These do not reconcile cleanly and need a single current (2026) supplier quote for the actual project quantities before any number is written into the manuscript.
- Batch-to-batch consistency is a real, named risk: pigment must be dosed by weight, not by scoop or volume, and mixing time/sequence must be repeatable, or sequential pours (or sequential owner-made block runs) show visible color banding or streaking between batches. This is a bigger risk for this project than for a single contractor pour, because the project's own stated plan is around 100 owner-made units a day over an extended production run, i.e., many small batches over months, not one large integrally-colored pour.
- White Portland cement (roughly 2-2.5x the cost per ton of ordinary gray cement in one 2026-ish source: about $250-300/ton white vs. $100-120/ton gray) is the base needed for true light or pastel integral colors, because gray cement's own color otherwise muddies a light pigment. Structural strength is reported comparable between white and gray cement.

**Applied color (stains, dyes, limewash).**

- **Acid stain.** A chemical reaction with the free lime in cured concrete, penetrating roughly 1/16 inch and permanently altering the surface mineralogy rather than sitting on top of it. Produces mottled, variegated results, limited to earth tones (rust, brown, amber, tan) plus a narrower range of blues/greens from other metallic salts. Requires neutralizing and rinsing the acid after the reaction, then sealing. Results are reported as genuinely unpredictable (dependent on the specific slab's age, porosity, calcium content, and curing history), so a sample panel poured from the actual project mix is necessary, not a chip from an unrelated slab. Reported cost around $6/sqft (material plus labor, unsourced year). UV-stable and does not peel once sealed, unlike some coatings.
- **Water-based stain or dye.** Pigment carried in a water, acrylic, or (for dyes specifically) acetone vehicle. Produces a much wider color range than acid stain (not limited to earth tones) and a more even, predictable, paint-like result, generally easier for a first-time owner-builder to apply. Reported weakness: dyes especially are not inherently UV-stable and can fade within months outdoors without a UV-stable topcoat/sealer, making acid stain or integral pigment the more durable exterior choice and water-based stain/dye better suited to interior floors. Application technique differs from acid stain: a bone-dry slab can cause the stain to flash-dry unevenly, so the surface is often pre-dampened. Reported cost around $2/sqft for solid-color water-based stain (unsourced year).
- **Limewash or pigmented plaster over masonry** is a distinct, much older technique found in the Luis Barragán research (see Regional and Worldwide Precedent below): pigment carried in a lime or cement plaster applied over a masonry substrate, rather than staining the masonry itself. This is a real option for a painted-look masonry wall that is not bare block or brick, worth flagging separately from stain/dye because the substrate is covered, not colored in place.

**Polished and ground concrete.**

- A multi-step diamond grinding sequence, working from coarse metal-bond diamond grits through progressively finer resin-bond grits (reported progression roughly 30-50 grit up through 800-3000 grit for a high-gloss result), with a chemical densifier (commonly a lithium silicate solution) applied partway through to harden and seal the surface from within.
- The degree of aggregate exposure is a specification choice, not an accident: a "cream" finish grinds only the surface paste (no aggregate visible), a "salt-and-pepper" finish exposes fine aggregate lightly, and a full aggregate exposure grinds deeper into the coarse aggregate for a terrazzo-like look. Depth of grind interacts directly with embedded radiant tubing cover depth; the Interiors chapter (section 14.7) already covers that coordination in code-referenced detail and should not be duplicated here.
- Reported cost range is wide across sources: $3-18/sqft overall, with $7-12/sqft cited as the typical quality-residential range (2026, source region unstated).
- One caution surfaced in the research: "CSP" (Concrete Surface Profile, an ICRI Guideline 310.2 scale of 1 to 10) is a coatings-adhesion surface-roughness standard, not the same thing as the polishing grit sequence. Several casual sources conflate the two. This needs to be kept separate and verified before any manuscript text cites "CSP" in a polishing context.

**Off-form / smooth / rubbed finishes.** A smooth, low-void ("fair-face") result comes from new or well-maintained formwork (plywood or steel), a consistent release agent, and careful consolidation, without any post-cure texturing step. A traditional hand finishing method, rubbing the stripped surface with a carborundum stone and a cement-paste grout to fill small surface voids, is a documented way to clean up a smooth-form wall after stripping without changing its basic flat character. This is the finish family Tadao Ando's work (below) is built on: a smooth, "silk," off-form surface from well-prepared formwork rather than any applied treatment.

**Surface defects (quality problems specific to appearance, not structure).** Bugholes (small surface cavities from trapped air against the form face), honeycombing (larger voids from poor consolidation or mix segregation), cold joints (a visible line where one placement's surface had already begun to set before the next lift was placed), and color blotching (uneven pigment distribution or inconsistent curing/moisture conditions across a face) are the most commonly cited defects specific to architectural (appearance-critical) concrete work, as distinct from a structural defect. Reported prevention: smooth, clean, properly-released forms; correct vibration (enough to consolidate, not so much that it works paste unevenly to the surface); continuous placement to avoid cold joints; and use of a set-matched repair mortar where a patch is unavoidable, since patches are reported as very difficult to color/texture-match invisibly.

**Quality-control systems and reference standards found, all real and citable, none yet verified against the project's own code library:**

- **ACI PRC-303-25** (superseding ACI 303R-12 as of November 2025): the main US guide to cast-in-place architectural concrete practice, covering materials, forming, placing, curing, treated surfaces, finishing, and inspection specifically for concrete that will be exposed to view. This is the primary standard to verify and cite for a Code layer on cast-in-place architectural concrete.
- **ACI 117**, Specification for Tolerances for Concrete Construction and Materials: sets dimensional and surface tolerances (flatness, levelness, alignment) that apply to exposed and architectural concrete specifically, in addition to ordinary structural tolerances.
- **ASTM C979/C979M**: standard specification for pigments used in integrally colored concrete (powder pigments as admixtures, including effects on set time, air content, and compressive strength).
- **Sichtbeton (German Concrete Association, DBV "Merkblatt Sichtbeton")**: a four-tier exposed-concrete quality classification, SB1 (low requirement, utility spaces) through SB4 (highest requirement, signature architectural surfaces), each tier specifying allowable bughole size and density, color evenness, formwork panel/joint layout, and tolerances. This is a German guideline, not adopted in Oregon or the US, but it is a real, transferable classification concept: an owner-builder writing a one-page specification for "what does an acceptable board-formed wall look like" could adapt the SB-tier logic as an unofficial in-house standard without citing it as code.
- **Sample/mockup panel practice**: industry practice runs from small pre-bid samples (roughly 12x12x2 inches, to establish general color/texture direction) up to a full production mockup (commonly around 4x4 feet by 6 inches thick), built and approved before the real work starts, then left standing on site as the accepted reference standard for the rest of the job. For a solo owner-builder, building a small sample panel outdoors in the actual planned material and technique, and observing it through at least one wet Oregon season before committing an entire elevation to it, is a direct, low-cost, high-value transfer of this practice.

### 2. Brick And Block As Visual Material

**Bond patterns (brick).** Running/stretcher bond (all stretchers, each course offset half a brick, requires metal ties rather than headers to tie wythes together, the most common modern pattern and the default in most veneer and cavity-wall work); Flemish bond (alternating header-stretcher-header within each course, historically ties two solid wythes together across their thickness, produces a checkerboard face pattern); English bond (alternating full courses of stretchers and full courses of headers); common/American bond (running bond with a full header course inserted every fifth to seventh course, widespread in US masonry roughly 1850-1920); herringbone (bricks at 45 or 90 degrees in a zigzag, more common as a paving or accent panel than a full structural wall); basketweave (paired bricks alternating horizontal/vertical, mainly a paving or accent pattern). In a modern insulated cavity wall (this project's own wall type), true structural headers cannot reach across the cavity to the inner CMU wythe, so any header-pattern look (Flemish, English, common bond) on the outer brick wythe has to be built with half-bricks ("blind headers," cut to show a header face without physically penetrating the cavity); the tie system (already specified in Structural System and Concrete Design, section 6.7) does the real structural connection instead. This is a direct, concrete interaction between an aesthetic choice (which bond pattern) and an already-written structural chapter, worth a specific cross-reference.

**Bond patterns (CMU).** Running bond is the structural default and the strongest pattern (interlocking joints). Stack bond (all head joints aligned vertically, a grid look rather than a running pattern) removes that interlock and is reported to require added horizontal joint reinforcement by code where used (one source's figure: minimum horizontal reinforcement area equal to 0.00028 times the gross vertical cross-sectional area of the wall; unverified, needs checking directly against ORSC R606 / TMS 402, which Structural System and Concrete Design already cites for other masonry reinforcement rules). Stack bond is a real, code-relevant example of a pure look decision (the grid pattern) carrying a real structural cost (added steel), worth flagging precisely because it is not obvious from the outside.

**Mortar joint profile as a design element, and its water performance in a wet climate.** Concave (the mortar tooled into a smooth inward curve, compresses and densifies the joint surface, generally reported as the best-performing profile for shedding water, and the most common modern profile); raked (mortar recessed back from the brick face, throws a strong shadow line and makes the brick read in sharper relief, but is reported to shed water worse because it creates a ledge where water can sit); struck (sloped upward, rarely recommended on an exterior wall because the lower edge is a place for water to collect and enter); weathered (sloped downward, sheds water well, a traditional profile); grapevine (a concave profile with a decorative center groove tool run down it, a colonial-American detail, now rare/specialty); V-joint (a crisp centered V-groove, a middle option between concave's water performance and raked's shadow drama). For a wet Pacific Northwest climate specifically, concave or weathered are the profiles the water-performance literature favors; choosing raked or struck for the shadow look is a real durability trade-off that should be stated as a trade-off, not hidden.

**Mortar and joint color.** The same iron-oxide pigment family used for integral concrete color (ASTM C979-type material) is also used to tint mortar; sibling C's draft already covers the mixing chemistry and dosage in detail and is the chapter target for that procedure. The design fact this file adds: joint color changes how a whole wall reads more than most people expect. Mortar closely matched to the unit color minimizes the visible grid and makes the wall read as one continuous mass; mortar strongly contrasting with the unit color emphasizes every joint and the coursing pattern becomes the dominant visual feature. This is a real, statable, zero-added-cost design lever (choosing a mortar sand/pigment already being purchased) rather than a material choice with its own price tag.

**Brick color and texture, and how "range" is actually specified.** ASTM C216 (Standard Specification for Facing Brick) defines three appearance grades that directly answer "how much color and texture variation is normal": Type FBX (narrow color range, high mechanical/dimensional perfection, defects checked at a 15-foot viewing distance, for a controlled, uniform look); Type FBS (wider color and size range, general use, checked at 20 feet, the common general-purpose grade); Type FBA (nonuniform, wide variation by design, no size or color limits, explicitly intended "to produce characteristics of older brick," i.e., the grade to specify for an intentionally irregular, handmade-looking wall). This is a real, citable, three-tier system that maps directly onto the brief's "color range, unit texture" question and gives the project language to specify exactly the degree of uniformity wanted when ordering brick. Regional Pacific Northwest manufacturers found: Mutual Materials and Interstate Brick, both offering many named colors/blends (examples found: Canyon Mist, Cascade Spice, Homestead Used, Seattle Cream) and named textures (matte/smooth-lightly-pitted; antique/highly-textured-rough-ridges; ruff/deep-wide-combed-ridge), plus special-order custom colors and sizes. This is a real regional sourcing lead for an Oregon build specifically.

**Decorative brick coursing at transitions.** Soldier course (units on end, narrow face out, commonly used at lintels over openings and as a decorative border/cap course); rowlock course (units on edge, narrow face out, commonly used at sloped sills under windows because the orientation sheds water); sailor course (units on end, wide face out); water table (a coursing or material change marking the transition from foundation to above-grade wall, historically often expressed with a projecting or rowlock course). This vocabulary applies directly to the existing "brick shelf" foundation detail already named in The Wall System skeleton (section 8.9) and is a place where a required structural transition (the foundation ledge that carries the brick wythe) can also be treated as a visible design line, not just a hidden structural detail.

**Corner treatment.** Quoins: contrasting units (different size, color, texture, or projection from the field material) at the outside corners of a wall, historically both a structural reinforcement for weak rubble corners and a purely decorative accent; producible in brick, in a distinct CMU unit, or in a cast concrete unit at the outside corners of this project's double-wythe wall.

**Architectural (decorative) CMU finish families, beyond plain gray block.**

- **Split-face:** two units cast joined and mechanically fractured apart after curing, exposing the internal aggregate in a rough, stone-like broken texture on one face. Reported cost roughly $3.50-4.50 per standard 8x8x16 block, roughly $10-15/sqft installed for an interior block wall (2026-ish, region unstated).
- **Ground-face (burnished):** the cast face is ground smooth after curing, exposing internal aggregate in a polished, terrazzo-like texture, usually finished with a light sealer for sheen and protection during handling. Reported cost roughly $6.50-9.00 per standard block, typically special-order.
- **Slump block:** a wetter, higher-slump mix that visibly sags and rounds at the edges when stripped from the mold, deliberately mimicking hand-formed adobe. Unit width can vary as much as about 1 inch block to block by design, associated historically with Southwest US Spanish-colonial/adobe-revival architecture, and it requires accepting (not fighting) that dimensional variability at the detailing stage. This is directly analogous to the question this project already faces with its own owner-made block: whether a hand-cast, slightly irregular unit should be disguised to look factory-uniform, or whether the irregularity should be treated as the intended look, the way slump block treats it.
- **Integral color CMU:** reported as a modest per-unit premium over plain gray in most manufacturer literature, with natural gray typically the no-charge stock color and every other integral color typically special-order, often with a minimum quantity and a mold setup charge. Exact current dollar figures were inconsistent across sources and need a 2026 regional supplier quote.

**How the double-wythe wall reads from outside.** The project's own wall assembly (inner CMU wythe, insulated cavity, outer brick wythe, tied together, per Structural System and Concrete Design section 6.7 and The Wall System skeleton) is, from the street, simply a brick building; the inner CMU wythe and the cavity are invisible once built. The visual decisions that are actually available at that outer brick face are: which bond pattern, which brick color/texture grade (per ASTM C216 above), which mortar joint profile and color, how the coursing lands at window heads/sills/datum lines (aligning coursing to openings so no course lands as an awkward sliver is reported as a basic rule for a clean street-read elevation), how the wall terminates at the foundation (the water-table vocabulary above), how it terminates at the roofline (parapet/coping, covered below under Composition), and how corners are treated (quoins, or a plain mitered brick corner). None of this changes the structural tie-and-cavity design already specified; it is entirely a finish-layer decision sitting on top of an already-engineered assembly, which is a useful framing for keeping this chapter out of the structural chapter's territory.

### 3. Architectural Composition

**Massing and proportion, and the specific mechanics of avoiding a "bunker" look.** Two basic moves recur across the design-guideline sources found: **addition** (attaching subordinate volumes to a main mass: entry projections, wings, dormers, bays) and **subtraction** (carving into a main mass: recessed entries, courtyards, terraces, light wells). A large simple volume reads as oppressive specifically when it has no subordinate parts; breaking it into one clearly primary mass plus smaller, clearly secondary masses (a **hierarchy**, not several equal-weight blocks competing) is the reported fix. **Articulation** (expressing a volume's parts through a material change, a plane offset, or a shadow line, sized to be legible to a person standing at the building, not just visible in a drawing) is the specific technique named for keeping a large mass from reading as one undifferentiated block. This is the direct, mechanism-level answer to the brief's "bunker/blank look" concern: it is not a vague call for "interest," it is a specific set of two moves (addition, subtraction) plus one organizing principle (hierarchy) plus one execution technique (articulation at human scale).

**Proportion systems.** The golden ratio (phi, approximately 1.618:1) is the most historically cited proportioning system, used to relate facade width to height, window dimensions to wall dimensions, and room dimensions to each other. Le Corbusier's Modulor (a 20th-century system deriving two linked Fibonacci-based dimension series from human body measurements combined with the golden section) is a documented system actually applied to a concrete residential building at full scale (Unite d'Habitation, Marseille, 1952), making it a concrete-specific precedent rather than only a classical/decorative one. Alternative systems exist and are reported as equally legitimate: root-rectangles, simple whole-number ratios (2:3, 4:9), or a modular grid keyed to the actual masonry unit being used (basing window widths, pier widths, and wall-panel lengths on multiples of the CMU or brick module, e.g. multiples of the nominal 8x8x16-inch CMU, so that no unit has to be cut to fit). This last option is flagged as probably the most directly useful for this project specifically, because it ties a proportion decision to reducing cut-block and cut-brick waste, which is a labor and cost question as well as a look question.

**Fenestration rhythm.** Consistent spacing and sizing of window openings reads as visual order; aligning window heads and sills to a common horizontal datum line across an elevation is a repeated recommendation. In masonry construction specifically, aligning that datum line to a full coursing line (so no window lands mid-course, forcing an awkward cut course above or below it) is both a composition rule and a bricklaying-efficiency rule; this is a second direct link between an aesthetic choice and the Masonry Craft chapter's laying work, worth cross-referencing there. The balance of solid wall to glazed opening on each elevation is set on the performance side by the passive-solar/thermal-mass glazing percentages (a separate flagged research area, Passive and Bioclimatic Design, not this file's territory; cross-reference rather than duplicate); this file's territory is which elevation reads as more solid vs. more open once that performance target is set, and how the openings on the solid elevations are grouped and sized to read as intentional rather than as leftover holes.

**Roof form.** Flat and shed roofs are the two forms most associated with a modern/mass-masonry aesthetic, and both are already the named structural options in Structural System and Concrete Design (section 6.10): concrete deck plus protected-membrane roof as the primary engineered option, concrete deck plus standing-seam metal as the engineered alternate, and a wood-truss-plus-standing-seam fallback on the prescriptive path. This file's territory is the visual consequence of each choice, not re-deriving the structural material decision already written there: a flat roof behind a parapet reads as a clean horizontal cap with no visible roof plane at all from the ground; a flat roof with a visible fascia/reveal at the edge instead reads as a thin distinct line capping the mass; a shed roof, sloped in one direction, allows a clerestory glazing band at the high wall (daylight and a sense of volume inside) and reads from outside as one dynamic tilted plane rather than a flat cap. Parapet design specifically: coping (the capping detail on top of a parapet wall) is available in metal, concrete, or stone, needs a continuous, properly mitered joint system to actually stay watertight, and functions as both the waterproofing termination and a visible datum line at the top of the building; a parapet extending several feet above the roof plane (for a usable roof deck, already noted as an option in Structural System and Concrete Design's solar-integration discussion) becomes a significant part of how the building's roofline reads from the ground, not just a safety rail.

**Entry composition.** A recessed or covered entry (an application of the "subtraction" massing move above) is also the direct answer to the Pacific Northwest's own weather requirement: deep roof overhangs (commonly cited in the 18-to-36-plus-inch range for wet climates specifically) or a full covered porch keep rain off the entry and the door hardware. This is a case where the region's climate and a standard composition technique point to the same physical move, worth stating as one fact (a covered, recessed entry serves both the composition and the weather-protection requirement) rather than as two unrelated topics.

### 4. Expressed Interior Concrete: Light, Shadow, And Combining Materials

**Light and shadow on exposed interior concrete.** The clearest documented technique base is Tadao Ando's residential work: apertures (narrow slits, cruciform cuts, circular skylights, subterranean light wells) cut through thick concrete walls at specific locations and orientations to admit a defined shaft or bar of light at a specific time of day, rather than diffusing daylight evenly. The mechanism is directly statable without any evaluative language: an opening's orientation sets which time of day it admits direct sun (an opening facing east admits a morning light shaft, one facing west an evening shaft, a narrow vertical slit near solar noon on a south wall admits a narrow bar of light that moves across the interior surface through the day). Ando's own preferred concrete surface for this work is a smooth, low-void, off-form finish (varnished formwork boards, careful consolidation), not a textured or applied finish, because the light-and-shadow effect depends on a clean plane to fall across. This project's own wall is thicker than a typical single wythe (inner CMU, cavity, outer brick, combined thickness to be confirmed once The Wall System chapter states the final cavity/insulation dimension), which is directly relevant: a deep opening through a thick wall throws a defined shadow onto its own reveal in a way a thin wall cannot, independent of any applied design intent, simply as a consequence of the wall's thickness.

**Combining concrete with wood, steel, and glass.** Reported pairings, stated as material facts rather than mood: wood (warmer surface temperature to the touch than concrete because of its lower thermal conductivity, used at built-ins, ceilings, stair treads, and window returns where a room is touched directly); steel (thin, dark, linear elements, railings, stair stringers, window mullions, shelf brackets, which read by contrast because they are thin against masonry's thickness, not because of any inherent "industrial" quality); glass (maximizes transparency and contrast against a mass wall, generally used to punctuate specific openings rather than to dissolve the massing everywhere, since a mostly-glazed elevation gives up the thermal-mass benefit the rest of the house is built around). Lighting color temperature is the specific, checkable fact behind "warming" a concrete interior: warm-white lighting in roughly the 2700-3500K range is repeatedly cited as the practical mechanism, as against a cooler color temperature that reads as harsher against exposed concrete or CMU.

**Exposed concrete ceilings specifically.** An exposed structural concrete ceiling (already a real option under this project's floor-system choices in Structural System and Concrete Design, section 6.9) needs acoustic treatment planned in, not added later as a fix: reported options include fire-rated sprayed acoustic material or suspended acoustic elements, with reported reverberation-time (RT60) reductions in the range of 0.3-0.8 seconds in a mid-sized room. Lighting for an exposed concrete ceiling is typically surface-mounted or suspended from the deck rather than recessed into it (a recessed can needs a pocket the structural slab does not have), which is a coordination point for the electrical rough-in, not just a look decision.

### 5. Patina And Graceful Aging

**How concrete and masonry actually change over years, stated as physical mechanisms.** Rain-streak staining develops where water runs unevenly down a facade; efflorescence salts, airborne dirt, and particulates concentrate specifically at drip lines below any horizontal projection, sill, or unsealed joint, so streaking is not random, it follows the building's own water-shedding geometry. Carbonation (the reaction of atmospheric CO2 with the cement paste at the surface) slightly densifies the outermost skin of concrete over years, which is part of why old exposed concrete develops a different, less porous surface than when freshly stripped; carbonation is also the mechanism that eventually threatens reinforcement corrosion once it reaches the rebar, so this fact sits at the boundary between an aesthetics question (how does old concrete look different from new) and the durability-engineering territory of the separate flagged area, Designing for Centuries (cross-reference, not duplicate).

**Self-cleaning surface treatments.** A photocatalytic titanium-dioxide additive or surface treatment (a real, purchasable product category; a named example found is the product class marketed as TX Active) uses UV exposure to break down organic surface grime and some airborne pollutants, and is documented (manufacturer and some academic sources) to reduce facade cleaning frequency and staining/discoloration significantly over time, with one source claiming over 90 percent reduction in manual washing needs and other sources citing pollutant reductions up to 60 percent in high-particulate areas. This needs its own research pass to confirm current US residential availability, application method (integral admixture vs. surface-applied coating), and 2026 pricing before it goes further than a flagged lead.

**Efflorescence, specifically, as a patina/appearance question rather than only a moisture-problem question** (moisture-intrusion efflorescence as a defect is likely covered elsewhere; the framing added here is the appearance/timeline distinction). Primary efflorescence (white-to-brownish soluble-salt deposits) commonly appears within roughly the first 72 hours after construction, while the material is shedding its own construction/curing moisture, and is largely a cosmetic, self-limiting event on new masonry. Secondary efflorescence recurs later and signals an ongoing water-intrusion problem, not a curing artifact, which is a useful distinction for reading what a bloom of white salt on a wall actually means at different points in the building's life. Low-alkali cement in mortar and grout is reported to reduce the salt supply at its most common source (cement-based materials, more often than the clay brick itself).

**Moss, algae, and lichen: a specifically Pacific Northwest, specifically relevant regional condition.** Oregon State University Extension confirms moss and algae growth on hard exterior surfaces is a standard condition in the wet, mild western-Oregon/Willamette Valley climate, concentrated wherever shade and moisture combine (north-facing walls, areas under an unguttered roof drip line, low-sun-exposure areas from site planning or tree cover). Biological growth is reported to both retain surface moisture against the wall (which can accelerate freeze-thaw and mortar erosion over years) and to produce mildly acidic metabolic byproducts that speed material breakdown. Prevention is reported as a design-plus-maintenance combination rather than a one-time fix: adequate roof overhang and gutter control to keep bulk rainwater off the face, sun exposure where site planning allows it, and periodic washing. The material-choice consequence stated plainly: a rougher, more porous surface texture (split-face, exposed aggregate, bush-hammered) holds more moisture and organic material in its texture than a smooth, ground, or polished surface, so the texture decision made under Concrete Surface Expression above has a direct, statable maintenance consequence specific to this climate, not a generic one.

### 6. Regional And Worldwide Precedent

**Pacific Northwest Regional style (1935-1960).** The historic regional baseline this project's concrete-and-masonry material palette departs from: extensive unpainted wood, heavily influenced by Japanese post-and-beam construction and massing, associated architects Paul Hayden Kirk, John Yeon, and Robert Rummer. Useful as the departure point to name explicitly (this house is not that style) rather than a model to imitate. Contemporary regional practice, one named lead: Tom Kundig / Olson Kundig, whose work is reported to pair raw steel, concrete, and wood, which is evidence that the concrete-plus-wood-plus-steel-plus-glass material combination already has a working precedent in this exact climate.

**Tadao Ando (Japan).** Covered above under interior light and shadow; the other transferable fact is his material discipline: unplastered, off-form exposed concrete as the default wall finish, with formwork boards varnished to leave a smooth ("silk") surface rather than a heavily grained or textured one, and thick walls used deliberately for privacy and thermal stability as well as for the light-aperture technique.

**Luis Barragán (Mexico).** A different, non-concrete-surface route to color: pigment carried in a lime or cement plaster applied over a masonry substrate (reported as often a rough pea-gravel mortar texture applied to brick), rather than staining or integrally coloring the masonry itself. The reported mechanism worth carrying forward: surface texture (rough vs. smooth) changes how a saturated color reflects light and reads to the eye, not just the pigment itself. Also reported: periodic repainting as routine maintenance in Mexico's intense sun, a maintenance fact specific to that climate and UV exposure level, worth flagging as needing a Pacific-Northwest-specific equivalent (almost certainly a much longer repaint interval here) rather than importing the number directly.

**Laurie Baker (India).** The strongest direct lead found in this entire research pass, because it addresses the same physical problem this project has already chosen to solve with a cavity wall. Baker's rat-trap bond is a brick cavity-wall bonding pattern, brick laid on edge rather than the usual flat/bed orientation, that is reported to reduce brick quantity by roughly 25 percent compared with solid brickwork while adding an insulating air gap, all within a single-wythe-thickness-looking wall (as distinct from this project's two-wythe-plus-separate-cavity assembly). This is close enough to the project's own cavity-wall thinking, and different enough in its specific bonding geometry, to be worth full dedicated research: whether rat-trap bond or a similar reduced-material brick bonding technique has any application to this project's own brick outer wythe, either as its own technique or as a source of ideas for reducing brick quantity/cost in the outer wythe. Baker's other reported techniques: brick jali (perforated screen walls in patterned brickwork, admitting patterned daylight and airflow while maintaining privacy, a hot-climate ventilation technique that would need real adaptation, not direct transfer, for Oregon's cooler, wetter climate); arches used in place of steel or concrete lintels over openings; niches cast or built into walls that add strength and provide shelving in one move; ferrocement roof structures at reduced material use.

**Southwest United States (slump block / adobe-revival).** Already covered under CMU finishes above. The transferable idea, restated in the composition context: an entire regional masonry tradition treats visible dimensional irregularity in a manufactured unit as the intended look, directly precedented and directly comparable to the question this project faces with its own owner-made block.
