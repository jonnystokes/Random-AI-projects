## (a) Oregon CDL line for towing heavy equipment

**The 26,001-pound combination threshold.** Oregon defines "commercial motor vehicle" by weight: a vehicle or combination that "has a gross combination weight rating or gross combination weight of 26,001 pounds or more, whichever is greater, inclusive of one or more towed units, with a gross vehicle weight rating or gross vehicle weight of more than 10,000 pounds," ORS 801.208(1)(a), or a single vehicle with GVWR/GVW of 26,001 pounds or more, ORS 801.208(1)(b) (https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_801.208). Oregon DMV states the licensing consequence directly: a CDL is required to drive "a trailer, or multiple trailers, with a total gross vehicle weight rating or actual gross vehicle weight of more than 10,000 pounds when the gross combination weight rating or actual gross combination weight, whichever is greater, of all vehicles combined is 26,001 pounds or more" (Class A territory), or any single vehicle at 26,001 pounds or more (Class B) (https://www.oregon.gov/odot/dmv/pages/driverid/cdlget.aspx). Note the "whichever is greater" wording: actual loaded weight can cross the line even where ratings do not.

**No personal-use exemption for equipment hauling.** ORS 801.208(2) exempts only: (a) emergency fire vehicles operated by firefighters, (b) emergency vehicles operated by qualified emergency service volunteers, (c) "a motor home used to transport or house, for nonbusiness purposes, the operator or the operator's family members or personal possessions," and (d) "a recreational vehicle that is operated solely for personal use" (https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_801.208). A pickup pulling an equipment trailer is neither a motor home nor a recreational vehicle. Contrast the federal definition, where a CMV must be "used in commerce," 49 CFR 383.5 (Group A: GCWR 26,001 pounds or more with towed units in excess of 10,000 pounds) (https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title49-vol5/xml/CFR-2024-title49-vol5-sec383-5.xml): Oregon's statute omits the commerce element, so an owner-builder towing their own excavator at 26,001+ pounds GCWR/GCW with a 10,001+ pound trailer needs a Class A CDL in Oregon even with no business purpose involved. The DMV page's listed exceptions match the statute (personal-use recreational vehicles, emergency fire vehicles, emergency service volunteers) and include no owner-equipment or personal-hauling exception (https://www.oregon.gov/odot/dmv/pages/driverid/cdlget.aspx).

**Farm exemption: real but narrow, and it does not fit construction hauling.** Oregon's farm endorsement on a regular Class C license substitutes for a CDL only for vehicles that are "controlled or operated by a farmer," "used to transport agricultural products, farm machinery or farm supplies to and from a farm," "not used in the operation of a common or contract motor carrier," and "operated within 150 miles of the farmer's farm" (Oregon DMV Farm Endorsement Application, Form 735-6776, rev. 1-25: https://www.oregon.gov/odot/forms/dmv/6776fill.pdf; Class A (Y) and Class B (Z) endorsement classes described on the form). Hauling an excavator to build a house is not transporting farm machinery to or from a farm for a farming operation, so the endorsement realistically does not apply to an owner-builder unless the person is a bona fide farmer moving farm equipment for farm purposes. There is no other personal-use carve-out on the DMV commercial licensing page (https://www.oregon.gov/odot/dmv/pages/driverid/cdlget.aspx).

## (b) Jobsite diesel and fuel transport thresholds

**Purely private hauling is outside the HMR entirely.** The hazardous materials regulations do not apply to "transportation of a hazardous material by an individual for non-commercial purposes in a private motor vehicle, including a leased or rented motor vehicle," 49 CFR 171.1(d)(6) (https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title49-vol2/xml/CFR-2024-title49-vol2-sec171-1.xml). An owner-builder hauling fuel for their own build, not in support of any business, is in this category.

**Materials of trade (the framework once the hauling supports a business, e.g. a contractor's or self-employed builder's rig).** A "material of trade" is a hazardous material other than hazardous waste carried on a motor vehicle to protect the health and safety of the operator or passengers, to support the operation or maintenance of a vehicle, or "by a private motor carrier ... in direct support of a principal business that is other than transportation by motor vehicle," 49 CFR 171.8 (https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title49-vol2/xml/CFR-2024-title49-vol2-sec171-8.xml). Quantity limits, 49 CFR 173.6(c)-(d) (https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title49-vol2/xml/CFR-2024-title49-vol2-sec173-6.xml): Packing Group I, 0.5 kg (1 pound) or 0.5 L (1 pint) per package; Packing Group II and III liquids and solids (this bracket covers gasoline and diesel), 30 kg (66 pounds) or 30 L (8 gallons) per package, so standard 5-gallon cans qualify; gas cylinders up to 100 kg (220 pounds) gross; and an aggregate cap of 200 kg (440 pounds) of all materials of trade on one vehicle. Within those limits the material "is not subject to any other requirements of this subchapter" beyond 173.6 itself, meaning no shipping papers, placards, or formal hazmat training (49 CFR 173.6, same govinfo URL).

**Diesel transfer tanks and the 119-gallon line.** Diesel (flash point at or above 38 C / 100 F) may be reclassed as a combustible liquid, 49 CFR 173.150(f)(1), and "the requirements in this subchapter do not apply to a material classed as a combustible liquid in a non-bulk packaging unless the combustible liquid is a hazardous substance, a hazardous waste, or a marine pollutant," 49 CFR 173.150(f)(2) (https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title49-vol2/xml/CFR-2024-title49-vol2-sec173-150.xml). Non-bulk means a maximum capacity of 450 L (119 gallons) or less as a receptacle for a liquid; greater than 450 L (119 gallons) is bulk packaging, 49 CFR 171.8 (https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title49-vol2/xml/CFR-2024-title49-vol2-sec171-8.xml). So a pickup transfer tank of diesel at or under 119 gallons is effectively outside the HMR even in business use. Over 119 gallons, combustible liquids pick the rules back up in bulk form: shipping papers, package marking, identification numbers on the bulk package, and Subpart F placarding, 49 CFR 173.150(f)(3) (same 173.150 URL).

**Placard thresholds.** Each bulk packaging or transport vehicle containing any quantity of a placard-required hazardous material must be placarded, 49 CFR 172.504(a); the relief for non-bulk loads is 172.504(c): "a transport vehicle or freight container which contains less than 454 kg (1,001 pounds) aggregate gross weight of hazardous materials covered by table 2" need not be placarded, and Class 3 FLAMMABLE and COMBUSTIBLE are Table 2 materials (https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title49-vol2/xml/CFR-2024-title49-vol2-sec172-504.xml). Practical stack-up for the book: fuel cans within materials-of-trade limits never approach placarding (440-pound aggregate cap); a diesel transfer tank at or under 119 gallons is non-bulk and outside the HMR; a bulk (120+ gallon) diesel tank in business use is where placards and shipping papers begin.
