# Chapter 1: Purpose, Principles, And How To Use This Book

I have wanted to build a long-life concrete home in Oregon for about ten years. This book is the plan.

It contains the decisions that determine build quality: the land, the structure, the service systems, the legal path, and the required records. Each chapter is self-contained. Cross-references use topic names, and the code lookup appendix and the glossary list individual topics for direct lookup.

## 1.1 What The Book Is For

This book serves four functions:

1. A statement of what the house is meant to be, with the reasons.
2. A record of the design decisions, with the reason for each, in a form open to review and revision.
3. The decision framework and trade-off data needed to finalize design decisions before construction begins.
4. Context for builders, inspectors, designers, engineers, and curious readers to understand the project on first reading.

The record is what makes the work transferable. With the decision and its reason written down, a contractor can build what the decision specifies without meeting the person who decided it. An inspector can check the built work against the written specification instead of against someone's memory. Years later, whoever makes a repair can follow the original reasoning: the record states what was installed, why that option was selected, and what the replacement must match.

The book is written to serve as the working code reference for the systems in this house. Every chapter about a regulated system lists the controlling code sections, the table values, the inspection triggers, and the required records. The official sources remain the legal authority.

## 1.2 The Long-Life Goal

The project's named goal is a thousand-year structural shell. The operational definition: every structural decision is evaluated against a multi-century service life, instead of the thirty-to-fifty-year horizon of production housing. Replacement events over the building's life are confined to components designed for replacement.

The structural means of that service life: low-permeability concrete (concrete that water and oxygen penetrate slowly), pozzolanic admixtures (additives such as fly ash or silica fume that increase density and strength as the concrete ages), corrosion-protected reinforcement, and generous concrete cover over the reinforcing steel. The structural chapter specifies each.

Components with design service lives shorter than the structural shell include:

- roofing surfaces
- sealants
- windows
- plumbing fixtures
- electrical equipment
- smart-home devices
- interior and exterior finishes

Each is maintained or replaced on its own cycle. The documentation and maintenance chapter lists the expected service life and the replacement planning for each.

The long-life requirement applies to the components that are difficult or destructive to replace:

- the foundation
- the structural concrete and reinforced masonry
- the major load path from roof to foundation to soil
- the envelope assemblies that protect the structural shell from water, vapor, and freeze-thaw cycles

Design rules for the service systems:

- Route them for access: filters, valves, pumps, panels, and controls must be reachable for service without opening a finished surface.
- Label every component with durable labels, so a future owner can identify each circuit, valve, run, and device without tracing it.
- Size them for the measured demand of this house, taken from the load calculations and fixture counts in the system chapters, instead of a generic assumption.
- Locate them so that replacement requires no demolition of structural work.

The structural shell consists of concrete, reinforced masonry, and steel reinforcement, with no wood framing. The absence of wood framing determines the applicable code path, the fire-behavior assumptions, and the engineering requirements throughout the book. Wood is specified in two contexts only: comparison against the concrete or masonry detail that replaces a wood detail, and non-structural elements (interior trim, cabinetry, finished floors).

## 1.3 Design Principles

Six principles rank design options that each meet code. The list is in priority order: when two principles conflict, the higher-listed principle governs.

| Principle | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Durability | Hard-to-replace components receive the larger share of attention and budget. Cost comparisons use the full service life, computed over equal time horizons. |
| Reliability | Operation without constant intervention. Failure modes must be slow and visible; where one option warns before failure and another does not, the warning option is selected. |
| Repairability | Worn components must be replaceable without structural demolition. Service routes remain accessible, labels remain legible, and replacement parts remain available or interchangeable. |
| Independence | Basic functions must operate with no internet connection, no vendor account, and no subscription. Internet access adds convenience only. |
| Simplicity | Add complexity only when a simpler system cannot meet the requirement. |
| Clarity | Drawings, records, and labels must identify what was built, for a future occupant or contractor, without guesswork. |

Worked example for the durability comparison: a hundred-year component is priced against five sequential twenty-year replacements over the same hundred years, with the labor and disruption of each replacement included. Computed this way, the higher purchase price is often the lower total cost.

The principles rank choices. Four working rules govern execution:

- **Overbuild the components that are hard to correct later.** Foundation, structure, envelope. The added cost during construction is a fraction of the correction cost after burial or loading.
- **Measure instead of assuming.** Where a decision depends on a value (bearing pressure, blower-door rate, well yield, insulation R-value), measure it. The testing and commissioning chapter specifies the program.
- **Design service systems for replacement.** Mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and smart-home equipment have shorter service lives than the shell; each installation includes a replacement route.
- **Record each decision with its reason.** Records enable later repair, inspection, and sale without guesswork.

Where code permits more than one compliant detail, select the detail that is easiest to inspect, repair, and explain.

## 1.4 The Four-Layer System

Most technical topics are presented in up to four labeled layers. Each layer is written for a different reader and provides a different category of information.

| Layer | Reader | Information provided |
|---|---|---|
| `Simple` | Anyone, regardless of construction background or education | The definition of the system and its function in this house |
| `Applied` | The owner-builder doing the work: comfortable with science and physics, not with trade jargon | The actions, their order, and the checks and records for each |
| `Professional` | Builders, designers, engineers, building officials, and inspectors | The mechanisms, parameters, tolerances, and coordination rules that control the work |
| `Code` | Anyone verifying compliance: owner, inspector, attorney, insurer, future buyer | The requirements, the exact limits, and the sources |

No two layers contain the same information.

A `Simple` block defines the system or component in ordinary words and states its function in this house. Technical terms that must appear are defined in plain language on the same line.

An `Applied` block lists the actions in work order, with the checks and records for each. Trade terms are defined in passing. Where a prerequisite skill exists, the block identifies it.

A `Professional` block states the mechanism, parameter, or coordination rule that controls the work, in trade vocabulary: allowable bearing pressure, lateral earth pressure, lap length, vapor drive, voltage drop. It is complete enough for a professional to check the design against. The glossary defines the terms.

A `Code` block states the rules. Every entry consists of three parts: the requirement, the exact limit or value, and the source. Example:

```
Anchor bolts must tie the sill plate to the foundation.
  1/2-inch bolts minimum, embedded 7 inches, spaced 6 feet or less, one bolt within 12 inches of each plate end.
  Source: ORSC R403.1.6 (2023 edition).
```

An entry without a source does not appear in a `Code` block.

The layers are distinguishable without labels: `Simple` is short plain prose; `Applied` is steps and field checks; `Professional` is dense declaratives with numbers; `Code` is the three-part rule list.

A layer is included only where it provides information absent from the other layers. Many sections include two or three layers; some include one.

## 1.5 Supporting Elements

The four layers contain the technical content. A supporting element is included where a section requires comparison data, cost figures, failure modes, or compliance records outside the four layers.

The supporting elements used in this book:

- a chapter overview identifying the topic and the related material in other chapters, by topic name
- a section topic statement identifying the section's subject
- a decision matrix comparing options across criteria
- a comparison table for materials, methods, or products
- a design-rationale note recording the reason for a choice made for this house
- a make-versus-buy analysis where more than one acquisition path exists
- an equipment list with specifications
- a cost note in real-dollar ranges, with the year of the estimate
- a field-failure panel listing typical mistakes with their detection and correction
- an inspector-readiness summary
- a records list specifying the project-binder contents from the section
- a worked example: one scenario computed with real numbers

## 1.6 Reading Patterns

Five reading patterns are supported.

**Cover to cover.** The chapter order: largest decisions first (purpose, code, land, legal, soil, structure), then the specific systems (plumbing, electrical, HVAC, interiors, smart home, materials), then process and closeout (build sequence, inspections, testing, documentation, business framework, appendices).

**Single-chapter reference.** Each chapter functions as a standalone reference for its system.

**Third-party handoff.** Each chapter includes the code citations, tolerances, and records requirements for its system, so a subcontractor, engineer, or inspector can work from that chapter alone.

**Layer-targeted reading.** The `Applied` blocks alone constitute the complete action list. The `Code` blocks alone constitute the compliance reference. The `Simple` blocks alone describe the house for a reader with no construction background.

**Index lookup.** The code lookup appendix and the glossary list topics for retrieval when the chapter location is not obvious.

Cross-references use topic names ("the foundation chapter"), never chapter numbers, because chapter order may change.

## 1.7 The Project At A Glance

This book is written for one specific project: a long-life concrete-and-reinforced-masonry home in Oregon, built primarily by the owner under the homeowner permit path, with licensed trade subcontractors used where law or risk requires.

| Element | Direction |
|---|---|
| Structural shell | Reinforced masonry walls, structural concrete elements, no wood framing |
| Foundation | Engineered foundation matched to site soils |
| Seismic design | Designed for the site's seismic design category; most of western Oregon is SDC D0, D1, or D2, which makes engineered design the default |
| Roof | Structural slab or engineered roof framing, with a long-service weather surface (metal preferred over asphalt for this design horizon) |
| Envelope | Continuous water, air, vapor, thermal, and soil-gas control layers across all assemblies |
| Mechanical, electrical, plumbing | Designed for access and replacement on shorter cycles than the shell |
| Smart home | Local-first; cloud as a convenience layer only |
| Compliance | Oregon specialty codes (ORSC, OESC, OPSC, OMSC), Oregon statutes (ORS) and administrative rules (OAR), and adopted standards (ACI, ASCE, ASTM, TMS, ASHRAE, NFPA, UL, ANSI) as referenced |
| Owner role | Owner-builder under ORS 701.010 and the BCD homeowner acknowledgement, with licensed subcontractors for LP gas, septic, well, refrigerant work, and anything beyond owner skill |
| Documentation | Project binder maintained from permit application through certificate of occupancy and into long-term ownership |

## 1.9 The Build At A Glance

The full project runs in the sequence below. Each phase depends on the ones before it, and several phases cannot begin until an inspection of the prior work passes. The build-sequence chapter states the detailed schedule, dependencies, and site coordination; this list is the start-to-finish order of the work.

1. **Qualify the land.** Zoning allows the dwelling, the septic evaluation passes, water and legal access exist, and the title carries no blocking restriction. A failure at this phase ends the project before design begins.
2. **Design and engineer.** The structural path, the foundation matched to the site soils, the envelope, and the service systems are fixed; a licensed engineer stamps the structural drawings for most Oregon sites.
3. **Permit.** The plans, the energy forms, and the supporting documents go to the building department, plan-review comments are resolved, and the permits issue before ground disturbance.
4. **Develop the site.** The access, the staging and laydown areas, the temporary power and water, and any shop or block-yard are built.
5. **Earthwork and foundation.** The excavation, the subgrade, the footing and foundation reinforcement, and the below-grade waterproofing and drainage are placed and inspected before backfill.
6. **Walls to roofline.** The reinforced masonry or concrete walls go up with their ties, flashing, embeds, and bond beams, and each wall stage passes inspection before the next begins.
7. **Roof and dry-in.** The roof structure and the weather surface go on, the envelope closes, and the building becomes watertight.
8. **Rough-in.** The plumbing, electrical, and mechanical systems run inside the shell and pass the rough-in inspections before they are covered.
9. **Insulate and close.** The insulation, air sealing, and interior surfaces are completed, and the blower-door test runs where the compliance path requires it.
10. **Finish.** The interior finishes, fixtures, cabinetry, and equipment are installed, and the systems are commissioned.
11. **Occupancy.** The final inspection passes, the certificate of occupancy issues, and the as-builts and the maintenance plan are recorded.

Some steps can overlap or reorder; the inspection hold points cannot. Concrete cannot be poured, walls cannot be closed, and the house cannot be occupied until the matching inspection passes.

## 1.10 Out Of Scope

The following topics are outside this book:

- **Wood-framed residential construction**, except comparison against the concrete or masonry detail that replaces a wood detail.
- **Light-frame prescriptive provisions** that do not apply to concrete or reinforced-masonry homes.
- **Speculative real-estate development**, flipping, and contractor-led project delivery.
- **General home-maintenance advice** unrelated to the systems in this project.
