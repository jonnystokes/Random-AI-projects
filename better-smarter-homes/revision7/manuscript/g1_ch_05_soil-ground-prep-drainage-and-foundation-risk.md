# Chapter 5: Soil, Ground Prep, Drainage, And Foundation Risk

This chapter specifies what has to be true about the soil and the site before concrete is placed. Foundation work makes the ground part of the structural system. Soil that is firm, uniform, dry enough, and documented simplifies the build. Soil that is weak, wet, expansive, filled, or unpredictable can move a well-built foundation.

This chapter spans the work between the land purchase and a subgrade ready for concrete: soil identification, field and laboratory testing, drainage planning, and the foundation-type decision. It also includes the ground-side site work that shares the same soil evidence: backfill and excavation protection, supplemental site-water management, retaining walls and terracing, driveway culvert crossings, and earthwork equipment selection. Foundation construction itself (excavation, foundation detail, slab placement, waterproofing, concrete ordering and placement) is in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement chapter. The structural design (load path, reinforcement, wall assembly) is in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter.

Detailed test procedures (DCP operation, pocket penetrometer technique, hand auger, test-pit excavation, jar test, ribbon test, dry strength, toughness, Atterberg limits, Proctor compaction) are summarized in this chapter and detailed step-by-step in the Testing, Commissioning, And Quality Control chapter. This chapter explains what each test proves, what the result means, and which results push the project from owner-builder screening into geotechnical engineering.

## 5.1 Soil Support Requirements

The footing, slab, basement wall, or mat foundation works only if the soil below carries the load without moving more than the structure can accept. Concrete tolerates compressive load well and differential movement poorly. A concrete house puts more dead load on the soil and is less forgiving when that load is applied to soil with non-uniform support.

**Simple**

The ground holds up the concrete, the concrete holds up the walls, and the walls hold up the roof. If the ground is wrong, everything stacked on top moves with it for the life of the building. Soil testing at the start costs hundreds of dollars; the foundation problems it prevents cost tens of thousands once the concrete is in.

**Applied**

Build a soil file before the foundation design is committed:

- save the desk research (NRCS, DOGAMI, county GIS, OWRD well logs)
- mark the proposed building footprint on a site sketch
- photograph the site in dry and wet conditions
- dig test pits where safe and allowed
- use hand augers or small probes to compare depths
- run field tests where they make sense (DCP, PP, ribbon, jar, dry strength, toughness)
- bag and label representative samples for laboratory testing if cohesive soil is present
- record groundwater observations with date and seasonal context
- keep every soil decision tied to a drawing, photo, test, report, or inspection

Collect site clues, maps, photos, test results, and professional input before the foundation design is fixed. Discovering the site on excavation day is the most common source of expensive foundation surprises.

**Professional**

Foundation design integrates allowable bearing pressure, total settlement, differential settlement, lateral support, groundwater elevation, frost depth, and the expected volume change of fine-grained soils. A concrete house raises the stakes because the dead load is higher and the structure tolerates differential movement poorly.

For shallow foundations, the bearing surface must be accepted at design elevation. The record should identify whether the foundation bears on undisturbed natural soil, engineered fill, improved ground, rock, or a deep-foundation element. If the design uses a presumptive bearing value, the soil classification must support that value. If the design uses a higher or project-specific value, the geotechnical basis belongs in the record.

For a multi-century structural design horizon, the soil work has to address not only first-year bearing but cyclic moisture, freeze-thaw, seismic response, and drainage stability over decades.

**Code**

```
Foundations follow the residential code's foundation chapter.
  Footings, foundation walls, columns, slabs, and crawl spaces, keyed to the design loads and criteria in R301.
  Source: ORSC Chapter 4 (2023 edition, based on the 2021 IRC; effective October 1, 2023, mandatory April 1, 2024).

Questionable soil triggers testing.
  Where expansive, compressible, shifting, or otherwise questionable soil is likely, the building official may require a soils investigation report.
  Source: ORSC R401.4.

Presumptive bearing values are fixed by soil category.
  Crystalline bedrock 12,000 psf; sedimentary or foliated rock 4,000 psf; sandy gravel and gravel 3,000 psf; sand, silty sand, clayey sand, silty gravel, clayey gravel 2,000 psf; clay, sandy clay, silty clay, clayey silt, silt, sandy silt 1,500 psf. Any higher value requires geotechnical evaluation.
  Source: ORSC Table R401.4.1.

Compressible or shifting soil must be removed or stabilized.
  Removal to a depth and width sufficient for stable moisture content in the active zone, or stabilization by chemical, dewatering, or presaturation methods.
  Source: ORSC R401.4.2.

Footings must match the soil.
  Footing design must fit the soil's bearing capacity and behavior, at a minimum depth of 12 inches below finished grade or below the frost line, whichever is deeper (frost line: 12-18 inches in Oregon valleys, 24-36 inches in cold mountain zones).
  Source: ORSC R403.1; ORSC R403.1.4.

Designs beyond the prescriptive limits require engineering.
  Structural elements exceeding the prescriptive provisions must follow accepted engineering practice; most concrete-and-masonry homes in western Oregon (SDC D0-D2) use engineered foundations.
  Source: ORSC R301.1.3.

Foundation-condition information may be recorded.
  Recording with the county clerk is available under the building-code framework where required.
  Source: ORS 455.440.
```

## 5.2 What The Soil Evidence Must Prove

Foundation soil has three requirements: identified, documented, and matched to the foundation design.

The soil investigation resolves eight questions. Each has its own evidence, its own failure mode, and its own design response.

| Question | Plain meaning | What a bad answer causes |
|---|---|---|
| Bearing | Can the soil carry the load without yielding? | Footings sink, punch into the ground, or tilt |
| Settlement | Will the soil compress under sustained load? | Slabs crack, doors bind, walls move |
| Uniformity | Is the support similar across the footprint? | One corner moves differently than another; differential settlement loads the structure unevenly |
| Drainage | Where does water go, and how fast? | Basements leak, clay swells, soil under footings softens |
| Shrink and swell | Does fine-grained soil change volume with moisture? | Foundations lift, drop, or crack with seasons |
| Frost | Can freezing water lift the soil? | Slab edges and shallow footings heave in winter |
| Seismic behavior | What happens during shaking? | Loose, saturated soils can lose strength (liquefaction) |
| Excavation behavior | Can the hole be dug safely? | Walls cave, slump, flood, or strip the bearing layer |

**Applied**

Sort observations and decisions:

Observations to record:

- the test pit shows gray soil and seepage at 4 ft depth
- a dry clay surface has wide cracks and slickensides
- the hand auger hits random brick, wood, ash, or old pipe
- one corner has much softer DCP readings than the others
- a nearby foundation has diagonal cracking and patched drainage
- vegetation indicates seasonal saturation (water-loving species in low spots)

Decisions that follow:

- move the footprint
- remove organic or disturbed soil
- specify and verify engineered fill
- change the foundation type
- add laboratory testing
- hire a geotechnical engineer
- reject the site

Do not design from a single clue. Collect enough evidence to identify the pattern.

**Professional**

The soil investigation produces a design basis: allowable bearing pressure, estimated settlement behavior, groundwater elevation used for design, expansive-soil classification, fill acceptance criteria, frost-protection assumption, and any limits on excavation or backfill.

| Design question | Main evidence | Possible design response |
|---|---|---|
| Bearing strength | Soil classification, test pits, DCP, pocket penetrometer, geotechnical report | Footing sizing, mat design, soil replacement, engineered foundation, deep foundation |
| Settlement risk | Organic content, soft clay, loose fill, compressible layers, water table | Over-excavation, preload, controlled fill, deep support |
| Excavation behavior | Soil cohesion, groundwater, depth, adjacent loads | Slope, bench, shore, shield, dewater, sequence work |
| Moisture behavior | Surface flow, mottling, seepage, water table, nearby wells | Grading, perimeter drains, capillary break, waterproofing, sump |
| Volume change | Clay plasticity, slickensides, dry cracks, Atterberg limits | Moisture control, soil replacement, stiffened slab, pier-and-grade-beam |
| Seismic response | DOGAMI hazard layers, groundwater, loose sand or silt | Geotechnical analysis, densification, deep support, alternate footprint |
| Frost behavior | Frost depth, soil type, water availability | Footing depth, insulation, drainage, non-frost-susceptible fill |

## 5.3 Soil Families And Field Clues

A short field vocabulary is enough to start recognizing site conditions. Formal classification (USDA texture class, Unified Soil Classification System per ASTM D2487, AASHTO) belongs in the field record and becomes the input to foundation design.

**Simple**

The main soil families and what they do under a building:

- **Gravel.** Drains fast. Supports loads well when compacted. Caves easily when excavated.
- **Sand.** Drains fast. Caves when excavated. Liquefies when saturated and shaken.
- **Silt.** Smooth or floury feel. Bad with water. Bad with frost.
- **Clay.** Sticky, holds shape. Some clays swell badly with moisture.
- **Organic soil (topsoil, peat, decomposed material).** Not building soil. Strip and dispose.
- **Fill.** Suspect until proven by test. Old fill can hide debris, organics, and unconsolidated zones.

**Applied: field clue table**

| Field clue | Likely meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Dark soil with roots and visible organic material | Topsoil or organic horizon | Strip and dispose; do not use as structural fill |
| Visible gritty grains | Sand or sandy soil | Check density, drainage, and excavation stability |
| Sticky soil that ribbons | Clay | Check plasticity, drainage, and shrink-swell risk |
| Smooth, flour-like feel; weak when wet | Silt | Check frost susceptibility and water behavior |
| Gray or blue-gray color | Wet or poorly drained condition; possible seasonal saturation | Investigate water table and drainage |
| Red or orange mottling on a gray matrix | Seasonal wetting and drying, water table fluctuation | Treat the seasonal high water table as real |
| Buried debris (concrete, wood, trash, pipe, ash) or random soil-color layers | Old fill or disturbed soil | Investigate extent; engineer around or remove |
| Slick, shiny clay faces (slickensides) | Possible expansive clay or old movement surfaces | Sample for laboratory test before relying on the design |
| Strong organic or rotten smell | Organic or anaerobic wet soil | Do not place footings on it |
| White crust or efflorescence | Salts or evaporite minerals | Investigate for chemical aggressiveness to concrete |

The owner-builder field tools that produce useful records:

- visual classification per ASTM D2488
- ribbon test for clay behavior
- jar test for rough sand, silt, and clay proportions
- dry-strength and toughness checks
- test pit profile (where safe and allowed)
- hand-auger profile
- pocket penetrometer readings on cohesive soil
- DCP profile for relative strength with depth
- laboratory Atterberg limits for any cohesive soil with PI in question

**Professional: Unified Soil Classification System (USCS)**

The USCS divides soils by particle size and plasticity. ASTM D2487 governs laboratory classification; ASTM D2488 governs visual-manual classification in the field.

| Symbol | Description | Behavior in foundations |
|---|---|---|
| GW, GP | Well-graded or poorly graded gravel | Excellent bearing when compacted; drains freely |
| GM, GC | Silty gravel, clayey gravel | Good bearing; drainage depends on fines content |
| SW, SP | Well-graded or poorly graded sand | Good bearing when compacted and confined; drains freely |
| SM, SC | Silty sand, clayey sand | Moderate bearing; drainage degraded by fines |
| ML | Inorganic silt | Poor bearing when wet; frost-susceptible |
| CL | Lean clay (low plasticity) | Moderate bearing; minor shrink-swell |
| OL | Organic silt or clay (low plasticity) | Not building soil |
| MH | Elastic silt (high plasticity) | Poor bearing; problematic |
| CH | Fat clay (high plasticity) | Strong shrink-swell; expansive-soil concern |
| OH | Organic silt or clay (high plasticity) | Not building soil |
| Pt | Peat | Not building soil |

Soil classification separates what the material is from how it behaves. A clay can have adequate short-term bearing strength and a long-term volume-change problem. A sand can drain well and be a cave-in or liquefaction concern. Gravel can be excellent natural support or unstable uncontrolled fill.

Each test pit and auger profile should record, by depth interval: color, texture, moisture, consistency, organics, inclusions, groundwater elevation, and the contact between layers. Sharp transitions indicate fill. Natural stratification indicates undisturbed soil. The bearing layer for the foundation is the material at footing elevation after stripping, excavation, replacement, and compaction; not the surface layer.

**Code**

```
Laboratory classification produces the USCS symbol.
  Grain-size distribution plus Atterberg limits produce the two-letter symbol engineers and inspectors expect.
  Source: ASTM D2487, Standard Practice for Classification of Soils for Engineering Purposes.

Field classification follows the visual-manual procedure.
  The protocol for describing each soil layer, including the ribbon, dilatency, toughness, and dry-strength tests.
  Source: ASTM D2488, Standard Practice for Description and Identification of Soils.

Field soil description has a national standard.
  Texture-class definitions, Munsell color charts, structure descriptors, and consistence terms.
  Source: USDA-NRCS Field Book for Describing and Sampling Soils v3.0; USDA-NRCS Soil Survey Field and Laboratory Methods Manual (SSIR-51).

Parcel-specific soil data is free.
  Soil series, drainage class, map-unit description, and engineering interpretations for any Oregon parcel; the most useful free desk-research resource for this work.
  Source: NRCS Web Soil Survey, websoilsurvey.nrcs.usda.gov.
```

## 5.4 Desk Research Before The Site Visit

Desk research identifies what the site is likely to contain, so the field work targets the right questions. The design basis comes from the field evidence and, where required, the geotechnical report.

**Applied: desk research checklist**

| Source | What it provides |
|---|---|
| NRCS Web Soil Survey (websoilsurvey.nrcs.usda.gov) | USDA soil series, drainage class, map-unit description, engineering interpretations for the parcel |
| DOGAMI HazVu (oregongeology.org/hazvu) | Mapped Oregon geologic hazards: landslide, liquefaction, earthquake, tsunami, volcanic |
| DOGAMI SLIDO | Mapped landslides and landslide susceptibility |
| County GIS (Lane, Marion, Washington, Multnomah, Clackamas, Deschutes, Douglas, others) | Contours, floodplain layers, wetlands, utilities, old roads, tax-lot history |
| FEMA flood data (msc.fema.gov) or local equivalent | Floodplain and base-flood elevation |
| Oregon Water Resources Department well-log database | Well logs near the site; depth, soil layers, water level |
| Oregon Design Criteria Hub (oregon.gov/bcd) | Frost depth, seismic design category, snow, wind, weathering site criteria |
| County onsite-wastewater records | Site evaluations for adjacent parcels often document local soil behavior |
| OSU/SEAO snow load lookup (snowload.seao.org/lookup.html) | Site-specific ground snow load |

Save the maps, screenshots, exported reports, dates, and parcel identifier in the project binder. Each saved map needs the parcel boundary and the date; both are required for the record.

**Professional**

The desk pass narrows what the field pass needs to confirm. Specific outputs:

- expected USDA soil series and engineering interpretations
- expected drainage class (well-drained, moderately well-drained, somewhat poorly drained, poorly drained, very poorly drained)
- mapped seasonal high groundwater
- limiting layer depth (clay seam, bedrock, or hardpan that controls drainage)
- mapped landslide susceptibility
- mapped liquefaction hazard
- mapped flood hazard
- design-load values for frost, snow, wind, and seismic

Where the desk data shows an expansive series, a high-groundwater series, a fill horizon, mapped landslide susceptibility, mapped liquefaction, or a poorly drained class, the field investigation needs to address those items specifically.

## 5.5 Field Testing And What Each Test Proves

Owner-builder field tests are screening tools. They produce a soil class and a relative ranking across the footprint. They do not produce the bearing-pressure number that goes into footing sizing, except in the narrow case of pocket penetrometer on cohesive soil. The authoritative bearing values come from ORSC Table R401.4.1 presumptive values combined with proper soil classification, or from a geotechnical investigation when the project exceeds presumptive territory.

### 5.5.1 Pocket Penetrometer

Of the four common field tools, the pocket penetrometer is the only one with a defensible direct chain from reading to allowable bearing pressure. It works on saturated cohesive (clayey) soils and only on cohesive soils.

**Professional**

The pocket penetrometer reads unconfined compressive strength (qu) directly, typically in tons per square foot or kg/cm². For saturated cohesive soil:

- qu = unconfined compressive strength (the PP reading)
- su = undrained shear strength = qu / 2 (definition for saturated clay, φ = 0)
- qult = ultimate bearing capacity ≈ 5.14 × su = 2.57 × qu (Skempton's bearing factor for a strip footing on saturated clay)
- qall = qult / FS, with FS = 3 (the common safety factor; Bowles, Coduto, and Das converge here)

Working chain: **qall ≈ 0.86 × qu** for a strip footing on saturated clay with FS = 3. Rounded conservative field rule: **qall (psf) ≈ 1,500 × PP reading (tsf)**. That gives 1,500 psf at qu = 1.0 tsf, 3,000 psf at qu = 2.0 tsf.

Calibration band for cohesive soil:

| qu (PP reading) | Consistency | Allowable bearing screening | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| < 0.25 tsf (< 500 psf) | Soft clay | Below typical residential need | Stop; redesign or excavate |
| 0.25–0.5 tsf | Medium clay | ~500–750 psf | Engineering required |
| 0.5–1.0 tsf | Stiff clay | ~750–1,500 psf | Acceptable for screening; verify with PE if bearing approaches 1,500 psf |
| 1.0–2.0 tsf | Very stiff clay | ~1,500–3,000 psf | Acceptable for prescriptive footings; PE evaluation if approaching 2,000 psf |
| > 2.0 tsf | Hard clay | > 3,000 psf | ORSC R401.4 requires geotechnical evaluation above 2,000 psf |

The pocket penetrometer is invalid on granular soil (sand, gravel, silty sand). On those materials the cone reads grain-size effects, not cohesion, and the reading is meaningless. Use density-based methods on granular material.

**Applied**

- take three to five readings per location and use the lowest, not the average
- run readings at footing-elevation depth, not at the surface
- run readings on freshly exposed, undisturbed soil; not on a smear from a shovel face
- log each reading with depth, location, moisture condition, and time
- calibrate the instrument against known-stiffness soil before each session
- use a single operator for consistency on a project

### 5.5.2 Dynamic Cone Penetrometer (DCP)

The DCP is a layer-detection and relative-comparison tool. It is not a bearing-capacity test for residential footings.

**Professional**

ASTM D6951 is the binding standard. Its scope statement limits the test to undisturbed soil or compacted materials measured for in-situ California Bearing Ratio (CBR) and notes that the test is typically used for pavement applications. The strongest US secondary references (the MnDOT User Guide to the Dynamic Cone Penetrometer and Webster, Brown, and Williams (USACE, 1994)) are also pavement-rooted. Application to shallow residential footings is by extension, not by direct standard.

Two reasons the DCP-to-bearing-pressure conversion should not be used for residential design:

1. The chain is doubled-correlated. DCP-to-CBR correlations are well-established (R² of 0.85–0.95 for granular soils, lower for clays). CBR-to-bearing-pressure is not. The Portland Cement Association rule of thumb (qall ≈ 1.5·CBR + 5, in tsf) is a pavement-design extension into shallow-foundation territory. Compounding two empirical correlations turns a screening number into a guess.
2. Field reproducibility outside trained operators is poor. Webster's USACE work reported coefficient of variation around 40% even under controlled conditions.

What the DCP does well for residential screening:

- detects layered subsurface conditions; a slope change on a penetration-vs-depth plot identifies a strata boundary, often within ±1 inch
- identifies suspect zones (high penetration rate at shallow depth → "this site needs geotechnical investigation")
- verifies compaction quality on engineered fill once a material-specific calibration is in hand
- compares relative strength across a footprint

What the DCP does not do for residential screening:

- produce a bearing-pressure number
- substitute for ORSC Table R401.4.1 presumptive values
- act as a density meter without material-specific calibration

**Applied**

Use the DCP in three bands, all framed as screening:

| Penetration rate | Band | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Less than 10 mm/blow | Dense | Confirms a competent layer for prescriptive footing; verify by classification |
| 10–50 mm/blow | Variable | Material-dependent; useful for layer detection and relative comparison only |
| Greater than 50 mm/blow at 0–3 ft depth on natural ground | Loose | Site needs geotechnical investigation; do not rely on prescriptive footings |

Do not publish DCP-to-bearing-pressure conversion tables in the project record as design values. Where the DCP shows a competent layer, classify the soil and use ORSC Table R401.4.1 presumptive values. Where the DCP shows a loose or variable condition, the next step is a geotechnical investigation.

### 5.5.3 Ribbon Test

The ribbon test is a USDA-NRCS field procedure for soil texture. It identifies clay content roughly. It is a warning indicator, not a Plasticity Index predictor.

**Applied**

| Ribbon length | Indication | Next action |
|---|---|---|
| Less than 1 inch | Sandy or silt loam, low clay content | Likely non-plastic; prescriptive foundations probably acceptable from a plasticity standpoint |
| 1–2 inches | Loam or clay loam, moderate clay | Run a pocket penetrometer; consider Atterberg limits if foundation is critical or other warning signs present |
| Greater than 2 inches | Clay, silty clay, or sandy clay | Stop and order Atterberg limits laboratory testing; the lab cost is small and replaces guesswork with a number |

For a long-life concrete house, any ribbon longer than 2 inches automatically triggers Atterberg testing. The cost asymmetry (a roughly $50–$150 lab test now versus expansive-soil damage to the foundation over decades) makes the decision straightforward.

**Professional**

The ribbon test predicts USDA texture class (sand/silt/clay proportions) and tracks clay content. It cannot distinguish PI = 25 (medium expansion) from PI = 45 (high expansion); both ribbon similarly because clay mineralogy (kaolinite, illite, montmorillonite) controls plasticity, not clay percentage alone. The ASTM D2488 toughness/thread test (roll a 3 mm thread, evaluate toughness at the plastic limit) is closer to a PI estimate but still does not replace lab Atterberg limits.

### 5.5.4 Jar Test, Dry Strength, And Toughness

These are corroborating field tests. They support the visual classification but do not replace lab values for design.

**Applied**

| Test | Procedure | What it indicates |
|---|---|---|
| Jar test | Shake a soil sample with water in a clear jar; allow to settle 24 hours; read the layered thicknesses (sand bottom, silt middle, clay top) | Rough proportion of sand, silt, and clay |
| Dry strength | Form a soil ball; allow to dry; attempt to crumble | High resistance indicates clay; low resistance indicates silt or sand |
| Toughness | Roll a soil thread to about 3 mm at the plastic limit | Tough threads at the plastic limit indicate higher PI (more clay activity) |
| Dilatency | Place a moist sample in the palm; tap the back of the hand | Surface water appears for silty soils; remains absent for clayey soils |

These tests are part of the ASTM D2488 visual-manual classification protocol and feed the field soil description.

### 5.5.5 Test Pits And Hand Auger

Test pits and hand augers physically expose the soil profile.

**Applied**

| Method | Use | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Test pit (excavated by mini-excavator or by hand to safe depth) | Direct visual inspection of layers, contacts, fill, organics, groundwater | Limited to safe excavation depth; OSHA trenching rules apply at 5 ft and deeper |
| Hand auger (4–6 in. diameter, 4–10 ft typical) | Quick profile sampling at multiple locations | Limited to depths the auger can reach; difficult in dense gravel or rocky soil |
| Power auger (truck-mounted) | Deeper profile at fewer locations | Requires equipment access; often hired with operator |

Safety: any test pit deeper than 5 ft requires sloping, benching, or shoring per OSHA 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P (Excavations). Stay out of unprotected pits below that depth. Soil that looks stable in summer can collapse with no warning when a truck passes nearby.

Record each pit and auger by depth interval: color, texture, moisture, consistency, organics, inclusions, groundwater, contact with adjacent layers. Photograph the pit wall with a tape measure for scale. Bag a sample from each layer for laboratory testing if that layer is at or near footing elevation.

**Code**

```
The DCP standard is a pavement standard.
  The scope is explicitly limited to pavement applications; residential foundation use is by extension, which is why this book limits the DCP to screening.
  Source: ASTM D6951/D6951M, Standard Test Method for Dynamic Cone Penetrometer in Shallow Pavement Applications.

The two working DCP references are public.
  The most-cited US operations guide (free from the MnDOT digital library) and the foundational DCP-CBR correlation paper whose equations the ASTM annex reprints.
  Source: MnDOT User Guide to the Dynamic Cone Penetrometer (Burnham and Johnson, Mn/DOT Report 1993-05); Webster, Brown, and Williams (USACE Waterways Experiment Station, 1994).

Excavations 5 feet and deeper require protection.
  Sloping, benching, or shoring; the soil-type classification (Type A, B, C) selects the protective system.
  Source: OSHA 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P.

The field and laboratory classification standards pair.
  Visual-manual field description (ribbon, dilatency, toughness, dry strength) and the laboratory USCS classification.
  Source: ASTM D2488; ASTM D2487.

Atterberg limits are a laboratory determination.
  Liquid limit, plastic limit, and plasticity index. ASTM withdrew D4318 in 2026 with the replacement still in development; cite the last published edition (D4318-17e1) until the successor publishes.
  Source: ASTM D4318-17e1 (withdrawn 2026; successor pending).

Compaction and density verification each have their standard.
  Laboratory maximum dry density and optimum moisture (Standard and Modified Proctor); field density by nuclear gauge or sand cone.
  Source: ASTM D698; ASTM D1557; ASTM D6938; ASTM D1556.
```

## 5.6 The Site Evaluation Procedure

The full site evaluation follows five phases, each with a defined purpose and output. Phases 1–2 happen before the land purchase closes. Phases 3–5 happen during design and pre-construction.

### Phase 1: Pre-Purchase Drive-By

**Purpose:** screen the parcel without spending serious money.

**Applied**

- run the desk research (NRCS, DOGAMI, county GIS, OWRD, FEMA)
- visit the parcel in person; walk the proposed building area
- look for surface water flow patterns, standing water, soft ground, springs, erosion
- look for evidence of old grading or fill (terraces, rip-rap, abandoned roadbed)
- look for vegetation patterns (water-loving species in low spots, drought-stressed species on dry knolls)
- note slope, exposure, access for trucks
- photograph the site from multiple angles with a date stamp

**Output:** go/no-go on offering, with a list of items the offer should be contingent on.

### Phase 2: Owner Contingency Investigation (After Offer, Before Close)

**Purpose:** verify the assumptions before the purchase contingencies expire.

**Applied**

- order the septic site evaluation if onsite wastewater is required
- pull adjacent OWRD well logs to estimate water source and depth
- visit the site after rain to see water behavior
- if the seller permits, dig two or three small test pits in the proposed building area
- run pocket penetrometer readings on cohesive soil exposed in test pits
- run a few DCP profiles to identify any obvious soft zones
- bag samples from any cohesive layer at footing elevation for possible Atterberg testing
- contact the local building department about any flagged issues from the desk pass (mapped landslide, mapped liquefaction, mapped floodplain)
- if any major hazard is mapped, hire a one-day geotechnical consult before closing

**Output:** confirmation that the site supports the planned house, or a list of required price adjustments / additional conditions.

### Phase 3: Detailed Site Investigation (After Close, Before Design Finalization)

**Purpose:** produce the soil profile that the foundation design uses.

**Applied**

- excavate test pits at four to six locations across the proposed footprint (corners, center, any zone with visible variation)
- log each pit by depth interval per ASTM D2488
- run pocket penetrometer at footing elevation in each pit if cohesive
- run DCP profiles at the same locations to bedrock or refusal
- bag samples from each layer at or near footing elevation
- send cohesive samples for laboratory testing (Atterberg limits, USCS classification, optionally Proctor)
- record groundwater elevation in each pit; revisit after rain to confirm seasonal high
- mark any zones of concern on the site sketch
- if the project crosses any of the "geotechnical engineer required" thresholds in §5.12, hire the engineer now

**Output:** a soil profile suitable for foundation design. Either ORSC Table R401.4.1 presumptive values are confirmed, or a geotechnical report establishes design values.

### Phase 4: Design And Engineering

**Purpose:** lock the foundation type and the engineered fill specification.

**Applied**

- the structural engineer (or the homeowner using prescriptive code) selects the foundation type from the soil profile
- the geotechnical report (if any) is referenced in the structural drawings
- engineered fill specifications, if needed, are included in the permit submittal
- frost protection, drainage, and waterproofing details are integrated with the foundation design
- the inspection schedule is mapped to the construction sequence

**Output:** stamped structural drawings (where engineered) or prescriptive design tied to ORSC R401.4.1 (where prescriptive); permit submittal complete.

### Phase 5: Pre-Construction Verification

**Purpose:** confirm field conditions match the design before placing concrete.

**Applied**

- proof-roll the building pad with a loaded truck or compactor; document soft areas
- excavate to design footing elevation
- verify the bearing soil matches the design assumption (visual classification, PP if cohesive)
- photograph the bearing surface with a reference scale
- request the footing inspection from the building official
- correct any discrepancies before the first pour

**Output:** a verified bearing surface ready for footing forms and reinforcement.

## 5.7 Bearing Capacity Reference

The starting point for residential foundation bearing is ORSC Table R401.4.1.

## 5.8 Atterberg Limits And Expansive-Soil Thresholds

Atterberg limits are laboratory determinations of the moisture contents at the boundaries of soil behavior: liquid limit (LL), plastic limit (PL), and shrinkage limit. The Plasticity Index (PI = LL − PL) is the most useful single number for evaluating expansive-clay risk.

**Simple**

Some clays swell when wet and shrink when dry, with enough force to lift and crack a foundation. A $50-$150 lab test on a bag of soil produces one number, the Plasticity Index, that grades the risk. Low numbers mean ordinary foundations work. High numbers mean the foundation gets engineered around the movement, and the cost of ignoring that is a cracked foundation that moves every season.

**Professional**

The IBC § 1803.5.3 expansive-soil definition treats a soil as expansive when all of the following are met, or when Expansion Index alone exceeds 20:

- PI ≥ 15
- ≥ 10% passing the #200 sieve
- ≥ 10% finer than 5 µm
- Expansion Index > 20 (ASTM D4829)

The Holtz & Gibbs (1956) classification is the most-cited reference:

| PI range | Swell potential |
|---|---|
| 0–15 | Low |
| 10–35 | Medium |
| 20–55 | High |
| > 35 | Very high |

For an Oregon long-life concrete house, the working thresholds:

| PI | Project posture |
|---|---|
| < 15 | Treat as non-expansive; prescriptive foundations acceptable |
| 15–20 | Screening-level concern. Drainage and moisture-control design required. No irrigation within 5 ft of foundation. Consider thickened-edge slab or perimeter footing with continuous reinforcement |
| 20–25 | Mandatory mitigation discussion in the design. Engineered foundation likely required: deeper footings below the active zone, drainage envelope, isolation joints, post-tension consideration |
| 25–35 | Engineered foundation required. Options: over-excavate and replace with non-expansive fill, lime stabilization, pier foundations bypassing the active zone |
| > 35 | Active-mitigation territory: deep piers to non-expansive stratum, structural slab on void forms, or full engineered approach with site-specific design |

The active zone is the depth of soil that experiences seasonal moisture variation. Foundation elements that bear inside the active zone move with the seasonal cycle; elements that bear below the active zone do not. ACI 332 Chapter 12 (Residential Concrete in Expansive Soils) covers the engineered residential approach.

The engineered mitigation options, in increasing order of intervention:

- **Over-excavation and replacement.** ORSC R401.4.2 requires removal of compressible or shifting soil to a depth and width sufficient for stable moisture content in the active zone; the removed material is not reused as fill inside the active zone.
- **Chemical stabilization.** Lime or cement treatment reduces the soil's plasticity and swell potential directly. Practitioner guidance pairs stabilization with a secondary system (piers, void forms, or a stiffened slab) rather than relying on it alone.
- **Drilled piers.** Piers transfer the load through the active zone to a stable bearing stratum. Typical residential pier depths run roughly 8 to 30 feet, set by the depth of the active zone and the soil profile.
- **Void forms.** Degradable void forms beneath grade beams and slabs on piers leave a space that heaving soil can expand into without loading the structural element. The void depth is sized from the geotechnical report's Potential Vertical Rise (PVR) value, not from a generic number.

Volumetric swell is mineralogy-dependent and is measured for a specific soil by the ASTM D4546 laboratory swell test; no single swell percentage generalizes across clays, and this book states none.

Trees on shrink-swell soil dry the soil around their roots and produce localized shrinkage settlement. Published guidance places trees roughly 20 to 30 feet from the foundation on expansive soil. A 2023 parametric study (Springer, residential footings on expansive soils) reports significant shrinkage settlement at a horizontal distance equal to about half the tree's mature height, with most root-driven drying concentrated in the top 10 feet of soil. The design response: new plantings sit outside those distances, and the geotechnical evaluation documents any existing large tree inside them.

**Code**

```
The Atterberg test produces LL, PL, and PI.
  Casagrande cup or fall-cone methods for the liquid limit. ASTM withdrew D4318 in 2026 with no replacement published yet; laboratories cite the last edition until the successor issues.
  Source: ASTM D4318-17e1 (withdrawn 2026; successor pending).

The Expansion Index is its own test.
  The EI value used in the model-code expansive-soil definition.
  Source: ASTM D4829.

One-dimensional swell or collapse is a laboratory determination.
  Swell or collapse strain measured on a soil specimen under load; the direct measurement behind any project-specific swell value.
  Source: ASTM D4546.

The model code defines expansive soil by four criteria.
  PI of 15 or more, 10% or more passing the #200 sieve, 10% or more finer than 5 micrometers, and Expansion Index over 20 (or EI alone over 20).
  Source: IBC section 1803.5.3.

Residential construction on expansive soil has its own code chapter.
  Stiffened slab, pier-and-grade-beam, and post-tensioned slab options for the engineered residential approach.
  Source: ACI 332-20, Chapter 12.

Expansive or shifting soil triggers investigation and treatment.

```

## 5.9 Compaction And Engineered Fill

Engineered fill is fill placed and compacted to a specification. It is used to raise the building pad above existing grade, replace unsuitable soil, or create a uniform bearing layer. Loose, uncompacted, or undocumented fill is not engineered fill and cannot support a foundation without engineering.

**Applied**

Volume thresholds typical across Oregon jurisdictions:

| Fill thickness under footing | Typical jurisdiction posture |
|---|---|
| Less than 12 inches | Often accepted with visual observation and no formal documentation; confirm the threshold with the local building department |
| 12–24 inches | ASTM D698 (Standard Proctor) or D1557 (Modified Proctor) tests plus density verification at 1-foot lift intervals |
| Greater than 24 inches, or critical fill (under footing, retaining, or below grade) | PE-stamped fill specification, daily inspection log by a qualified soils technician, density testing at specified frequency (typically one test per 500–2,500 sq ft per lift), and final PE letter certifying compliance |

The owner-builder cannot self-certify engineered fill at the larger volumes. The documentation has to come from a qualified third party (a registered design professional or an AASHTO-certified soils technician).

The recommended owner-builder posture before placing any structural fill: commission a one-day geotechnical consult. Cost is typically $800–$2,500 for a residential site in 2026 Oregon dollars. The deliverable is a fill specification with density targets, lift thicknesses, moisture-conditioning protocol, and testing frequency. The owner-builder performs the placement; a separate testing agency or the same geotechnical firm performs the in-place density verification. This produces the documentation the building official needs and protects the long-life goal.

**Professional**

The DCP-to-Proctor relationship is not universal. A penetration rate that means "95% Standard Proctor" for a well-graded sandy gravel is a different rate than "95%" for a silty clay. The MnDOT framework establishes a material-specific DCP penetration-rate target based on laboratory Proctor results plus DCP on lab specimens at known densities. Once the calibration exists for a specific material, DCP becomes a reasonable QA tool for compacted fill. Without calibration, DCP on fill is useful only for relative comparison and gross identification.

Standard fill construction sequence:

1. strip topsoil and organics from the fill footprint
2. proof-roll the exposed subgrade with a loaded truck or compactor; document soft areas for over-excavation
3. place fill in lifts of specified thickness (typically 6–8 inches loose, 4–6 inches compacted)
4. moisture-condition each lift to ±2% of optimum
5. compact each lift to the specified density target (typically 95% Standard Proctor or 92% Modified Proctor)
6. test density per the project specification frequency
7. record each test by station, depth, density, moisture, and lift number

Common lift-construction failure modes:

- placing fill on uncompacted, unverified subgrade
- placing fill thicker than the compaction equipment can densify
- placing fill at moisture content too dry (cannot reach density) or too wet (pumping, weak when loaded)
- using uncontrolled fill (crushed concrete with rebar, demolition debris, frozen soil, organic material)
- skipping the density verification step

**Code**

```
The Proctor tests set the compaction target.
  Standard energy (D698) and higher energy (D1557) each produce a maximum dry density and optimum moisture content; the fill specification names which one governs.
  Source: ASTM D698; ASTM D1557.

Field density verification uses the sand cone or the nuclear gauge.
  The sand cone is the reference method; the nuclear gauge is the common, faster field method.
  Source: ASTM D1556; ASTM D6938.

The code hooks for fill are the soil-test and engineering rules.
  Soil tests when questionable conditions are likely, active-zone treatment for compressible or shifting soil, and the engineered path when fill exceeds prescriptive thresholds.
  Source: ORSC R401.4; ORSC R401.4.2; ORSC R301.1.3.

The building official can demand documentation and record it.
  Authority to require additional documentation on suspect soils, and recordation of foundation-condition information with the county clerk where required.
  Source: ORS 455.058; ORS 455.440.
```

## 5.10 Backfill Against Foundation Walls

Backfill (the soil returned against a foundation or stem wall after the wall is built) loads the wall laterally from the day it is placed. The two recurring failure modes are backfill placed before the wall is braced, and heavy equipment compacting directly against the wall.

**Simple**

A below-grade concrete wall resists soil pressure through its connections at top and bottom. Backfill placed before the floor above is framed pushes on a wall with a free top edge. The wall leans inward or cracks. The repair means excavating the backfill again and rebuilding or reinforcing the wall.

**Applied**

- delay backfill until the concrete has cured 5 to 7 days (roughly 75 percent of design strength; full design strength at 28 days) and the wall is laterally braced at the top
- where the floor that braces the wall is not yet framed, install temporary diagonal bracing: heavy timbers bearing at about two-thirds of the wall height, spaced roughly 12 feet apart for an 8-foot wall
- use free-draining granular material (clean sand, crushed gravel, or crushed stone) against the wall; reject high-plasticity clay, or blend moderate-clay native soil with granular material after testing it
- place backfill in lifts 6 to 12 inches loose; compact each lift before placing the next
- keep powered vibratory compactors in an outer zone away from the wall; compact the zone directly against the wall with a hand-guided plate compactor or rammer
- where fill loads both sides of a wall, backfill both sides together in even lifts
- photograph the bracing and the lift sequence for the project record

**Professional**

Compaction targets from materials-testing practice: roughly 90 percent of Standard Proctor maximum dry density (ASTM D698) in the zone against the wall, placed with light equipment, and 93 to 95 percent in the outer general-backfill zone. Granular backfill compacts predictably, drains to the perimeter footing drain, and applies a stable lateral pressure. Cohesive backfill swells when wet and shrinks when dry, which applies a cyclic lateral load to the wall and its connections.

This sequence is practitioner and materials-testing guidance, not a single ORSC section. The code threshold behind it: a wall standing unbraced during backfill is, for that duration, a wall without permanent lateral support at the top. The height and soil condition at which that becomes unsafe is a structural question for the wall's designer, not a fixed number. Backfill height limits and wall design are in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement chapter.

**Code**

```
Foundation walls with tall unbalanced backfill require engineered design.
  A concrete or masonry foundation wall supporting more than 48 inches of unbalanced backfill without permanent lateral support at both top and bottom requires design per accepted engineering practice.
  Source: ORSC R404.1.1 (2023 edition).
```

## 5.11 Protecting The Open Excavation Through The Wet Season

An open excavation and an exposed bearing surface both degrade in wet weather. Excavation-side protection keeps the walls from collapsing; bearing-side protection keeps the subgrade from losing the strength the design assumed.

**Simple**

Rain changes an excavation. Soil that stood firm when dry can slump without warning when saturated. A bearing surface soaked by rain or frozen overnight loses strength, and concrete placed on it settles. The protections are sloped or shored excavation walls and a clean, firm, unfrozen bearing surface at the pour.

**Applied**

- have the competent person (the individual responsible for excavation safety under the OSHA standard) classify each soil deposit before anyone enters. The classification is Stable Rock, Type A, Type B, or Type C
- slope or bench the excavation walls to the maximum angle for the classification. Use shoring or a trench shield where sloping is not practical
- reclassify after rain. A soil classified in dry conditions can behave one class weaker when saturated. That flattens the required slope or forces a shift to shoring
- keep workers out of an excavation with accumulated water unless the added precautions of 1926.651(h) are in place
- before the pour, remove loose soil, mud, standing water, debris, and ice from the bearing surface
- where the subgrade softens between excavation and pour, use one of three corrections:
  - wait for it to dry
  - over-excavate the softened material and replace it with compacted fill or clean aggregate
  - place a lean-concrete mud mat (a thin unreinforced leveling course, commonly 2 to 3 inches) to seal the surface
- schedule the footing inspection close to the pour date. An inspection that finds mud, ice, or standing water fails

**Professional**

Maximum allowable slopes for excavations less than 20 feet deep, by OSHA soil classification: Stable Rock, vertical (90 degrees); Type A, 3/4 horizontal to 1 vertical (53 degrees); Type B, 1:1 (45 degrees); Type C, 1.5:1 (34 degrees). Oregon OSHA adopted the federal excavation standard as Division 3, Subdivision P, effective September 1, 1990, and it remains the controlling excavation-safety rule in Oregon.

The mud mat is standard wet-weather practitioner practice, not an ORSC-mandated element. Its use and thickness are a means-and-methods decision made in the field by the contractor and inspector for the specific subgrade condition. The concrete-side placement requirements (subgrade condition at the pour, cold-weather placement) are in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement chapter.

**Code**

```
Each soil deposit is classified before the protective system is chosen.
  A competent person classifies the deposit as Stable Rock, Type A, Type B, or Type C; the classification selects the slope, shoring, or shield.
  Source: OSHA 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P, Appendix A (adopted as Oregon OSHA Division 3, Subdivision P).

Maximum excavation slopes are fixed by soil type.
  For excavations less than 20 feet deep: Stable Rock vertical; Type A 3/4:1 (53 degrees); Type B 1:1 (45 degrees); Type C 1-1/2:1 (34 degrees).
  Source: OSHA 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P, Appendix B.

Work in an excavation with accumulated water requires added precautions.
  Support or shield systems, water removal with monitoring by the competent person, or equivalent protection.
  Source: OSHA 29 CFR 1926.651(h).

The bearing surface must be clean at concrete placement.
  Loose soil, mud, standing water, debris, and ice removed from the space concrete will occupy.
  Source: ORSC R403.1; ACI 318 §26.5.2.
```

## 5.12 When To Hire A Geotechnical Engineer

The owner-builder field pass is screening. It identifies soil class, relative strength across the footprint, drainage condition, and obvious failure modes. It does not replace geotechnical engineering when the project crosses into engineered-foundation territory.

A geotechnical engineer is required, or strongly advised, when any of the following is true:

- the desk pass shows mapped landslide, liquefaction, or unstable-slope hazard
- the field pass shows non-uniform soil across the footprint
- the field pass shows seasonal high water table at or near footing elevation
- the field pass shows organics, peat, or undocumented fill at footing depth
- Atterberg PI ≥ 15 (the discussion threshold for an Oregon long-life concrete house) or PI ≥ 20 (the engineered-foundation threshold)
- bearing pressure required by the design exceeds 2,000 psf
- engineered fill is needed under footings or under a slab with structural-load duty
- the building has a basement with hydrostatic exposure
- the building has a retaining condition that exceeds prescriptive limits (typically retaining wall > 4 ft tall, or any retaining wall with surcharge)
- the seismic design category is D0/D1/D2 (most of western Oregon) and the foundation includes any nonstandard element
- the soil report from a neighboring site or an existing OWRD well log shows problematic conditions

Pass-through workflow with the geotechnical engineer:

1. share the desk-pass output (NRCS, DOGAMI, county GIS, well-log search)
2. share the field-pass output (test pits, hand-auger profiles, PP, DCP, photos)
3. share the planned building footprint and structural concept
4. ask for the soil report deliverable that the building official will need: design bearing pressure, settlement estimate, groundwater elevation used for design, expansive-soil classification with Atterberg results, fill acceptance criteria, frost-protection assumption, and limits on excavation or backfill

The geotechnical report is a project document. It belongs in the project binder and feeds the foundation design.

## 5.13 The Soil Problems Catalog

Ten patterns cover most of what goes wrong on Oregon residential sites.

| Problem | What it looks like | Design response |
|---|---|---|
| Insufficient bearing capacity | Soft cohesive layer at footing depth; loose granular at footing depth; PP < 0.5 tsf in cohesive soil | Move to deeper bearing layer; over-excavate and replace; widen footings; engineered foundation |
| Excessive settlement | Compressible organic layer; thick soft clay; loose granular; nearby foundations show settlement cracks | Over-excavate and replace; preload; deep foundation; surcharge |
| Expansive (shrink-swell) clay | PI ≥ 15; slickensides; dry-season cracks; seasonal foundation movement on neighboring homes | Stiffened-edge slab; pier-and-grade-beam; over-excavate and replace; drainage envelope; ACI 332 Ch 12 path |
| Collapsible soil | Loose silt or fine sand, often wind-deposited (loess); low density at depth; collapses on wetting | Pre-wet and compact; remove and replace; deep foundation |
| Frost-susceptible soil | Silty soil at footing depth in cold zones; documented frost heave on neighboring homes | Footings below frost line; FPSF (frost-protected shallow foundation) per R403.3; non-frost-susceptible fill |
| Liquefaction-prone soil | Loose saturated sand or silt; mapped liquefaction hazard; SPT N-values below 15 | Densification (vibroflotation, deep dynamic compaction); deep foundation to non-liquefiable layer; alternate site |
| Variable / inconsistent soil | Mixed fill; old buried foundations; non-uniform DCP across footprint | Geotechnical investigation; remove and replace problem zones; mat or stiffened foundation to bridge variability |
| Old fill / anthropogenic soils | Buried debris, ash, scrap, demolition material; non-uniform colors and densities | Treat as unknown; over-excavate to native; or full engineered fill program with documentation |
| Slope stability issues | Mapped landslide; SLIDO susceptibility; tilted trees, slumped scarps | Geotechnical analysis; deep foundation; alternate footprint; site rejection |
| Karst / soluble bedrock | Limestone or evaporite bedrock; sinkholes; sudden ground depressions | Specialized geotechnical investigation; deep foundation; alternate site |

## 5.14 Drainage Hierarchy

Foundation drainage is a layered system. Water that passes one layer becomes the next layer's load.

**Simple**

Water causes most long-term foundation damage, and the protection comes in layers: the ground slopes away from the house, the gutters route the roof water off, a buried drain pipe collects what soaks down, a coating on the foundation wall blocks what reaches it, and a stone layer plus a plastic sheet under the slab stop moisture from rising into the floor. Each layer removes the water the previous layers passed.

**Professional**

The hierarchy, from outside in:

| Layer | What it does | Failure consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Site grading | Surface water flows away from the foundation | Surface water pools at the foundation; capillary draws moisture into the slab edge |
| Roof drainage and downspouts | Roof water is collected and routed away from the foundation | Concentrated discharge saturates the perimeter soil and loads the perimeter drain |
| Perimeter footing drain (R405) | Subsurface water around the foundation is collected and discharged | Hydrostatic pressure builds against the foundation wall; water enters cracks and joints |
| Foundation dampproofing or waterproofing (R406) | Resists water passage through the foundation wall | Below-grade walls leak; finishes spall; reinforcement corrodes |
| Capillary break (R506.2.2) | Aggregate base under the slab interrupts capillary rise from the subgrade | Slab moisture; finish failure; vapor drive into the conditioned space |
| Vapor retarder (R506.2.3) | ASTM E1745 Class A membrane below the slab | Moisture migration through the slab into the conditioned space |
| Soil-gas retarder and vent (Appendix AF) | Subslab depressurization for radon counties | Soil gas accumulates in the conditioned space |

The inspection and practitioner literature identifies three conditions as the most common causes of chronic foundation moisture: a flat or negative (toward-the-house) grade, downspouts discharging directly at the foundation, and gutters that are missing, undersized, or clogged. On expansive soil the damage driver is soil moisture variation near the foundation, not moisture alone; wet-dry cycling drives the differential heave and settlement. The design goal on expansive soil is a near-constant moisture content at the foundation perimeter, and grading plus downspout control is the first measure applied toward it.

Gravity daylight discharge is preferred over a sump wherever the fall exists, because a sump adds power dependency and pump-failure risk. On a rural property, a wet-season power outage is the same storm event that raises the water table.

The slow failure mode of a perimeter drain is fines migration: clay and silt particles wash into the drain stone and pipe perforations and clog the system gradually. A geotextile mismatched to the soil's grain size can itself clog within roughly a year in fine-grained soil. A correctly specified non-woven geotextile, optionally with a sand buffer layer between native clay and drain stone, extends functional life toward 30 to 50 years. The selection standard (apparent opening size matched to soil gradation, ASTM D4751 and related geotextile standards) sits behind the ASTM paywall; ORSC R405.1 requires only "an approved filter membrane material" without naming a standard, so the product specification comes from the manufacturer's listing and the local inspector's approval.

**Applied**

Each layer requires a verifiable check:

- site grading: 6-inch fall within 10 ft of the foundation per ORSC R401.3. Impervious surfaces within 10 ft (patio, driveway, walkway) slope at least 2 percent away from the building
- roof drainage: gutters present and sized. A common Oregon convention is 1 square inch of downspout area per 100 square feet of roof-drainage area. Downspouts discharge at least 5 ft from the foundation, onto splash blocks or piped extensions
- perimeter drain, verified against each placement detail:
  - placed at or below the top of the footing
  - 4-inch diameter pipe, perforated, holes down
  - bedded on at least 2 inches of washed gravel or crushed rock
  - covered with at least 6 inches of the same material
  - wrapped in or overlain by an approved filter membrane
  - surrounded by clean ¾-inch or #57 stone
- discharge: daylight the drain by gravity wherever the grade provides the fall. Use a sump (a pit with a pump lifting the water out mechanically) only where gravity discharge is not feasible. The sump most often sits at the low corner of the foundation
- curtain drain on a sloping site: a shallow interceptor drain (commonly 12 to 24 inches deep) placed upslope of the house. It cuts off surface and shallow lateral groundwater before the water reaches the foundation. It supplements the footing drain and never substitutes for it
- filter fabric: match the geotextile to the soil's grain size. In fine-grained (clay or silt) soil, specify a non-woven geotextile. Consider a sand buffer layer between native soil and drain stone
- waterproofing: continuous coverage with sealed laps and detailed terminations at penetrations and corners
- under-slab: capillary break of clean stone, vapor retarder lapped 6 in. and sealed, soil-gas retarder where Appendix AF applies

The Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement chapter contains the construction-side detail (drain bedding, daylight discharge geometry, sump pit construction, filter fabric overlap). The Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, And Radon chapter contains the above-grade water-control layers.

**Code**

```
Surface water must drain away from the foundation.
  Grade falls 6 inches in the first 10 feet; impervious surfaces within 10 feet slope at least 2 percent away from the building; where a lot line, wall, slope, or other barrier prevents the 6-inch fall, a drain, swale, or other approved alternate is required. Surface drainage is diverted to an approved point of collection.
  Source: ORSC R401.3 (2023 edition).

Roof drainage must be collected where it would discharge near the building.
  Gutters and downspouts required where roof drainage would otherwise discharge within 5 feet of the building or cause erosion; discharge routed to an approved location.
  Source: ORSC R903.

Below-grade habitable space requires a perimeter drain.
  Drains required around concrete or masonry foundations that retain earth and enclose habitable or usable space below grade, placed at or below the top of the footing or below the bottom of the slab. A gravel or crushed-stone drain extends at least 1 foot beyond the outside edge of the footing and at least 6 inches above the top of the footing, covered with an approved filter membrane. A perforated pipe sits on at least 2 inches of washed gravel or crushed rock at least one sieve size larger than the pipe openings, with at least 6 inches of the same material over it and an approved filter membrane. Discharge by gravity or mechanical means to an approved drainage system.
  Source: ORSC R405.1 (2023 edition).

Where gravity discharge is not feasible, the drain discharges to a sump.
  Sump pit with mechanical discharge; gravity daylight discharge is the code's stated preference over mechanical lift. Sump construction detail is in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement chapter.
  Source: ORSC R405.2.

Foundation walls get dampproofing, or waterproofing where water is known.
  Dampproofing for typical walls; waterproofing where a high water table or other severe soil-water condition is known to exist.
  Source: ORSC R406.1; ORSC R406.2.

The slab sits on a clean stone base with a vapor retarder.
  Minimum 4 inches of clean 3/4-inch or #57 crushed stone (gas-permeable for radon control), then a 10-mil minimum ASTM E1745 Class A vapor retarder with joints lapped 6 inches.
  Source: ORSC R506.2.2; ORSC R506.2.3.

Seven Oregon counties require radon-resistant construction.
  Passive subslab depressurization in Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, and Yamhill counties: 3-inch gas-tight vent pipe, soil-gas retarder, sealed slab joints.
  Source: ORSC Appendix AF.
```

## 5.15 Foundation Type On A Wet Site

The three foundation types rank by groundwater exposure, lowest to highest: slab-on-grade, crawlspace, basement. Which one to build is the owner's decision, made against the site's actual seasonal water-table depth from the site evaluation, the cost difference, and the value of below-grade space.

**Simple**

On a wet site, the foundation type sets how much water pressure the structure must manage. A slab-on-grade sits above the water table and avoids below-grade pressure almost entirely. A crawlspace keeps the floor above the ground but still needs the same drainage details. A basement puts walls and floor below grade, where seasonal groundwater presses against them. It needs the most drainage and waterproofing work.

**Professional**

Slab-on-grade practice on a wet site: the pad is raised with compacted engineered fill where needed, a 4-to-6-inch minimum layer of free-draining aggregate is placed under the slab, and that layer drains by gravity to daylight where the grade allows. A crawlspace reduces hydrostatic exposure relative to a basement by the height of the crawlspace clearance. It requires the same capillary break and drainage detailing as a slab, plus a sealed ground vapor barrier. A basement is the option most likely to need active water management (a sump) rather than passive drainage alone. That case arises where the seasonal high water table reaches or exceeds the basement floor elevation.

A basement on a site with real seasonal hydrostatic pressure is engineered construction by definition. ORSC R404.1.1 removes any foundation wall subject to hydrostatic pressure from groundwater from the prescriptive path. The waterproofing class changes with the water. ORSC R406.2 below-grade waterproofing applies where seasonal high groundwater is within 24 inches of the footing, or where the soils evaluation designates it. A dampproofing coating (R406.1) resists moisture vapor and incidental wetting only; it is not rated to resist standing hydrostatic head. A documented waterproofing example for the R406.2 condition is a 60-mil peel-and-stick polymer-modified asphalt membrane.

The drainage philosophy behind the perimeter-drain requirement is pressure relief, not sealing. The R405 footing drain lowers the water table locally around the foundation so the wall and slab never resist full hydrostatic head. Sealing against full head without relief requires the structure, its waterproofing, and often its dead weight to be designed for that pressure. That approach costs more than relief and is more failure-sensitive. A clogged relief system degrades over years; a leak in a sealed system admits pressurized water immediately.

Buoyant uplift applies to any below-grade structure that is empty or lightly loaded while the water table stands above its base. The exposed structures: a pool, an empty cistern or tank, or a basement shell before it carries the building's dead load. The documented remedies are added dead weight (a thicker base slab or ballast), tension piles or ground anchors, or a hydrostatic relief system under the slab connected to a sump. A normally loaded, occupied basement usually carries enough dead load once construction is complete. The exposure window is during construction and for any dedicated water-storage vault (§5.19).

**Code**

```
Foundation walls subject to groundwater pressure require engineered design.
  Engineered per R301.1.3 where subject to hydrostatic pressure from groundwater, or where supporting more than 48 inches of unbalanced backfill without lateral support at top and bottom.
  Source: ORSC R404.1.1 (2023 edition).

Waterproofing replaces dampproofing where groundwater stands close to the footing.
  Below-grade waterproofing required where seasonal high groundwater is within 24 inches of the footing, or where the site's soils evaluation designates it; dampproofing applies elsewhere.
  Source: ORSC R406.1; ORSC R406.2.
```

## 5.16 Supplemental Site-Water Management

A rural lot has no municipal storm system. The mandatory grading (R401.3) and footing drain (R405) protect the foundation; the rest of the lot's water (yard, driveway shoulders, work pads) is managed with supplemental features the building code does not reach. The sizing conventions below are published general practice, not Oregon code requirements. Whether the county's own ordinance sets a minimum design storm, a required feature list, or a stormwater plan for a rural residential lot is a local question; the owner confirms it with county planning or public works at design time.

**Simple**

Rain runs off the roof, the driveway, and the work pads. Each of those flows gets a planned destination. The common tools are a dry well, a rain garden, and a swale. A dry well is a buried gravel-filled pit that soaks water into the ground. A rain garden is a planted low spot that holds runoff while it soaks in. A swale is a shallow graded channel that moves water. Each one is sized from the area that drains into it.

**Applied**

- size a dry well by the one-inch rule: store the first inch of rainfall from the contributing area. One inch of rain on 1 square foot is about 0.623 gallons. A 500-square-foot roof section therefore requires roughly 311 gallons of storage. A typical residential dry well is about 4 ft by 4 ft by 3 ft
- size a rain garden at 10 to 20 percent of its contributing area. An alternate sizing divides the contributing area by 6 (clay soil) to 8 (sandy soil). The divided area holds about 1 inch of runoff at about 6 inches deep. A 1,200-square-foot roof needs roughly 150 to 200 square feet of garden. Place it at least 10 ft from the foundation with 6 to 12 inches of amended soil media
- size a swale to its peak flow by the Rational Method: Q = C x i x A. Q is peak discharge in cubic feet per second. C is a runoff coefficient for the surface type. i is rainfall intensity for the design storm. A is the contributing area. Confirm the design storm (commonly a 2-year or 10-year event for ordinary conveyance) with the county
- route first-flush diverter water (the discarded first, dirtiest runoff from a roof collection system, detailed in the Water Independence chapter) into these same features. Never route it onto or near the septic absorption area
- treat the block-yard production pad as new contributing area. Give the pad its own perimeter swale or berm directed to the same downstream feature. Re-check that feature's capacity by the Rational Method against the enlarged total area
- keep every concentrated discharge point outside the septic setbacks in the Code block below

**Professional**

A compacted, graded production pad behaves hydrologically as a near-impervious surface: it sheds a higher fraction of rainfall, faster, than the vegetated ground the original drainage sizing assumed. The integration principle is to enlarge the accounted contributing area rather than treat the pad as a separate, unaccounted surface. No source treats a residential block-production pad as its own regulated category; the pad is ordinary impervious area in the site calculation.

First-flush water is measurably dirtier than the rain that follows it. The first 10 to 20 gallons of runoff from an average roof carries up to 90 percent of the accumulated pollutants: bacteria from bird and animal droppings and decomposed insects, sediment, and metals leached from the roofing (commonly zinc, varying with roofing type). No Oregon code provision governs roof-runoff disposal relative to a food garden. General practice routes first-flush water to non-edible use (an ornamental drip line or the grading swale); acceptance into a food garden is an owner judgment made with the specific roofing material in mind.

The state permit trigger is the DEQ 1200-C construction stormwater permit. Coverage is required where construction disturbs one acre or more of soil with the potential to discharge to surface waters, counted across the whole common plan of development: house pad, driveway, septic disturbance, and block-yard pad together, not separately. Below one acre the state permit generally does not apply, except where DEQ documents a water-quality violation at the site or finds significant pollutant potential. The county's own grading or erosion-control ordinance can still apply below the state threshold. The actual disturbed-acreage and discharge-path determination for this site is made with DEQ and the county at design time.

**Code**

```
Construction disturbing one acre or more requires stormwater permit coverage.
  DEQ 1200-C NPDES general permit where one acre or more of soil disturbance has potential discharge to surface waters, or the site is part of a common plan of development reaching that threshold; application at least 30 calendar days before soil disturbance; written erosion and sediment control plan required.
  Source: Oregon DEQ 1200-C Construction Stormwater General Permit (effective December 15, 2025; expires December 14, 2030).

Roof and surface drainage may not saturate the septic absorption area.
  The initial and replacement absorption facility site must not be subjected to excessive saturation from artificial drainage of ground surfaces, driveways, roads, or roof drains.
  Source: OAR 340-071-0220(1)(i) (amended by DEQ 29-2025, effective January 1, 2026).

Drainage features keep fixed distances from the septic system and the foundation.
  Absorption area (including replacement area) to building foundation line: 10 feet; to property line: 10 feet. Septic tank or treatment unit to foundation line: 5 feet; to property line: 5 feet.
  Source: OAR 340-071-0220, Table 1.
```

## 5.17 Retaining Walls And Terracing For Cut And Fill

Creating a level building pad, a driveway grade, or a block-yard pad on a sloped parcel produces cut faces and fill that a retaining wall holds back. Which wall type fits which height is set by two Oregon code triggers and by cost.

**Simple**

Oregon draws the engineering line at two places. A wall holding back more than 48 inches of soil, unsupported at the top, must be engineered. A wall over 24 inches with an extra load must be engineered at any height. Extra loads include a driveway, a parked vehicle, or a structure above the wall. Below both lines, several wall types can be owner-built without a stamped design.

**Applied**

Match the wall type to the retained height and load:

| Wall type | Practical height range | Position against the Oregon triggers |
|---|---|---|
| Gravity segmental retaining wall (SRW) | Up to roughly 4-6 ft | Mass alone resists the soil; a single tier under 48 inches with no surcharge needs no stamp |
| Gabion (stone-filled wire basket) | Up to roughly 6 ft | Gravity system; roughly $15-30 per square foot (2026); locally sourced stone reduces hauling cost |
| Owner-made CMU gravity wall | Under 4 ft, no surcharge | Same legal position as any other gravity wall; above the triggers it takes the same engineered reinforced-masonry design (TMS 402) as other block walls |
| Geogrid-reinforced SRW / MSE wall | Roughly 6 to 50+ ft | Inherently an engineered design at any height; the economical engineered option at greater heights |
| Cast-in-place concrete cantilever | Any substantial height | Engineered design and quality-controlled construction at any real retained height |

Terracing steps for staying under the threshold:

- keep each tier at or under 48 inches of unbalanced fill. Allow no surcharge on the tier
- offset each upper wall horizontally at least twice the height of the wall below it. Two 4-foot tiers need at least 8 feet between them, in addition to each wall's own base width
- where the offset is less than twice the lower wall's height, have the combination engineered as a single taller wall
- obtain a per-wall engineering quote from a local structural engineer. Request it once the wall's height, length, and surcharge condition are known. The whole-house structural engineering figure of $4,000-10,000 (2026) covers the house only. It does not price a standalone retaining wall

**Professional**

ORSC R404.1.1 contains two independent triggers. Height: a retaining wall not laterally supported at the top that retains more than 48 inches of unbalanced fill requires design per accepted engineering practice, with a minimum factor of safety of 1.5 against sliding and overturning. Load: any retaining wall over 24 inches that resists lateral loads in addition to soil requires the same design regardless of height. A wall under 4 feet with no surcharge is exempt from mandatory engineering in any material; a wall of any height carrying a surcharge is not.

The tier-offset rule comes from segmental-retaining-wall industry technical guidance (Allan Block TS1297; the Masonry and Hardscapes SRW FAQ): the zone of influence extends horizontally about two times the wall height behind the lower wall, so an upper wall placed inside that zone applies a surcharge to the lower wall, and independence requires a setback of at least twice the lower wall's height. This is published industry guidance, not a code section; where the tiers interact, the stability analysis models them as one wall.

The cost pattern above the threshold: a geogrid-reinforced SRW or MSE wall carries lateral pressure as tension in geogrid layers extending into the retained soil, which makes the reinforced soil mass act as a coherent gravity block; published cost comparisons report reinforced/MSE systems 25 to 50 percent cheaper than a cast concrete cantilever wall once the height exceeds roughly 10 feet. Above the threshold, the choice among wall systems is decided by cost, labor, appearance, and available skill.

Terracing trades footprint for engineering: the mandatory setbacks between tiers, plus each tier's base width and any access space, consume horizontal ground that a single tall engineered wall does not. On a constrained pad or a narrow buildable envelope, the single engineered wall recovers more flat area per foot of slope; on an open slope, the terraced gravity walls stay within owner-builder labor and skill.

**Code**

```
Retaining walls above the height or load thresholds require engineered design.
  More than 48 inches of unbalanced fill without lateral support at the top; or any wall over 24 inches that resists lateral loads in addition to soil; minimum factor of safety 1.5 against sliding and overturning.
  Source: ORSC R404.1.1 (2023 edition).
```

## 5.18 Driveway Culverts And Watercourse Crossings

Access evaluation is in the Land, Site, And Feasibility chapter; construction sequencing for the crossing is in the Build Sequence And Site Coordination chapter. This section specifies sizing, protection, and inspection of the crossing itself.

**Simple**

A single-access rural driveway that crosses a ditch or seasonal stream depends on one culvert (a pipe carrying the water under the road). An undersized or unprotected culvert overtops or scours out in a storm. The washout closes the only access to the property. Sizing comes from the water draining to the crossing, not from a standard pipe size.

**Applied**

- obtain the county road department's driveway-approach and culvert standard; the county sets the controlling design storm and minimum diameter
- calculate peak flow by the Rational Method (Q = C x i x A, defined in the swale sizing of §5.16) from the actual upstream drainage area and local rainfall-intensity data; hire a civil engineer for anything beyond a minor ditch crossing
- protect the outlet with a headwall and rip-rap energy dissipation to prevent scour
- check ODFW fish-distribution data for the watercourse; a crossing on fish-bearing water triggers fish-passage review before construction or replacement
- inspect the crossing each fall before the wet season; clear the inlet of debris and check the outlet for scour
- budget roughly $500-3,000 (2026) per culvert crossing, varying with size and installation difficulty

**Professional**

Oregon county standards set floors that override a smaller calculated size. Klamath County requires driveway culverts sized to the 25-year, 24-hour storm with a 12-inch minimum diameter. Where a defined channel exists, 18 inches is the more common practical minimum, for two reasons. A larger culvert passes debris that clogs a smaller one. An 18-inch corrugated metal pipe is also among the strongest standard sizes per unit weight to install. Deschutes, Klamath, Lincoln, and other counties each publish their own road-approach and culvert standard. The final diameter, slope, and headwater design for this property is a site-specific hydrology calculation against the controlling county standard. That calculation is produced by a survey of the parcel and its road authority.

A second access track priced at design time uses the same unit costs as any gravel road: roughly $4-10 per square foot (2026) including site preparation, 6 to 12 inches of compacted gravel base, and drainage. For a 12-foot single-lane track that is roughly $50-120 per linear foot before culverts. The total cost of an alternate route is route-specific (length, terrain, easements) and is priced by a road contractor from the actual alignment. A secondary track graded while the excavation equipment is already mobilized for the driveway and house pad adds only its own construction cost. The same track cut after a washout adds a separate emergency equipment mobilization.

**Code**

```
Fish passage is required at new or replaced artificial obstructions.
  Applies in fish-bearing waters where an artificial obstruction, a category that includes a road culvert, is newly constructed, replaced, or has a fundamental change in permit status.
  Source: ORS 509.585.

The crossing bed width is fixed by the active channel width.
  Bed under or within an open-bottomed or closed-bottom crossing structure at least 1.2 times the active channel width, plus 2 feet.
  Source: OAR 635-412-0035 (Fish Passage Criteria).
```

## 5.19 A Cistern Inside The Foundation Footprint

A cistern (a storage tank for collected water) can be integrated into the foundation as a dedicated basement room or a below-slab vault. Whether to build one at all is the owner's decision, made in the Water Independence chapter; this section states what the choice does to the foundation design. The consequences below are settled inputs to that decision.

**Simple**

A wall holding water is a different structure from a wall holding soil. It is designed to a stricter concrete standard. It is waterproofed on the side the water touches. The design also covers the empty case. An empty tank below the water table can be pushed upward by groundwater.

**Professional**

A vault deliberately holding a standing head of water sits inside both engineering triggers already stated in this chapter: ORSC R404.1.1 (engineered design for walls subject to hydrostatic pressure) and ORSC R406.2 (waterproofing, not dampproofing, where the design resists real head). A foundation-integrated cistern is outside the prescriptive path regardless of what the surrounding house foundation qualifies for.

The design standard changes. Ordinary residential foundation walls are designed to ACI 332 and ACI 318; a structure built to hold liquid falls under ACI 350, Code Requirements for Environmental Engineering Concrete Structures. ACI 350 requires liquid-tightness, not only load capacity: it specifies a load factor of 1.7 for lateral liquid pressure (1.4 in ordinary design) and tighter crack-width and reinforcement-detailing limits. A cistern in the foundation is designed to ACI 350, not detailed as an extension of the surrounding ACI 332 wall.

The drainage logic inverts. The chapter's perimeter-drain philosophy is pressure relief around the foundation; the water inside a cistern is deliberate, not a condition to relieve. The cistern shell requires its own dedicated positive-side waterproofing, and where used, a crystalline waterproofing admixture that self-seals minor cracking under sustained water contact. The detailing keeps the building's R405 perimeter drain from draining the cistern and from loading its outer face. Any wall shared between the cistern and an occupied basement space requires waterstops at every construction and control joint, and the cistern's floor slab is typically thicker and separately reinforced from the surrounding footings.

The empty-tank case governs flotation. A cistern stands empty at known times: before first fill, after a draw-down, and during inspection or maintenance. The structural design covers the empty-tank uplift case explicitly, by added dead weight, tie-down anchors, or a hydrostatic relief detail sized for the empty condition (§5.15 states the general uplift mechanism).

The pour sequence changes. The tank's base slab sits below the building's ordinary footing elevation, so the cistern excavation goes deeper than the footings and comes first. The tank base and walls are typically placed as a separate, earlier structural pour with waterstops cast into every joint, before the surrounding foundation walls and footings are formed. This converts the single foundation pour into at least two coordinated placements; the pour sequencing is in the Build Sequence And Site Coordination chapter.

**Code**

```
Liquid-retaining concrete structures follow their own design code.
  Load factor 1.7 for lateral liquid pressure (versus 1.4 in ordinary structural design), with crack-width and reinforcement-detailing limits set for liquid-tightness.
  Source: ACI 350-20.

A foundation wall holding water is engineered and waterproofed.
  Engineered design where subject to hydrostatic pressure; below-grade waterproofing rather than dampproofing where the wall resists a head.
  Source: ORSC R404.1.1; ORSC R406.2.
```

## 5.20 Earthwork Equipment For The Soil And Site Work

The earthwork scope (driveway cut, footing excavation, utility trenches, septic and drain-field work, block-yard pad preparation) splits between two machine classes. Ownership, rental, and the wider tool inventory are in the Tools, Equipment, And Owner-Builder Shop chapter.

**Simple**

A mini excavator digs deep, precise holes and trenches with vertical walls. A compact track loader (the tracked machine in the skid-steer class) moves and spreads material fast across the site. Most projects need both, at different phases and for different lengths of time.

**Applied**

Match the machine to the task:

| Task | Primary machine | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Footing excavation | Mini excavator | Digging depth and precision; a roughly 3-ton machine reaches 8-10 ft |
| Utility trenches | Mini excavator | Clean vertical trench walls; control near existing utilities |
| Septic tank and drain-field installation | Mini excavator | Depth and precision |
| Driveway cut (rough grade) | Mixed | Excavator for the initial cut through undisturbed sloped ground; loader for finish grading and base spreading |
| Block-yard pad preparation | Compact track loader | Grading and compacting a flat surface is a moving-and-spreading task |
| Bulk material moves (gravel, spoils) | Compact track loader | Travel speed roughly 7-10 mph against an excavator's 2-3 mph |

Rental structure:

- rent for tasks that occur in a defined window; consider ownership only where the same machine is needed repeatedly across a long timeline
- the excavator serves more phases (footings, then trenches, then septic, then later drainage work), which argues for the longer rental period, or ownership where the timeline spans multiple seasons
- the loader's tasks cluster in the early site-work phase, which fits a shorter single rental
- weekly rates run roughly 20 to 40 percent cheaper per day than daily rates; monthly rental gives the best per-day value for sustained use
- estimate total machine-hours from the earthwork takeoff before deciding rent against buy; no published break-even figure exists for this decision, and the break-even comes from those hours, regional rental rates, and used-equipment pricing

## 5.21 Oregon Soil Patterns

Regional patterns useful for setting expectations before the field pass:

| Region | Common soil patterns | Common foundation issues |
|---|---|---|
| Willamette Valley lowlands | Silty clay loam (Holcomb, Dayton, Amity series); seasonal high water table; some organic horizons | Drainage management; possible expansive-clay concerns; high water table in winter |
| Cascade foothills | Stony loam over weathered basalt; locally rocky; seasonal seepage | Variable bearing across footprint; rock excavation in places; landslide susceptibility on steeper slopes |
| Coast Range | Wet, organic-rich profile; rapid drainage in some areas, poor in others; high rainfall | Drainage design priority; possible undocumented fill; high water table |
| Coastal plain | Sand and silty sand near shore; locally peat; tsunami/liquefaction overlays | Liquefaction risk in some mapped zones; salt-and-acidic-soil concerns for buried metal |
| Deschutes Basin / east of Cascades | Pumice, scoria, volcanic ash; loess in places; basalt at varying depth | Collapsible loess in some areas; rock at variable depth; frost depth deeper than valley |
| High Desert (Harney, Malheur) | Silt-rich loess; clayey alluvium in basins; basalt and tuff bedrock | Frost depth; potential expansive clay in basin sediments; dust and surface erosion |

These are screening expectations, not design values. The NRCS Web Soil Survey gives parcel-specific data, and the field pass produces the actual profile.

## 5.22 Site Decision Framework

Sort the site at the end of the soil pass into one of these acceptance levels.

| Acceptance level | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Reject | Major hazards or unworkable conditions; cost or risk exceeds the project envelope | Walk away; or extensive geotechnical engineering and the cost recovery that comes with it |
| Conditional | Workable with documented fill, foundation type adjustment, or geotechnical engineering | Engage the engineer; budget for the fill and foundation work; carry the conditions into design |
| Acceptable with design | Workable within prescriptive code if drainage and frost protection are correct | Standard design path with attention to drainage and detail |
| Strong | Uniform, well-drained, predictable soil with documented bearing | Standard design path; lowest-risk foundation construction |

The decision process:

1. compile the desk-research package (NRCS, DOGAMI, county GIS, OWRD, FEMA)
2. complete the field pass (test pits, hand auger, PP, DCP, ribbon, jar, dry strength)
3. send any cohesive samples for laboratory Atterberg testing if PI is in question
4. classify the soil per ASTM D2487/D2488
5. apply the bearing screening (presumptive table, PP for cohesive)
6. apply the expansive-soil screening (PI thresholds)
7. evaluate drainage, slope, frost, and seismic exposure
8. cross-check against the geotechnical-engineer thresholds in §5.12
9. decide acceptance level
10. document the decision in the project binder

## 5.24 The Twelve Principles Of Ground Preparation

A self-check list before construction begins:

1. Soil is part of the structure. Treat the bearing layer with the same care as the concrete.
2. Classify before designing. ASTM D2487/D2488 classification comes before any bearing assumption.
3. Dig test pits before committing the design. Direct observation of the profile replaces assumptions.
4. Order Atterberg testing on any ribbon over 2 inches. The lab test replaces guessing at clay plasticity.
5. The pocket penetrometer is the only field tool with a defensible direct chain to bearing pressure, and only on cohesive soil.
6. Use the DCP for layer detection and compaction QA only. It produces no bearing value for residential footings.
7. Engineered fill requires third-party documentation. Owner-compacted fill without density records has no standing with the building official.
8. Drainage is layered. Each layer removes water before it reaches the next.
9. Frost depth is a permit-time fact. Use the local jurisdiction value.
10. Expansive-soil thresholds run conservative for a long-life house: PI of 15 triggers the mitigation discussion, PI of 20 triggers an engineered foundation.
11. On borderline sites, the geotechnical consult ($800-$2,500, 2026) is small relative to correcting a mismatched foundation.
12. Verify the bearing surface before concrete: photograph it, classify it, and confirm it matches the design assumption.
