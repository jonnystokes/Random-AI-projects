# Chapter 25: Documentation, Maintenance, And Future Upgrades

This chapter specifies owning the house after the build: the records to keep and why, the project binder and its backup, warranty documentation, the maintenance program, the budget contingency and maintenance reserve, technology replacement planning, the provisions that make future upgrades cheap, the documentation future owners and responders need, and how to handle a pause or an incident. The house is designed for a long service life, but it still needs records, maintenance, and a way for future people to understand what was built.

Some of this is legally consequential in Oregon and some is practice guidance. The first sale of the house requires the permit record verbatim, several sale disclosures draw on the binder, and one structural-code exemption records into the deed. The maintenance intervals and technology planning bands are recommendations anchored to industry sources, not requirements, and every question of sale timing, insurance, or tax is routed to the relevant professional rather than answered here.

## 20.1 The Record As The House's Memory

The project binder is the memory of the house, and it should not depend on one person's recollection, one laptop, one installer, or one paper folder. The record is useful during construction, at move-in, during repairs, during an insurance claim, and at resale.

**Simple**

The walls, pipes, wires, drains, and equipment outlive the moment they were installed. If no one records where things are and why choices were made, future maintenance becomes guessing. The binder is the house's reference for future occupants, contractors, and buyers. It records what was permitted, what was installed, where the hidden things run, and what needs service.

**Applied**

Document these record areas:

| Record area | What it helps with |
|---|---|
| Property and permits | Ownership, approvals, inspections, and the first sale |
| Design | What the house was meant to be |
| As-built | What was installed and where |
| Materials and warranties | Products, suppliers, and replacement parts |
| Systems | Plumbing, electrical, HVAC, network, and shutoffs |
| Testing | What was tested, when, by whom, and what failed |
| Maintenance | What needs service, cleaning, inspection, or replacement |

**Professional**

The binder is built during construction (the materials and inspection chapters describe the closeout binder it grows from) and carried for the life of the house. Its most valuable content is the record that cannot be recovered later: the permit numbers, the as-built routes, the test results, and the photographs of every concealed condition before it was covered. The record habit is what makes any later professional consultation cheaper, because the professional starts from fact rather than from an excavation.

**Code**

```
The project binder continues the closeout binder.
  Built during construction per the materials and inspection chapters; carried for the life of the house; the unrecoverable records (permit numbers, as-built routes, test results, pre-cover photographs) are its core.
  Source: project documentation standard (see the materials and inspection chapters).
```

## 20.2 Records To Keep Permanently

Oregon gives the owner-builder a concrete reason to keep the permit record forever: the first sale of the house requires the permit numbers stated verbatim, and any later sale draws the seller's disclosure answers from the binder. The keep-forever list is short and specific.

**Simple**

When a never-lived-in house sells, Oregon lets the seller skip the long disclosure form only by handing over the exact building permit numbers. Lose the permit record and that option is gone. Construction claims also have a ten-year clock that starts when the house is finished. So the records that prove what was built stay valuable for at least a decade and, in practice, forever.

**Applied**

Keep permanently:

- The building permit numbers and issuing jurisdiction (required word-for-word at the first sale).
- The inspection record and the certificate of occupancy.
- The approved, stamped plan set and every revision, and the engineering documents (calculations, general notes, and any special-observation letters).
- The energy-path documents (the measure-selection form and the blower-door and duct results).
- The septic record (the construction permit, the as-built diagram, the completion certificate, and the current service contract) and the well record (the log, the pump record, and the initial water panel).
- The utility service records, the product approvals and evaluation reports relied on at plan review, all test reports, and the photo archive of concealed work.
- Any structural-code-exemption permit that was recorded into the deed.
- The smoke-alarm and any carbon-monoxide-alarm compliance evidence for a sale (working alarms are a statutory sale condition).

**Professional**

The first sale of a never-occupied dwelling is excluded from the full seller's property disclosure only if the seller gives the buyer the statutory statement naming the building permit numbers and the issuing jurisdiction (ORS 105.464 and the ORS 105.470 exclusion). Any later sale requires the full disclosure form answered from actual knowledge, where a "yes" answer often requires attaching the underlying report, so the binder becomes the seller's defense file (ORS 105.462 to 105.490). Oregon's statute of repose bars construction claims ten years after substantial completion for a residential structure (ORS 12.135(2)(b), 2025 numbering), and for an owner-built home the clock starts at the earlier of the certificate of occupancy or first occupancy (ORS 12.135(1)(e)). While the builder-owner still occupies the home the section's shield does not apply (the possession-and-control exclusion, ORS 12.135(7)(b)). After a sale, the buyer's construction claims run against that completion-dated clock, not the sale date. Sale-time alarm duties sit in the Lofgren and Zander Memorial Act: working smoke alarms are a transfer condition (ORS 479.260 to 479.297), and a carbon monoxide alarm is a transfer condition only where the dwelling contains a statutory carbon monoxide source (a fuel-burning device, or an attached garage opening into living space, ORS 105.836(2), 105.838). An all-electric house with no attached garage therefore has no CO-alarm sale duty, and its alarms are documented anyway. Warranty, insurance, and resale value never expire, so "permanent" is the honest retention recommendation; the jurisdiction keeps its own official copies, but retrieval decades later is not guaranteed, which is why the owner's copy is the reliable one.

**Code**

```
The first sale of a never-occupied dwelling uses the permit-number statement.
  Full disclosure excluded only if the seller provides the statutory statement naming the building permit numbers and issuing jurisdiction, verbatim; later sales answer the full form from actual knowledge.
  Source: ORS 105.464; ORS 105.470; ORS 105.462-105.490.

Construction claims are barred ten years after substantial completion.
  Residential structures: ten years from the earliest of written acceptance, certificate of occupancy, or owner occupancy; the shield does not protect a person in possession and control when the claim accrues.
  Source: ORS 12.135(2)(b), (1)(e), (7)(b) (2025 edition numbering).

Alarms are sale conditions under the Lofgren and Zander Memorial Act.
  Working smoke alarms at transfer (ORS 479.260-479.297); a CO alarm at transfer only where a statutory CO source exists (fuel-burning device or attached garage communicating with living space).
  Source: ORS 105.836(2); ORS 105.838; ORS 105.844; ORS 479.260-479.297.
```

## 20.3 The Selling-Too-Soon Caution

The owner-builder exemption rests on owner-occupancy intent, so developing a house with intent to sell can convert the owner into a contractor requiring a license. This is a legal risk pattern to understand and route to an Oregon attorney, not a timeline to plan around here.

**Professional**

Oregon treats a property owner who develops a structure with intent to sell as a contractor or developer requiring CCB licensure; the owner-builder exemption depends on building for one's own occupancy, and the legal chapter documents that framework. Separately, where the owner-builder uses the narrow structural-code exemptions available under ORS 455.320 (for certain ceiling-height, room-size, and temperature items), the permit noting that noncompliance is recorded with the county clerk into the permanent deed record, so that record travels with the title and is kept accordingly. The manuscript states these statutes and the risk pattern; the question of how soon a particular house can be sold is a legal question routed to an Oregon attorney, because the time-window presumptions are fact-specific and change.

**Code**

```
Building with intent to sell can require contractor licensure.
  The owner-builder exemption rests on owner-occupancy intent; development for sale is CCB-licensed activity.
  Source: ORS 701.005; ORS 701.010 (see the legal chapter).

A structural-code exemption records into the deed.
  The permit noting the noncompliance is recorded with the county clerk and travels with the title.
  Source: ORS 455.320.
```

## 20.4 The Project Binder

The binder is one physical set of originals plus a full digital mirror, protected by a three-copy backup rule so a single fire or theft does not erase the house's history. It is organized by system and named consistently so a future user can find a record without the person who built it.

**Simple**

One paper binder holds the originals in a fire-resistant box. A digital copy mirrors it, and the digital copy follows the three-two-one rule: three copies, two kinds of storage, one away from the house. A fire that takes the house cannot be allowed to take the proof of the house with it.

**Applied**

- Keep one physical binder of originals (permits, certificate of occupancy, stamped plans, and warranties) in a fire-resistant box, and a full digital mirror organized by system folder (site, foundation, structure, envelope, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, energy, smart home, and administration).
- Follow the three-two-one backup rule: three copies, on two kinds of media, with one off-site (a local drive, a network drive, and one encrypted cloud copy).
- Name files by date, system, and document, and label every photo with location, date, and phase; the pre-cover photographs are the most valuable records.
- Record a narrated video walkthrough before insulation and again before drywall.
- Secure the digital archive (encryption, unique passwords with multi-factor authentication) and leave a "where everything is" note so the archive survives its creator.

**Professional**

No regulation sets a binder standard, so this is practice guidance built on the three-two-one backup principle, which exists for exactly the lightning strike or fire that destroys both the house server and the local backup at once. The digital mirror is organized so a contractor about to open a wall, a buyer's inspector, or the household's next steward can find the relevant record, and the owner-drawn as-builts (the utility trench routes with depths and photos, the septic and well coordinates, and the in-slab radiant and conduit maps) are cheap at build time and irreplaceable later. The binder is the owner handoff, and its extracts serve the audiences described later in the chapter.

**Code**

```
Panel circuit identification is the one required as-built.
  Circuit directory legibly identifying every circuit's purpose.
  Source: OESC/NEC 408.4 (2023 edition).

The rest of the record system is practice guidance.
  Three-two-one backup; physical originals in a fire-resistant box; owner-drawn as-builts (trench routes with depths, septic and well coordinates, in-slab radiant and conduit maps).
  Source: project documentation standard.
```

## 20.5 Warranty And Manufacturer Documentation

Equipment warranties condition coverage on correct installation, documented startup, and proof of required maintenance, so a one-page record per product keeps the coverage intact. Federal law also means owner installation by itself cannot void a warranty; a denial requires the warrantor to show the owner's work caused the defect.

**Simple**

A warranty claim is won or lost on paperwork assembled years earlier: the model, the serial, the receipt, the startup sheet, and the service log. Federal law adds a protection owner-builders should know. A company cannot void a warranty just because the owner installed the product or used an unbranded part. It has to show that the installation or part caused the failure.

**Applied**

For each major product, keep a one-page record with the model and serial, the purchase date and receipt, the installer and license number, the registration confirmation, the startup or commissioning sheet, and the service log. Register the product within the manufacturer's stated window, keep the installation to the manufacturer's instructions, and keep the maintenance records (annual HVAC service, water-heater flush and anode records) that the warranty requires.

**Professional**

Residential equipment warranties follow a pattern (confirmed per selected product at specification time): registration within a window often activates or extends coverage, coverage is conditioned on installation per instructions and documented startup (heat pumps in particular), and a claim requires the model, serial, proof of purchase, and installer identity. Written consumer warranties are governed by the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act (15 U.S.C. 2301-2312): a written warranty on a product over $10 is designated full or limited (2303), its terms are disclosed in plain language (2302(a), 16 CFR Parts 701 and 702), and the tie-in prohibition (2302(c), 16 CFR 700.10) bars conditioning coverage on branded parts or authorized service. Under the FTC's interpretive rule the warrantor may exclude defects caused by unauthorized parts or service, but denial requires demonstrating that causation, so owner installation alone is not lawful grounds to void coverage; the FTC has enforced this against warranty-void language repeatedly since 2018. Where a warranty conditions coverage on installation by a licensed professional and the certified startup, the practical answer is the hired startup visit the testing chapter already schedules, which satisfies the condition and documents it. The per-product record template belongs in the project-binder appendix.

**Code**

```
Written consumer warranties follow the federal warranty act.
  Full-or-limited designation (products over $10); plain-language disclosure of terms; pre-sale availability.
  Source: 15 U.S.C. 2302, 2303; 16 CFR Parts 701, 702.

A warranty cannot be conditioned on branded parts or authorized service.
  Tie-in sales prohibition; a warrantor may deny coverage only by demonstrating the unauthorized part or service caused the defect.
  Source: 15 U.S.C. 2302(c); 16 CFR 700.10.

The claim record is assembled at installation.
  Model, serial, purchase receipt, installer identity, registration confirmation, startup sheet, service log.
  Source: manufacturer warranty terms (verified per product at specification).
```

## 20.6 Maintenance: Replacement Horizons And The Recurring Calendar

Maintenance has two layers: the replacement horizons that say when a component reaches end of life, and the recurring tasks that keep components reaching that horizon. The horizons are anchored to an industry life-expectancy source and are guidelines, not warranties.

**Simple**

Everything in the house has a service life. The money for each replacement is easiest to find when it has been saved a little at a time. A water heater lasts about ten years, a heat pump fifteen to twenty, gutters about twenty. The concrete structure itself, properly built, has no replacement date.

**Applied**

Plan replacement against representative horizons (assuming recommended maintenance):

| Component | Representative horizon |
|---|---|
| Tank water heater | About 10 years (tankless 20 or more) |
| Furnace / heat pump / AC | 15 to 20 / about 16 / 10 to 15 years |
| Aluminum gutters | About 20 years (copper 50 or more) |
| Bituminous foundation waterproofing | About 10 years (and compromised immediately if cracked) |
| Security systems and heat/smoke detectors | 5 to 10 years |
| Concrete footings and foundation | Lifetime, if properly built |

Run the recurring calendar season by season: clean gutters twice a year (more under conifers), check the roof and flashing annually and after wind events, walk exterior sealants annually, survey concrete and masonry cracks annually with logged widths, verify footing-drain outlets and grading each fall, exercise plumbing shutoffs and the temperature-and-pressure valve annually, function-test pumps quarterly, test GFCI, AFCI, and smoke and CO devices monthly, change HVAC filters every one to three months, and confirm the radon fan monthly on its manometer.

**Professional**

The replacement horizons are drawn from the InterNACHI standard life-expectancy chart, which states plainly that its values assume recommended maintenance and are guidelines rather than warranties. One figure from that chart (a fifty-year life for wireless networks and home automation) is not used, because vendor support ends long before the hardware fails; the technology section states the real planning bands. The recurring-task intervals are practice guidance published as a season-by-season calendar. Roof flashing connections, not the roof surface, are the usual failure point, so the seasonal roof check focuses there; surge protective devices are sacrificial and are replaced after major events; and paint life is driven by preparation and product quality, so exposed elevations are inspected annually rather than on a fixed clock.

**Code**

```
The replacement horizons come from the industry life-expectancy chart.
  InterNACHI standard estimated life-expectancy chart; values assume recommended maintenance and are guidelines, not warranties; the chart's fifty-year network/automation figure is not used.
  Source: InterNACHI standard estimated life expectancy chart for homes.
```

## 20.7 Recurring Outside-Party Inspections

Several tests recur on a schedule set by a code, a utility, a warranty, or an insurer rather than by the owner, and Oregon's 2026 septic rules put alternative-system owners under a perpetual service contract. The program tracks who can demand proof and what proof satisfies them.

**Simple**

Some inspections never stop. An alternative septic system must stay under a professional service contract for its whole life. A sold house gives the new owner 30 days to get their own contract. Wells get tested at sale and yearly by good practice. Insurers increasingly ask for their own proof: roof age, wood-stove installs, wildfire work.

**Professional**

Backflow assemblies on a public connection are tested annually by a certified tester (from the testing chapter). Oregon's onsite-septic operation and maintenance rules were consolidated into OAR 340-071-0132 effective January 1, 2026. Alternative-treatment, sand-filter, and similar systems carry a perpetual, continuous service contract with no automatic expiration, and legacy contracts executed on or before December 31, 2025 expire December 31, 2027 unless they meet the new requirements. Owners of contracted systems no longer file annual reports or pay the annual report fee; that duty moved to the maintenance provider, with holding-tank owners still reporting. A sale, purchase, or contract termination opens a 30-day window to get under contract before the agent may order a compliance inspection with its fee. Well-water testing is mandatory at sale and recommended annually, and radon retesting follows the EPA and AARST cycle. Smoke and CO alarm maintenance follows the manufacturer and the statutes that apply at a rental or sale transition. A utility may run a backflow program or a net-metering interconnection inspection where solar is added, and a warranty may require annual professional HVAC service. Insurers in Oregon increasingly require wood-stove, roof-age, and wildfire-mitigation inspections; those are policy-level facts confirmed with the insurer.

**Code**

```
Alternative septic systems carry a perpetual service contract.
  Contract continuous until decommissioning, no automatic expiration; legacy contracts (executed on or before 2025-12-31) expire 2027-12-31 unless compliant; owner annual reports and fees ended (provider reports instead; holding tanks excepted); 30-day contract-transition window at sale, purchase, or termination before a compliance inspection may be ordered.
  Source: OAR 340-071-0132 (effective 2026-01-01); OAR 340-071-0140(7).

The other recurring schedules belong to their counterparties.
  Backflow annually (public connections, OAR 333-061-0070(6)); well testing at sale (ORS 448.271) and annually by practice; radon per the EPA/AARST cycle; warranty and insurer inspections per the contract and policy.
  Source: OAR 333-061-0070; ORS 448.271; policy and warranty terms (verified with the counterparty).
```

## 20.8 Budget Contingency And The Maintenance Reserve

A build carries a contingency reserve that shrinks as the design matures, and an owner-builder carries the high end because owner-builder overruns run larger than a professional builder's. Ongoing maintenance is funded by a component sinking fund built from the replacement horizons rather than by a single percentage.

**Simple**

The contingency is the honesty line in the budget. Early sketches get a 15-to-25-percent cushion, finished drawings 5 to 10, and construction itself 3 to 5. Owner-builders overrun more than professionals: up to about 30 percent, against 5 to 15. So the cushion is set at the high end and tracked in writing.

**Applied**

- Carry a design/feasibility contingency of roughly 15 to 25 percent early, stepping down to 5 to 10 percent at complete construction documents and 3 to 5 percent during construction.
- Structure the reserve as three labeled buckets: owner scope-change, construction-unknowns, and design, and track drawdowns as budget lines.
- Plan for the owner-builder overrun reality by carrying the high end of the range.
- Fund ongoing maintenance with a component sinking fund built from the replacement horizons (set aside each year the annual share of each component's replacement cost).

**Professional**

Standard planning contingency runs 5 to 10 percent for well-documented new construction and 10 to 20 percent for complex or high-risk work, higher at early design and lower as documents mature; the owner-builder evidence (professional-builder overruns of 5 to 15 percent versus owner-builder overruns up to 30 percent) supports carrying the high end. The three-bucket structure follows the guidance that a single undifferentiated contingency invites overruns and disputes. Schedule, rework, seasonal, and inspection-delay reserves have no citable percentage, so they are planned as mechanisms (pours and dry-in scheduled around the wet season, a one-inspection-cycle buffer per phase, and a predefined pause procedure) rather than numbers. The common one-to-four-percent-of-value maintenance reserve is not printed as a rule without a source; the defensible alternative is the component sinking fund, which the binder can compute honestly from the horizons.

**Code**

```
Contingency is planned by phase and spent on paper first.
  Roughly 15-25 percent at early design, 5-10 percent at complete documents, 3-5 percent during construction; owner-builder overruns (up to ~30 percent versus 5-15 for professionals) argue for the high end; three labeled buckets with tracked drawdowns.
  Source: construction-contingency planning references (industry ranges; see the chapter 20 sources in the research record).

The maintenance reserve is a component sinking fund.
  Annual set-aside equal to each component's replacement cost divided by its remaining horizon; computed from the replacement-horizon table.
  Source: project financial-planning standard (practice guidance).
```

## 20.9 Technology Replacement Cycles

Consumer network gear, hubs, cameras, and cloud-dependent devices reach end of life when vendor support and standards move on, not when the hardware wears out. The plan uses support-driven planning bands, checked at purchase.

**Simple**

Electronics in the house become obsolete when the vendor stops supporting them, not when the hardware wears out. The updates end, the standard moves on, and working hardware becomes unsafe or incompatible. So the plan budgets by support life. Network gear and cameras get five to eight years, hubs five to seven, backup batteries three to five.

**Professional**

Technology replacement is support-driven rather than wear-driven, and the planning bands are published with an "as of 2026, verify at purchase" tag rather than as fixed lifespans or year-specific product recommendations. Security systems and smoke and heat detectors run 5 to 10 years, hubs and controllers about 5 to 7 years (a 2019-or-earlier hub typically never received the current interoperability standard), uninterruptible-power-supply batteries 3 to 5 years, network video-recorder drives 3 to 5 years under continuous write, access points and routers and switches 5 to 8 years, cameras 5 to 8 years, and mains-powered smart switches 10 or more years. Locks are the split case: the hardware runs 10 or more years, the batteries run months, and the electronics are support-limited. The interoperability standard's own churn (a border-router certification rule that changed at the start of 2026) illustrates why the bands are support-driven and are re-checked at purchase.

**Code**

```
Technology end-of-life is support-driven.
  Planning bands (as of 2026, verified at purchase): security and detectors 5-10 years; hubs and controllers 5-7; UPS batteries and recorder drives 3-5; access points, routers, switches, and cameras 5-8; mains smart switches 10+; lock hardware 10+ with support-limited electronics.
  Source: vendor support-lifecycle practice (see the smart-home chapter's replacement reserve).
```

## 20.10 Smart-Home Backup And Vendor-Sunset Defense

The smart-home stack is protected by encrypted backups on the three-two-one rule, by local-control device choices that survive a vendor outage, and by keeping the pairing and account records that a rebuild needs. The detailed architecture is in the smart-home chapter; here it is the ownership-phase discipline.

**Applied**

- Run automated encrypted controller backups on the three-two-one rule, and test a full restore onto spare hardware once a year.
- Keep the controller configuration in version control (or rely on the controller's snapshot lineage).
- Choose devices that work locally (a local mesh radio or a local-API connection) so automations survive an internet or vendor outage, and prefer open-protocol or interoperability-certified devices.
- Save every device's pairing and setup codes in a password manager, and record each device's account and firmware version in the binder.
- Never expose the controller directly to the internet; use a VPN or the controller's own cloud relay for remote access, with multi-factor authentication.

**Professional**

A mature local-first controller ships automated encrypted scheduled backups with off-site targets and restores across hardware, which makes the three-two-one rule practical for the smart-home stack. The vendor-sunset defenses are to prefer interoperability-certified or open-protocol devices with local fallback, to save the pairing codes that a device rebuild requires, and to record the account and firmware state so the archive survives the vendor. The network segmentation and the consumer-IoT security baseline carry over from the smart-home and testing chapters.

**Code**

```
The smart-home stack is backed up and rebuildable.
  Automated encrypted controller backups on the three-two-one rule; annual restore test onto spare hardware; pairing codes in a password manager; account and firmware state recorded; no direct internet exposure.
  Source: practice guidance (see the smart-home chapter); NIST IR 8425 consumer-IoT baseline.
```

## 20.11 Future-Upgrade Provisions

The cheapest future upgrade is the one the build left room for. Spare conduit, reserved panel capacity, kept-clear roof and wall zones, and accessible shutoffs turn a future addition into a connection instead of a demolition.

**Simple**

An empty conduit costs a few dollars at the pour and thousands to add through finished concrete. So the build leaves paths. Spare conduit runs to the attic, garage, roof, and mechanical pad, with labeled spare breaker spaces and a clear south-roof zone for solar. None of this is required for this house; all of it is cheap insurance.

**Applied**

Leave these provisions:

- Spare conduit: at least a 1-inch run from the panel area to the attic and to the crawl space or basement, 2-inch sweeps from the service area to the garage (EV), roof (solar), and exterior mechanical pad, and site conduits to any gate or outbuilding trench while it is open.
- Service and panel capacity: size the service and leave labeled reserved breaker spaces for EV charging, a heat pump, and a battery.
- Solar and battery readiness: a penetration-free, reinforced south-roof mounting zone documented on the as-builts, and reserved wall space with clearances by the panel for an inverter and battery.
- A ventilated network-rack location on a dedicated circuit with conduit home-runs and spare patch capacity.
- Accessible shutoffs and listed equipment clearances documented on the emergency map, spare capped roof penetrations where a future flue is plausible, a water-treatment bypass loop with a drain and power stub, and an equipment-swap path sized into the doors and halls.
- Accessibility provisions: a zero-step-capable entry, grab-bar blocking in bathroom walls, and 36-inch door rough openings where they are cheap.

**Professional**

These are voluntary provisions: no EV-ready or solar-ready requirement reaches a one- and two-family dwelling under the 2023 ORSC. Oregon's EV-ready statute (HB 2180, 2021) is implemented through the structural code for its covered building types, the ORSC's Chapter 11 contains no EV or solar content, and the model solar-ready appendix is affirmatively not adopted and is preempted from local adoption. Everything in this section is therefore the project's own choice rather than compliance. Empty conduit and reserved capacity cost little during construction and a great deal to retrofit through finished concrete, and the photo archive and as-builts are themselves the provision that makes any future remodel start from knowledge. Bidirectional charging and utility demand-response are moving targets, so the chapter's job is the conduit and the panel capacity, not the product.

**Code**

```
No EV-ready or solar-ready requirement reaches this dwelling type.
  HB 2180 (2021) EV-readiness is implemented through the structural code for its covered occupancies; 2023 ORSC Chapter 11 contains no EV or solar provisions; the solar-ready appendix is not adopted and is preempted from local adoption.
  Source: HB 2180 (2021) and its OSSC implementation; ORSC R101.2.3.2 and Chapter 11 (2023 edition).

The provisions themselves are practice guidance.
  Spare conduit, labeled reserved panel capacity, solar and battery zones, rack location, accessible shutoffs, and accessibility blocking, documented on the as-builts.
  Source: project design standard (voluntary).
```

## 20.12 Documentation For Future Parties And Handling A Pause

The binder produces a tailored extract for each future party, and a pause or incident is handled by process (weather-in, secure, document, and notify) with every decision routed to its professional. The book teaches the procedure, not the outcome.

**Simple**

Different people need different slices of the record. A buyer needs the permits and test results. A contractor needs the photos of the wall about to be opened. A firefighter needs one laminated page at the panel showing every shutoff. If the house is ever rented, Oregon's new well-testing duties for landlords come with it.

**Applied**

Provide the right extract to each party:

- Buyers: the disclosure-supporting set (permits and certificate of occupancy, well and septic records, radon results, warranty transfers, and manuals).
- Tenants: an operating subset (system operation, filter schedule, emergency shutoffs, and alarm testing), plus the well-testing notices where the rental sits on an exempt well.
- Contractors: the as-builts, panel schedule, and the photo archive of the wall about to be opened.
- Emergency responders: a laminated one-page map at the panel showing the gas, water, and power shutoffs, the solar rapid-shutdown, the battery location, and the septic and well locations.
- Inspectors, insurers, and appraisers: the permits and approved plans, and the construction-quality dossier of specifications, engineering, photos, and receipts.

For a pause: weather-in and secure the structure, drain or winterize as needed, photograph the condition, notify the insurer, lender, and building official factually, and calendar the permit deadlines. For a failed inspection, get the correction in writing (the code section cited), fix it, re-inspect, and document.

**Professional**

The editorial rule is to describe processes and factual triggers, not outcomes or entitlements, and to route each domain to its professional: sale timing, liability, and liens to an Oregon attorney; insurance coverage during a vacancy to the insurer in writing; lender draw and completion terms to the lender; and injuries to medical care first and then the factual reporting duties. If the house is ever rented on its exempt well, the landlord well-testing law applies (HB 3525, Oregon Laws 2025, chapter 574; ORS 90.321). The landlord tests the well: arsenic within 30 days of a new well, each listed contaminant at least annually, with a four-year pause earned by clean results. Results go to the tenant within 30 days, the pre-rental written notice is given, and the failed-test retest and health-notice rules apply. The duty is operative January 1, 2027, with a catch-up test before June 1, 2027 for units already rented. The book teaches the mothball procedure, the inspection-failure hygiene, and the records habit that makes any later consultation cheaper; it does not give medical, employment, or financial-planning advice beyond building the pause into the contingency structure.

**Code**

```
Renting on an exempt well brings the landlord testing duties.
  Arsenic within 30 days of a new well; each contaminant at least annually, with a four-year pause after qualifying clean tests; results to the tenant within 30 days; pre-rental written notice; failed-test retesting and health handout; operative January 1, 2027, catch-up before June 1, 2027.
  Source: HB 3525 (Oregon Laws 2025, chapter 574); ORS 90.321.

The pause and extract procedures are practice guidance.
  Audience extracts (buyer, tenant, contractor, responder, insurer); pause procedure (weather-in, secure, winterize, photograph, notify, calendar); professional routing for sale, liability, insurance, and lender questions.
  Source: project documentation standard.
```
