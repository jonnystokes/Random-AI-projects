# Chapter 8: The Wall System, Foundation To Roofline

This chapter specifies the assembly of the exterior wall: what gets built, in what order, from the first course above the foundation to the bond beam (the reinforced, grouted top course that ties the wall together) below the roof. It contains:

- what the wall must do (structure, moisture, thermal, in one assembly)
- the single-wythe default wall, first course to bond beam
- the double-wythe cavity wall, the owner's complete alternative path
- owner-made wall systems beyond both paths, and their code route
- mortar, grout, ties, the cavity, insulation, and movement joints
- openings, attachments, the base detail, and the roofline
- the full wall section as a reference detail, the known failure modes, and the code entries

Two walls are specified. The single-wythe reinforced concrete-block wall (a wythe is one vertical thickness of masonry) with exterior insulation is the project default; it has a prescriptive code path. The double-wythe cavity wall (concrete block inside, brick outside, insulation and a drainage gap between, steel ties across) is the complete alternative path; no prescriptive path exists for it, and it is designed by a licensed structural engineer from the first drawing. The engineering of both walls is in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter. The control-layer physics is in the Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, And Radon chapter. The hand skills of laying are in the Masonry Craft chapter. Unit manufacture and testing are in the Owner-Made Blocks And Alternative Concretes chapter. This chapter contains the assembly: the order, the interfaces, and the details that make the parts one wall.

## 8.1 What The Wall Must Do

**Simple**

What does an exterior wall have to do besides hold up the roof? Three functions run at once. It carries the roof and its own weight down to the foundation. It keeps rain and ground moisture out of the structure. It slows heat flow between indoors and outdoors. In this house, each function belongs to a named layer. The masonry carries load. A drained gap behind the outer face collects water and returns it outside. A continuous insulation layer slows heat. Each section below names the chapter that contains that layer's science. This chapter states where each layer sits and when it goes in.

Oregon splits the moisture problem in two. West of the Cascades, walls stay wet for months at a time. East of the Cascades, walls are drier but freeze harder and more often. Both sides need the same wall. The sections below state where the emphasis shifts.

**Professional**

Structure. The wall resists gravity load from the roof and floors, plus out-of-plane wind and seismic load. It delivers both to the foundation. The single-wythe wall follows the prescriptive reinforced-masonry provisions. The double-wythe wall is engineered under TMS 402/602 with no prescriptive option. Load paths, reinforcement schedules, and engineering triggers are in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter (Reinforced Masonry Walls; What Must Be Engineered).

Moisture. Both walls are drainage walls rather than barrier walls. A barrier wall depends on its face material stopping all water at the surface. A drainage wall assumes some water passes the face; a drained gap intercepts it and returns it outside through flashing and weeps. The Brick Industry Association's Technical Note 28D is the industry reference for brick veneer over block backing. It states the double-wythe premise: water that penetrates the brick runs down its back face to flashing at the bottom of the air space and drains out. The single-wythe default performs the same function with a ventilated rainscreen gap behind the cladding, detailed in the Envelope chapter (Above-Grade Wall Assembly).

Thermal. Both walls place continuous insulation outboard of the structural masonry. The placement holds the mass at conditioned temperature and preserves its heat-storage value. Whole-wall targets, material selection, and the thermal-mass reasoning are in the Envelope chapter (Wall Insulation Strategy).

Climate emphasis. The Willamette Valley and the coast (ORSC climate zone 4C) deliver more rainfall and more days of wall saturation. There, the drainage detailing dominates: functioning weeps, a clear cavity, correct flashing (§8.7). The high desert east of the Cascades (zone 5B) delivers fewer wet days and harder freeze cycles. There, freeze-thaw-rated materials and absorption control dominate: Grade SW brick, water-repellent admixtures, penetrating sealers (§8.3, §8.15). Both regions require the same fundamentals. The difference is which failure mode is statistically more likely at a given site.

## 8.2 The Single-Wythe Default, First Course To Bond Beam

**Simple**

The default wall is one thickness of reinforced concrete block. Insulation attaches to its outside face. Cladding hangs on furring strips over the insulation, with a ventilated gap behind it. The block is the structure; every other layer attaches to the block. For most configurations this wall has a prescriptive code path (standard code tables approved at plan review without an engineer's calculations). That is why it is the project default. The exceptions that still require an engineer are listed in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter (What Must Be Engineered).

**Applied**

Build the default wall in this order. Each step names the chapter that specifies its values.

1. Verify the foundation interface before the first course: stem-wall elevation, level, dowel positions, tolerances. The interface values are in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement chapter.
2. Mark the layout on the foundation: corners, every opening, and every control-joint location. A movement joint not planned before the wall goes up cannot be added cleanly afterward (§8.9).
3. Dry-lay the first course to prove the module: whole blocks at corners and openings, no cut slivers. Coursing arithmetic is in §8.3.
4. Lay the first course in a full mortar bed of Type S mortar. The mortar rules are in §8.5.
5. Build the corner leads (built-up corners that set line and level), then fill between them course by course to a string line. Ladder-type joint reinforcement goes into the bed joints (the horizontal mortar layers) at the spacing on the structural drawings. Laying technique is in the Masonry Craft chapter.
6. Set vertical rebar and grout the cores in lifts. Lift limits, cleanouts, and inspection triggers are in §8.5.
7. Set the lintel (the beam over an opening) as the wall reaches each opening's head height (§8.12).
8. Lay the bond-beam course at the top of the wall. Grout it with the roof-anchorage embeds set in the wet grout, not drilled in afterward (§8.13).
9. Attach the envelope layers last, in plane order: barrier on block, insulation on barrier, furring to block, cladding on furring. Selection and detailing are in the Envelope chapter.

**Professional**

The neighboring chapters keep their scopes on the default wall. Reinforcement schedule, grout strength, stack-bond provisions, and GFRP scope: the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter. Water, air, vapor, and thermal control layers: the Envelope chapter. On this wall, all four control layers attach in one plane sequence outboard of the block. The assembly question reduces to attachment order and fastener penetration management, both detailed in the Envelope chapter (Above-Grade Wall Assembly). This chapter's sections on mortar and grout (§8.5), movement joints (§8.9), openings (§8.12), attachments (§8.13), and problems (§8.15) apply to both walls' block wythes equally.

## 8.3 The Double-Wythe Cavity Wall: The Owner's Alternative Path

This wall is an engineered path. No prescriptive code tables exist for it, and every structural value in it comes from the project's engineer of record. The sections below state what the assembly is, what is settled by industry standards, and which decisions the engineer makes.

**Simple**

The alternative wall is two masonry walls a few inches apart, built as one system. The inner wall is reinforced concrete block; it carries the structure. The outer wall is brick; it carries only its own weight (the trade term for such a facing wall is veneer). Corrosion-resistant steel ties cross the gap and hold the two together against wind and earthquake. Insulation boards sit in the gap against the block. At least one inch of the gap stays open. Water that gets past the brick falls down the open gap to flashing at the bottom. It leaves through weep holes (small openings left in the brick joints). The owner can make the inner blocks (the Owner-Made Blocks And Alternative Concretes chapter). The owner can stack both wythes (the Masonry Craft chapter). The engineering is not owner work.

### What The Brick Is, Structurally

The largest design decision in this wall is the structural classification of the outer brick wythe. The engineer of record makes it and states it on the structural drawings. TMS 402 permits three readings:

- **Anchored veneer.** The brick resists no load except its own self-weight and transfers lateral load to the block backing, which resists the full load alone. Tie design follows the veneer tie tables. The Brick Industry Association states this is the classification of nearly all multi-wythe masonry walls built today. Its Technical Note 28D is written around it.
- **Noncomposite (cavity) wall.** Each wythe is individually engineered to resist a share of the load in proportion to its stiffness. Tie spacing and air-space limits tighten: the maximum air space drops to 4-1/2 inches, against 6-5/8 inches for veneer.
- **Composite wall.** Both wythes act as one structural section. That requires ties or masonry headers engineered to transfer shear across the joint between them. BIA Technical Note 28D reports masonry headers are rarely used in contemporary construction. They lack ductility, they restrain the differential movement of §8.9, and they conduct water across the wall.

This project plans the anchored-veneer reading: the best-documented path, the most commonly permitted, and the one the free industry literature is written for. The engineer of record confirms or changes the classification. The choice is a design decision, not a fact fixed by the materials.

### The Build Order

**Applied**

1. Foundation, brick shelf, and base flashing first, per §8.11. The air space below the lowest flashing is filled solid; the lowest flashing sits above finished grade.
2. Build the inner block wythe: the default-wall sequence (§8.2, steps 2 through 8), with two additions. The bed-joint ladder reinforcement carries the eye side of the adjustable ties (§8.6). The cavity face gets the air/water barrier where the drawings require one (Envelope chapter).
3. Fasten the insulation boards to the block's cavity face, staggered and taped per the Envelope chapter. Keep a minimum 1-inch clear air space to the future brick line.
4. Lay the brick veneer from the base flashing up. Place weeps immediately above the flashing. Hook the pintles (the hook halves of the two-piece ties) into the eyes (the loop halves in the block joints) at the scheduled spacing. Keep the cavity clean as the courses rise (§8.7). The tie system is two-piece, so the brick can rise after the block wythe is complete.
5. Detail every opening per §8.12 and every movement joint per §8.9. Mark both joint grids on the foundation before the first course of either wythe.
6. Terminate the top of the wall per §8.13.

### One 8-Inch Grid For Two Materials

**Professional**

Concrete block and modular brick share one vertical module. A nominal 8-inch block course equals exactly three brick courses with their bed joints. The Concrete Masonry and Hardscapes Association's modular-layout guidance (TEK 05-12) states the coordination rule. Both wythes are dimensioned to a single 8-inch vertical grid. Every feature that must land on a full course in both materials is set at a whole multiple of 8 inches above the floor datum. That list includes the foundation top, window heads and sills, the bond-beam soffit, and the eave or parapet line. Three brick courses and one block course then resolve together at every such line.

Course alignment is a structural requirement. The adjustable tie's maximum vertical offset between its pieces is 1-1/4 inches (§8.6). Wythes whose coursing drifts apart over the wall height exceed the offset and void the tie design. Masons hold alignment two ways: the grid arithmetic, and fine-tuning bed-joint thickness within tolerance at specific courses. Setting the actual elevations is a drafting task. The architect performs it once the finish-floor elevation, opening heights, and roof line are fixed.

### The Brick Itself

**Professional**

The exterior wythe is specified as Grade SW (severe weathering) brick under ASTM C216. The ASTM brick standards recommend Grade SW wherever the weathering index is 50 or greater. The index map is inside the purchased standard. The project confirms the requirement through the brick supplier or the building department. BIA Technical Note 28D states Grade SW is the default for most of the United States. It cautions against Grade MW on exterior faces even where a map might permit it: limited availability and mix-up risk on site outweigh the saving. For this project, Grade SW is the exterior specification on both sides of the Cascades. The freeze-thaw exposure east of the mountains is the bounding case (§8.1).

## 8.4 Beyond The Two Paths: Owner-Made Wall Systems And Their Code Route

Owner-made materials open wall options past both standard paths: mortarless interlocking compressed earth block, structural ferrocement, and hybrids. None of them has a prescriptive Oregon path. All of them route through the same code mechanism, and this section states that mechanism, the testing that stands behind it, and which materials belong in the load path. The full material comparison (durability data, seismic literature, makeability, cost) is in the Owner-Made Blocks And Alternative Concretes chapter.

**Simple**

The code contains an approval route for materials it does not list. The building official can approve any material or method proven equivalent to what the code prescribes. Equivalent means as strong, as durable, as fire-resistant, and as safe. The applicant supplies the proof, usually engineered testing. Approval is case by case and jurisdiction by jurisdiction. None of this applies to reinforced concrete block; it is already in the code. Earth block and ferrocement require all of it.

**Professional**

*Interlocking dry-stack compressed earth block (CEB).* A mortarless interlocking wall has no joint bond strength, only friction and mechanical interlock, so a standard mortared-prism test does not describe it. The published research path adapts ASTM C1314 (masonry prism compression) by stacking dry-interlocked units. It separately tests the dry bed joints for shear and friction capacity. Unit strength is tested to ASTM D1633 (molded soil-cement cylinders), the correct standard for stabilized earth block; ASTM C90 governs conventional concrete units. The closest adopted precedent in the United States is New Mexico's Earthen Building Materials Code (14.7.4 NMAC). It permits dry-stacked compressed earth block with demonstrated adhesion, a 10-inch minimum wall thickness, and mandatory exterior stucco and interior plaster. It describes a water-cohesion method closer to adobe practice than to a mechanically interlocking system, and it is New Mexico law, not Oregon's. An interlocking CEB wall in Oregon requires project-specific engineering plus the adapted testing above, submitted through the alternative-materials provision.

*Structural ferrocement.* Ferrocement is a thin cement-mortar shell reinforced with layers of small-diameter wire mesh. Compiled values from the engineering references and the one national code chapter located (Bangladesh National Building Code, Chapter 12, Ferrocement Structures):

- cement-to-sand ratio: 1:1.5 to 1:3 by volume; 1:1.5 to 1:2 for loadbearing work
- water-cement ratio: 0.35 to 0.60; 0.40 to 0.45 recommended for structural work
- mesh: wire 0.5 to 1.5 mm diameter at 5 to 25 mm openings; most structural work uses 0.5 to 1.0 mm wire at 5 to 10 mm openings, in multiple layers separated by small-diameter skeleton rods
- section thickness: 10 to 60 mm
- structural benchmark: 35 N/mm2 (roughly 5,000 psi) cylinder strength at 28 days, per the Bangladesh chapter

None of these sources is an adopted U.S. standard. ACI 549 is the U.S. committee document; it is checked before any ferrocement value is treated as controlling. In Oregon, a loadbearing ferrocement element has the same status as interlocking earth block: engineered design plus alternative-materials approval.

*The ferrocement-formwork hybrid.* A ferrocement shell used as stay-in-place formwork, filled or backed with owner-made concrete, is buildable in principle, with precedent in non-loadbearing work (tanks, hulls). No code path or standard test protocol for it as a wall system was located. Proving it would require three tests: compression of witness panels (test panels built alongside the real work, per ASTM C1314 adapted to the composite section), interface shear between shell and fill, and water penetration where the shell is the finished face (§8.15). It is a creative option that requires project-specific engineering and testing; it is not a proven system.

*What belongs in the load path.* Conventional reinforced concrete block, owner-made or purchased, has a settled code path, decades of documented performance, and no special testing burden. It is built to the prescriptive provisions or engineered under TMS 402. The earthen and ferrocement alternatives have real engineering literature and no Oregon path. Either one adds engineering and testing cost, and schedule risk, that the owner weighs against the appeal of the material. This project keeps reinforced concrete block in the load path. The alternatives are candidates for non-structural and outbuilding use until proven. The weighing is in the Owner-Made Blocks And Alternative Concretes chapter.

**Applied**

Ask the building department two questions, in writing, before designing around any non-listed material:

1. Whether an ASTM C1314-based prism test on the specific unit and mix is acceptable strength evidence.
2. Whether a stamped engineer's evaluation suffices, or a third-party ICC-ES-style report is required.

Record the answers in the project binder before buying molds or mixing test batches.

**Code**

```
Alternative materials, designs, and methods may be approved outside the prescriptive code.
  The building official approves an alternative found satisfactory and equivalent to the code's requirements in quality, strength, effectiveness, fire resistance, durability, and safety, and has the authority to require tests as evidence of compliance.
  Source: ORSC R104.11.
```

## 8.5 Mortar And Grout

**Simple**

Mortar and grout look alike and are not the same material. Mortar is stiff, so a block or brick stays put the moment it is set. Grout is fluid, so it can flow down a narrow block core and wrap the steel inside. Ordinary concrete is neither: its coarse gravel jams in a block core. The three are not interchangeable. One documented owner-builder mistake sits here: packing block cores with leftover mortar instead of grout. The paragraphs below state what fails when that happens.

### Mortar By Location

**Professional**

| Wall location | Mortar type | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Inner block wythe (loadbearing, seismic system) | Type S (Type M acceptable) | ORSC R606.2.8.3 excludes Type N from the lateral-force-resisting system in Seismic Design Category D0-D2; sourced Code entry in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter (Reinforced Masonry Walls) |
| Brick veneer wythe | Type N preferred by BIA Technical Note 28D for veneer flexibility and workability; unresolved seismic conflict, below | Engineer of record resolves before mortar is ordered |
| Below-grade and earth-contact courses | Type M or S | Masonry-industry practice for strength and lower absorption against soil moisture |
| Bond-beam bed joints | Type S | Same wythe, same seismic basis |

The veneer mortar conflict: BIA Technical Note 28D prefers Type N for veneer because a veneer carries only its own weight. Type N's flexibility accommodates the differential movement of §8.9 better than the stiffer Type S does. The same document reports that the TMS code prohibits all Type N mortar in Seismic Design Categories D and E. Most of western Oregon is SDC D1 or D2 (§8.10). Whether the prohibition reaches a true anchored veneer, which itself carries no seismic load, is a TMS 402 interpretation. The engineer of record makes it for this project. Until then, the veneer mortar type is an open specification.

### Grout: The Material And Its Placement

**Professional**

Grout is ASTM C476 material placed at 8 to 11 inches of slump (the settlement of a fresh mix, a fluidity measure); mortar is ASTM C270 material placed stiff. The slump difference is the function. Grout is proportioned with more water than hydration needs, because the surrounding block is absorptive. The block wicks the excess water out within minutes of placement. The grout ends up at a lower effective water-cement ratio in place. Reducing the mix water to make placement tidier removes the flow that lets the grout reach and encase the rebar.

ASTM C476 defines two grout types. Fine grout (cement, sand, water) is required where the grout space is narrow or the cells are congested with reinforcement. The reason is physical: coarse aggregate bridges across narrow gaps and wedges against bars. Coarse grout (with pea-gravel aggregate) is permitted where the space allows it and uses less cement per volume. The standard's exact cell-dimension thresholds are in the purchased text. The engineer of record or the masonry supplier states which grout each cell condition requires, and the value goes on the drawings.

Placement limits are sourced in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter (Reinforcement, Grout, And Lap Lengths) and restated here because the assembly sequence turns on them: 8-foot maximum lift; cleanouts at the wall base once a pour exceeds 5 feet; special inspection during grouting once a pour exceeds 8 feet. The lift limit is physical. A tall column of fluid grout exerts hydrostatic pressure that can exceed the bond strength of fresh mortar joints. The joint fails and grout blows out through the face of the wall.

Every lift is consolidated (worked with a rod or vibrator to close air voids), then reconsolidated 10 to 15 minutes after placement. By then the block has drawn off part of the mix water and the grout has begun to settle. A single pass is not sufficient; the water loss itself opens the voids the second pass closes. Grout placed and never reconsolidated leaves a shrinkage gap at the top of the cell. An exposed top-of-cell gap becomes a channel that carries water straight down a grouted core (§8.13). A numeric shrinkage percentage for C476 grout was not verifiable in the free literature; the manufacturer's data sheet supplies it if the number is needed.

*The mortar-in-cores mistake.* Stiff mortar packed into cores in place of grout cannot flow around the bar or into the cell corners. It bridges and leaves voids. Three failures follow. The bar loses its bond, so the wall never develops the design tension capacity. The voids against unprotected steel become corrosion initiation points. And mortar is not an ASTM C476 material, so an inspector who finds the substitution has grounds to reject the work outright.

### Self-Consolidating Grout

**Professional**

Self-consolidating grout flows into the cell and consolidates around the rebar under its own weight. No rodding or vibration pass is needed. For a one-person crew that cannot reliably vibrate every lift, that is the entire case for it. Two quality-control measures govern it. Slump flow is the spread of the mix on a flat plate; the target is roughly 24 to 30 inches. The visual stability index is a 0-to-3 rating of whether aggregate and paste stay mixed during the spread; the target is 1 or less. The risks are cost and segregation. The flow comes from admixture dosing rather than added water, at a price premium per volume. A field-mixed batch without plant-grade proportioning control can segregate and defeat its own purpose. The practical split for this project: self-consolidating grout at tall lifts, congested cells, and bond-beam pours where rodding access is worst; conventional grout with disciplined reconsolidation everywhere else. A 2026 price premium was not sourced in the research; the ready-mix supplier quotes it before the grout schedule is fixed.

### Weather Windows

**Professional**

TMS 602 sets the temperature triggers for masonry work. The values below come from industry summaries of the purchased standard. They are confirmed against the primary text, or the masonry contractor's standard practice, before they are relied on as exact thresholds.

- Cold: below 40°F ambient, sand or water is heated so the mortar stays between 40 and 120°F. Below 20°F, enclosures and supplemental heat keep the work area above freezing. After construction, the masonry is held above 32°F for 24 hours; 48 hours for grouted masonry unless the grout cement is Type III.
- Hot: above 100°F, or above 90°F with wind over 8 mph, a hot-weather procedure submittal is triggered. Above 115°F (or 105°F with the same wind), equipment is shaded. Above a 100°F mean daily temperature, new masonry is fog-sprayed at least three times a day for three days.

For an Oregon owner-builder the cold trigger is a normal seasonal scheduling constraint. The hot triggers are realistic east of the Cascades in summer. The build calendar in the Build Sequence And Site Coordination chapter accounts for both.

## 8.6 Ties And Anchorage

**Simple**

A wall tie is a short piece of corrosion-resistant steel crossing the gap between the wythes. Ties serve three functions. They connect the wythes. They transfer wind and earthquake push and pull from the brick to the block. And they let the wythes slide slightly against each other, because brick and block move in opposite directions over the years (§8.9). When ties corrode away, nothing holds the brick to the structure.

**Professional**

*Tie system for this wall.* BIA Technical Note 28D recommends ladder-type or tab-type joint reinforcement with adjustable two-piece ties for brick veneer over block backing. Corrugated Z-ties are for solid units only; they are not used on hollow block. Truss-type joint reinforcement is recommended against for this wall. Its diagonal cross wires conflict with the vertical bars and grout in a reinforced inner wythe. Its horizontal stiffness can restrain the wythes into bowing.

*Prescriptive tie schedules by air space* (BIA Technical Note 28D Tables 1 and 2), for adjustable ties:

| Air space | Adjustable-tie minimums | Wall area per tie |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 4-5/8 in. | W1.7 wire; pintle wire W2.8; one pintle leg minimum; 1-1/4 in. maximum vertical offset | 2.67 sq ft |
| Over 4-5/8 to 6-5/8 in. | W2.8 wire; two pintle legs minimum; barrel ties 1/4 in. outside diameter minimum; 2 in. maximum from the brick's inside face to the end of the adjustable part | 2.67 sq ft |
| Over 6-5/8 in. | No prescriptive table; ties are engineered | Engineered |

Non-adjustable tie coverage, per tie:

- W1.7 wire: 2.67 square feet
- W2.8 wire: 3.50 square feet
- sheet-metal unit ties (0.06 inch thick, 7/8 inch wide minimum): 3.50 square feet
- joint reinforcement alone, air space 4-5/8 to 6-5/8 inches: 1.77 square feet

*Spacing.* Maximum 32 inches on center horizontally and 24 inches on center vertically. The 24 is the code value; BIA's text reads 25 inches, and the discrepancy goes to the engineer of record at plan review. Openings larger than 16 inches get additional ties: within 12 inches of the opening, at a maximum of 3 feet on center around it. In Seismic Design Category D and above, the tributary wall area per tie is reduced; the exact reduction is in the purchased TMS 402 text (§8.10).

*Embedment.* Every tie leg is embedded at least 1-1/2 inches in the brick bed joint. Mortar cover from the exterior face is 5/8 inch minimum. A tie not fully embedded sheds its load to the neighboring ties that are, and the overload propagates. Embedment discipline is a laying-technique subject in the Masonry Craft chapter.

*Corrosion.* The cavity is designed to get wet, so every tie stays damp for its service life. Galvanizing is a sacrificial coating with a finite life. When it is consumed or damaged, the carbon steel underneath rusts. Rust occupies more volume than steel, and the expanding tie cracks the bed joint it sits in. The diagnostic signature is horizontal cracking at regular intervals matching the tie spacing. Enough failed ties and the veneer loses its out-of-plane support: bowing, then detachment. The specification that removes the mechanism is stainless steel (ASTM A580 Type 304 or 316) throughout. Stainless and carbon-steel components are not mixed in one assembly; the pairing corrodes galvanically (contact between dissimilar metals accelerates corrosion). The material table is in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter (Wall Ties For Cavity Walls).

*Water conduction.* The two-piece tie geometry is itself the water break: the mechanical gap between eye and pintle interrupts the solid metal path from wet wythe to dry wythe. An integral drip on the tie is product-specific. The manufacturer's literature is checked before the order, and the check is recorded with the submittals.

**Code**

```
Brick veneer must be anchored to its backing with ties at limited spacing.
  Maximum 32 inches on center horizontally and 24 inches on center vertically.
  Source: ORSC R703.8.4.1.

Openings must have additional veneer ties around them.
  Ties within 12 inches of openings larger than 16 inches, spaced not more than 3 feet on center around the opening perimeter.
  Source: ORSC R703.8.4.1.1.
```

## 8.7 The Cavity: Air Gap, Drainage, Weeps, And Flashing

**Simple**

The gap between the wythes is the wall's drainage system. Wind-driven rain passes through brick. The design assumes this. Water that passes the brick runs down its back face and lands on flashing (a waterproof sheet crossing the bottom of the gap). It leaves through weep holes above the flashing. The system works only if the gap stays open top to bottom. Mortar droppings that pile up on the flashing, or smear across the gap, give water a bridge to the block wythe. Keeping the cavity clean while laying is a hand skill in the Masonry Craft chapter. This section states the dimensions and the details.

**Professional**

*Air space.* Code minimum 1 inch. BIA Technical Note 28D recommends 2 inches minimum, because narrower spaces are hard to keep clean and to tool without dragging mortar across. Maximum without engineered ties: 6-5/8 inches for veneer, 4-1/2 inches for a noncomposite wall. Where insulation occupies part of the cavity, a nominal 1-inch clear air space remains between the insulation face and the back of the brick.

*Flashing locations.* Flashing is installed at:

- the base of the wall
- above and below every opening
- shelf angles
- the tops of walls
- beneath copings
- every other interruption of the air space

This project's block-backed veneer needs no shelf angle below roughly 50 feet of height. The working list is the wall base, every window and door head and sill, and the top-of-wall detail (§8.13).

*Flashing extent.* The vertical leg extends at least 8 inches up the block backing. It terminates in a bed joint of the inner wythe or a reglet (a slot cut for flashing) in concrete. Laps are 6 inches minimum, sealed. Where a flashing run is not continuous, its ends turn up at least 1 inch into a head joint as end dams. The outer edge extends to the face of the brick at minimum, preferably past it as a drip. Below the lowest flashing, the air space is filled solid with mortar or grout; no open cavity exists below grade. Flashing and weeps sit far enough above finished grade (the code allows up to 10 inches) that landscaping or regrading cannot bury them.

*Weeps.* Code: minimum 3/16-inch diameter, maximum 33 inches on center, located immediately above the flashing. BIA recommendation for this project: open-head-joint weeps (a brick head joint left free of mortar), at least 2 inches high, at 24 inches on center, with insect screens. Placement is exact. Weeps one or two courses above the flashing line do not drain the flashing they were meant to serve. Rope wicks and weep tubes are the named alternatives where an open joint is unacceptable in appearance.

*Mortar droppings.* Droppings become a failure when they accumulate into a pile or smear touching both wythes. Mortar is absorptive; a continuous bridge wicks water across by capillary action (water pulled through fine pores by surface tension) instead of letting it fall to the weeps. The same bridging happens at a single tie carrying enough squeezed-out mortar. Workmanship is the primary control: a bed joint beveled away from the cavity, a cavity board (a batten drawn up the gap as courses rise) that catches droppings, and disciplined joint tooling. All three are Masonry Craft chapter subjects. Mortar-collection devices in the cavity are secondary. They do not remove the workmanship requirement, and they can move the bridging higher in the wall. BIA Technical Note 28D notes many device manufacturers require the flashing at least 6 to 8 inches above the top of the collection device, so the protection holds when droppings accumulate on the device.

**Applied**

The drainage sequence, in build order. A step out of order breaks the chain.

1. Install the flashing first: fully supported, leg up the backing, terminated into a bed joint or reglet.
2. Form end dams at every discontinuity, both ends of every sill, head, and interrupted base run.
3. Extend the flashing edge to or past the brick face as a drip.
4. Place weeps immediately above the flashing at the scheduled spacing. Screen them. Leave them open through construction.
5. Keep the cavity above clear of droppings for the full height of the wall.

**Code**

```
The veneer wall must drain through weepholes.
  Minimum 3/16-inch diameter, maximum spacing 33 inches on center, located immediately above the flashing.
  Source: ORSC R703.8.6.
```

## 8.8 Cavity Insulation Strategy

**Simple**

The insulation sits in the gap, fastened to the block. Clear open air stays between it and the brick. The gap is the wall's wet zone: the design sends water down through it on purpose. So the insulation must tolerate lifelong wetting from the brick side without absorbing water or breaking down. Not every insulation qualifies. Material choice and whole-wall targets are Envelope chapter subjects. This section states what may go in the cavity and how it stays dry and continuous.

**Professional**

*Materials rated for the wet zone*, per BIA Technical Note 28D, each tied to its material standard:

- expanded or extruded polystyrene board (ASTM C578)
- polyisocyanurate board (ASTM C1289)
- mineral wool board (ASTM C612; semi-rigid, fire-resistant, non-absorptive)
- spray polyurethane foam on the block's cavity face (ASTM C1029), usable as the air and water-resistive barrier; foam shrinkage can open drainage and air-leak gaps at edges and transitions unless membrane strips cover them
- for the block cores, not the air space: water-repellent vermiculite (ASTM C516) or silicone-treated perlite (ASTM C549); EPS bead granular fill is recommended against

BIA's selection rule: a cavity insulation product must be intended for the wet zone and have an established use history there. The Envelope chapter's verdict (Wall Insulation Strategy) ranks the options. Mineral wool has the most favorable durability profile. EPS is the code-safe economical choice. Polyiso is acceptable where the cavity drains and the foil facing is accounted for.

*Condensation control.* The insulation's placement sets where the dew point falls inside the wall section. BIA Technical Note 28D's stated goal for this wall type: force condensation to occur in the drainage cavity, the one location built to collect and discharge water. A wider air space and continuous cavity insulation both push the condensation plane into that wet zone, away from the block wythe and the interior finish. An uninsulated cavity fails the energy-code target regardless of condensation. It is not a viable configuration for this project.

*Thermal bridges.* Ties crossing the insulation are the unavoidable bridge. The penalty and the thermally-broken-tie options are tabulated in the Envelope chapter (Wall Insulation Strategy). A shelf angle is a far larger bridge per foot than the ties. The mitigation is a standoff shelf angle, which lets the cavity insulation run continuously behind its vertical leg. This project's block-backed veneer avoids shelf angles entirely below roughly 50 feet of wall height, unless the engineer of record adds one.

*Continuity.* The boards go on in two staggered layers with offset joints, taped both faces, per the Envelope chapter's installation spec. The nominal 1-inch clear air space to the brick holds everywhere. The insulation face is never the drainage surface; the back of the brick is.

*Other uses of the cavity.* The cavity is committed to two functions: drainage and continuous insulation. Routing services through it (plumbing, electrical, low-voltage) is a compromise. Everything in the cavity must be rated for a chronically damp environment. Every penetration through the flashing plane or the outer wythe is a new leak point on the one plane the design keeps continuous. No industry guidance endorsing a cavity service chase in a drained masonry wall was located. A service cavity on the interior side of the block is the documented alternative. Thermal-mass placement is already settled by the assembly: the block wythe sits inside the insulation line, the arrangement the Envelope chapter's thermal-mass principle requires.

## 8.9 Differential Movement

**Simple**

Brick and concrete block move in opposite directions for their whole lives. Fired brick absorbs moisture from the air and grows slowly and permanently. Concrete block loses its manufacturing water and shrinks. The movements are small per foot and continue for decades. Each wythe gets its own relief joint: expansion joints in the brick, control joints in the block. The two joint systems have separate spacing rules. They do not need to line up. Without the joints, the same movement appears as cracks at corners and openings.

**Professional**

*The ties' role.* Adjustable two-piece ties are built with mechanical slip: maximum 1-1/4-inch vertical offset, roughly 1/16-inch horizontal play. The slip lets the brick grow relative to the block without either wythe loading the other through the connection. The ties still transmit part of any restrained stress, so a movement-joint failure in one wythe is never fully isolated from the other.

*Expansion joints (brick).* A vertical expansion joint is a full gap through the brick wythe: compressible filler and backer rod (a foam cord that sets the sealant depth), closed with an elastomeric sealant at the face. It is built at its widest and closes over the wall's life as the brick grows. Filler and sealant are selected for compressibility first. An expansion joint packed tight, or filled with mortar, no longer absorbs movement; the wall then cracks elsewhere, exactly as with no joint.

*Control joints (block).* A control joint is a deliberate weak plane in the block wythe. The vertical joint is bond-broken (building paper, a plastic strip, or a purpose-made rubber or PVC control-joint unit). Shrinkage cracking then concentrates at that sealed, planned line instead of through a block face somewhere else. It opens over the wall's life. Its face finish matches an expansion joint's, backer rod and sealant, and its behavior is the opposite: built to open, where the expansion joint is built to close. A control joint mortared solid is no longer a weak plane; the shrinkage then cracks the block face somewhere else.

*Brick spacing rules* (BIA Technical Note 28D, confirmed against the retrieved document):

- vertical expansion joints at not more than 25 feet in walls without openings; 20 feet in walls with multiple openings
- a joint within 10 feet of each building corner
- a joint at inside corners of plan offsets and setbacks
- joints at the corners of large openings
- horizontal expansion joints under shelf angles only; a wall with no shelf angle needs none

BIA Technical Note 18A's sizing formula reached this research only through secondary summaries: spacing = joint width x filler compressibility / 0.09, giving 23 feet 2 inches for a 1/2-inch joint at 50 percent compressibility. The 20-and-25-foot rules above are the confirmed working values. The formula is re-verified against the free BIA primary text before it is used for a non-standard layout.

*Block spacing rules* (CMHA TEK 10-2C, the document the residential code text identifies for control-joint spacing):

- control joints at not more than 25 feet or 1.5 times the wall height, whichever is less
- condition: at least 0.025 square inches of horizontal joint-reinforcement steel per foot of wall height
- additional joints at stress concentrations: wall-height or thickness changes and openings
- the first joint within half the normal spacing of each corner

Nine-gauge ladder reinforcement at 16 inches on center satisfies the steel condition. This project's inner wythe already carries it for seismic crack control (Structural System And Concrete Design chapter). The TEK values also reached this research through secondary summaries. They are re-verified against the free CMHA primary text at layout time.

*Omission.* Restrained brick growth accumulates as compression along the wall. It releases at the weakest points: diagonal step-cracking through mortar joints, spalled corners, and in severe cases outward bowing of the veneer. Restrained block shrinkage cracks the wythe vertically through the cores at an opening, a height change, or a corner. Movement joints are the planned locations for movement. Where they are omitted, the same movement arrives as cracks at unplanned locations.

*Appearance.* Every required expansion joint is a visible vertical line on the finished wall. Sealant color changes its prominence, not its existence. Treating the mandatory joint lines as deliberate reveal features, or minimizing them, is a facade decision for the Aesthetics And Architectural Expression chapter.

**Applied**

1. Lay out the block control-joint grid first: the 25-foot/1.5H rule plus every corner, opening, and height-change trigger.
2. Lay out the brick expansion-joint grid separately: the 20/25-foot rule plus its corner, offset, and opening triggers.
3. Mark both grids on the foundation before the first course of either wythe. Record both on the as-built drawings.

## 8.10 Seismic Behavior In Oregon SDC D0/D1

**Simple**

Most of western Oregon sits in high seismic design categories. The Willamette Valley and the I-5 corridor are mostly D1; the coast is D2. The site-to-category table is in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter. In every one of those categories, this wall is designed by a licensed structural engineer. The requirement has no exception. This section states what the engineer adds for earthquakes.

**Professional**

The publicly documented shape of the TMS 402 seismic veneer provisions, stated as the kind of requirement the engineer applies rather than as citable values:

- In Seismic Design Category C and above, the veneer's top and sides are isolated from the structure. Frame drift (the sideways sway of the structure in an earthquake) is then not driven into the brick as in-plane load.
- In SDC D and above, the maximum wall area tributary to each tie is reduced below the standard prescriptive value. Secondary sources state a reduction to roughly 75 percent: about 2.0 square feet per tie against the standard 2.67. The exact percentage and section number are confirmed in the purchased TMS 402-22 text, or by the engineer of record, before any number appears on the drawings.
- In SDC E and F, each story's veneer weight is supported independently rather than stacked story to story.

Two related specifications route through the same engineer. One is the veneer mortar type (the Type N question of §8.5). The other is the tie schedule after the SDC reduction (§8.6). The baseline stands in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter (What Must Be Engineered): a two-wythe loadbearing assembly has no prescriptive path in any seismic category.

**Applied**

Bring to the first engineering meeting:

- the parcel's seismic design category from the site-to-category table
- the intended wythe classification (anchored veneer, §8.3)
- the candidate tie product's data sheet
- the veneer mortar question: does the Type N prohibition reach an anchored veneer
- the air-space width and insulation thickness targets (§8.7, §8.8)

## 8.11 Foundation And Base Detail For Two Wythes

**Simple**

Two walls need a wider seat than one. The footing and stem wall for the double-wythe wall are sized by the engineer for both wythes; there is no table value. The brick is allowed to step out slightly past the edge of its seat (a corbel), within strict limits. At the bottom of the wall, the gap between wythes is filled solid. The first flashing sits above the finished ground. From there up, the cavity drains. Below the sill line, the foundation chapter governs. This section starts where the first course lands.

**Professional**

*Footing width.* Sized by the engineer of record from the two wythe thicknesses, the eccentricity of the combined load, and the site's bearing capacity from the geotechnical report (the soil engineer's site investigation). It is a project-specific calculation; the owner brings the inputs. The single-wythe comparison: the default wall's footing carries one structural wythe plus light cladding, needs no corbel or bond-break detailing, and is generally narrower.

*Corbel limits* (BIA Technical Note 28D). Where the brick oversails its seat, the overhang per course is limited to one-third of the unit width and one-half of the unit height. The total corbel is limited to one-half of the unit width. The corbel is what lets the stem wall run narrower than the full two-wythe width, with the brick stepped out on top.

*Bond break.* A bond break (flashing or a slip layer) between the brick wythe and the foundation is recommended. The brick then bears on the concrete without adhering to it, and the differential movement of §8.9 occurs at the base instead of cracking the brick. The structural nuance is the engineer's: a wythe separated from its seat by a bond break has a pinned base condition rather than fixed. That distinction matters to the calculation, not to the construction sequence.

*Base moisture geometry.* The lowest through-wall flashing sits above finished grade; below it the air space is filled solid with mortar or grout. With a basement, through-wall flashing at the bottom of the cavity keeps cavity water from tracking down into the basement wall. Weeps and flashing stay high enough above grade that landscaping cannot bury them (§8.7).

*Slab-edge thermal break.* The code slab-edge insulation requirement (R-15 to 24 inches depth; sourced entry in the Envelope chapter, Slab Edge And Below-Grade Envelope Interface) connects to the cavity insulation plane, not to the brick face. On this wall the insulation plane lives between the wythes. The detail that keeps the thermal break continuous runs from the slab edge up into the cavity. A break in that connection makes the brick shelf an uninsulated bridge at exactly the location the code provision targets.

*Sulfate attack below grade.* Sulfates in soil or groundwater react with tricalcium aluminate (C3A) in ordinary portland cement to form ettringite. Ettringite occupies more volume than its reactants. The growth pressure cracks hardened mortar and grout from within. ASTM C150 limits C3A in the sulfate-resisting cements: 8 percent for Type II (moderate exposure), 5 percent for Type V (severe exposure). Which type the site needs is a geotechnical fact. A soil or groundwater sulfate test is read by the engineer against the ACI 318 exposure categories (Structural System And Concrete Design chapter, Concrete Durability And Mix Discipline). A low water-cement ratio matters as much as cement type: a denser matrix slows sulfate migration regardless of chemistry. The physical complement is keeping bulk water off the below-grade wythe, by parging (a troweled mortar coat on the wall face) and dampproofing or waterproofing per the Envelope chapter (Slab Edge And Below-Grade Envelope Interface). The sulfate test goes into the geotechnical investigation scope before the below-grade mortar and grout are specified.

*Decorative base coursing.* A soldier course (bricks stood on end, narrow face out) marks a transition line. A rowlock course (bricks on edge, end face out) caps a sloped projection and sheds water off its face. Both are standard vocabulary at a water table or base transition. A flush decorative band engages no structural rule. A projecting one stays inside the corbel limits above. No decorative unit interrupts the flashing plane at the base; the same continuity rule as everywhere else (§8.7). Whether to use either is a facade decision for the Aesthetics And Architectural Expression chapter.

## 8.12 Openings: Windows, Doors, And Narrow Slits

**Simple**

An opening interrupts every layer of the wall at once. Structure, drainage gap, insulation, and air barrier all stop at its edges. Each interrupted layer gets its own detail at each edge. In this wall the window sits deep, at the block face, on the warm side of the insulation. That is the industry's standard detail for this wall type. It also produces the house's deep window reveals. The trade-off is less daylight into the room and an exterior jamb return to detail.

**Professional**

*The governing principle*, per BIA Technical Note 28D: anything that bridges the cavity at an opening can conduct air and water. The perimeter sealant joint at the window-to-masonry interface is the primary defense against both.

*Air-barrier continuity.* Where the air barrier is on the cavity face of the block, membrane transition strips wrap from that plane into the rough opening. An interior air seal closes the gap between frame and inner wythe. A second transition strip can serve as the cavity closure. The window connects to the air barrier on the structural wythe, never to the brick. The Envelope chapter (Above-Grade Wall Assembly) states the same plane rule for the default wall.

*Cavity perimeter seal.* Mineral wool, pre-compressed joint filler, or sealant closes the cavity around the opening: at the head between lintel and inner wythe, below the sill, and matching seals at the jambs. The closure keeps cavity airflow off the window frame.

*Sill.* The sub-sill flashing is a pan: end dams turned up at the sides and the back, seams and corners fully sealed or soldered. The pan catches what passes the window's own seals and returns it to the cavity's drainage path. The exposed sill slopes to drain at 15 degrees minimum recommended. It overhangs the wall face 1-1/2 inches minimum recommended, with a weep at its underside.

*Head.* Flashing over the lintel with its own weep and cavity seal. Water intercepted above the opening then routes around it through the cavity rather than toward the frame.

*Insulation continuity.* The cavity insulation runs continuously behind the window buck, holding the same 1-inch clearance to the brick as in the field of the wall. It does not stop short at the opening.

*Setback.* BIA Technical Note 28D's drawn details place the frame at or near the block face, aligned with the insulation plane. Thermally, a frame at the insulation plane stays integrated with the continuous insulation. A frame flush with the brick sits entirely outside it and bridges around it. The deep setback and the thermal optimum coincide here. The real trade-offs of depth are daylight penetration and the exterior reveal detailing, not thermal or structural cost. No quantified U-factor comparison between setbacks was located; if one is wanted, it is a further-research item, not a number to estimate.

*Lintels.* Steel angle lintels are ASTM A36, 1/4 inch thick minimum. Bearing is at least 4 inches on the brick each side. The horizontal leg is at least two-thirds of the veneer thickness. Deflection is limited to L/600. The code span table runs from 6 feet (3x3x1/4-inch angle) to 14 feet (6x3-1/2x5/16-inch angle), by angle size and stories above. Past the table, the lintel is engineered. A corner window (no pier at the corner) and a ribbon window (one long span) both push past prescriptive spans and take engineered lintels. Whether a long-span lintel is expressed as a visible band or concealed behind brick-faced coursing is a facade decision for the Aesthetics And Architectural Expression chapter.

*Narrow slit openings.* An opening over 16 inches in either dimension triggers the perimeter tie rule (§8.6). A tall slit therefore carries perimeter ties down both jambs for its full height: more perimeter detailing per square foot of glass than a standard window. Its lintel is short and inside the span table. Its jamb seals are long and must run unbroken over the full height, which is a workmanship exposure. A slit's head, sill, and jamb details are otherwise identical to a standard window's. The tall jamb seal is proven on the mock-up panel (§8.15) before it is built into the house.

**Code**

```
Steel angle lintels over veneer openings must be sized from the code span table.
  Allowable spans from 6 feet (3x3x1/4-inch angle) to 14 feet (6x3-1/2x5/16-inch angle), by angle size and number of stories above; longer spans require engineered design.
  Source: ORSC R703.8.3.1.
```

## 8.13 Attachments, Projections, And The Roofline

**Simple**

Everything that bolts to this wall bolts to the inner block wythe. Nothing spans the gap. A planned connection is cast or grouted in during construction. An unplanned connection is drilled into cured concrete, to the engineer's design. No part reaches into the open cavity: not a joist, a ledger, or a bracket. A part sticking into the gap becomes a shelf that water crosses. The top of the wall gets a cap that sheds water outward.

**Professional**

*Planned connections.* Wedge inserts or embed plates cast into concrete during the pour take bolted or welded attachments afterward, without drilling cured, reinforced material. This is the preferred method for every connection known before the pour. The embed schedule belongs on the structural drawings and in the pour checklist (Structural System And Concrete Design chapter, cast-in embeds).

*Post-installed anchors.* For connections not planned at pour time, structural post-installed anchors go into cured concrete, grouted masonry, or hollow masonry. Anchor type, embedment, and edge distance are engineered per connection. Edge distance is what protects the wall's cover over its reinforcement, so anchor layout is not field-improvised.

*Ledgers.* A wood-floor ledger bolts only into solid-grouted cells. The cells that will receive ledger or hanger bolts are identified and grouted solid before the ledger goes on; hollow cells hold no bolt. Joist hangers then hang from the ledger.

*Roof bearing.* A wood roof bears on a plate anchored by bolts grouted into the top-of-wall cells or bond beam. Minimum embedment is four bolt diameters or 2 inches, more where uplift requires it, with vertical reinforcement engaged where the engineer requires. Nuts go hand-tight with plate washers between nut and plate; the roof framing straps to the plate. Roof anchorage values are the engineer's. The assembly rule this chapter adds is sequence: embeds in the wet grout, not drilled after (§8.2).

*The cavity exclusion.* Framing members bear on the inner wythe only and stop at its cavity face. A joist end projecting into the air space forms a ledge and a moisture path across the drainage plane. The same exclusion that governs insulation and services (§8.8) governs structure.

*Porches and entry overhangs.* Three structural options exist:

1. a cantilevered reinforced-concrete slab or beam out of the wall or bond beam, engineered for the cantilever moment
2. an independently posted roof with its inboard edge on the bond beam, which removes the cantilever from the wall
3. a bracket or knee-brace system on cast-in embeds, for lighter canopies

Each is its own engineered element. A cantilever calculation is not an extension of the roof diaphragm design, even where the two connect at the bond beam. The owner brings the porch dimensions to the engineer of record as a separate design item.

*Roofline termination.* Three options, decided after the roof-system choice, not before:

- **Parapet with coping.** A parapet is a wall extending above the roof surface; the coping is its protective cap. The cavity continues up through the parapet to keep the wythe separation. BIA Technical Note 28D flags the dimensional trap. A 12-inch parapet with brick on both faces leaves a nominal 4-inch center wythe. Cells that small do not take rebar and grout, so a reinforced-masonry parapet may not be buildable at typical widths. The stated alternative on a concrete structure is a concrete stem-wall parapet. The coping slopes toward a drip on at least one side, over through-wall flashing directly beneath it, with sealant at every anchor penetration. Formed sheet metal, stone, and fired clay are the named coping materials.
- **Exposed fascia.** A conventional overhanging eave for a sloped roof. It avoids the parapet's structural-thickness problem and gives up the flush masonry-to-sky line.
- **Soldier-course cap.** A decorative termination for an eave or low parapet edge. It does not replace the coping's function. Without flashing and a drip beneath it, the top of the wall saturates exactly as an unflashed wall base would (§8.7).

The parapet-versus-fascia choice presumes the roof geometry. The parapet detail presumes the structure at the roofline. The Structural System And Concrete Design chapter's roof-structure decision and the Roof System And Roof-Wall Junctions chapter therefore come first; this wall detail follows them.

*Drips and rain streaking.* Streaking concentrates below any horizontal projection that lacks a drip. Water collects at the projection, wraps back under it by surface tension, and runs down the same strip of wall for years. The repeated run deposits dirt, salts (§8.15), and biological growth in a visible streak. The sill and coping minimums above (15-degree slope, 1-1/2-inch overhang, drip edge) are streak control and water control in one detail. Detailing every projection to those minimums is what keeps streaking minimal. Deliberately omitting a drip to cultivate a weathering pattern is a real design choice with a real durability cost. It belongs to the Aesthetics And Architectural Expression chapter, made knowingly.

The finding and the mechanism are Building Science Corporation research report RR-0104, retrieved and read for this book. Interior polyethylene converts that seasonal drive into accumulation, because it blocks the inward drying the drive needs. This wall's countermeasures are the drained, vented cavity as the outward drying path and the absence of any interior low-permeance sheet. The assembly avoids the failure condition the report documents.

The single-wythe default totals several inches less, because its cladding is not a second masonry wythe. That difference produces the deep reveals at every opening (§8.12).

## 8.15 Problems And Solutions

**Simple**

Masonry walls fail in known, documented ways. Nearly all of them involve moisture. This section is the catalog. Each entry states the mechanism, the prevention built into this chapter's details, and the fix when the failure appears anyway. The repair order is constant across all of them: the water source first, then the symptom.

### Efflorescence

Efflorescence is the white crystalline deposit on masonry faces. It requires three ingredients at once (BIA Technical Note 23A): soluble salts in the wall system, water to dissolve them, and a migration path to a surface. The water evaporates at the surface; the salt stays. The salts most often come from the mortar, grout, block, or soil rather than from the brick. The moisture most often comes from rain reaching the masonry.

Two kinds, with different meanings. New-building bloom appears in the first year, driven by the construction water in fresh mortar and grout working its way out. It is expected and generally ends with the wall's first full drying cycle. Recurring efflorescence after the first year signals an ongoing external water source, usually a flashing, weep, or cavity defect. The response to recurrence is inspection of the drainage system (§8.7), not another cleaning.

Four prevention levers, each tied to its mechanism:

- low-alkali cement (the ASTM C150 option): cement alkali content is a principal salt contributor
- integral water-repellent admixture (below): less absorbed water available to dissolve salts
- a functioning flashing-and-weep system: the dominant lever against recurrence
- covered material storage: pallets left uncovered through an Oregon wet season absorb the water that feeds the first-year bloom

### Integral Water-Repellent Admixtures

An integral water-repellent (IWR) admixture (a chemical added at batching) is mixed through the mortar batch. The repellency then runs through the joint instead of sitting on its surface. Two confirmed products and dose rates: GCP DRY-BLOCK mortar admixture at 16 to 24 ounces per 3 cubic feet of mortar; Euclid HYDRAPEL as a powder dosed per its current data sheet. Both are designed as two-sided systems. The block or brick is plant-dosed with the matching admixture from the same manufacturer family. A mortar-side treatment alone, against untreated absorptive units, delivers less than the rated result. Chemistries are not cross-compatible between manufacturers. The coordination item: matching plant-dosed units are confirmed with the block and brick suppliers before the system is specified.

No independent third-party percentage for absorption or efflorescence reduction was located, and none is invented here. Before the system is relied on for a durability-critical wall, the manufacturer's third-party test reports are requested: water absorption to ASTM C1403, bond strength to ASTM C1714 or C952.

### Coatings That Peel And Walls That Spall

A film-forming paint or sealer blocks the masonry's surface pores. Moisture already in the wall, or arriving through any defect, can no longer evaporate outward. In a freezing climate it freezes behind the film. The expansion pushes the coating off, or spalls the masonry face itself. The coating converts a manageable moisture load into a freeze-thaw mechanism.

The vapor-open alternative is a penetrating silane or siloxane repellent. It reacts inside the pores and lines them, repelling liquid water while leaving the pore structure open to vapor. Two application limits govern. The wall must be dry when treated; moisture in the pores blocks penetration and cure. The wall must be free of active salt contamination; sealing over efflorescence traps the salts and the water cycle that feeds them. Silane needs an alkaline substrate to bond. On aged, carbonated masonry, siloxane is the broader choice. The sequence is fixed: the moisture source is found and corrected, the wall dries, then the sealer goes on. Applying the sealer first traps the moisture, and the salt cycle it feeds, inside the wall.

### Cracks: Reading Them Before Repairing Them

The first question about a crack is whether it is moving. The field method is a crack monitor: a two-piece gauge scribed to read width and offset, or two reference marks. It is read across a full seasonal cycle, because brick and block movement runs on annual moisture and temperature cycles (§8.9). Measurable change across the cycle means active; none means dormant.

The fix follows the diagnosis, not the appearance:

- Repointing (removing and replacing failed joint mortar) suits genuine mortar deterioration and small dormant cracks confined to joints. Repointing an active crack lasts one movement cycle.
- Route-and-seal (widening the crack, installing backer rod and elastomeric sealant) suits dormant cracks too wide or irregular for repointing. It tolerates limited ongoing movement if the sealant is sized for what the monitoring showed.
- A crack that recurs after repointing, or repeats at intervals along a wall, marks a missing movement joint. The fix is to cut and build a proper expansion or control joint at that location per §8.9, not to repoint the same line again.

### Failing Ties And Leaking Cavities: Retrofit Without Demolition

Diagnosis is a borescope (a camera probe on a flexible stem) survey. Small holes are drilled through mortar joints, never through units, at representative locations. A camera then shows the cavity's condition: droppings, standing water, tie corrosion, insulation state.

Remedial helical ties re-establish the wythe connection from outside, through small pilot holes, without rebuilding. The documented product family: dry mechanical pinning systems (Helifix DryFix), veneer-stabilization anchors for detached wythes (TorkFix), and grouted helical ties for solid masonry (CemTie). Deficient weeps are retrofit by cutting head-joint mortar out at correct spacing immediately above the flashing line. Retrofit cavity clearing has no standard tool; it is borescope-guided work through small access openings. Keeping the cavity clean during construction (§8.7) avoids the problem. No 2026 cost range for this work was located; quotes come from a masonry-restoration contractor.

### Proving A Detail Before It Is Built Into The House

The mock-up is the test bed: a real section of the actual assembly, built by the same mason who will build the wall. It includes both wythes, the specified ties, the insulation, and the flashing-and-weep detail under test. Workmanship variables are half of what the test proves.

The field water test is ASTM C1601: a spray rack at a fixed rate for a timed period against the wall face, watching the cavity side. ASTM E514, often cited for masonry water testing, is a laboratory method for fabricated specimens, not a field procedure. An owner-run informal spray to the same pattern is a legitimate self-check without third-party weight. A certified C1601 test is the version an inspector or engineer can rely on for a disputed detail. Alongside the water test, the mock-up settles appearance and function by inspection: joint profile and color, open weeps, and water introduced above the flashing discharging at the weeps rather than disappearing into the wall.

### Where The Durability Money Goes

No quantified 100-year life-cycle comparison of the upgrade options exists in the sources, and none is fabricated here. What the mechanisms above settle:

- Grade SW brick is compliance rather than an upgrade; Grade MW is not a legitimate exterior alternative in this climate (§8.3). Incremental cost: effectively zero, because the lower grade is not an option.
- Tighter weep spacing and complete flashing (BIA's 24 inches against the code's 33) add linear feet of the same materials at construction time. They act directly on the failure class behind most of this section. The failure they prevent, a saturated wall assembly, is disruptive to open and repair after the fact.
- IWR admixture and penetrating sealer are real per-unit premiums with unquantified repair-avoidance. The decision inputs are the suppliers' 2026 quotes plus the third-party test data requested above.
- Mortar formulation: the TMS code reduces allowable flexural tension for masonry-cement and air-entrained cement-lime mortars. A mortar with less flexural capacity is more crack-prone under the differential-movement stresses of §8.9. That structural reduction is part of the mortar price comparison.

## 8.16 Code And Standards

**Simple**

Two kinds of documents govern this chapter. The Oregon Residential Specialty Code is adopted law. Its entries below are the enforceable minimums for veneer anchorage, drainage, lintels, and alternative materials. Industry standards and technical notes (TMS, ASTM, BIA, CMHA) supply the design values the code identifies. The purchased ones are flagged where their exact numbers still need primary-text confirmation. Reinforcement, grout-lift, and mortar seismic entries are in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter. Envelope and insulation entries are in the Envelope chapter.

**Code**

```
Brick veneer must be anchored to its backing with ties at limited spacing.
  Maximum 32 inches on center horizontally and 24 inches on center vertically.
  Source: ORSC R703.8.4.1.

Openings must have additional veneer ties around them.
  Ties within 12 inches of openings larger than 16 inches, spaced not more than 3 feet on center around the opening perimeter.
  Source: ORSC R703.8.4.1.1.

  Minimum 3/16-inch diameter, maximum spacing 33 inches on center, located immediately above the flashing.
```
