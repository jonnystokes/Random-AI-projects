# Chapter [NN]: Designing For Centuries: Durability Engineering

This chapter states the mechanisms that decide whether reinforced concrete and masonry last decades or centuries. It then states the protections this project selects against each mechanism, with costs and the inspection duties each one creates. It extends the concrete-durability-and-mix-discipline section of the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter, which specifies the baseline long-life mix, the exposure-class table, and the cover values. Curing practice and construction joints are stated in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, and Concrete Placement chapter. The control layers that keep water away from the structure are stated in the Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, and Radon chapter. This chapter treats the degradation of the materials behind those layers. The maintenance duties defined here are scheduled in the Documentation, Maintenance, and Future Upgrades chapter.

## [NN].1 What A Thousand-Year Shell Requires (The Deterioration Clock)

**Simple**

Can a house be engineered to last a thousand years? No code, test, or model can verify that number. Reinforced concrete has existed for about 170 years. Its oldest surviving buildings are barely past 120. So the goal works as a design rule, not a certified outcome. Every structural decision is evaluated on a multi-century horizon. This chapter lists each way concrete and steel degrade. It also lists the protections that slow or remove each one.

### The Honest Engineering Position

The project's durability claim is stated in three separable parts, each with its own evidence status.

1. What is done. The structural shell is designed to the most conservative applicable ACI 318 and ACI 332 severe-exposure provisions. The added measures in this chapter stack on top: added cover, supplementary cementitious materials, corrosion-resistant reinforcement at the high-risk elements, drainage-detail discipline, and the optional active protections.
2. What the evidence supports. The code exposure classes are calibrated against decades of North American field performance, not centuries. Eurocode-family durability tables stop at a stated 100-year design working life (the service period a structure is designed to reach). Reinforced concrete as a technology has no track record beyond roughly 170 years, because it dates only to the mid-1800s.
3. Where judgment begins. Any reasoning past that evidentiary ceiling is labeled in this book as engineering judgment, not verified fact. Extrapolating the 50-to-100-year cover logic further is one example.

### What The Track Record Contains

The oldest reinforced-concrete high-rise, the Ingalls Building in Cincinnati (completed 1903), is still standing and in active use. It was converted to a hotel in 2021, designated an ASCE National Historic Civil Engineering Landmark in 1974, and added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1975. That is a strong first 120 years, and it is the technology's entire demonstrated span. Every genuinely multi-century structure in the durability literature is unreinforced or non-concrete. The examples: the Roman Pantheon and Colosseum foundations at roughly two thousand years, and England's estimated 30,000 surviving homes built before 1700, in masonry, stone, and continuously maintained timber. Those survivals reflect resilient materials plus centuries of active maintenance and partial rebuilding, not a design-time specification. The 2026 research pass for this chapter found no published case study, anywhere, of a single-family residence engineered to a stated multi-century design life. This project documents its reasoning partly because that record does not yet exist.

### The 500-Year Precedent

The nearest engineered precedent for a stated multi-century requirement is nuclear waste containment. Under 10 CFR Part 61, the intruder barrier over Class C low-level radioactive waste must remain effective for 500 years. The dose basis is 500 millirem per year to a hypothetical future intruder. Active institutional control (site monitoring and access restriction) is assumed for only the first 100 years; after that the barrier must perform passively. Three of its design principles transfer to a house: depth of cover over the vulnerable element (the same principle as concrete cover over rebar), low permeability and high density in the barrier material, and redundancy through independent protective layers. One principle does not transfer: zero-maintenance passive performance. A house receives ongoing owner maintenance indefinitely. This project's durability plan assumes that maintenance rather than designing for abandonment.

### How The Source Documents Relate

ACI 318 Chapter 19 is the mandatory minimum: exposure classes (the code's severity ratings for an element's environment) with required strength, water-cement ratio, and air content. ACI PRC-201.2-23 (Guide to Durable Concrete) is a non-mandatory companion. It is organized as one chapter on mass transport followed by one chapter per degradation mechanism, and the ACI 318 commentary cites it as the rationale behind the Chapter 19 minimums. Nothing in the accessible text of the guide authorizes less protection than the code requires. For this project it functions as the engineering case for exceeding the minimums. The full text of both documents is purchased ACI material, so the section-by-section relationship is verified by the engineer of record against the primary text, not by this book. ASCE 7 treats ordinary Risk Category II buildings against a nominal reference period commonly understood as approximately 50 years for its statistical load bases. No explicit service-life number appears in the accessible ACI 318 excerpts. Applying the most severe exposure class by default across the shell adds real margin, but ACI publishes no table converting that choice into added service years. A project-specific number comes only from service-life modeling with real inputs, stated in the corrosion section of this chapter.

## [NN].2 Reinforcement Corrosion, Carbonation, And Cover

**Simple**

Steel inside sound concrete does not rust at first. The cement chemistry keeps the pore water highly alkaline. That alkalinity maintains a thin protective oxide film on the bar. Two slow processes destroy the film. Carbon dioxide from the air lowers the alkalinity from the surface inward. Salt reaching the bar breaks the film at single points. Rust then grows, and rust occupies 2 to 6 times the volume of the steel it replaces. The expansion cracks the concrete from the inside. Cracks admit more water and salt, so the damage accelerates. Cover (the concrete between the bar and the surface) sets the travel time for both attacks. More cover means more years before either attack reaches the steel.

**Professional: the corrosion mechanism.** Embedded steel is protected by a passive oxide layer (a microscopically thin film that blocks further rusting), stable at the high pH of uncarbonated concrete. Carbonation destroys that layer across the whole bar surface. Chloride destroys it locally, producing pitting (corrosion concentrated in small, deep spots) that is harder to detect than uniform corrosion. Rust is a mixture of iron oxide and hydroxide phases (goethite, lepidocrocite, magnetite, ferrihydrite, and others). The proportions depend on oxygen availability, moisture, and chloride presence. Each phase has a different density, so the expansion ratio is inherently a range. Corrosion products occupy 2 to 6 times the volume of the parent steel, depending on which phases dominate (Zhao et al., Corrosion Science, 2011, by X-ray diffraction and thermal analysis; the same range appears in 2024 rust-expansion cracking research).

### Carbonation And The Curing Condition

Carbonation (the reaction of airborne carbon dioxide with the cement paste) advances as depth x = k times the square root of t. That is the Tuutti relationship, also the fib Model Code form; k is a coefficient calibrated to the specific mix and exposure, and t is time. Two sourced facts bound this project's case. First, carbonation requires intermediate humidity, roughly 50 to 70 percent relative humidity, because water-saturated pores block carbon dioxide diffusion. Concrete that stays wet most of the year carbonates more slowly than concrete in a wet-dry cycling exposure. Shaded and below-grade concrete in western Oregon fits the wet description. Second, supplementary cementitious materials (fly ash, slag, and silica fume, the pozzolanic additives in the project mix) increase carbonation depth at equal water-cement ratio. Published accelerated tests at 0.5 water-binder ratio found depths similar to plain concrete up to roughly 30 percent fly ash replacement. Depths roughly doubled above that, and roughly tripled at the highest replacement tested. Published cover comparisons agree. Concrete with up to 35 percent fly ash needed about 40 percent more cover to reach the same 50-year carbonation service life as plain concrete. Concrete with 40 to 70 percent fly ash needed more than double.

This project's specified mix (20 to 30 percent Class F fly ash or 30 to 50 percent slag, water-cement ratio 0.40 to 0.45) sits inside or just above the range where the effect becomes measurable. Two responses follow, both already in the structural chapter's mix table. The fly-ash replacement stays in the 20 to 30 percent band rather than the 40-plus band where depth roughly doubles. Moist curing runs 7 days minimum and 14 days preferred for SCM mixes, against 3 days for plain mixes. The curing requirement is where the real risk concentrates. The pozzolanic reaction that eventually densifies the pore structure runs slower than portland hydration. Curing cut short leaves a coarser, more carbonation-permeable pore structure than either a cured SCM mix or a plain mix would have. No source ran the carbonation model with this project's actual cement chemistry, site humidity, and cover. A predicted depth-at-age figure for this house is produced by the structural engineer of record from real inputs, not stated here.

### The Chloride Threshold: Two Numbers, Kept Separate

Two different quantities share the name chloride threshold: a research value and a code value. The research value is the concentration at the bar surface that initiates corrosion. RILEM Technical Committee 235-CTC and FHWA-reviewed studies place acid-soluble thresholds for black bar at roughly 0.17 to 0.26 percent by weight of cement, averaging near 0.21 percent. Across test methods and exposure conditions, the broader literature cites roughly 0.2 to 1.5 percent. That value is condition-dependent, and the literature does not converge on one number. The code value is different: a ceiling on the water-soluble chloride already in the mix at placement, set by ACI 318-19 Table 19.3.2.1 per corrosion exposure class. That ceiling is what the mix design must meet. The research range explains why the ceiling tightens as external chloride exposure rises. The table values in this section's Code entry are corroborated across independent professional secondary sources. The engineer of record cross-checks them against the purchased ACI 318 text before construction documents issue.

### Where Chloride Comes From On This Project

Chloride exposure divides by geography and by the owner's own practice. On a coastal parcel, wind-driven salt spray is the driver, and the documented zone is graduated. Exposure is most severe within roughly 1,500 feet of breaking surf, moderate to about 1 mile, and lighter to about 3 miles. Some studies find accelerated metal corrosion 5 to 10 miles inland under certain wind and topography. Any exterior concrete face inside that band is a legitimate severe-corrosion-class (C2) candidate. On an inland or Cascades-foothill parcel, marine chloride is not a credible driver and most of the shell can sit at C0 or C1.

Two chloride sources remain live on any site, coastal or inland, because they follow the owner's product choices rather than the climate. The garage slab receives whatever deicing chemical vehicle tires track in from public roads and the driveway. Any driveway-adjacent wall or slab edge receives runoff and splash from a deiced driveway surface. Both locations are C1 candidates on product choice alone; with routine chloride deicer use they are C2 candidates. A below-grade wall near a salted public road follows the same logic. Most Oregon state highways receive deicing chemicals in winter storms, and rural county road practice varies, so the county's winter maintenance practice is confirmed before the exposure class is assigned. The final class assignment for each element is the structural engineer of record's determination once the parcel, its distance from the coast, and the adjacent road's practice are known.

The project controls its own deicing practice; chloride exposure at the garage and driveway elements is a maintenance decision, not an environmental given. Rock salt (sodium chloride) is documented as one of the worst deicer choices for concrete. It penetrates the surface and causes flaking and spalling (shallow surface breakoff), with concrete under about one year old the most vulnerable. It is also the direct chloride dose that raises the exposure class at the garage and driveway elements. Calcium magnesium acetate (CMA) is a non-chloride, biodegradable deicer; Michigan DOT testing found it sharply reduces salt-induced scaling compared with rock salt. Its limits: effective only to roughly 15 degrees F (about the same working floor as rock salt), weaker at active melting than at pre-storm anti-bonding treatment, and priced at several times rock salt per pound. Plain sand adds traction without any melting and without any chloride, and it is swept up after the ice season to keep it out of drains. The product choice recurs every winter and stays with the owner; the durability consequence of each option is as stated here.

**Applied**

1. Stock sand, or a sand and CMA blend, as the default winter traction material for the driveway and walks.
2. Use CMA when actual melting is needed, applied before the storm where possible.
3. Keep every sodium chloride and calcium chloride deicing product off the project's own concrete, permanently.
4. Use no calcium chloride accelerator in any concrete mix on this project, in any season.
5. Record the products used each winter in the maintenance log.

### Cover: The Buffer And Its Limit

Carbonation and chloride ingress both advance with the square root of time, so doubling the cover roughly quadruples the time to corrosion initiation. That relationship makes cover the strongest single passive lever. Two independent code traditions size the reasonable margin. Eurocode 2's cover formula adds a deviation allowance commonly cited at roughly 10 mm (about 3/8 inch) above the calculated durability minimum. BS 8500's step from a 50-year to a 100-year design working life moves cover by a comparably small increment for a fixed exposure class and cement type. This project's specified cover, code minimum plus 1/2 inch for severe exposure per the structural chapter, sits at or slightly above both reference points. The research pass confirms that figure rather than increasing it. Past that level, added cover trades against three real costs for a diminishing return. Potential surface crack widths grow, because the ACI 318 crack-control provisions are calibrated to standard cover ranges. Dead load grows. Formwork and bar-chair cost grows across the whole shell.

BS 8500 also documents the trade-off logic itself. At a fixed exposure class, cover and concrete quality are interchangeable levers: a specifier can raise the strength class and hold cover, or add cover and hold the mix. The exact 50-to-100-year numeric deltas sit inside the purchased BS 8500-1 standard and are not restated here. The standard stops at 100 years. Extending its logic further is the labeled engineering judgment of this chapter's opening section, not a code-backed number.

### Modeling The Clock

Life-365 is the free service-life tool the structural engineer of record can run for this house. It is maintained by an industry consortium (NRMCA, the Slag Cement Association, the Silica Fume Association, and several admixture manufacturers) as a downloadable application and an online version at life-365.org. It takes real inputs:

- a US location for temperature data
- an exposure condition
- the cover
- the water-cement ratio
- the SCM type and replacement rate
- the steel type

Its output is a predicted time to corrosion initiation, plus a life-cycle cost comparison (purchase, maintenance, and replacement over the analysis horizon) between protection options. The model is a deterministic diffusion calculation calibrated to published exposure data. Its output is therefore cited as the engineer's model estimate for this mix and exposure, in the same standing as a structural load calculation. It is never cited as a measured or guaranteed service life.

The fib Model Code 2010 full-probabilistic durability method exists above that: calibrated diffusion and carbonation coefficients, site weather functions, and a reliability calculation against a target failure probability. Its documented applications are bridges, tunnels, and marine infrastructure. No residential application was found, and the method needs specialist software and expertise beyond ordinary residential practice. Commissioning one is a decision left to the structural engineer of record. This project's stated path is the code's deemed-to-satisfy route (complying through the exposure-class tables rather than by site-specific calculation), plus, at the engineer's option, a deterministic Life-365 run with the project's real inputs.

### The Champlain Towers South Lesson

The 2021 partial collapse of Champlain Towers South in Surfside, Florida is the corrosion failure with the best public forensic record. NIST's National Construction Safety Team measured corrosion steel loss by weighing rusted bar samples recovered from the building against pristine samples of the same original size. It also ran accelerated corrosion tests: bar embedded in concrete, soaked in salt water, with current driven through it to compress decades of corrosion into weeks. The June 2026 technical findings identify two corrosion-related contributing factors. Pool-deck reinforcement corrosion weakened slabs and slab-column connections. Prolonged water exposure from garage ponding and flooding degraded some basement columns. The stated conclusion: design deficiencies, construction deviations, added loads, and long-term corrosion collectively reduced capacity. Long-term corrosion was most likely the final element that eroded already-critically-low safety margins. The detailed steel-section-loss percentages await NIST's full final report and are rechecked there when published.

The transferable finding is locational. The corrosion became catastrophic where water ponded or flowed chronically against structural concrete, not wherever concrete existed. Two practices on this project trace directly to that mechanism. First, every location where water could realistically pond against structure receives explicit waterproofing and drainage design review and sign-off, not standard-detail assumption. On this project that means the garage floor and its drainage, the below-grade wall perimeter drains, and any exterior slab or deck over habitable space. Second, corrosion damage stays invisible until it is severe, so those same pond-prone locations enter the periodic inspection regime in this chapter's maintenance section. The check is visual: staining, cracking, or efflorescence (a white mineral deposit left where water evaporates at a concrete surface) at every drainage low point.

**Code**

```
The chloride content of the fresh mix is capped by corrosion exposure class.
  Water-soluble chloride ion, percent by weight of cement, nonprestressed concrete: 1.00 at C0, 0.30 at C1, 0.15 at C2; prestressed concrete, 0.06 in every class.
  Source: ACI 318-19 Table 19.3.2.1.
```

## [NN].3 Chemical Attack: Sulfate, Effluent, Leaching, And Alkali-Silica Reaction

**Simple**

Sulfate is a mineral salt present in some soils and groundwater. It reacts with hardened cement paste and forms minerals that occupy more space. That growth cracks the concrete from within. This attack targets the paste, not the steel. A soil test states how much sulfate the site contains. The mix is then matched to that number. A septic drain field changes the soil chemistry around it. So the soil near the drain field is tested separately.

**Professional: the sulfate exposure classes.** ACI 318-19 Table 19.3.2.1 defines sulfate classes by water-soluble sulfate in soil (percent by dry weight) and dissolved sulfate in water (ppm), with a mix response per class. In the table, f'c is the specified compressive strength and w/cm the water-to-cementitious-materials ratio. The values below are corroborated across two independent professional secondary sources. The engineer of record cross-checks them against the purchased primary table before construction documents issue.

| Class | Sulfate in soil (% dry weight) | Sulfate in water (ppm) | Mix response |
|---|---|---|---|
| S0 | under 0.10 | under 150 | No sulfate-driven requirement; 2,500 psi general structural minimum |
| S1 | 0.10 to under 0.20 | 150 to under 1,500 | f'c 4,000 psi minimum; w/cm 0.50 maximum |
| S2 | 0.20 to 2.00 | 1,500 to 10,000 | f'c 4,500 psi minimum; w/cm 0.45 maximum |
| S3 | over 2.00 | over 10,000 | f'c 4,500-5,000 psi; w/cm 0.45-0.40; Type V cement with a pozzolan or slag passing ASTM C618/C989 sulfate-resistance testing, or a cementitious combination demonstrated equivalent |

The structural chapter's assignments (S0 above grade in mild exposure, S1 for below-grade walls in potentially aggressive soil) are consistent with these thresholds. The standard's higher classes remain available where a site requires them. Where a wall's actual soil test returns S2 or S3, the mix steps up to the combination above: higher strength, lower w/cm, Type V cement plus tested SCM. That step goes beyond the general Type I/II-with-SCM language in the structural chapter's table.

### Effluent Chemistry Near The Drain Field

Concrete near wastewater faces two mechanisms that a native-soil sulfate test does not capture. Effluent sulfates react with calcium hydroxide in the paste to form expansive ettringite (a crystal that grows inside the hardened paste) and thaumasite, raising porosity and weakening the concrete. In anaerobic conditions, microbial action converts sulfur compounds into biogenic sulfuric acid, which actively dissolves cement paste. That is a more severe mechanism than passive sulfate-ion exposure. The published record comes mostly from municipal wastewater plants and sewers rather than residential drain fields. The chemistry transfers, because the wetted zone around a dispersal area is deliberately kept damp. Its sulfate and biological activity can differ meaningfully from the surrounding native ground.

Two siting geometries create the risk, and both are avoidable at the site-plan stage. In the first, the dispersal area sits close enough that its wetted zone reaches capillary distance from a wall face. That recreates the wick-and-evaporate geometry described in the freeze-thaw and weathering section, with effluent-modified chemistry. In the second, grading lets dispersal-area moisture pool against a foundation instead of moving away. The legal minimum setback between a drain field and a structure is a DEQ and county rule, confirmed at permit time. The Water Independence chapter states the septic and greywater siting rules.

**Applied**

1. Order the standard soil sulfate and chloride tests (the Soil, Ground Prep, Drainage, and Foundation Risk chapter).
2. Where a below-grade wall sits near the planned drain field, order a second sample. Take it from the field's anticipated wetted zone, in soil or shallow groundwater.
3. Assign that wall's sulfate class from the drain-field-adjacent sample, not the general-site sample.
4. Before construction, check the septic site plan against the foundation footprint for the two geometries above. Record the check in the design-review file.

### Calcium Leaching

Continuously flowing water dissolves calcium hydroxide and calcium-silicate-hydrate out of cement paste, raising porosity and reducing strength over decades. The documented cases are dams. A 1912 Colorado arch dam's abandonment is attributed mainly to calcium leaching. A survey of 34 inspected dams in China found leaching among the main deterioration factors. The exposure premise in every documented case is sustained flow through or against mass concrete, under hydraulic gradient, across a service life exceeding 100 years. A house's roof drainage and foundation drains are designed to move water off and away quickly. This mechanism therefore engages at a residence only where the drainage design fails chronically. The failure examples: a drain outlet that stays wet against the wall, or a roof detail that concentrates flow onto one wall section for decades. This chapter assigns no mix-design response to leaching. The counter is the drainage discipline in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, and Concrete Placement chapter, plus the chronic-wet-spot checks in this chapter's maintenance regime.

### Alkali-Silica Reaction

The ASR mechanism and the project's verification procedure are stated in the structural chapter's concrete-durability-and-mix-discipline section, and are not restated here. That procedure is an ASTM C1567 accelerated mortar-bar test of the actual aggregate, cement, and SCM dosage together, read against the 0.1 percent expansion threshold. This chapter adds one option to it: the ternary SCM blend in the mix-design section below, documented to suppress ASR expansion while also raising sulfate and freeze-thaw resistance in the same mix.

**Code**

```
The sulfate exposure class is set by measured soil and water sulfate.
  Soil: S0 under 0.10, S1 0.10 to under 0.20, S2 0.20 to 2.00, S3 over 2.00 percent by dry weight. Water: S0 under 150, S1 150 to under 1,500, S2 1,500 to 10,000, S3 over 10,000 ppm.
  Source: ACI 318-19 Table 19.3.2.1.

The mix response scales with the sulfate class.
  S1: f'c 4,000 psi, w/cm 0.50. S2: f'c 4,500 psi, w/cm 0.45. S3: f'c 4,500-5,000 psi, w/cm 0.45-0.40, Type V cement plus a sulfate-resistance-tested pozzolan or slag, or a demonstrated-equivalent combination.

## [NN].4 Freeze-Thaw And Physical Weathering

**Simple**

Freezing damages concrete only when its pores are nearly full of water. Water expands about 9 percent as it freezes. In a saturated pore, that expansion pressurizes the surrounding paste. A drier wall face freezes with little or no damage. Damage also depends on how often the site crosses the freezing point. A foothill site crosses freezing far more often than the valley floor. So the freeze class comes from site data, not from a regional guess.

**Professional: assigning the freeze-thaw class from site data.** Two recognized tools convert a specific location into a freeze-thaw severity rating. The Portland Cement Association's national freeze-thaw exposure frequency map (PCA, 2002) classifies locations directly. The brick standards' weathering index (ASTM C216 and C62) computes a numeric index from NOAA climate normals (the published 30-year average weather statistics). The index combines annual freezing-cycle days with winter precipitation. For any location near a grade threshold, the standards direct the specifier to analyze local climatological summaries, including microclimate and altitude. Both tools require a named location as input. The Willamette Valley floor sits toward the milder end of Oregon's freeze-thaw exposure. A Cascades-foothill parcel at higher elevation can rate meaningfully higher on both scales. Cycling frequency rises with elevation and with the cold-air-pocket topography common in foothill drainages. Until the parcel is chosen, no defensible index number exists for this project. Once it is chosen, the geotechnical or structural engineer pulls that location's NOAA climate-normals station data and runs the weathering-index method or reads the PCA map. The freeze-thaw class (F0 through F3) is assigned from that result, not from the valley baseline.

**Two mix designs on one project.** An exposed exterior element (a driveway, an exterior stair, a detached structure at a higher-elevation corner of the site) can rate a more severe freeze class than the house's above-grade walls. The ACI 318 framework assigns the air content and w/cm per element, not per project. Running two mix designs on one small residential schedule is common practice. Ready-mix suppliers routinely deliver different specified mixes to one site on different trucks or days. Residential contractors regularly order a separate higher-air, lower-w/cm mix for exterior flatwork. The added burden is administrative: two mix tickets, two sets of slump and air tests (the standard fresh-concrete field checks), and explicit plant and crew communication about which pour receives which mix. Whether this project needs the second mix depends on the site-specific class determination above. The decision belongs to the owner and the structural engineer of record.

**The overhang as a freeze-thaw measure.** Field measurements of wind-driven rain confirm that roof overhangs measurably reduce wall wetting. Protection is strongest at the wall area near the roofline and decreases with distance below it. Larger overhangs lose that protection more gradually down the wall than small ones. Freeze-thaw damage requires freezing in a saturated state, so a wall face kept measurably drier passes through fewer damaging cycles at the same air temperature. A generous overhang is therefore a durability measure for the concrete or masonry face below it, not only a rain-shedding feature. No published figure converts overhang depth into a cycling reduction percentage. The effect enters this project as a qualitative design preference, not as a change to the exposure-class table. Overhang sizing is stated in the Roof System and Roof-Wall Junctions chapter. Seasonal shading geometry is stated in the Passive and Bioclimatic Design chapter.

### Physical Salt Attack

Physical salt attack needs three things at one detail: salt-bearing moisture, a capillary path, and an evaporative face. Salt in solution rises through concrete by capillary action (the wicking of water through fine pores) from damp soil or groundwater. Above the wet zone, the moisture evaporates at an air-exposed surface and leaves salt crystals growing in the near-surface pores. The crystallization pressure scales and spalls the surface in a band just above ground level or just above the wet zone. The documented field locations are the exposed parts of basement walls, piers, abutments, and slabs on grade. A dry climate is not required. Oregon's wet-winter, dry-summer cycle supplies the wet-then-evaporate sequence at any detail that holds a chronically damp base under an exposed face.

Three details on a residential site produce that geometry:

- a slab edge or wall base over soil kept damp by irrigation, a downspout discharge, or poor grading, with an exposed face above it
- a retaining wall with a wet retained side and an exposed face open to sun and wind drying
- a planter built directly against a structural wall, where irrigation water wicks into the wall and evaporates above the planter's soil line

The check is the same for all three. Trace every chronically wet zone that touches concrete. At each contact, either a physical break (a waterproof membrane, a capillary break, or a gap) separates the wet zone from the wall or slab, or the wetness is shown to be non-chronic. These checks appear in the consolidated design-review checklist in the detailing section of this chapter.

**Code**

```
Exterior brick is specified against the site's weathering index.
  The index combines annual freezing-cycle days with winter precipitation from NOAA climate normals; near a grade threshold, the specifier analyzes local climatological summaries, including altitude.
  Source: ASTM C216; ASTM C62.
```

## [NN].5 Low-Permeability Mix Design And Pozzolans/SCMs

**Simple**

The structural chapter's long-life mix table is the base defense. Less mixing water leaves fewer connected pores. Additives such as fly ash and slag react slowly and fill pores over years. Fewer connected pores means slower travel for salt, carbon dioxide, and water. This section adds three upgrades to that base. A two-additive blend that counters three attacks at once. A lab test that proves the delivered concrete is as dense as ordered. And admixtures that seal pores or reseal new cracks.

### The Ternary Blend

Peer-reviewed testing documents that a ternary blend (cement plus two supplementary cementitious materials) outperforms any single SCM against this project's combined threats. The best-documented combination is fly ash plus silica fume. Silica fume at roughly 3 to 6 percent, combined with high-calcium fly ash at roughly 20 to 30 percent, strongly reduced alkali-silica-reaction expansion while producing high sulfate resistance in the same mix. A 25 percent fly ash with 5 to 8 percent silica fume combination significantly improved resistance to combined freeze-thaw and sulfate attack. The interaction runs in both directions. The fly ash restores the workability (how easily the fresh mix flows and compacts) that silica fume reduces at higher dosages. The silica fume supplies the early strength gain that fly ash slows. One blend therefore addresses freeze-thaw, ASR, and sulfate together.

The batching constraint is metering. Silica fume is supplied as a densified powder or slurry and is dosed at a small percentage of total cementitious material. Consistent dosing therefore requires plant-grade metering accuracy. No study located addresses hand or small-mixer batching of a three-component blend. The position stated here is a reasoned inference from that metering sensitivity, flagged as such. This project specifies the ternary blend only for ready-mix-plant-batched pours. Hand-batched and small-mixer work, including owner-made blocks and small site pours, uses a single coarser SCM (fly ash or slag alone) per the Owner-Made Blocks and Alternative Concretes chapter.

### The Acceptance Test: Proving The Density

A specified low-permeability mix is verified, not assumed. The rapid chloride permeability test (ASTM C1202) classifies a concrete sample by the electrical charge passed, in coulombs. The confirmed bands relevant here: the low class at 1,000 to 2,000 coulombs, and the moderate class at 2,000 to 4,000. This project's acceptance criterion for the long-life mix is the low class. Concretes with slag or pozzolan contents at this project's specified replacement rates commonly test below 1,500 coulombs, inside that band. The field-practical alternative is surface resistivity (AASHTO T358, measuring how strongly the concrete resists electrical current). The Florida and Louisiana DOTs adopted it for its lower cost and faster turnaround than RCPT. Its confirmed correlated bands: high chloride penetrability below 12 kilo-ohm-cm, moderate at 12 to 21, and low at 21 to 37. The classification tables' further tiers ("very low" and "negligible") sit in the purchased standards, and the engineer confirms them there before quoting them. The Testing, Commissioning, and Quality Control chapter states who orders the test and where the report is filed.

### Permeability-Reducing Admixtures: Two Categories, Not Interchangeable

Independent literature (a peer-reviewed critical review and FDOT-funded research) separates the two admixture categories by mechanism. Hydrophobic pore-blockers line pore and capillary walls with a water-repellent layer of soaps, oils, or long-chain fatty acids. The documented limits: the coating does not reach every pore uniformly, it cannot protect larger voids or cracks, and it resists only a few centimeters of hydrostatic head (the pressure of standing water) before water enters the still-open capillary network. Water-absorption-style tests are flagged as likely to overestimate hydrophobic performance. Crystalline admixtures are hydrophilic. They react with water and unhydrated cement to grow an insoluble crystalline structure that physically seals pores, capillaries, and micro-cracks. The reaction reactivates whenever water reaches unreacted admixture. A crack that forms after cure can therefore trigger new crystal growth at that crack and reseal it. Hydrophobically treated concrete has no equivalent response to a new crack. The review also documents the shared gap: field-test data correlating lab performance is thin for both categories.

The project consequence: for below-grade walls and any element under hydrostatic pressure, the crystalline category is the documented choice. Hydrophobic products suit lower-pressure, rain-and-splash exposures only. Specifying both on one element duplicates one mechanism, per the additive-versus-redundant table at the end of this chapter.

### Ultra-High-Performance Concrete: One Small Element At Most

UHPC reaches 22,500 to 29,000 psi compressive strength in commercial mixes, with permeability and durability far past ordinary concrete. Its cost excludes whole-shell use. Proprietary pre-bagged products have sold above $2,000 per cubic yard, and fiber reinforcement adds $250 to $500 per cubic yard. Non-proprietary fiber-inclusive mixes start near $850 per cubic yard, and PCI-sponsored non-proprietary development targets $600 to $800 per cubic yard. Ordinary ready-mix runs roughly $100 to $150 per cubic yard (2025-2026 figures). That is 4 to more than 20 times the project's ordinary long-life mix. The infrastructure literature confines UHPC to small-volume, high-value joints and connections, where the premium on a small quantity barely moves the project total. The same logic admits at most one deliberately chosen element here: a critical footing run, a water cistern, or a bond beam at a stress concentration. No small-batch or precast UHPC purchase by an individual owner-builder is documented. Sourcing would run through a pre-bagged proprietary product (Ductal and similar) or a regional precaster. Residential-quantity terms are confirmed by direct inquiry before this option enters the design.

**Code**

```

  RCPT acceptance: the low chloride-permeability class, 1,000-2,000 coulombs. Surface-resistivity equivalent: 21-37 kilo-ohm-cm (moderate 12-21, high penetrability below 12).

## [NN].6 Corrosion-Resistant Reinforcement (Epoxy, Galvanized, Stainless, GFRP/Basalt)

**Simple**

Most of the shell keeps ordinary steel bar. A few locations face salt or wet, aggressive soil for centuries. Those locations get bar that resists corrosion by material. The options: a zinc coating, solid stainless steel, or glass-fiber bar containing no metal. The comparison table below lists the cost per foot of each option.

### The Epoxy-Coated Record

Epoxy-coated rebar (ECR) has the best-documented failure history of the group, and the mechanism is specific. FDOT used ECR from the late 1970s in several major segmental bridges along US 1 in the Florida Keys, whose substructures receive cyclic seawater splash and spray. Corrosion appeared first in 1986 at the Long Key Bridge substructure. Investigation then found extensive ECR corrosion in four of the five major Keys structures. The mechanism proceeds in three steps. Coating disbondment first: loss of adhesion between epoxy and steel, starting at manufacturing imperfections and aggravated by fabrication, handling, and yard exposure. Chloride penetration to bare steel at the disbonded spots second. Severe underfilm corrosion third, worsened by macrocell action (a corrosion circuit between separated areas of the same connected steel). FDOT discontinued new ECR use. The failure requires both chloride reaching the coating defects and sustained wet-dry cycling. ECR performs adequately in the much larger national population of structures without marine splash exposure. The project position follows that boundary. ECR is not selected at this project's highest-chloride locations (the garage slab, driveway-adjacent elements, or any coastal-band face), where the Keys history applies directly. Elsewhere it remains a code-recognized option that this project has no reason to prefer.

### Galvanized, Stainless, And Stainless-Clad

Hot-dip galvanized bar adds a zinc layer that corrodes sacrificially before the base steel. At roughly a 10 to 15 percent material premium over black bar (2026), it is the well-precedented step-up for elements that are moisture-exposed but not chloride-loaded: below-grade walls in ordinary, non-aggressive soil. Solid stainless bar is the documented choice for the highest-consequence, highest-chloride, hardest-to-repair elements. The sources reviewed document 75-plus years of corrosion-resistant stainless service with little to no rust. It costs several times black bar. Stainless-clad bar puts stainless at the exposed surface over a carbon-steel core. Its market pricing was not independently confirmed in the 2026 research pass and comes from a direct supplier quote before it is specified.

### GFRP And The Seismic Boundary

Glass-fiber-reinforced-polymer bar does not corrode, because it contains no metal. Its structural limit is ductility. GFRP is linearly elastic to failure, without steel's yielding, so it cannot supply the energy dissipation that the seismic-force-resisting system (the set of members designed to absorb earthquake forces) requires. ACI 440.11 is the governing code document, and its seismic scope requires care on this project. The research sources for this chapter describe GFRP as viable for members outside the seismic-force-resisting system even at an Oregon site. The named examples: slab-on-grade temperature-and-shrinkage reinforcement, a detached low-risk accessory structure, and a freestanding garden or retaining wall. The structural chapter's wall-tie and reinforcement section states the stricter reading of the same document: all elements permitted in SDC A, non-seismic-force-resisting elements in SDC B and C, and the residential SDC D categories excluded. Oregon sites are generally D0 or D1, and the two readings differ exactly for the secondary-element case. This book does not resolve that difference. The engineer of record verifies any proposed GFRP element directly against the current ACI 440.11 text before it is specified. The corrosion benefit claimed for GFRP is counted only where that verification passes. The strongest candidate remains slab-on-grade temperature-and-shrinkage bar: embedded in the one element that is hardest to inspect or repair after the pour, and priced at or below epoxy-coated steel.

### Basalt FRP

Basalt fiber bar (BFRP) is marketed on the fiber's natural alkaline resistance, and the peer-reviewed comparison runs the other way for modern products. After 360 days of accelerated aging at 55 degrees C, BFRP-to-concrete bond strength (the grip between bar and concrete) fell 24.8 percent, against 10.4 percent for GFRP. That is more than double the loss under identical conditions. A separate seawater study found BFRP retaining 86 percent of bond strength in alkaline solution and 82 percent in seawater at three months, without a GFRP benchmark in the same test. The stated mechanism: modern ECR-glass and AR-glass fibers in a vinyl-ester matrix meet the ACI 440 and ASTM D7957 acceptance criteria. The resin matrix and the fiber-matrix interface supply the stability that raw basalt fiber alone does not guarantee. BFRP's North American code recognition also trails GFRP's. Any BFRP product proposed for this project shows a current ICC-ES report (an independent code-compliance evaluation report) or equivalent before it is relied on structurally.

## [NN].7 Active Protection (Corrosion Inhibitors, Sacrificial Anodes, Cathodic Protection)

**Simple**

Rebar corrosion is an electrical process as much as a chemical one. That opens a second kind of defense. A block of zinc wired to the rebar corrodes preferentially. The steel it protects corrodes only after the zinc is consumed. Oregon protects its coastal bridges with powered versions of this idea. A house uses only the unpowered version: small zinc anodes cast into the concrete at the riskiest spots. They need no wires to the outside, no power, and no attention.

### Embedded Galvanic Anodes At Construction Time

Embedded galvanic anodes (zinc units that corrode in place of the steel they protect) are a named, commercially available product category for new construction, not only a repair technology. The documented example is Vector Corrosion Technologies' Galvashield N. The units wire into the reinforcing grid before the concrete is placed. Each is a high-purity zinc core cast around a steel tie wire inside a formulated cement mortar with internal pH of 14 or higher, which keeps the zinc electrochemically active over its service life. The manufacturer states a dual mechanism. While active, the anode maintains alkalinity and drives chloride ions away from the adjacent steel. It also draws ions into voids near the steel, and those persist as a residual protective layer after the zinc is consumed. The system uses no external power and no monitoring. Installation adds no construction-sequence complexity, because the units tie into the cage during ordinary reinforcement work. The candidate locations on this project are the ones this chapter ranks highest-risk: a below-grade wall in confirmed aggressive soil, and any at-grade wick-and-evaporate zone identified in the freeze-thaw and weathering section. No published residential per-unit price was found in the 2026 pass. Anode count and spacing are a corrosion-engineering design input, and the cost comes from a direct vendor quote.

### Corrosion Inhibitor Admixtures

An inhibitor admixture is a low-dose, per-cubic-yard mix addition that reduces the corrosion rate at the steel. The literature positions it as a supplement to upgraded reinforcement and cover, not a substitute: it lowers the underlying driver without removing it. It appears in the stacking logic at the end of this chapter as a secondary layer at high-risk elements.

### Impressed-Current Cathodic Protection: The Bridge-Scale Tool

Cathodic protection makes the reinforcing steel the protected electrode of a powered electrical circuit. ODOT ran four impressed-current projects on coastal reinforced-concrete bridges, using arc-sprayed zinc coatings (zinc melted and sprayed onto the concrete surface) as anodes. The projects: Cape Creek Bridge (1990-1992), Yaquina Bay Bridge south arches (1991-1995), Depoe Bay Bridge (1993-1997), and the Yaquina Bay south approach (1995-1997). The program followed the roughly $45 million demolition and replacement of the historic Alsea Bay Bridge. Protecting the remaining bridges cost less than replacing them. Documented figures: an average near $51.63 per square foot of protected concrete, and project totals of about $2.4 million (Cape Creek), $4 million (Depoe Bay), and $12.7 million (Yaquina Bay). The zinc anode coatings last roughly 25 years before replacement. The technology does not scale down to a house, for two independent reasons. It requires an external power source and a monitored electrical system for the life of the protection, a commitment with no documented residential precedent. And its cost structure is bridge-scale. At roughly $52 per square foot (1990s dollars), even a modest foundation wall area would run to a sum out of proportion to a whole house budget. The embedded galvanic anode reaches a related passive effect for a per-unit product price. What transfers is ODOT's cost logic, not the equipment: protecting an existing structure cost far less than replacing it. That is the same arithmetic behind this chapter's prevention spending.

### Choosing Among The Active Options

No published study compares upgraded reinforcement, inhibitor admixtures, and proactive anodes on a common cost-per-added-service-year basis. That number also cannot be constructed without this project's actual wall areas, rebar tonnage, and local quotes. The durability literature treats the three as complementary layers rather than competitors. Reinforcement metallurgy addresses the steel itself. The admixture lowers the corrosion driver in the whole mix. The anode protects the zone around it. Which layers stack and which duplicate is stated in the additive-versus-redundant table at the end of this chapter. For a below-grade wall with a confirmed-aggressive soil result, the defensible next step is a project-specific, quote-based comparison prepared by the structural engineer or a corrosion consultant.

## [NN].8 Self-Healing And Autogenous-Healing Concrete

**Simple**

Hairline cracks form in all concrete. Two mix technologies close small new cracks without repair work. A crystalline admixture grows sealing crystals wherever water enters. A bacterial additive contains dormant bacteria and their food supply. Water in a crack activates the bacteria, and they deposit limestone that fills it. The crystalline option is established and is already in this project's below-grade mix. The bacterial option reached the US market in 2024 and is still being priced for house-scale orders.

### Crystalline Self-Sealing

The crystalline admixture specified for this project's below-grade elements is described in the mix-design section of this chapter. Its self-sealing property is the relevant point here: the crystal-growth reaction reactivates whenever water reaches unreacted admixture, so a crack that forms years after the pour triggers new crystal growth at that crack. That response to future cracks, which hydrophobic pore-blockers do not share, is the reason the crystalline category was selected.

### Bacterial Self-Healing

Basilisk Self-Healing Concrete, a Netherlands-developed product, mixes dormant bacterial spores and a calcium-lactate nutrient into the concrete. When a crack forms and water reaches the spores, the bacteria activate and metabolically produce limestone (calcium carbonate) that fills the crack; the published capability closes cracks up to about 1 mm wide. The product entered the US market in roughly September 2024 through a US partner (Restoration Partners LLC). What remains unconfirmed for this project: pricing, minimum order quantities, whether it is sold as a ready-mix additive at custom-home scale, and any documented single-family installation. The technology is therefore past the watch-only stage on availability, and it enters this project's specification only after a direct inquiry to the US distributor answers those four questions.

### Projected Service Life Versus Observed Service Life

Claims of extreme service life for novel binders illustrate the difference this chapter maintains between projection and observation. Geopolymer binders proposed for nuclear waste containment are tested by short-term standardized leach protocols plus mechanistic modeling. The common protocol pairs a 7-day product consistency test with a 90-day semi-dynamic leach test. Published results report leach-rate reductions against portland-cement barriers, over 95 percent lower for cesium and strontium in one review. Those figures come from the short tests, not from long observation. A multi-millennial figure sometimes attached to these materials was not confirmed in the project research and is not cited in this book. The methodology itself transfers usefully. A short-term permeability or leach test, run before and after simulated freeze-thaw cycling, gives an owner-made mix a defensible comparison against a portland-cement baseline. It does so without claiming field data that does not exist. That test-to-prove procedure is stated in the Owner-Made Blocks and Alternative Concretes chapter.

## [NN].9 Durability By Detailing (Overhangs, Drainage, And Avoiding Water And Salt Traps)

**Simple**

Every mechanism in this chapter requires water. Geometry decides where water sits against the structure. So the drawings themselves are a durability tool. The protections in this section are line-drawing decisions made before construction. They are priced into the design rather than added to it. A drier wall corrodes more slowly than a wet one. It also passes through fewer damaging freeze cycles and grows no salt bands.

### The Durability Design Review

The mechanisms behind this checklist are stated earlier in this chapter. Overhangs, wall wetting, and the wick-and-evaporate salt geometry are in the freeze-thaw section. Drip-edge and overhang detailing at the roof perimeter is stated in the Roof System and Roof-Wall Junctions chapter. Chronic ponding is in the corrosion section. Drain-field chemistry is in the chemical-attack section. The two detailing subjects not covered earlier, surface sealers and the deliberately unreinforced element, follow the checklist.

**Applied**

1. Run this checklist on the final site plan and elevations, before permit submittal.
2. Trace every chronically wet zone: soil contact, irrigation, downspout discharge, planters, retained earth. Mark each point where one touches concrete.
3. At each contact, confirm a capillary break (a layer too coarse for water to wick through), a membrane, or a gap, or remove the source of chronic wetness.
4. Confirm no planter is built directly against a structural wall.
5. Confirm each downspout discharges at the distance the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, and Concrete Placement chapter specifies.
6. Check the dispersal layout against the foundation footprint for the two chemical-attack geometries. Check it against the DEQ setbacks in the Water Independence chapter.
7. Mark every location where water could pond against structure: garage floor, perimeter drains, exterior slabs over habitable space. Require waterproofing and drainage sign-off at each.
8. Confirm the roof overhang shelters exposed concrete and masonry faces (the Roof System and Roof-Wall Junctions chapter).
9. Record each element's assigned exposure classes on the structural drawings, with the soil-test reports attached.
10. File the marked-up set in the design-review file.

### Penetrating Sealers

A silane or siloxane penetrating sealer bonds to the walls of the concrete's surface pores and lines them with a water-repellent layer. The pores stay open, so water vapor still passes and the wall assembly keeps its drying ability. That is the documented functional difference from a film-forming coating, which blocks vapor. The wall's vapor strategy is stated in the Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, and Radon chapter, and a penetrating sealer does not change it. Reduced bulk water uptake reduces the water available to drive efflorescence, leaching, and freeze-thaw saturation. That reduction is mechanism-consistent, and no independent long-term field study quantifying it was located, so the mechanism is stated here without a percentage. Service life is a manufacturer-published range: 7 to 10 years for one product (claiming a 95 percent water-absorption reduction over that span) and over 10 years for another. This project treats sealing as a recurring maintenance item on a 7-to-15-year cycle. The actual interval comes from the specified product's published warranty and service-life data.

### The Deliberately Unreinforced Element

The entire reinforcement-corrosion problem exists because of embedded steel. An element designed with no steel at all exits that failure mode permanently. The only multi-century concrete precedent that exists, the unreinforced Roman work in this chapter's opening section, is exactly this construction. Four candidates on this property qualify: a freestanding garden wall, a root cellar, a well house, and a retaining wall below the height at which gravity-wall design stops working. Three shared conditions define the category. The element is low-consequence: its failure threatens neither life safety nor the dwelling. Its loads are resisted by mass and geometry alone (adequate base width, taper, and a footing below frost depth). And no code provision requires reinforcement for it. Sizing any specific element as unreinforced is a structural-engineering determination made case by case against its actual height, loads, and soil. Tabby (a historic lime-shell concrete: oyster shells burned to a lime binder, mixed with water, sand, ash, and broken shell as aggregate) is one candidate owner-made material for this category, and Oregon's active coastal oyster farms are a possible shell source. The mass-design options are stated in the What Concrete Uniquely Enables chapter, and the owner-made materials for such elements, including the tabby method and its shell-sourcing inquiry, in the Owner-Made Blocks and Alternative Concretes chapter.

### Buried Membranes And The Structure Behind Them

The What Concrete Uniquely Enables chapter specifies the permanent-burial waterproofing systems for bermed walls and earth-covered roofs: hot rubberized asphalt, bentonite composite panels, redundant pairing, and pre-backfill electronic leak detection. It also states the data gap: no independently monitored 30-plus-year field study exists for any membrane system under permanent cover. This chapter states the service-life reasoning behind that assembly. Burial removes the two dominant degradation drivers of exposed membranes, ultraviolet exposure and thermal cycling. What remains against a buried membrane is installation defects, penetrations, and sustained hydrostatic exposure. The pre-backfill survey and the drainage plane address those. Because no membrane system can be verified to the structure's design life, the design assigns the multi-century requirement to the concrete itself, not to the membrane. The buried wall's own protections therefore assume imperfect membrane performance and direct soil contact. Exposure classes are assigned from the actual soil chemistry. The crystalline admixture is selected for hydrostatic self-sealing. Cover runs at code-plus-1/2-inch, reinforcement metallurgy steps up where the soil test requires it, and the optional anode and embedded-sensor measures go to the least accessible runs. The membrane system lowers the water reaching the wall; the concrete's design assumes some water arrives anyway. The ordinary re-excavatable foundation case remains the Envelope chapter's subject.

## [NN].10 The Century-Scale Inspection And Maintenance Regime

**Simple**

This chapter's opening section stated that a house is maintained, not sealed and abandoned. Maintenance for a concrete shell is short and periodic. Most of it is looking at known weak points once a year. The list of weak points comes from this chapter's own mechanisms. Water marks, rust stains, white mineral deposits, and new cracks are the visible signals. Each signal appears years before the damage becomes structural. Correcting a drainage fault when a stain first appears is maintenance work. Corrosion left for years becomes a structural repair.

### The Inspection Logic

Corrosion damage stays invisible until it is severe. The visible surface signals (staining, cracking, efflorescence, scaling bands just above grade) precede structural loss by years. The Champlain Towers South record in the corrosion section is the reason the regime below concentrates on pond-prone and chronically wet locations. Where an element is permanently buried and cannot be inspected without excavation, the regime substitutes construction-time instrumentation.

### Embedded Monitoring At The Uninspectable Elements

Cast-in wireless sensors are a real product category. Giatec's SmartRock line embeds on the rebar before the pour and reports without wires. Its iCOR device measures corrosion potential (a voltage reading that indicates active corrosion), corrosion rate, and in-place electrical resistivity of reinforced concrete non-destructively. Pricing is by vendor quote, and the product line is positioned toward commercial construction. Whether the vendor sells at single-house quantity is confirmed by direct inquiry before the design relies on it. The placement logic follows inspectability, not risk alone. An above-grade element can be tested from outside by a technician at ten-to-twenty-year intervals. A cast-in sensor therefore adds the most value at the elements external testing cannot reach: the footing's most soil-exposed run and any bermed wall. The embedded galvanic anodes, where installed, require no scheduled action; that absence of a monitoring duty is a documented property of the product category, per the active-protection section. The reading schedule and the record location are set in the Testing, Commissioning, and Quality Control chapter. The readings file into the binder per the Documentation, Maintenance, and Future Upgrades chapter.

## [NN].11 Options, Costs, And What This Project Specifies

**Simple**

This chapter names many protections. They are not all bought at once. Some address different attacks and stack usefully. Others solve the same attack twice, and the second purchase adds no protection. This section sorts the whole toolkit into those two groups. It then states the full specification for the one element that matters most. It ends with the list of decisions that stay open until the site and design exist.

### Additive Versus Redundant

Sorting each technology by the mechanism it addresses shows which combinations stack and which duplicate.

**Genuinely additive, specified together where the element's risk justifies each layer.** Each of the following operates at a different point in the same causal chain:

- drainage and waterproofing detailing: reduces the water that reaches the concrete at all
- generous overhangs: reduce wall wetting and saturated freeze cycles
- SCMs and low w/cm: slow mass transport through the pore structure
- added cover: lengthens the path to the steel (time scales with the square of the depth)
- crystalline admixture: reseals the cracks that form despite everything above
- corrosion-resistant reinforcement: protects the steel itself if ingress arrives anyway
- penetrating sealer: reduces bulk water uptake at exposed exterior faces

**Redundant pairings, where the second purchase duplicates the first:**

- A galvanic anode next to stainless bar. The anode is most cost-effective paired with black or galvanized steel at a confirmed high-risk location; stainless already resists what the anode would protect against. This is the clearest documented redundancy in the research.
- A hydrophobic pore-blocker and a crystalline admixture in one element. Both reduce water ingress through the pore structure; the crystalline category performs under hydrostatic pressure and reseals new cracks, which the hydrophobic category does not, so the crystalline option alone is the documented choice.
- Impressed-current cathodic protection with anything: it is not a residential-scale option at any pairing, per the active-protection section.

### The Maximum-Durability Specification For The Highest-Consequence Element

The footing and foundation stem wall are the project's candidate for the single highest-consequence, hardest-to-repair element: permanently buried, structurally critical to everything above, and reachable after construction only by excavation. The stacked specification, every component from this chapter's own sections:

- mix at the conservative end of the structural chapter's specified range: w/cm 0.40, f'c 5,000 psi
- the ternary blend (roughly 25 percent fly ash with 5 to 8 percent silica fume), plant-batched for metering accuracy
- cover at code minimum plus 1/2 inch, the cross-code-corroborated margin
- hot-dip galvanized bar as the element baseline, solid stainless at any portion the soil test places in S2/S3 or C2
- a crystalline admixture, for hydrostatic performance and crack resealing below grade
- embedded galvanic anodes at the confirmed-aggressive portions, tied in before the pour
- one embedded wireless corrosion sensor at the most soil-exposed run, the location external testing cannot reach without excavation

No total dollar figure exists for this stack against the baseline, because the total depends on footing and stem-wall quantities the structural design has not yet produced. Each component is a per-unit or per-cubic-yard cost stated in its own section. The maximum-durability character comes from stacking several such measures at one deliberately chosen element, not from any single high-cost technology. Specifying the full stack, a partial stack, or the structural chapter's baseline alone is the owner's and structural engineer's decision at design time.

- no sodium chloride or calcium chloride deicer on the project's concrete, and no calcium chloride accelerator in any mix
- epoxy-coated bar not selected at the highest-chloride locations

## [NN].12 Code And Standards

**Simple**

The durability rules in this chapter come from three kinds of documents. Code sets the floor: exposure classes with required mix values. Guides explain the mechanisms behind the floor, without legal force. Standards define the tests and materials, so a specification can be checked. This section states which document plays which role, and which values still await primary-text confirmation.

**Professional: the document hierarchy for this chapter.** ACI 318 Chapter 19 and ACI 332 Chapter 4 are the mandatory layer, entering this project through the engineered structural design. The structural chapter states the adoption path. ACI PRC-201.2-23 is guidance: it is cited in this book's Professional prose as mechanism rationale and never as a requirement. BS 8500 and Eurocode 2 are foreign standards with no authority in Oregon. This chapter uses their 50-to-100-year adjustment logic as a cross-check and labels any extrapolation from it as engineering judgment. Life-365 is a consortium-maintained model, cited only as the engineer's estimate when run. 10 CFR Part 61 is federal law for waste containment, cited in this chapter as precedent, never as an applicable requirement.

Three value sets in this chapter are corroborated through independent secondary sources but await primary-text confirmation. They are the ACI 318-19 Table 19.3.2.1 chloride and sulfate limits, the upper tiers of the ASTM C1202 and AASHTO T358 classification tables, and the BS 8500 Table A.4 versus A.5 numeric deltas. The engineer of record confirms each against the purchased text before construction documents issue. One recheck is date-bound rather than paywall-bound. NIST's full final Champlain Towers South report, with the measured steel-section-loss data, was unpublished as of 2026. The corrosion section's account is updated against it when it appears.
