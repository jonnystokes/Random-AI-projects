# Chapter 7: Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement

This chapter contains the foundation construction work: foundation type selection, excavation and subgrade acceptance, slab stack and under-slab provisions, basement wall waterproofing, and foundation drainage. It continues through concrete sourcing (ready-mix versus owner-mixed), pour-day planning, placement, finishing, curing, backfill, and the records that prove the work was done correctly.

The Soil, Ground Prep, Drainage, And Foundation Risk chapter provides the soil class and the bearing assumption the foundation design starts from. The Structural System And Concrete Design chapter provides the structural design, the engineered-or-prescriptive path, and the reinforcement detailing. The work in this chapter produces placed, cured, documented concrete from those inputs.

The chapter is written for the engineered design path as the primary case, the typical posture for a long-life concrete-and-reinforced-masonry residence in western Oregon. It notes the prescriptive-path simplifications where they apply.

## 7.1 What The Foundation Must Do

The foundation is the connection between the structural shell and the soil. Every load travels through it, every drainage decision is set by it, and many of the things hidden by it become inaccessible after the slab and walls go up.

**Simple**

The foundation holds the house and spreads its weight onto the soil. It controls how water reaches and leaves the building. It ties the structure to the ground so wind and earthquakes cannot lift or shift it. Many items get embedded in the foundation during construction: plumbing rough-in, electrical grounding, radon piping, anchor bolts, utility sleeves. None of them can be added later without breaking concrete. Everything that goes inside the foundation is planned before the first pour.

**Applied**

Before the foundation type is chosen, answer these questions in writing and keep the answers in the project binder:

- What soil or engineered fill will support the foundation? (See the soil chapter.)
- Which foundation type is being used: spread footings with stem walls, full basement, slab-on-grade, frost-protected shallow foundation (FPSF), pier-and-grade-beam, or mat foundation?
- What is the frost-protection path: depth below frost line, FPSF per ORSC R403.3, ASCE 32-01 design for unheated structures, or solid rock?
- Where does water go during winter? What is the drainage outlet: daylight discharge, sump pump, or storm system?
- Is the building pad higher than surrounding drainage, with a 6-inch fall in the first 10 feet per ORSC R401.3?
- Will the house have a basement, crawl space, slab-on-grade, or a combination?
- What plumbing, radon piping, electrical grounding, sleeves for utilities, anchor bolts, and embeds need to be in place before concrete?
- Which inspections happen before each pour and before any work is covered?
- Who can approve a field change, and what is the change-order process?

If any answer changes during construction, stop and resolve it before the next pour.

**Professional**

Foundation work integrates bearing pressure, settlement, differential movement, frost protection, groundwater, hydrostatic pressure, lateral earth pressure, uplift, drainage, vapor drive, soil gas, reinforcement detailing, penetrations, concrete durability, inspection timing, and backfill sequencing.

Subgrade, aggregate, vapor retarder, insulation, soil-gas layer, reinforcement, concrete, wall connection, waterproofing, drains, and backfill all interact, and hidden coordination errors between them become permanent foundation defects.

For a long-life concrete house, foundation durability is set by:

- concrete mix design appropriate to exposure class (typically F1 above grade in mild exposure; F1 or F2 below grade in contact with soil; W1 or W2 where moisture is present)
- adequate cover to reinforcement (3 inches cast against earth; 2 inches exposed to weather; 1.5 inches not exposed)
- effective drainage that keeps the perimeter and underside dry
- waterproofing that maintains continuity at terminations and penetrations
- protection of the work from freezing or hot weather during the curing window

**Code**

```
Surface water must drain away from the foundation.
  6-inch fall within the first 10 feet, directed away from the building; swales or piped drains where 10 feet is not available.
  Source: ORSC R401.3 (2023 edition).

Questionable soil triggers testing.
  The building official may require a soils investigation where expansive, compressible, or shifting soils are likely.
  Source: ORSC R401.4.

The foundation sections divide the work.
  Footings (sizing and seismic reinforcement), foundation and retaining walls (prescriptive tables and the engineered path), foundation drainage (perimeter drain around below-grade habitable space), dampproofing as the default with waterproofing where water is known, and slabs-on-grade (aggregate base, vapor retarder, edge insulation).
  Source: ORSC R403; R404; R405; R406.1 and R406.2; R506.

Foundation inspections come before every pour.
  Footing, foundation wall, and under-slab inspections are required before concrete where administered by the jurisdiction.
  Source: ORSC R109.1.1.

Concrete design and durability follow the concrete codes.
  The residential concrete code, the general structural concrete code, and the exposure-class durability chapter with its mix-design limits.
  Source: ACI 332-20; ACI 318 (edition per Oregon adoption); ACI 318 Chapter 19.
```

## 7.2 Choosing The Foundation Type

Foundation type is selected from soil class, frost depth, groundwater elevation, building height, basement decision, slope, and structural-engineer recommendation.

**Professional: foundation type selection matrix**

| Foundation type | Best fit | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Spread footings with stem walls and slab-on-grade above | Most common Oregon residential. Good drainage, moderate frost depth, no basement | Frost depth must be reachable with conventional excavation; not for high water table |
| Thickened-edge monolithic slab | Non-expansive soil with adequate bearing; heated buildings pairing with the FPSF insulation details | Footing and slab placed as a single pour; the perimeter thickening must reach the prescriptive footing dimensions; not for expansive soil |
| Full basement (engineered) | Where below-grade habitable space is wanted; soil supports it; site permits drainage | Hydrostatic exposure raises waterproofing demand; engineered foundation required for SDC D0/D1/D2 |
| Daylight basement | Hillside sites where one or more walls are above grade | Easier waterproofing than fully buried basement; site must have the slope |
| Crawl space (vented or conditioned) | Where slab-on-grade is undesired; allows MEP access from below | Larger envelope and more inspection burden than slab |
| Frost-protected shallow foundation (FPSF) per R403.3 | Heated buildings where reaching frost depth requires deep excavation; rocky sites; ICF or slab-on-grade homes | Heated-only (64 °F monthly mean minimum); separate detail for unheated attached spaces |
| ASCE 32-01 FPSF | Unheated buildings, semi-heated buildings, designs outside R403.3 prescriptive | Requires non-frost-susceptible soil and engineered design |
| Mat foundation | Difficult soil, high water table, large open floor plan, or distributed load | Engineered; concrete-intensive; effective for problem soils |
| Pier-and-grade-beam | Expansive soil with deep active zone; uplift exposure; sloped sites | Engineered; reinforcement detailing critical; geotechnical investigation required |

For an Oregon long-life concrete-and-reinforced-masonry home in SDC D0/D1, the typical foundation is engineered: spread footings with reinforced concrete stem walls and a slab-on-grade above, or a full basement with engineered foundation walls and a structural slab. Frost depth, soil class, and the basement decision drive the variation.

**Professional: thickened-edge monolithic slab and raft variants**

A thickened-edge monolithic slab places the footing and the slab as a single pour, with the slab edge deepened to the prescriptive footing dimensions at the perimeter. The single pour removes one form set, one concrete delivery, and the curing gap between staged pours. Published cost comparisons run roughly $5-$10 per square foot of foundation for a monolithic slab (2026). A separate footing, stem wall, and slab runs $6-$14 per square foot. The thickened-edge configuration is one of the accepted frost-protected shallow foundation geometries; the insulation details in §7.9.2 attach to it directly.

A raft slab omits the thickened perimeter and distributes the building load across the full slab area: a mat foundation at shallow depth. The larger bearing area reduces contact pressure, which suits soft or compressible soil. The ORSC R403.3 prescriptive path does not cover a raft configuration. A raft is designed by the structural engineer of record, under ASCE 32-01 or as a full structural mat.

**Professional: coastal tsunami siting**

For a parcel inside a mapped tsunami inundation zone, foundation design leaves the residential prescriptive path entirely. FEMA P-646 (Guidelines for Design of Structures for Vertical Evacuation from Tsunamis, third edition, 2019) is the design guideline for structures intended to survive tsunami loading, and ASCE 7-16 Chapter 6 is the load standard for tsunami hydrodynamic and debris-impact forces. P-646 design requires more than ordinary coastal construction. Its elements: seismic detailing combined with tsunami loading, progressive-collapse continuity, engineered breakaway wall details, and foundations deeper than a standard elevated pier design. FEMA Technical Bulletin 9 covers ordinary coastal flood breakaway walls under the National Flood Insurance Program; it is a separate framework from P-646 tsunami design. No general cost delta between P-646 design and standard elevated coastal construction is published; the delta is a site-specific engineering output. The structural engineer of record produces it from three inputs. The inputs: the parcel's DOGAMI tsunami inundation zone designation, the jurisdiction's determination on whether P-646 applies to the occupancy, and the site's geotechnical data. A single-family residence is not automatically required to meet P-646; the standard applies where the jurisdiction requires it or the owner elects it.

The Soil, Ground Prep, Drainage, And Foundation Risk chapter contains the soil-class screening matrix that feeds foundation-type selection.

## 7.3 Basement Or Slab-On-Grade

The basement decision is large. It controls excavation depth, waterproofing demand, drainage strategy, structural cost, and habitable space. For a long-life concrete house, the site facts decide it.

**Simple**

A basement is a concrete box buried in wet ground. The ground pushes water at it from every side for the life of the house. On a dry, sloped lot, water drains away by gravity and a basement works well. Its added floor area costs less per square foot than above-grade area. On a flat lot with a high water table, dryness depends on pumps, power, and waterproofing that never lapses. The site conditions make this choice, and the tables below sort them.

**Applied: decision criteria**

| Factor | Favors basement | Favors slab-on-grade |
|---|---|---|
| Soil class | Stable; well-drained; bearing layer well below grade | Marginal soil at depth; high organic content below grade |
| Groundwater | Seasonal high water table well below proposed basement floor | High water table at or near proposed basement floor |
| Slope | Hillside site allows daylight basement | Flat site requires fully buried basement, harder waterproofing |
| Drainage outlet | Gravity discharge to daylight or storm system available | No gravity outlet; sump pump and backup power required |
| Habitable space | Below-grade habitable space wanted | Single-level living, no basement needed |
| Budget | Excavation, foundation, waterproofing, and drainage cost is acceptable | Project cost favors slab-on-grade |
| Long-life durability | Basement walls in well-drained soil with proper waterproofing perform well | Basement in marginal soil or high water table is a long-term maintenance burden |

For a fully buried basement in a high-water-table site, correct waterproofing, drainage, and sump-pump backup are all required. The combined cost is substantial. The cost of doing it wrong is higher: water in the basement is a chronic problem that recurs after every storm.

A daylight basement (one or more walls above grade) reduces hydrostatic exposure and simplifies waterproofing. It produces below-grade habitable space with less risk than a fully buried basement.

**Professional**

Habitable basement requirements (ORSC R305 ceiling height ≥ 7 ft; R303 light and ventilation; R310 emergency escape with 5.7 sq ft clear opening minimum) apply when the basement is intended for living space. Non-habitable basements (mechanical, storage) have lower requirements but still need fire separation and egress for occasional access.

Crawl space (R408) is a different rule set: ventilated (R408.1) or unventilated/conditioned (R408.2) with a ground vapor retarder. Foundation walls dampproofed per R406.1; waterproofing per R406.2 only where severe site conditions warrant.

**Code**

```
Habitable basements must meet ceiling-height minimums.
  7 feet in habitable rooms, hallways, bathrooms, kitchens, and habitable basements; 6 feet 8 inches in non-habitable basements, with exceptions for beams, ducts, and similar projections.
  Source: ORSC R305.

Habitable basements and sleeping rooms need escape openings.
  Minimum net clear opening 5.7 square feet (5.0 at grade), 24 inches minimum height, 20 inches minimum width, sill no more than 44 inches above the floor.
  Source: ORSC R310.

The dwelling needs at least one egress door.
  Source: ORSC R311.

Crawl spaces take ventilation or conditioning, plus access.
  1 square foot of vent per 150 square feet of ground area, or the unvented conditioned-crawl provisions; ground vapor retarder; 18x24-inch minimum access opening.
  Source: ORSC R408; R408.2; R408.4.

Below-grade walls enclosing interior space must be dampproofed.
  Acceptable materials: bituminous coating, 3 lb/yd2 acrylic-modified cement, 1/8-inch surface-bonding cement (ASTM C887), or any R406.2 waterproofing material; masonry walls get 3/8-inch Portland cement parging first.
  Source: ORSC R406.1.

Known water conditions require waterproofing instead.
  Acceptable systems: two-ply hot-mopped felts, 55-lb roll roofing, 6-mil PVC or polyethylene, 40-mil polymer-modified asphalt, 60-mil flexible polymer cement, 1/8-inch cement-based fiber-reinforced coating, or other approved methods.
  Source: ORSC R406.2.
```

## 7.4 Oregon Foundation Inspection Rhythm

Foundation inspections are gates. Concrete cannot proceed past a hold point until the inspection is approved. The inspection rhythm has to be built into the construction schedule, not added afterward.

**Applied: inspection sequence**

For a typical Oregon residential foundation:

| Inspection | When called | What the inspector verifies |
|---|---|---|
| Erosion and sediment control | Where required by the jurisdiction; before site disturbance | Silt fence, inlet protection, stabilized construction entrance |
| Footing | Before concrete placement | Bearing surface (undisturbed soil or approved engineered fill), footing depth, footing width per Table R403.1, reinforcement size, grade, spacing, splices, cover, anchor bolt locations marked, UFER ground bonded to vertical rebar, no debris/water/ice in forms, slope (top level, bottom not exceeding 1 in 10) |
| Foundation wall (stem wall) | Before concrete placement; sometimes combined with footing inspection in jurisdictions that pour monolithic | Reinforcement continuity from footing, anchor bolts, sleeves, blockouts, dampproofing readiness on the soil side |
| Foundation drainage | Before backfill; sometimes combined with stem-wall inspection | Drain rock, perforated pipe, filter fabric, dampproofing or waterproofing membrane, dimple drainage board where used |
| Under-slab (pre-pour) | Before slab placement | Subgrade compaction, capillary break aggregate, vapor retarder, soil-gas retarder where applicable, under-slab insulation, sub-slab plumbing, electrical conduit, radon piping per Appendix AF where applicable |
| Slab placement | During or immediately after; some jurisdictions inspect during finishing | Final review of placement, embeds, control joints |

Marion County, Lane County, Multnomah County, and the Portland Permitting & Development model are representative. The Oregon BCD "Cite It Write It" checklist consolidates the most common code violations found at residential inspections; it is the most useful single document for self-checking before scheduling each inspection.

Local jurisdictions vary in scheduling. Some combine footing and pre-slab into one inspection; others split. Confirm the local rhythm at permit application. Marion County (503) 588-5147, Portland (503) 823-7388, and similar lines in other jurisdictions handle scheduling.

**Professional: pre-inspection self-check**

Before calling for a footing inspection, verify each item below. The list is synthesized from Marion County's Residential Footing and Foundation Checklist, the BCD Cite-It Write-It checklist, and the West Linn and Lane County checklists. The items match across Oregon jurisdictions; the form differs.

- footing on undisturbed soil or approved engineered fill, classified per the soil report
- footing 12 inches minimum below finished grade, or below the local frost line, whichever is deeper (R403.1.4)
- footing width per Table R403.1 for the soil bearing value used
- bottom horizontal #4 bar placed 3–4 inches from the bottom of the footing (R403.1.3, SDC D0/D1/D2)
- top horizontal #4 bar within 12 inches of the top of the stem wall (R403.1.3)
- vertical #4 at maximum 4 ft on center, with standard hook to the bottom of the footing extending 14 inches into the stem wall (R403.1.3, SDC D0/D1/D2)
- anchor bolts, 1/2 in. diameter minimum, embedded 7 inches, at 6 ft on center maximum (4 ft for SDC D2 two-story), within 12 inches of plate ends, with 3 in. by 3 in. by 0.229 in. plate washers (R403.1.6, R602.11)
- hold-downs and rebar for braced wall panels or portal frames tied in place (not wet-set) per R602.10.6
- UFER ground: uncoated #4, 20 ft minimum length, 3 inches from the bottom of the footing, 2 inches of concrete cover (R403.1.8)
- setback from descending slope per Figure R403.1.9.1 (1H:1V from toe)
- top of foundation 6 inches minimum above finished grade (R317 protection)
- top of foundation 12 inches plus 2% above street gutter discharge or drainage device inlet (R401.3)
- surface drainage 6 inches fall in 10 ft per R401.3
- drain tile or perforated pipe where habitable space below grade is enclosed (R405.1)
- forms straight, plumb, braced, and clean
- no debris, water, ice, or snow in forms (R403.1; ACI 318 §26.5.2)
- reinforcement clear-spacing per ACI 318 §25.2 (1 inch minimum or bar diameter, whichever is greater)
- chairs and spacers maintain cover at all locations
- penetrations, sleeves, embeds, and blockouts in approved locations

A pre-pour walkthrough with the inspector when scheduling resolves most issues before the truck arrives.

**Code**

```
Foundation inspections have fixed stages.
  Footing, foundation, and pre-slab inspections where administered by the jurisdiction.
  Source: ORSC R109.1.1.

Footings are sized by table and placed by depth rule.
  Width per Table R403.1 for the soil bearing value; depth 12 inches minimum below finished grade or below the frost line, whichever is deeper.
  Source: ORSC R403.1; R403.1.4.

High-seismic footings carry a fixed reinforcement pattern.
  Bottom #4 within 3-4 inches of the footing bottom, top #4 within 12 inches of the stem-wall top, vertical #4 at 4 feet maximum on center with a standard hook.

The footing contains the grounding electrode.

```

## 7.5 Excavation And Subgrade Acceptance

Excavation reaches the bearing layer the soil work identified, exposes the soil for verification, and produces the surface concrete will be placed on. The verification is the point: the exposed soil either matches the design assumption or triggers a documented change.

**Applied**

Excavation sequence:

1. Stake the building footprint with offset hubs that survive excavation.
2. Strip and stockpile topsoil. Topsoil is not building soil; do not use it for structural fill. Plan a stockpile location that does not interfere with construction access or drainage.
3. Excavate to the design footing elevation in stages. Over-excavate where soft zones, organics, or unsuitable fill are encountered.
4. Proof-roll the exposed subgrade with a loaded truck, vibrating compactor, or excavator track. Document any soft, pumping, or yielding areas.
5. Remove and replace soft zones with engineered fill per the geotechnical report or with a design-engineer-approved alternative.
6. Verify the bearing surface matches the design assumption: visual classification per ASTM D2488, pocket penetrometer reading on cohesive soil if applicable, photograph with reference scale.
7. Protect the exposed bearing surface from rain, frost, and traffic until concrete placement. A clean tarp over the surface is acceptable for short delays; longer delays require re-acceptance before pour.
8. Call for footing inspection.

Common discoveries during excavation that trigger redesign:

- soft zone or organic horizon at footing depth not identified in the field pass
- groundwater higher than expected; seepage into the excavation
- buried debris or undocumented fill
- bedrock at or above footing elevation (may allow shallower foundation; confirms bearing)
- expansive clay with active-zone evidence
- step in the bearing layer that the foundation design did not anticipate

Each of these is a stop point. Continuing to pour concrete over an unresolved discovery creates a permanent foundation defect. The fix is a documented field change with engineer-of-record approval and an inspection that verifies the change.

**Professional: bearing surface acceptance criteria**

The footing bears on either undisturbed natural soil at design elevation or on engineered fill placed and compacted to a documented specification. The acceptance record should document:

- date and weather at acceptance
- bearing layer classification (USCS symbol or USDA texture class plus consistency)
- bearing pressure assumption from the design (presumptive value from ORSC Table R401.4.1, or geotechnical report value)
- field check method (visual classification, pocket penetrometer, DCP, or third-party verification)
- photographs at footing elevation with a reference scale, taken at multiple locations across the footprint
- inspector's name, signature, and date

For owner-builder projects relying on presumptive bearing values, the acceptance is straightforward: the soil class is verified, the bearing surface is clean and dry, and the footing form is set on the verified surface.

For engineered foundations, the structural engineer of record may require a site visit at this stage to confirm the bearing surface matches the design. The geotechnical engineer may also visit to confirm the soil class. These visits are scheduled ahead of the building inspector's call.

**Professional: sulfate and pH analysis on the same borings**

The geotechnical and septic borings already planned for the project collect the soil samples that concrete exposure classification needs. ASTM C1580 (water-soluble sulfate in soil) determines the sulfate exposure class that sets cement type and mix limits; the exposure-class framework is in the Designing For Centuries chapter. Adding the sulfate analysis to soil already being sampled costs one laboratory fee, not a second drilling mobilization. One Oregon-area geotechnical laboratory's published fee schedule lists ASTM C1580 at $235 standard turnaround and $470 expedited (2023 fee schedule; a representative rate, not a project quote). Soil pH is a low-cost add-on run by the same laboratories. The dependency: when the boring scope is finalized, the owner directs the retained geotechnical engineer to add ASTM C1580 water-soluble sulfate and soil pH to the analysis list for the samples already being pulled. Sulfate-resistant cement, where the result warrants it, is a mix-design decision made before the first structural pour, not a retrofit.

**Code**

```
The bearing layer is classified in the field, and fill is density-tested.
  Visual-manual classification for the bearing layer; nuclear-gauge or sand-cone density tests where engineered fill is used.
  Source: ASTM D2488; ASTM D6938 or D1556.

Presumptive bearing values are fixed by soil category.
  Crystalline bedrock 12,000 psf; sedimentary or foliated rock 4,000 psf; sandy gravel and gravel 3,000 psf; sand, silty sand, clayey sand, silty gravel, clayey gravel 2,000 psf; clay, sandy clay, silty clay, clayey silt, silt, sandy silt 1,500 psf.
  Source: ORSC Table R401.4.1.

Compressible or shifting soil is removed or stabilized.
  Removal-and-replacement, dewatering, or chemical stabilization within the active zone.
  Source: ORSC R401.4.2.

Excavations 5 feet and deeper require protection.
  Sloping, benching, shoring, or shielding, selected by soil type.
  Source: OSHA 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P.

Every excavation starts with a locate request.
  Mandatory call-before-you-dig with two business days notice; the service is free.
  Source: ORS 757.541 and 757.542; dial 811 (Oregon Utility Notification Center).

Wood near the foundation top has a height rule.
  Top of foundation 6 inches above finished grade where wood elements (sill plates, adjacent wood) sit on it.
  Source: ORSC R317.
```

## 7.6 Compaction And Base Course

The aggregate base course under the slab does three things at once: distributes load, provides a capillary break to interrupt rising moisture from the subgrade, and serves as the gas-permeable layer for radon-county subslab depressurization where Appendix AF applies.

**Applied**

Standard slab base course for Oregon residential, top to bottom:

| Layer | Specification |
|---|---|
| Concrete slab | 4 inches minimum (R506.1); thicker for heavy use, garages, equipment pads |
| Vapor retarder | 10-mil minimum, ASTM E1745 Class A; lapped 6 inches; sealed at penetrations (R506.2.3) |
| Rigid insulation (where used) | XPS or EPS per ASTM C578; type matched to load and exposure |
| Soil-gas retarder (radon counties) | 6-mil polyethylene as a separate layer, OR a single 15-mil vapor retarder serves both functions |
| Aggregate base | 4 inches minimum of clean 3/4-inch or #57 crushed stone; gas-permeable for radon mitigation (R506.2.2) |
| Compacted subgrade | Natural soil or engineered fill, free of organic material; 95% Standard Proctor density minimum |

Subgrade preparation:

1. Strip topsoil and organics across the slab footprint.
2. Proof-roll the exposed subgrade. Document soft zones for over-excavation.
3. Place engineered fill (where required) in lifts of 6–8 inches loose, 4–6 inches compacted; moisture-condition each lift to ±2% of optimum; compact to 95% Standard Proctor.
4. Verify each lift density per the project specification frequency (typically one test per 500–2,500 sq ft per lift).
5. Place the aggregate base course in one or two lifts; level and compact lightly to consolidate.
6. Verify aggregate thickness at multiple points across the footprint.
7. Place the vapor retarder; lap and seal joints; turn up at footings/walls; seal penetrations.
8. Place the under-slab insulation if used (between vapor retarder and aggregate, or above aggregate and below vapor retarder; sequence depends on the assembly; see §7.7).
9. Install soil-gas retarder where Appendix AF applies (or rely on a single 15-mil vapor retarder serving both functions).
10. Install sub-slab plumbing, electrical conduit, radon piping, and any other embeds; mark all locations on the as-built drawing.
11. Verify the under-slab assembly before the inspector arrives.

**Professional**

Aggregate gradation: clean 3/4-inch crushed stone (#57 stone, or #67 stone) with less than 5% passing the #200 sieve is the standard. The combination of openness for drainage, gas permeability for radon, and capillary-break function requires limiting fines.

Subgrade-to-slab interface: ACI 302.2R-06 reversed earlier guidance that placed sand between vapor retarder and slab; the current recommendation places the vapor retarder directly below the slab, with no sand layer above it. The reason: bleed water rising from the concrete is preferable to bleed water trapped in a sand layer that re-introduces moisture into the slab over time.

Compaction equipment selection:

| Soil type | Recommended equipment |
|---|---|
| Granular fill (sand, gravel, crushed stone) | Vibrating plate compactor; reversible plate compactor; ride-on roller for larger areas |
| Cohesive fill (silty soil, clayey soil) | Jumping jack (rammer); sheepsfoot roller |
| Mixed | Combination of plate and rammer in lifts |

Compaction lift thickness is matched to equipment capacity. A small vibrating plate compactor handles 4–6 inches loose lift; a jumping jack handles 6–8 inches loose lift in cohesive material. Going thicker than the equipment can densify produces a lift that looks compacted on top but is loose below. Where a site transitions between granular and cohesive zones, compact each zone with its matching equipment and overlap the passes across the transition.

**Code**

```
Slab thickness has fixed minimums.
  3-1/2 inches non-habitable, 4 inches habitable, thicker for higher loads; vegetation, organics, and debris removed before placement.
  Source: ORSC R506.1; R506.2.1.

Habitable slabs sit on an aggregate base with a vapor retarder.
  4 inches minimum of clean crushed stone or gravel, then a 10-mil ASTM E1745 Class A vapor retarder, joints lapped 6 inches and sealed at penetrations. Exceptions: garages, utility buildings, unheated accessory structures, unheated storage under 70 square feet, carports, and flatwork, where the building official approves.
  Source: ORSC R506.2.2; R506.2.3.

Class A is the durable vapor-retarder class.
  Maximum permeance 0.1 perms after conditioning, tensile strength 45 lb/in minimum, puncture resistance 2,200 g minimum.
  Source: ASTM E1745, Class A.

Fill compaction and density verification follow the soil standards.
  Proctor maximum density and optimum moisture in the lab; nuclear gauge or sand cone in the field.
  Source: ASTM D698; D1557; D6938; D1556.

The slab construction guides set the retarder position.
  Vapor retarder directly below the slab; no sand between retarder and concrete.
  Source: ACI 302.1R-15; ACI 302.2R-06.
```

## 7.7 Slab Stack Order

The slab stack is the layered assembly under the concrete floor. Order matters; the wrong sequence produces moisture problems that show up months or years after occupancy.

**Professional: recommended slab stack for Oregon residential**

The standard slab-on-grade stack, top to bottom, accounting for ORSC R506 vapor retarder requirements, ORSC Appendix AF radon requirements where applicable, and ACI 302.2R guidance on vapor retarder placement:

```
[interior]
4–6 inch concrete slab (4 in. minimum in habitable conditioned space; 6 in. for garages or heavy load)
─────────────────────────────────────
10-mil ASTM E1745 Class A vapor retarder, lapped 6 in., sealed at penetrations
(15-mil recommended for long-life durability; same standard, more puncture resistance)
─────────────────────────────────────
Rigid insulation (XPS Type IV minimum at 25 psi for habitable; Type VI at 40 psi for garages)
R-10 minimum standard slab; R-15 minimum for hydronic radiant slab
─────────────────────────────────────
Soil-gas retarder (where ORSC Appendix AF applies; Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, Yamhill counties)

For a hydronic radiant floor, the same order applies, with these additions:

- insulation R-value increased to R-15 minimum (R-20 for aggressive long-life energy targets)
- PEX tubing tied to the top of welded wire reinforcement or a spaced bar mat in the slab itself
- vapor retarder must be intact below the tubing (radiant heat drives moisture upward; a compromised vapor retarder is a long-term problem)
- no insulation between the tubing and the slab top (heat goes up, not down)
- vertical edge insulation at the perimeter, R-10 minimum, full slab thickness
- mix design optimized for thermal lag and crack control: 5-sack minimum, 0.50 max water-cement ratio, 4,000 psi at 28 days

## 7.8 Vapor Retarder And Soil-Gas Control

The vapor retarder protects the conditioned space from moisture migration through the slab. The soil-gas retarder, where required by ORSC Appendix AF, prevents radon and other soil gases from entering the conditioned space. In radon counties, both functions are required; in practice, a single 15-mil ASTM E1745 Class A vapor retarder serves both code requirements.

**Applied: vapor retarder installation**

Specification language for the vapor retarder layer:

- 15-mil ASTM E1745 Class A retarder (10-mil is the code minimum; 15-mil is the long-life upgrade)
- joints lapped 6 inches minimum at all seams
- joints sealed with manufacturer's tape and mastic compatible with the membrane
- turned up 6 inches minimum at all foundation walls and footings
- penetrations sealed with manufacturer's pipe boots or hand-formed caulked seal
- permeance ≤ 0.03 perms after conditioning per ASTM E1745 §7.1

Compliant products by name (current 2026 market): Stego Wrap 15, W.R. Meadows Perminator 15, Insulation Solutions Viper VaporCheck II 15, Raven VaporBlock 15. Other products may comply; confirm the ASTM E1745 Class A designation on the product data sheet.

Installation discipline:

1. Install the retarder after the aggregate base is placed, leveled, and compacted, and after under-slab plumbing/electrical/radon piping is installed and inspected.
2. Roll the retarder out across the slab footprint, with seams parallel to the long axis of the building.
3. Lap each seam 6 inches over the previous course.
4. Tape each seam with the manufacturer's tape; press the tape with a hand roller for full adhesion.
5. Cut around penetrations carefully; install pre-formed pipe boots where available; hand-form a caulked seal for non-standard penetrations.
6. Turn the retarder up at footings and walls; seal the top edge.
7. Inspect the entire surface before the slab is placed; repair any tear, hole, or unsealed penetration.

A common owner-builder error is installing the vapor retarder before sub-slab plumbing rough-in is complete. Sequence: aggregate → plumbing rough-in → inspection → vapor retarder. Repairing a vapor retarder after a plumbing change is harder than waiting for the rough-in to finish.

**Professional: retarder failure modes with a long fuse**

Four installation defects produce moisture damage that appears months or years after occupancy:

- a punctured or torn retarder (foot traffic, rebar chairs, wheelbarrow loads during placement) left unrepaired at the pre-pour walkthrough
- seams left unlapped, or taped without roller pressure, leaving a vapor path through otherwise intact sheet material
- penetrations sealed with generic caulk instead of the manufacturer's boot or compatible mastic; generic caulk loses adhesion to polyolefin sheet over time even where the seal passes visual inspection on pour day
- moisture-sensitive flooring (wood, resilient flooring, some coatings) installed before the slab has dried to the flooring manufacturer's threshold

The fourth defect is independent of the retarder installation. A correctly installed retarder does not make a freshly placed slab ready for a moisture-sensitive finish; slab dryness is a separate measurement, taken with in-place relative humidity probes (ASTM F2170) or the calcium chloride test (ASTM F1869). The flooring-readiness procedure is in the Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, And Livability chapter.

**Professional: radon control under Appendix AF**

ORSC Appendix AF mandates passive subslab depressurization in seven Oregon counties: Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, Yamhill. The required system:

- 4-inch minimum aggregate base (gas-permeable layer for radon collection)
- 6-mil polyethylene soil-gas retarder above aggregate (separate function from the 10-mil vapor retarder, but a single 15-mil ASTM E1745 Class A retarder satisfies both code requirements simultaneously)
- 3-inch diameter ABS, PVC, or equivalent gas-tight vent pipe embedded vertically in subslab aggregate before slab is cast (AF103.6.1), with a T-fitting to ensure the pipe opening remains in the permeable layer
- all slab joints, control joints, and isolation joints sealed with polyurethane caulk or elastomeric sealant (AF103.4.2)
- sumps covered with gasketed lids (AF103.4.4)
- hollow block masonry foundation walls with continuous solid course or grouted course at or above grade (AF103.4.5)
- vent pipe accessible for future fan installation through attic (AF103.8)
- electrical circuit terminated in attic for future fan power (AF103.12)

The system is passive at construction (no fan installed). If post-occupancy radon testing shows elevated levels, a fan is added in the attic to convert passive depressurization to active. The system is designed for that retrofit.

ORSC Appendix AF (codified) is the binding requirement. ANSI/AARST CCAH (Reducing Radon in New Construction of One- and Two-Family Dwellings and Townhouses) is the supplemental best-practice standard. EPA refers to RRNC for new-construction rough-in and CCAH for activation and testing protocols. For the Oregon mandated counties, Appendix AF controls; CCAH adds testing and verification practices.

This chapter's radon scope is the under-slab layers and the slab penetration. The vent routing above the slab, the attic termination, the future-fan circuit detail, and post-occupancy radon testing are in the Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, And Radon chapter.

**Code**

```
Seven counties require passive radon control, element by element.
  4-inch permeable aggregate; soil-gas retarder above it; 3-inch passive vent pipe; sealed slab joints; gasketed sump covers; a solid or grouted course at or above grade in hollow-block foundation walls; vent accessible for a future fan; attic circuit for the fan's power.
  Source: ORSC Appendix AF (Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, Yamhill counties).

The vapor retarder minimum is 10-mil Class A.
  15-mil is the long-life upgrade at the same standard; Class A means maximum permeance 0.1 perms after conditioning, 45 lb/in tensile, 2,200 g puncture.

## 7.9 Under-Slab Insulation And Frost Protection

Under-slab insulation reduces heat loss from a heated slab and is required by ORSC Chapter 11 (residential energy provisions) for heated slabs. Frost-protected shallow foundations (FPSF) per ORSC R403.3 use perimeter and wing insulation to keep frost from reaching footings without going below the conventional frost depth.

### 7.9.1 Under-Slab Insulation

**Professional: material options under ASTM C578**

| Material | ASTM C578 type | Compressive resistance | Suitable for |
|---|---|---|---|
| EPS Type II | II | 15 psi | Light residential, no point loads |
| EPS Type IX | IX | 25 psi | Standard residential floor |
| XPS Type IV | IV | 25 psi | Standard residential floor (preferred for ground contact) |
| XPS Type VI | VI | 40 psi | Heavy residential, garage |
| XPS Type VII | VII | 60 psi | Industrial, high point load |
| EPS Type X | X | 100 psi | Heavy industrial |

For most Oregon residential applications: XPS Type IV (25 psi) is the prescriptive minimum for habitable space; XPS Type VI (40 psi) for garage slabs and any area expecting point loads; XPS Type VII for any commercial-grade variant.

Long-term creep is not a residential floor concern. Residential floor live load (40 psf) plus dead load (10 psf) totals 50 psf, or 0.35 psi. Even Type II EPS at 15 psi has a 40× safety margin against immediate compressive failure. Creep becomes a concern only above ~30% of rated compressive strength, which is the territory of structural slab edges, isolated footings on insulation, and heavy industrial floors.

Moisture exposure: XPS is closed-cell (~0.1% water absorption by volume in long-term tests), preferred for direct ground contact. EPS is open-cell to semi-closed (0.5–3% water absorption depending on type), acceptable for ground contact but more conservative when wet exposure is sustained. Both lose R-value when wet; protect with the vapor retarder above and the gravel layer below.

Edge loading: wall plates bearing on insulation are typically not allowed in prescriptive design. Extend the footing to bear on the subgrade, with insulation only under the non-bearing slab area. Garage door frames, jambs, and adjacent slabs use a thicker slab edge (6 in. or 8 in.) and Type VII insulation if any insulation extends under the door track.

**Specification language**

"Under-slab insulation: XPS rigid foam complying with ASTM C578 Type IV (25 psi minimum compressive resistance) for habitable space; ASTM C578 Type VI (40 psi minimum) for garages and equipment pads. Continuous installation with minimum 1-inch overlap or shiplapped edges, no gaps. Total R-value: R-10 minimum for residential floor; R-15 minimum for radiant slab. ICC ESR-required documentation submitted with permit. Examples: Owens Corning Foamular 250 (Type IV, R-10 at 2 in.), DOW Styrofoam HighLoad 40 (Type VI), Atlas Foamular ThermoPlus."

### 7.9.2 Frost-Protected Shallow Foundation

ORSC R403.3 governs FPSF for heated buildings. ASCE 32-01 governs FPSF for unheated buildings or designs outside R403.3 prescriptive limits.

**ORSC R403.3 prescriptive applicability:**

- heated building maintained at 64 °F (18 °C) minimum monthly mean during winter
- not for unheated spaces (porches, garages, utility rooms, carports); these need engineering or full frost-depth footings
- air freezing index from IRC Table R403.3(2); Oregon AFI typically less than 1,500 °F-days in Willamette Valley sites and higher in the Cascades and east of the mountains
- vertical wall insulation along the stem wall (typically 4 ft deep) plus horizontal wing insulation extending outward (varies by AFI: 12–48 inches depending on climate severity, increased at corners)

**Assembly specification:**

- vertical insulation: rigid foam, XPS preferred for ground contact, ASTM C578 Type VI or VII at ≥ 40 psi compressive resistance, extending from the top of the footing to the grade-level horizontal insulation, full perimeter
- horizontal wing insulation: XPS, ASTM C578 Type VII (60 psi) or higher, extending outward Dimension A per Table R403.3(1) for the site-specific AFI
- wing insulation slope: 1/4 in. per ft minimum, sloping away from foundation
- gravel cover: 6–12 inches of gravel or soil over the horizontal insulation to protect from impact and UV
- insulation protected from UV at all exposed locations: parge coat, metal flashing, or pressure-treated wood per R403.3.2
- for unheated attached spaces (garage, porch): treat per R403.3.1.1 with vertical insulation between heated FPSF and unheated slab, OR use full frost-depth footing for the unheated portion

For Willamette Valley AFI of approximately 500 °F-days, Dimension A is roughly 12 inches with R-4.5 horizontal insulation. For the Bend area at AFI of approximately 1,500 °F-days, Dimension A is roughly 24 inches with R-5.6. The exact values come from Table R403.3(1) for the project's air freezing index, and the site-specific AFI comes from ORSC Table R403.3(2) by county and region.

**ASCE 32-01 path:**

The standard is "Design and Construction of Frost-Protected Shallow Foundations" (American Society of Civil Engineers, 2001). Referenced by IRC R403.1.4.1 as one of the four frost-protection options: deeper than frost line, R403.3 prescriptive, ASCE 32-01, or solid rock. ASCE 32 covers unheated buildings, semi-heated, and provides more rigorous design methods than R403.3 prescriptive. Soil must be Non-Frost Susceptible (NFS), defined as granular with less than 6% by weight passing the #200 sieve, for the prescriptive simplifications. ASCE 32-01 remains the current edition.

## 7.10 Reinforcement, Sleeves, Grounding, And Embeds

Everything that is going to be inside the foundation has to be in place before the pour. Reinforcement, anchor bolts, sleeves for utility runs, the UFER ground, the radon vent pipe, hold-downs for shear-resisting walls, and any embedment for posts, columns, or special hardware. Once concrete is placed, adding any of these requires drilling, coring, or breaking, all of which compromise reinforcement and durability.

**Applied: pre-pour walkthrough**

The day before the pour, walk the formwork with a printed copy of the structural drawings, the architectural drawings, and the MEP rough-in drawings. For each item:

- reinforcement: size, grade, spacing, splices (lap length per the design), cover (chairs and spacers in place), continuity at corners and at the wall-footing interface
- anchor bolts: location relative to plate ends and corners, embedment depth, plate-washer dimensions, hold-down locations and rebar where required by braced wall panels or portal frames (ABW)
- UFER ground: uncoated #4 bar, 20 ft minimum length, 3 inches from the bottom of the footing, bonded to the vertical reinforcement, with a stub left exposed where the grounding electrode conductor will connect to the electrical service
- sleeves and blockouts: plumbing penetrations through stem walls, electrical conduit penetrations, sleeves for water service, sewer line, gas line if applicable; sized 1 in. larger than the pipe to allow slip
- radon vent pipe (in radon counties): 3-inch diameter, gas-tight, vertical, in the aggregate base, with the T-fitting positioned in the permeable layer and the vertical run extending up through the slab to the attic
- hardware: hold-downs, post bases, beam pockets, column embeds, special anchorage per the structural drawings; tied in place, not wet-set
- shear keys (where shown on drawings): formed in the top of the footing or stem wall to resist horizontal sliding of the wall above

Photograph everything. Date-stamp the photos. The pre-pour photos are part of the project record and protect against later disputes about what was installed.

**Professional**

Reinforcement detailing controls how the design strength develops. Common errors that appear as defects later:

| Error | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Lap length too short | Reinforcement does not develop full strength at the splice |
| Cover too small or too large | Corrosion exposure or reduced effective depth |
| Bars displaced during pour | Reinforcement in the wrong location relative to the design |
| Hooks not extended into stem wall | Footing-to-wall connection does not transfer the design force |
| Anchor bolts misplaced | Sill plate has to be drilled and shimmed; sometimes not legally correctable |
| UFER bond missing | Electrical inspector rejects the foundation; rework required |

The fix for all of these is the pre-pour walkthrough plus the inspector's footing inspection, both completed before the truck arrives.

**Professional: coated reinforcement and owner handling**

Where the design specifies coated reinforcement, the coating type sets the handling rules for self-placement. Hot-dip galvanized bar carries a zinc-alloy coating harder than the underlying steel; it tolerates bundling, dragging, field bending, and foot traffic on placed mats the same way uncoated bar does. Epoxy-coated bar carries a soft organic film that scuffs, chips, and cracks under that same handling, and cracks at bend radii during field bending. Every coating break on epoxy bar is a corrosion entry point at the location the coating was specified to protect. Inspectors commonly require field touch-up of damaged epoxy before approving a pour, an identify-and-patch step that adds time and depends on the crew finding every defect. For owner-builder self-placement (site bending, mat tying, walking placed reinforcement during later work), hot-dip galvanized bar is the practical choice on handling grounds. Minor zinc flaking at a bend is not cause for rejection; the galvanic protection mechanism continues to function at the bend. The corrosion-performance comparison between the coating types is in the Designing For Centuries chapter.

## 7.11 Basement Walls, Dampproofing, And Waterproofing

Below-grade walls are the single most demanding moisture-control element in the foundation. Code distinguishes between dampproofing (the default) and waterproofing (required where conditions warrant). For a long-life concrete house, waterproofing is the higher-margin choice in any conditions that approach the trigger.

### 7.11.1 Dampproofing Versus Waterproofing

**Dampproofing (ORSC R406.1): the default**

Required for foundation walls retaining earth and enclosing interior spaces and floors below grade.

Acceptable materials:

- bituminous coating (asphalt-based)
- 3 lb/yd² acrylic-modified cement
- 1/8-inch surface-bonding cement complying with ASTM C887
- any material permitted for waterproofing in R406.2

Masonry foundation walls require an additional 3/8-inch Portland cement parging applied to the exterior before dampproofing material.

Coverage area: from finished grade down to the higher of (top of footing) or (6 inches below top of basement floor).

**Waterproofing (ORSC R406.2): required where conditions warrant**

Triggered by "areas where a high water table or other severe soil-water conditions are known to exist." This is a building-official judgment call based on local site conditions.

Treat any of the following as triggers for waterproofing:

- bottom of footing within 12 inches of seasonal high groundwater
- site mapped as wet or hydric soil per NRCS Web Soil Survey
- basement floor below seasonal high groundwater
- documented neighbor groundwater issues
- clay soil with poor drainage and basement floor below grade
- any site where standing water has been observed in excavations
- most coastal Oregon sites with full basements
- most lower-elevation Willamette Valley sites with full basements

Acceptable materials per R406.2:

- two-ply hot-mopped felts
- 55-lb roll roofing
- 6-mil polyvinyl chloride
- 6-mil polyethylene
- 40-mil polymer-modified asphalt
- 60-mil flexible polymer cement
- 1/8-inch cement-based, fiber-reinforced waterproof coating
- other approved methods

### 7.11.2 Recommended Long-Life Waterproofing Assembly

For a long-life concrete house with any below-grade habitable space, the recommended waterproofing system combines multiple components:

| Layer | Specification |
|---|---|
| Substrate preparation | Foundation wall surface clean, dry, smooth; voids and tie holes patched with hydraulic cement; corners chamfered or formed with cant strips per manufacturer instructions |
| Primary membrane | 60-mil peel-and-stick polymer-modified asphalt (Henry Blueskin VP100 or WP200, GCP/Grace Bituthene 4000, Tremco TUFF-N-DRI, Polyguard 650; or equivalent listed in R406.2) |
| Penetration sealing | All penetrations, terminations, and corners detailed per the manufacturer's instructions, with primer, mastic, and reinforcing strips |
| Drainage board | Dimple drainage board (Cosella-Dörken Delta-MS, GCP Hydroduct 220, MiraDrain) over the membrane to channel water down to the perimeter drain |
| Protection | Where backfill is rocky or angular, an additional protection board (typically extruded polystyrene 1/2 in. or 1 in. thick, or fiber drain board) |
| Insulation (where used) | Continuous below-grade rigid insulation (XPS Type IV minimum) over the drainage board, where the energy-code path requires below-grade wall insulation |

This assembly is more involved than the code minimum. The cost difference between R406.1 dampproofing and a full long-life waterproofing system is roughly $5,000–$15,000 on a typical residential project. The long-term cost of a wet basement (recurring repair, finish replacement, mold remediation, reduced resale value) is substantially higher.

**Where below-grade waterproofing fails in the field**

Three failure patterns account for most below-grade leaks:

1. Dampproofing installed where waterproofing was required. A bituminous or cementitious coating resists soil moisture and vapor; it has no capacity against hydrostatic pressure. Where the water table stands at or above the basement floor level even briefly each season, a dampproofed wall leaks. The trigger list in §7.11.1 exists because the R406.2 trigger language is a building-official judgment call, and the recurring field error is under-calling that judgment on a borderline site.
2. Cold joints. The membrane covers the exterior wall face; it does not stop water tracking along the internal footing-to-wall joint behind the membrane. The joint takes its own detail (§7.11.5).
3. Penetrations, terminations, and corners. A membrane installs reliably over a flat wall face and poorly around an irregular penetration, and a rushed crew shortcuts the detail-heavy transitions, not the flat runs.

The countermeasures beyond the flat-wall assembly above:

- manufacturer transition strips, corner boots, and reinforcing fabric at every inside and outside corner and every penetration
- a waterstop at the footing-to-wall joint, independent of the wall membrane
- the membrane and drainage board carried continuously across the footing-to-wall transition rather than terminated at the top of the footing
- the dimple drainage board serving its second function, protecting the membrane from puncture during backfill

### 7.11.3 Foundation Drains And Sump

Foundation drains are required around concrete or masonry foundations enclosing habitable or usable spaces below grade (R405.1).

Standard specification:

- drain pipe: 4-inch perforated PVC or equivalent, holes facing down
- granular aggregate: 6-inch minimum depth around the pipe, 3/4-inch to 1-1/2-inch clean stone
- filter fabric: non-woven geotextile wrapping the aggregate to prevent fines migration
- discharge: gravity to daylight where the site permits, otherwise to a sump pump with above-grade discharge at least 5 ft from the foundation
- dimple drainage board behind waterproofing membrane to channel water down to the perimeter drain

Sump pump requirement and specification:

- sump pit per R405.2: 24 inches minimum depth, tight-fitting cover (gasketed cover required where ORSC Appendix AF applies in radon counties)
- pump sized for the expected groundwater inflow plus margin; typical residential pumps are 1/3 to 1/2 HP
- discharge above grade, minimum 5 ft from the foundation, oriented so the discharge does not return to the foundation
- backup power for long-life design: battery-backup pump as a secondary unit, or generator with automatic transfer for the primary pump
- check valve in the discharge line to prevent backflow

For a long-life house in a high-water-table site, the sump-pump system supplements the waterproofing; the waterproofing remains the primary barrier. Power outages in winter storms are the highest-risk failure mode, so backup power is required. The two configurations: a battery-backup secondary pump protects through short outages with no fuel and no startup step, at low cost; a generator with automatic transfer for the primary pump protects through multi-day outages and also powers the rest of the house, at several times the cost. Match the choice to the site's outage history.

### 7.11.4 Habitable Basement Considerations

A habitable basement triggers full habitable-space requirements under ORSC R305 (ceiling height ≥ 7 ft), R303 (light and ventilation), R310 (emergency escape with 5.7 sq ft clear opening minimum), and R303.10 (heating). Combined with the drainage and waterproofing requirements, a habitable basement multiplies the inspection burden and tightens the structural envelope.

A daylight basement (with at least one wall above grade) is dramatically easier to permit than a fully buried basement with habitable space. The above-grade wall provides natural light, easier emergency escape, and reduced hydrostatic exposure. For most Oregon sites with sufficient slope, a daylight basement is the practical path.

### 7.11.5 Construction Joints, Keyways, And Waterstops

A footing-to-stemwall or stemwall-to-slab joint is a cold joint: two placements meeting at a plane with no monolithic bond across it. Water tracks along that plane under capillary action or hydrostatic head unless the joint is detailed to stop it. The wall membrane treats the exterior face; the keyway and waterstop treat the joint itself, inside the concrete. The cold-joint engineering rules (permitted joint locations, spacing, and first-pour surface preparation) are in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter; this section is the water-sealing detail.

**Simple**

A foundation is poured in stages: footing first, walls next, slab last. Each line where two pours meet is a seam through the concrete. Water can move along a seam even when both pours are solid. A waterstop is a strip cast half into the first pour and half into the second. It blocks the water path inside the joint, where the outside membrane cannot reach.

**Applied: the joint detail**

For each below-grade construction joint (footing to stem wall, wall lift to wall lift, wall to slab in a water-holding room):

1. Form a continuous keyway in the top of the first pour. Use a beveled wood strip or a manufactured plastic keyway form, stripped after set. The keyway locks the second pour into the first for shear transfer. It also lengthens the water path across the joint.
2. Place a waterstop centered over the joint line before the second pour. Two families serve non-moving construction joints. Hydrophilic strips (bentonite or hydrophilic polymer) swell on water contact to seal the joint. They fasten with adhesive or nails and need no field welding. PVC dumbbell or ribbed profiles block the water path passively. PVC splices must be heat-welded.
3. Choose a delayed-swell hydrophilic product (a hydration-resistant outer layer) so rain or pour water does not swell the strip before the second placement.
4. Pack a crystalline slurry or grout into the keyway where the design adds the crystalline layer (§7.11.6).
5. Carry the exterior membrane and drainage board continuously across the joint on the soil side.

A keyway alone lengthens the water path; it does not seal it. Every joint expected to see hydrostatic pressure gets both the keyway and the waterstop. For a crew without waterstop-welding experience, the hydrophilic strip is the practical choice.

### 7.11.6 Integral Crystalline Waterproofing

**Simple**

Some waterproofing is mixed into the concrete instead of applied to its surface. A crystalline admixture reacts with water inside the concrete. It grows needle-shaped crystals that plug the pore channels water would pass through. If a hairline crack forms years later, new water contact restarts the reaction. The new crystal growth seals the crack. The admixture is a second layer behind the outside membrane. The membrane remains the primary barrier.

**Professional**

Crystalline waterproofing admixtures (Xypex, Kryton KIM, and equivalent products) are dosed at batching or applied as a surface slurry or dry shake. The chemistry reacts with water and unhydrated cement to form insoluble crystals in the capillary pore structure. Documented below-grade service includes foundation walls, basement slabs, elevator pits, tunnels, and water-retaining structures. Manufacturer and trade documentation reports self-sealing of hairline cracks up to roughly 0.5 mm (0.02 inch), including cracks that form years after placement, because unreacted crystalline material in the paste re-activates on new water contact.

The limit is movement. Crystal growth seals a static pore or fine crack and breaks at any joint or crack that continues to move under settlement, thermal cycling, or seismic load. Crystalline treatment supplements the membrane assembly (§7.11.2) and the waterstop detail (§7.11.5); it replaces neither, and it does not substitute for a movement joint where movement is expected. For this project, the admixture is a low-cost addition to below-grade wall mixes and to any water-retaining element (§7.13). Non-crystalline hydrophobic pore-lining admixtures and surface repellents are marketed mainly for above-grade absorption and efflorescence control; a below-grade hydrostatic claim for a specific product is verified against that product's own technical data and evaluation report before it is specified.

**Code**

```

  For walls retaining earth and enclosing interior space: bituminous coating, 3 lb/yd2 acrylic-modified cement, 1/8-inch surface-bonding cement (ASTM C887), or any R406.2 waterproofing material; masonry walls get 3/8-inch parging first.
  Source: ORSC R406.1; ASTM C887.

  Triggered where a high water table or severe soil-water conditions are known to exist; the determination is the building official's, from local conditions; six approved system families are listed.

## 7.12 Foundation Drainage And Water Outlet

The drainage system is layered: site grading sheds surface water; roof drainage routes roof water away; the perimeter footing drain collects subsurface water at the footing; foundation waterproofing or dampproofing resists water passage through the wall; the capillary break and vapor retarder block moisture at the slab-soil interface. Each layer removes water before it reaches the next.

This section contains the perimeter and outlet detail. The slab-side detail is in §7.6-7.8. The site-grading detail is in the Land, Site, And Feasibility chapter.

**Applied: perimeter drain detail**

| Element | Specification |
|---|---|
| Drain pipe | 4-inch diameter perforated PVC, holes oriented down, slope 1/8 in. per ft minimum toward outlet |
| Aggregate | 3/4-inch to 1-1/2-inch clean stone (drain rock), no fines |
| Aggregate depth | Minimum 6 inches below pipe; 12 inches above and around pipe |
| Filter fabric | Non-woven geotextile (4 oz minimum), wrapping the aggregate envelope, lapped at seams |
| Pipe location | At or below the area to be protected; typically at the bottom of the footing for full-perimeter drainage |
| Cleanouts | Riser to grade at every change in direction and at maximum 100 ft intervals on long runs |
| Outlet | Gravity discharge to daylight where site permits, or to sump pump with above-grade discharge |

**Drainage to daylight versus sump:**

| Outlet type | Best fit | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Gravity to daylight | Sloped sites where the drainage exit is below the perimeter drain elevation | Site geometry must support; the daylight exit must be protected from blockage by debris, animals, or freezing |
| Sump with pump and above-grade discharge | Flat sites where gravity outlet is not feasible | Requires reliable power; backup power strongly recommended for long-life design |
| Combined gravity-to-sump-overflow | Some sites use a hybrid: most flow to daylight, sump only when daylight outlet is overwhelmed | More complexity; useful in flood-prone or high-water-table sites |

**Roof drainage at the perimeter:**

Roof water concentrated at downspouts is the highest single load on perimeter drainage. Discharge downspouts at least 5 ft from the foundation. Use splash blocks, piped extensions, or rainwater collection. Connecting downspouts directly to the perimeter footing drain is permitted only with the building official's approval. In many jurisdictions, downspouts and footing drains are separated to avoid surcharging the perimeter system during storms.

## 7.13 Below-Grade Special Spaces: Root Cellar And Cistern

Two optional below-grade spaces interact with the foundation work in this chapter: a root cellar (a passively cooled food-storage room) and a cistern (a built-in water-storage tank). Both are decided before the foundation is formed, because both change the excavation, the waterproofing, and the slab moisture strategy in ways concrete does not permit retrofitting.

### 7.13.1 Root Cellar Within The Foundation Footprint

**Simple**

A root cellar keeps vegetables cold and damp without electricity. Below frost depth, the earth stays cool all year. A below-grade room reaches that temperature through its walls and floor. The storage target is 32-40 °F at 85-95 percent relative humidity. The best location is the coldest corner, against an exterior foundation wall.

**Applied: the passive design**

- Locate the cellar at the coldest below-grade corner, against an exterior foundation wall. Keep it away from the water heater, the mechanical room, and any heated slab zone.
- Ventilate with two ducts working by stack effect. A low intake duct delivers cool outside air near the floor. A high exhaust duct releases warm moist air near the ceiling. The residential convention is a 4-inch insulated duct for each, sized around a typical 50-150 square foot cellar.
- Fit each duct with a damper or adjustable register. Tune the airflow with a thermometer and hygrometer after construction. No published sizing table exists for larger cellars; the adjustable vent substitutes for the missing calculation.
- Leave the cellar floor as an unsealed earth or gravel zone. Ground moisture then maintains the humidity target passively. A sealed concrete floor would need active humidification to hold 85-95 percent relative humidity.

**Professional: the moisture-strategy exception**

The unsealed cellar floor contradicts the vapor-retarder and capillary-break specification in §7.6-7.8, which exists to keep ground moisture out of the conditioned slab. The resolution is physical separation: the cellar zone is isolated from the vapor-retarder-protected slab area by a stem wall, a sealed sill plate, or an equivalent break, so the two moisture strategies meet at a detailed boundary instead of overlapping. The boundary is drawn and resolved on the foundation plan before the under-slab inspection.

**The three locations (owner decision)**

| Option | Cost | Capacity and control | Access |
|---|---|---|---|
| Room inside the house foundation footprint | Lowest marginal cost; shares excavation, walls, and waterproofing already being built | Capacity limited to the floor-plan area spared; most stable temperature, buffered by the surrounding foundation | Interior door; no outdoor trip in winter |
| Standalone earth-coupled structure | Full separate excavation, structure, and waterproofing budget | Largest capacity; full design freedom on burial depth and vent geometry | Outdoor walk in all weather |
| Corner of a below-grade shop space | Shared cost only where the shop design already includes a below-grade level | Temperature stability depends on the shop's burial depth and mass | Outdoor walk to the shop |

The location decision is the owner's. The in-house room has the lowest cost and the best access where the floor plan can spare the area and the boundary detail above is acceptable. The standalone structure fits capacity needs the footprint cannot hold. The shop-corner option applies only where the shop plan in the Developing The Site: Shop, Block-Yard, And Outbuildings chapter already includes a below-grade component.

### 7.13.2 Cistern Coordination

Whether a cistern belongs inside the foundation footprint is decided in the Soil, Ground Prep, Drainage, And Foundation Risk chapter. The water-retaining structural design (wall thickness, reinforcement, crack-width limits) and the formwork design are in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter. The pour-sequencing implications are a stated dependency on the Build Sequence And Site Coordination chapter. This section coordinates the cistern with the foundation work this chapter specifies.

**Simple**

A cistern is a concrete tank for stored water, built into the foundation. Water weighs 8.3 pounds per gallon, so a full tank adds many tons of load. The tank must hold its water in and keep groundwater out at the same time. Each direction needs its own protective layer.

**Professional: what the cistern changes in this chapter's work**

- Structural load. A filled cistern imposes internal hydrostatic pressure on its own walls and floor, plus the stored water's dead load, in addition to the soil-side pressures the foundation already resists. The structural engineer of record sizes the tank as a water-retaining structure; an ordinary basement slab is not designed for that load.
- Waterproofing in two directions. The interior takes an NSF/ANSI 61-listed coating, the health-effects certification for potable water contact; high-solids epoxies formulated for concrete tank interiors carry the listing and a typical 15-20 year service life. The listing certifies potability; it does not address groundwater exclusion. The exterior takes the full §7.11.2 membrane and drainage-board assembly, applied as its own complete system and tied continuously into the adjacent foundation waterproofing at the shared boundary. Allowable extractable-solvent limits under NSF/ANSI 61 tightened in January 2023; the specific product's listing is confirmed current at purchase rather than read from an older data sheet. Crystalline admixture in the tank mix (§7.11.6) is the third layer.
- Joints. Every floor-to-wall and lift-to-lift joint takes the full §7.11.5 keyway-and-waterstop detail. A hydrophilic waterstop matches the cistern condition: sustained water contact is what activates and maintains its swelling seal. Fewer lifts mean fewer joints. A single full-height wall pour eliminates horizontal joints at the cost of formwork designed for full liquid-head pressure. One documented owner-built cistern used 1/2-inch plywood forms that bulged under placement pressure and consumed roughly 50 percent more concrete than estimated. The builder's recorded correction was 3/4-inch sheathing on studs at 16 inches on center. The actual formwork design for this project's tank height and pour rate comes from the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter's formwork rules.
- Drainage. The cistern footprint sits inside the perimeter footing drain coverage, so a leak in either direction drains away instead of ponding against the foundation. Cistern overflow and drain-down take a dedicated discharge path; the perimeter footing drain is not the overflow route.

**Code**

```
Materials in contact with stored drinking water must meet the drinking-water components standard.
  Health-effects certification for coatings and liners in potable service; extractable-solvent limits tightened January 2023.
  Source: NSF/ANSI 61.
```

## 7.14 Make-Versus-Buy: Ready-Mix Or Owner-Mixed Concrete

Concrete arrives at the project either as ready-mix delivered by truck from a commercial plant or as owner-mixed concrete batched on site from bulk cement, aggregate, water, and admixtures. Both paths are code-compliant. The decision is economic, logistical, and project-specific.

**Simple**

You can buy concrete by the truckload or make it on site from cement, sand, gravel, and water. Delivered ready-mix runs $180-$280 per cubic yard (2026). Truck delivery wins for big pours: fast, consistent, documented. Site mixing wins for small and repeated batches. Trucks charge minimum-load fees, and a mixer already running for grout or block-making changes the cost comparison. The law allows both, as long as the mix is right and the records prove it.

This section assumes the engineered design path or the prescriptive path with sufficient owner involvement to manage on-site mixing. Owner-mixed concrete is well-suited to the long-life, owner-builder posture of this project; ready-mix is the conventional path with its own advantages.

### 7.14.1 The Two Paths

**Ready-mix concrete** is delivered from a commercial plant in a rotating-drum truck. The plant batches the mix to a specified design (f'c, slump, air content, admixtures), prints a batch ticket with each load, and the truck typically discharges within 90 minutes of batching. Delivery is fast; mix consistency is high; documentation is supplier-provided. Minimum loads are typically 4–7 cubic yards with short-load fees of $100–$300 for partial loads. Cost runs $180–$280 per cubic yard delivered in 2026 Oregon dollars.

**Owner-mixed concrete** is batched on site from bulk cement, aggregate, water, and admixtures using a drum mixer, paddle mixer, pan mixer, or volumetric mixer. The owner controls the mix design directly, can produce small batches on demand, and avoids ready-mix minimum loads and short-load fees. Output rate is slower (typically 1.5–2 cubic yards per hour with a single 9 cu ft tow-behind drum mixer). Documentation is owner-prepared and follows ACI 318 §26.5 and ACI 332-20 framework.

### 7.14.2 The Decision Matrix

| Factor | Favors ready-mix | Favors owner-mixed |
|---|---|---|
| Pour size | Large pours (≥ 5 cu yd) where ready-mix minimum load is met | Small pours (< 4 cu yd) where short-load fees apply, and continuous low-rate work |
| Schedule | Single-day pours where speed matters | Multi-day or intermittent work; pour as work allows |
| Mix consistency | Project requires repeatable, verified mix | Owner has control over mix and has a calibrated process |
| Site access | Concrete truck can reach the work; pump-truck setup is feasible | Difficult truck access, remote site, or steep grade |
| Documentation | Supplier batch ticket meets the building official's expectations | Owner has the discipline to maintain batch records and field-test logs |
| Cost (typical) | Lower for pours above the minimum load | Lower for small repeated pours and where site mixing is already needed for grout or block production |
| Skill | Owner has limited concrete experience | Owner has experience or has invested in ACI Grade I Field Testing certification |
| Specialty mixes | Standard mix designs available from any plant | Owner is producing CMU, grout, or specialty mixes that need on-site batching anyway |
| Freeze-thaw exposure | Exterior flatwork exposed to freeze-thaw (driveway, exterior stairs, exposed slabs), where air content must be right on every load | Protected elements (footings, below-grade walls, interior slabs), specified at 1-2% air with no freeze-thaw exposure |

For most owner-builder long-life concrete houses, the practical answer is hybrid: ready-mix for the foundation pour and the slab pour (large volumes where ready-mix wins), owner-mixed for grout, small accessory pours, slab patches, and any CMU manufacturing the project includes.

Freeze-thaw exposure sharpens the split. Air entrainment is the batching variable most sensitive to mixing time, mixer type, and ambient temperature, and a plant's process control catches a dosing error before the load leaves the yard; on an owner-mixed batch, the field air test (§7.17) is the only real-time check. An under-entrained exterior slab scales and spalls under winter freeze-thaw cycling, and the correction after hardening is removal and replacement. The same batching error in a footing or an interior slab has no freeze-thaw consequence, because those elements carry no exposure and are specified at 1-2 percent air. The rule that follows: owner-mixing concentrates on protected elements, and exterior freeze-thaw-exposed flatwork is ordered as ready-mix.

### 7.14.3 Owner-Mixed Concrete: The Workflow

Owner-mixed concrete is permitted under ACI 318 §26.5 (Construction) and ACI 332-20 (Code Requirements for Residential Concrete). Oregon residential code does not require ready-mix delivery; it requires concrete that meets the specified mix design with documentation that proves it.

**Mix design: f'c = 4,000 psi target for typical residential structural pours**

| Component | Quantity per cubic yard | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Portland cement, ASTM C150 Type I or Type II | 470–520 lb (5.0–5.5 sacks) | Primary binder |
| Class F fly ash, ASTM C618 | 100–140 lb (15–25% replacement of cement) | Reduces long-term permeability; improves chemical resistance; the most useful single SCM for long-life concrete |
| Concrete sand, ASTM C33 fine aggregate | 1,200–1,400 lb | Fine aggregate fills void space in the coarse aggregate |
| Coarse aggregate, ASTM C33 (3/4-inch or smaller crushed stone) | 1,700–1,900 lb | Primary load-bearing aggregate |
| Water (potable, ACI 318 §26.4.1) | 250–280 lb (30–34 gal) | Hydration; total water includes aggregate moisture |
| Air-entraining admixture, ASTM C260 | per manufacturer (typically 0.5–2 oz per 100 lb cement) | 5–7% entrained air for freeze-thaw resistance |
| Water-reducing plasticizer, ASTM C494 Type A or F | per manufacturer (typically 4–8 oz per 100 lb cement) | Reduces water demand for workability |
| Optional macro-synthetic fibers, ASTM C1116 | 3–7 lb per cubic yard | Plastic-shrinkage-crack control |

Water-cement ratio target: ≤ 0.45 for ACI exposure classes F1/F2 (most Oregon residential exposure).

For higher-strength mixes (5,000 psi for slabs with point loads, exterior wear surfaces, or aggressive exposure), increase cement to 6.5 sacks per cubic yard, lower water-cement ratio to 0.40, and add silica fume (5–10% replacement) or higher fly ash replacement.

**Corrosion-inhibitor admixture (calcium nitrite)**

Calcium nitrite corrosion-inhibitor admixtures (GCP DCI, Sika CNI, Euclid Eucon CIA) contain roughly 30 percent calcium nitrite in solution and weigh about 10.6-10.7 lb per gallon. ### 7.14.6 Field Testing For Owner-Mixed Concrete

ACI 318 §26.12 governs testing. For an owner-mixed structural pour, the practical protocol:

| Test | Frequency | Standard |
|---|---|---|
| Slump | Each batch or each load | ASTM C143 |
| Air content (where air-entrained) | First batch and any time slump or appearance changes | ASTM C231 (pressure) or ASTM C173 (volumetric) |
| Concrete temperature | Each major pour, especially in hot or cold weather | ASTM C1064 |
| Compression cylinders | One set of 4 cylinders per major pour (footing, walls, slab). Test 1 at 7 days, 2 at 28 days (averaged), 1 hold at 56 days for re-test if 28-day result is suspect | ASTM C31 making, ASTM C39 testing |

The owner-builder can perform field tests after taking the ACI Grade I Field Testing Technician certification. This is a one-day course offered through state ACI chapters; cost is $400–$600 including the exam, valid 5 years. Self-perform slump, air, and temperature; ship cylinders to a CCRL-inspected laboratory for compression testing ($25–$50 per cylinder).

Acceptance criteria from ACI 318-19 §26.12.3.1:

1. Average of any 3 consecutive strength tests ≥ f'c
2. No individual strength test less than (f'c – 500) psi for f'c ≤ 5,000 psi

A failing individual cylinder triggers a low-strength investigation under §26.12.6: core testing, in-place testing, or structural evaluation. Failures are uncommon when mix design and water content are controlled.

### 7.14.7 Documentation For Owner-Mixed Concrete

Per pour, the operational record includes:

- mix design specification (cement, aggregate, water, admixture proportions)
- batch records (quantity of each component used per batch, water added, time of mixing, time of placement)
- delivery/placement timeline (start of placement, end of placement, ambient temperature, concrete temperature)
- field test record (slump, air, temperature, cylinder ID)
- curing log (curing method, duration, temperature, moisture-maintenance method)
- inspector approval (footing, foundation-wall, slab inspections per ORSC R109)

The documentation is no different in substance from a ready-mix batch ticket; the format is owner-prepared rather than supplier-provided. Building officials accept owner-prepared documentation when the documentation is complete and the field test results are within acceptance criteria.

### 7.14.8 Working With The Building Official

Some building officials are unfamiliar with owner-mixed structural concrete and may be cautious. The path to acceptance:

- present the mix design with ACI 318 §26.5 / ACI 332-20 / ASTM C685 references at the pre-pour meeting
- describe the field-testing protocol and the documentation framework
- offer to provide the cylinder test results before backfill or before the next inspection
- show the ACI Grade I Field Testing Technician certificate (or arrange for a third-party testing lab if the certification is not yet obtained)

A pre-pour meeting walking through the protocol resolves most concerns. The cited references are real, accepted, and routinely used in residential construction; the owner-builder is not asking for an alternative method, just for acceptance of a code-compliant production path.

### 7.14.10 Mixer Drum Cleaning On A Septic-And-Well Property

**Simple**

Concrete left in a mixer drum hardens into rock by the next morning. The fix is rinsing the drum right after every batch, while everything is still wet. Acid can dissolve buildup that a rinse missed. The strong acid used on concrete also damages the microbe population a septic system depends on. On a septic-and-well property, the acid choice and the rinse-water destination both matter.

**Applied: the routine**

1. After every batch, while the drum is still wet, rinse thoroughly with a pressure washer or hose. Keep the drum turning for several minutes so the tumbling water flushes residue before it stiffens.
2. Direct all rinse water to the washout containment in §7.14.9. Never discharge it onto open ground, toward the septic drain field, or inside the well's protection radius. Concrete rinse water is highly alkaline from residual cement.
3. For hardened buildup, apply a citric-acid solution: roughly 2 tablespoons per gallon of water. Let it work, then rinse to the same containment. Citric acid is a weak organic acid with low aquatic toxicity and little hazardous vapor. It is over 95 percent biodegradable within 28 days.
4. Reserve muriatic (hydrochloric) acid for severe neglected buildup only. Use full PPE, and send its rinse water to the same containment. Diluted 1:50 it still sits below pH 2.0. It is never poured to a drain, a septic system, or open ground.
5. Keep buildup light by never skipping step 1. Citric acid reacts more slowly than muriatic acid on thick cured deposits. The after-every-batch rinse is what keeps deposits within citric acid's range.

**Code**

```
Site-mixed structural concrete is permitted, with documentation.
  Production, transport, and placement rules for structural concrete; the residential code's testing section references the same acceptance framework.
  Source: ACI 318-19 section 26.5; ACI 318-19 section 26.12; ACI 332-20 section 3.3.

Three production standards distinguish the supply paths.
  Volumetric batching and continuous mixing (the closest standard to a permanent site rig), packaged dry-mix products, and plant-batched ready-mix.
  Source: ASTM C685; ASTM C387; ASTM C94.

The mix ingredients each have a standard.
  Cements, aggregates, fly ash and pozzolans, slag cement, silica fume, admixtures, fibers, and aggregate moisture measurement for batch-water adjustment.

## 7.15 Ordering Concrete And Reading Batch Tickets

For ready-mix orders, the batch ticket is the documentation that proves the concrete delivered matches the mix design specified. For owner-mixed concrete, the equivalent record is owner-prepared and contains the same content.

**Applied: placing a ready-mix order**

Ready-mix orders are placed by phone or through the supplier's online portal. Provide:

- project address
- date and time of pour
- volume in cubic yards (round up by 5–10% for waste)
- mix design (f'c, slump, air content, admixtures)
- aggregate size (typically 3/4-inch for residential)
- delivery interval (single load or staged delivery)
- placement method (chute, pump, conveyor); affects slump
- contact name and phone for site coordination
- driver instructions (gate code, access route, parking)

A typical mix-design call-out for residential structural concrete:

| Element | Specification |
|---|---|
| Footings | 3,000 psi, 4-inch slump, 4–6% air, 3/4-inch aggregate |
| Foundation walls | 3,500 psi, 4–5-inch slump, 4–6% air, 3/4-inch aggregate |
| Slab on grade (interior) | 4,000 psi, 4-inch slump, 1–2% air (low-air for finishability), 3/4-inch aggregate, fiber-reinforced if specified |
| Exposed slab (driveway, patio) | 4,000 psi, 4–5-inch slump, 5–7% air, 3/4-inch aggregate, F1 or F2 exposure |
| Reinforced masonry grout | per ASTM C476: fine grout 8–11-inch slump, 2,000 psi minimum at 28 days, max 3/8-inch aggregate |

Confirm the supplier's standard mix designations match these. Different plants use different mix-ID codes; reference the f'c value rather than the plant's internal code.

The table shows conventional residential values a plant will recognize. This project's long-life standard, set in the structural chapter, is 4,000 psi minimum with a 0.45 maximum water-cement ratio for all structural concrete; order to the project standard and treat the conventional values as the floor a plant may quote, not as the specification.

**Slump is ordered with admixture, not with water**

A stiff 3-4-inch-slump mix places poorly around congested reinforcement, and the field temptation is water added from the truck's tank. Added water raises the water-cement ratio above the design target and permanently reduces strength and durability, whatever it does for workability. The control point is the order. The order names the target slump, and the plant reaches it with a water-reducing admixture batched at the plant (ASTM C494 Type A normal-range, or Type F or G plasticizer). The admixture raises flowability by dispersing cement particles instead of adding water. A load that arrives too stiff goes back for plant correction or is rejected; site tempering stays within the ASTM C94 water-addition tolerance and is recorded on the ticket.

A second ordering error is one air content specified across all elements. Air content follows each element's exposure per the call-out table above. Over-specified air on a hard-troweled interior slab sets up the surface scaling failure described in §7.19; under-specified air on exterior flatwork risks freeze-thaw scaling regardless of strength.

**The batch ticket: what it must show**

Every load of ready-mix concrete arrives with a batch ticket. The ticket is a quality-control document, not just a delivery receipt.

Required content:

- supplier name and plant location
- truck number and driver name
- date and time of batching
- ticket number
- delivery address
- mix design ID and specification (f'c, slump, air, w/c ratio, aggregate size)
- quantity of each component (cement, fly ash if used, aggregate by weight, water at batch, admixtures by ounce)
- total water at batch (manufacturer's tolerance applies)
- water added on site (filled in by driver at delivery)
- discharge time (batch-to-discharge target ≤ 90 minutes per ASTM C94)

Read the ticket before discharge. If the mix design does not match the order, do not begin placement until the discrepancy is resolved with the plant.

**Field testing schedule**

For each pour, the field-testing schedule:

| Sample | Trigger |
|---|---|
| Slump | First load; any subsequent load that looks different in flow or appearance |
| Air | First load if air-entrained; any subsequent load with different appearance |
| Temperature | First load; any load when ambient conditions change (hot or cold weather) |
| Compression cylinders | One set per pour (footing, foundation walls, slab as separate pours); minimum once per 100 cubic yards on larger projects |

For an owner-builder pour with one truck of ready-mix and one set of cylinders, the test discipline is short: slump on arrival, air if applicable, temperature, and four cylinders per ASTM C31. Label each cylinder with date, pour location, and cylinder number. Store the set under wet burlap at 60–80 °F for 24 hours, then transport it to the testing lab.

## 7.16 Pour Day Planning And Placement

Pour day is the highest-coordination day in the foundation phase. Planning the day removes most of the things that go wrong; missing the planning often produces problems that cannot be fixed once concrete is in place.

**Applied: pour-day planning checklist**

Before the truck arrives:

- Forms set, plumb, braced, square, and clean
- Reinforcement placed, tied, with cover maintained by chairs and spacers
- All embeds, sleeves, blockouts, anchor bolts, hold-downs, UFER ground, and radon piping in place
- Vapor retarder (for slabs) installed, lapped, sealed, with penetrations boot-sealed
- Pre-pour inspection passed and signed off
- Crew assembled with assignments: truck spotter, chute manager, vibrator operator, screed operator, finisher
- Equipment ready: vibrators (internal and external as applicable), screeds, floats, edgers, control joint tools, finishing tools
- Pump truck or chute setup planned and rehearsed; alternate plan if equipment fails
- Water on site for cleanup and curing
- Plastic sheeting for cover if rain is forecast
- Curing materials staged: wet burlap or curing compound, plastic sheeting
- Field testing equipment: slump cone, air meter, thermometer, cylinder molds, labels
- Site access cleared for trucks; turnaround space verified
- Communication: phone numbers for the plant, the dispatch, and the inspector
- Inspector contact made if the pour requires inspection during placement (some elements do)

**Placement sequence: typical foundation pour**

1. Truck arrives. Driver presents batch ticket. Read the ticket: confirm mix design, time of batching, and total water.
2. Slump test on first truck. Reject the load if slump is outside specification.
3. Air content test if air-entrained mix specified.
4. Concrete temperature test. Within ACI 305/306 limits for the weather.
5. Cylinder set made from the first truck (or per the testing schedule).
6. Begin placement. Discharge into forms in horizontal layers, not in piles. Maximum free fall typically 3–4 ft to avoid segregation; use chutes, hoppers, or pumps for greater drops.
7. Vibrate each layer with internal vibrator (poker) at intervals matched to vibrator radius of action. Insert vertically, allow consolidation, withdraw at a rate that does not leave a hole.
8. Avoid over-vibration (segregation, slab edges with too much paste).
9. Move methodically along the form; do not skip ahead and return. Each layer should be placed and consolidated before the previous layer's surface starts to set.
10. As placement reaches each level on a wall or column, screed the top to design elevation.
11. For slabs: screed to elevation with a straightedge; bull-float to embed coarse aggregate and remove screed marks; allow bleed water to evaporate before final finishing.

**Professional: placement details that matter**

| Item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Free-fall height | Concrete dropped from height > 3–4 ft segregates: aggregate falls, paste stays. The result is honeycomb in walls and weak top layers in slabs. |
| Layer thickness | Placement layers > 18 in. trap entrapped air; consolidation is incomplete. Keep layers ≤ 12–18 in. and consolidate each. |
| Cold joint | A cold joint forms when fresh concrete is placed against concrete that has begun to set. Avoid by keeping placement continuous and not pausing for more than 30–45 minutes within a single element. |
| Vibrator dwell time | Too short: incomplete consolidation, voids. Too long: segregation, paste at the surface. Typical dwell is 5–15 seconds per insertion at proper spacing. |
| Pump pressure | High pump pressure can affect mix; high-pressure pumping can squeeze water out of the mix. Confirm the pump operator's experience with the specified mix. |
| Weather exposure during placement | Rain can wash cement from a fresh surface; cover with plastic sheeting if rain starts during placement. Hot sun and wind accelerate drying; mist or fog during placement and immediately after. |

**Professional: hot-weather sequencing and staffing**

Hot weather shrinks the set window that makes continuous placement possible, and the 30-45-minute pause limit in the sequence above becomes the binding constraint. The countermeasures are planned before pour day:

- Placement zones are sized to the crew's actual placement-and-consolidation rate, not to the volume ordered. The roster covers a truck spotter, a chute or pump operator, at least two placement-and-consolidation laborers, and a finisher trailing placement.
- Truck arrivals are staged to the placement rate, so one truck discharges as the crew finishes consolidating the previous load, and concrete already in the forms is never left exposed between trucks.
- The finishing crew follows placement closely. The tolerable gap between placement and floating compresses in heat, and a surface that crusts before floating produces plastic-shrinkage cracking.
- Fog-spray equipment runs continuously on exposed unfinished surfaces in hot, windy, or low-humidity conditions per ACI 305R. A hose stream is not a substitute; it adds water to the surface.
- The go/no-go decision comes from the ACI 305R evaporation-rate nomograph (concrete temperature, air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed), applied to the forecast before the trucks are ordered. The responses it triggers: moving the pour to early morning or evening, or adding shade and windbreaks.

Flash set is sudden premature stiffening, distinct from normal setting: high temperature drives cement hydration faster than the mix's retarder and water content can manage, sometimes compounded by an incompatible admixture combination or a load that sat too long en route. Water added on site does not recover a batch in flash set; the load is rejected. Prevention sits upstream of the pour. Its parts: a hot-weather retarder ordered for the forecast temperature, a batch-to-discharge budget realistic for the site's distance from the plant on a hot high-demand day (the ASTM C94 90-minute clock), and rejection of any load arriving with early stiffening.

**Professional: pump or chute, pour by pour**

A ready-mix chute reaches roughly 15-20 feet from the truck, varying by truck and chute extension. Within that reach of a truck-accessible position, concrete places by chute at no pumping cost. Beyond chute reach but at grade, a wheelbarrow relay or a motorized buggy extends reach at the cost of labor time, and stays viable for smaller pours. A pump becomes the practical choice on two triggers: the truck cannot reach the element at all (a long or soft access route, a placement point across the footprint from any truck position), or the placement point is elevated or confined.

Applied to this project's pour list:

| Pour | Likely method |
|---|---|
| Footings and foundation walls | Chute, where the excavation is truck-accessible around its perimeter; the site access plan decides pour by pour |
| Slab on grade (house footprint) | Chute plus crew relay where one side is truck-accessible; a line pump where the far edge exceeds chute-plus-relay reach |
| Radiant topping slab | Pump or specialty placement; an interior pour inside a closed envelope is the clear pump case |
| Reinforced masonry grout lifts | Grout pump or specialty grout placement into wall cavities; equipment matching is in the Masonry Craft: Laying And Stacking Block And Brick chapter |

The confirming input is the site's actual truck-access plan, checked pour by pour in the Build Sequence And Site Coordination chapter.

## 7.17 Fresh Concrete Checks

Fresh concrete checks happen during placement and confirm the concrete delivered matches the mix design ordered. They document the delivery, and they identify a bad load before it enters the forms.

**Applied: slump test (ASTM C143)**

1. Wet the slump cone and the base plate.
2. Place the cone narrow-end-up on the base plate.
3. Hold the cone in place by standing on the foot pieces.
4. Fill in three equal layers, rodding each layer 25 times with a 5/8-inch standard rod.
5. Strike off the top with the rod.
6. Lift the cone slowly straight up over 5–7 seconds.
7. Invert the cone next to the slumped concrete; place the rod horizontally across the cone top.
8. Measure the vertical distance from the rod to the highest point of the slumped concrete.
9. Record the slump in inches.

Slump within 1 inch of the specification is generally acceptable. Slump significantly higher than specification typically means too much water was added (reject the load if it exceeds the supplier's tolerance). Slump significantly lower may indicate the mix is too dry for placement; the supplier may add a measured amount of water (within ASTM C94 limits) or the load may need to be rejected.

**Applied: air content test (ASTM C231 pressure method)**

For air-entrained concrete:

1. Calibrate the air meter per the manufacturer's instructions before each pour day.
2. Sample fresh concrete per ASTM C172.
3. Fill the meter base in three layers, rodding each layer 25 times.
4. Strike off the top flush with the rim.
5. Clamp the cover, fill the petcocks with water, close the petcocks.
6. Pump pressure into the gauge, release the pressure into the chamber, and read the air content from the gauge.
7. Record the result.

Specified air content is typically 5–7% for freeze-thaw exposure; 1–2% for interior slabs that will not see freeze-thaw cycling.

**Applied: temperature test (ASTM C1064)**

1. Insert a calibrated thermometer into a sample of fresh concrete.
2. Allow 2 minutes for temperature stabilization.
3. Record the temperature.

Specified concrete temperature: minimum 50 °F at placement under ACI 306 cold-weather provisions; maximum 90 °F at placement under ACI 305 hot-weather provisions. Project specifications may tighten these.

**Applied: cylinders (ASTM C31)**

1. Sample fresh concrete per ASTM C172.
2. Fill cylinder molds in three layers, rodding each layer 25 times.
3. Strike off the top, place a cap on each mold.
4. Label each cylinder: date, pour location, mix design, cylinder number, technician initials.
5. Store cylinders on a level surface, protected from sun and freezing, at 60–80 °F for the first 24 hours.
6. After 24 hours, transport to the laboratory in cushioned containers (or as per the lab's instructions) for moist-curing until testing.

A typical "set" is 4 cylinders: 1 tested at 7 days for early indication, 2 at 28 days (averaged for the strength-test result), and 1 hold for re-test at 56 days if the 28-day result is suspect.

**Applied: cylinder molds and end preparation**

- Use 4x8-inch molds. ASTM C31 requires a mold diameter at least 3 times the nominal maximum aggregate size; at this project's 3/4-inch aggregate, 4 inches clears the minimum with margin. The 4x8 specimen uses less material and breaks on a smaller machine than the older 6x12 size, which remains necessary only above roughly 1.5-inch aggregate.
- Any non-absorbent, non-reactive, shape-stable, leak-free mold complies, metal or plastic. Single-use plastic molds bought by the case are the simplest path for the 15-30 cylinders a whole house build produces. Reusable split-shell steel molds pay off where trial batches, grout cylinders, and block-program QC keep them in use. Suppliers (Gilson, Humboldt, Forney) quote mold and pad pricing; confirm at purchase.
- Expect the lab to prepare cylinder ends with unbonded neoprene pad caps in steel retaining rings (ASTM C1231), the standard method for this project's 4,000-5,000 psi range. Pads are matched to the strength range and reused a manufacturer-rated number of tests. Bonded sulfur capping (ASTM C617) uses a molten, fume-producing compound and stays a laboratory method. End grinding applies to high-strength testing near 11,600 psi and above, far past this project's range.

**Professional: flexural beam specimens**

Acceptance for footings, walls, and slabs in this project runs on compressive strength under ACI 318 §26.12, tested on cylinders. Flexural modulus-of-rupture testing serves contracts with a written flexural specification, chiefly transportation pavement work. ASTM C78 uses third-point loading on 6x6x21-inch beams cast in two rodded or vibrated layers. ASTM C293, the center-point method, was withdrawn by ASTM in 2025 and produced higher readings than C78 on identical specimens; it is not specified for new work. Beams are cast only where the engineer of record writes a flexural acceptance criterion into the design, an unusual choice for a residence. A large exterior slab poured by a commercial paving crew to the crew's own flexural specification runs under that crew's QC protocol.

**Professional: the compression machine stays at the laboratory**

ASTM C39 requires the testing machine to be verified to ASTM E4. E4 verification means force accuracy within ±1.0 percent of indicated load at five load levels spanning 10-100 percent of rated capacity. Re-verification is due at least every 13 months and after any relocation or force-system repair. A homemade hydraulic frame with an aftermarket pressure gauge has no calibration path except the same paid verification a commercial machine receives. A building official has no obligation to accept an unverified result. Used commercial machines list from roughly $1,500 for older small-capacity units to $18,000-$50,000 for larger or automated machines (2026 market listings, order-of-magnitude only). Against this project's 15-30 total cylinders at $25-$50 per accredited-lab break, neither building nor buying a frame recovers its cost. The lab's CCRL or AASHTO accreditation stands behind its machine's E4 currency. That accreditation is the reason cylinders go to an accredited lab rather than to any lab.

**Professional: dry-cast block mixes sit outside these methods**

ASTM C31 is scoped to concrete with measurable slump; its text excludes mixes without measurable slump. A dry-cast block mix compacted under press pressure is not represented by a rodded or vibrated cylinder, and no standardized correlation exists between cylinder strength and pressed-unit strength. Owner-made units are qualified as units under ASTM C140. Specimens are capped per ASTM C1552, strength is computed on the net cross-sectional area, and samples run in six-unit sets (three for strength, three for absorption). Achieved density tracks batch-to-batch consistency within one press and one mix. It does not convert to a psi value without paired density-and-strength testing on that operation's own trial units. The block test program is in the Owner-Made Blocks And Alternative Concretes chapter. The lot-sampling frequency is a stated dependency there: the figure requires the current ASTM C140/C90 text, which sits behind the ASTM paywall. A wet-cast cylinder poured beside a dry-cast batch checks the recipe's proportions; it does not predict the pressed unit's strength.

**Applied: field-equipment calibration**

Treat calibration as a pre-pour-season checklist item, and keep the dated verification records in the project binder with the pour records (§7.21):

- Slump cone: verify dimensions annually against the ASTM C143 tolerance of ±1/8 inch on the 4-inch top, 8-inch base, and 12-inch height. A documented check is valid for 12 months.
- Air meter: calibrate annually per the manufacturer's ASTM C231 procedure. Any reference thermometer used in the verification must be accurate to ±0.5 °F with a certificate traceable to NIST.
- Thermometer: keep a current NIST-traceable calibration certificate for acceptance-relevant readings.

An official has no obligation to accept a reading from equipment without current verification. On an uneventful pour with passing lab breaks, calibration is rarely scrutinized. In a low-strength investigation (§26.12.6), review of test procedures is an explicit step, and an unverified slump cone or air meter undermines the field record at the moment it matters most. The annual verification also supports the pre-pour-meeting credibility on which acceptance of an owner-mixed pour depends (§7.14.8).

**Acceptance criteria (ACI 318-19 §26.12.3.1)**

1. Average of any 3 consecutive strength tests ≥ specified f'c
2. No individual strength test less than (f'c – 500) psi for f'c ≤ 5,000 psi (or less than 0.90·f'c for f'c > 5,000 psi)

Failure of criterion 2 triggers a low-strength investigation under §26.12.6. The investigation may include:

- review of test procedures and laboratory practice (cylinders broken incorrectly, equipment out of calibration)
- core testing of the in-place concrete per ASTM C42 (acceptance: average of 3 cores ≥ 85% of f'c, no individual core < 75% of f'c)
- non-destructive in-place testing
- structural evaluation by the engineer of record

Most low-strength investigations resolve favorably (the in-place concrete tests stronger than the cylinders). The engineer of record makes the final acceptance decision.

## 7.18 Durability Verification Beyond Strength

A 28-day cylinder verifies strength. Permeability and the air-void system, the properties that set freeze-thaw resistance and reinforcement protection, are separate properties with separate tests. This section identifies which of those tests fit a residential build.

**Simple**

A cylinder that breaks strong can still let water through too easily. Durability depends on two things: tight pores and well-spaced air bubbles. Different tests measure those properties, and most stay at the laboratory. For a house, the field tests in this chapter plus one lab check on a trial batch are enough.

**Professional: the air-void system**

Freeze-thaw resistance depends on the spacing of entrained air voids, not on total air content alone. The adopted limit for moderate freeze-thaw exposure is a maximum void spacing factor of 0.20 mm (0.008 inch), commonly paired with total air of 6 ± 1 percent and a specific surface of at least 24 mm² per mm³. The fresh-concrete pressure test (§7.17) measures total air only; a mix can meet its total-air target with a poor void structure when the air-entraining dosage, the mixing time, or the aggregate gradation is off. The spacing factor itself is measurable only by ASTM C457 petrographic examination of hardened concrete, a laboratory microscopy method used for mix qualification and forensic work, not for residential acceptance. Inter-laboratory standard deviation on the spacing factor approaches one-fifth of the average value, so a single C457 result is an indicator within that band, not a precise pass-fail number. For this project, the §7.17 pressure test on a mix with a qualified air-entraining admixture at the manufacturer's dosage is the sufficient field verification. C457 is reserved for a forensic question, such as unexpected scaling after construction.

**Professional: permeability indicators**

Three standardized tests indicate permeability class:

- RCPT (ASTM C1202): total electrical charge passed, in coulombs, through a 4-inch by 2-inch specimen at 60 volts DC over 6 hours. Dedicated laboratory apparatus.
- Surface resistivity (AASHTO T358): a four-probe handheld meter read on a saturation-conditioned specimen. The standard instrument retails at $4,590 (2026). A reading on a specimen outside the standard's saturation conditioning is not comparable to published durability classes; a drier specimen reads falsely high.
- Sorptivity (ASTM C1585): water-absorption rate by capillary suction, run with a balance, a shallow water tray, and a specified specimen-conditioning period. The simplest apparatus of the three.

The practical path for this project: a permeability specimen added to the trial-batch or witness cylinders already going to the accredited lab (§7.14.6), with the lab naming which of the three tests it runs in-house and at what price. Per-test pricing is not published and varies by lab. A surface-resistivity meter is not a single-house purchase at $4,590 against a complete field-and-lab testing program costing a fraction of that. The one defensible owner use of a borrowed meter is trend tracking across the owner's own batches under one consistent owner-applied conditioning routine; the trend is informative, and the absolute number is not comparable to a laboratory result. Longer moist curing improves the same pore-structure property every one of these tests measures; the curing durations are in §7.19.

## 7.19 Consolidation, Finishing, And Curing

Consolidation closes voids and produces a dense, durable concrete. Finishing produces the final surface texture and tolerances. Curing maintains moisture and temperature during the early life of the concrete to develop strength and durability.

**Simple**

Fresh concrete is full of trapped air pockets. A vibrating rod pushed into the mix shakes them out. Without that step, the wall comes out honeycombed and weak. After finishing, the concrete must cure: kept damp and above 50 °F for at least a week. Concrete gains strength through a slow chemical reaction with water. Drying out or freezing in that first week costs strength permanently.

**Applied: consolidation**

Internal vibration with an immersion (poker) vibrator is the standard method for walls, columns, and thick slabs. Insert vertically, advance methodically along the placement, withdraw at a controlled rate. Vibrator radius of action is typically 12–18 inches; insertions every 18–24 inches give overlapping action.

Signs of adequate consolidation:

- aggregate is no longer visible at the surface (paste flushes around it)
- entrapped air bubbles cease rising
- the surface levels out and develops a uniform sheen

Signs of over-vibration:

- segregation: paste at the surface, coarse aggregate at the bottom
- excessive bleed water
- form pressure increasing as the mix liquefies

External form vibrators are used on tall walls or congested reinforcement where internal vibration cannot reach. Screed-mounted vibrators consolidate slabs during initial finishing.

**Applied: finishing slabs**

1. Strike off (screed): use a straightedge or vibratory screed to bring the slab to design elevation, working back and forth in short strokes.
2. Bull float: a wide flat float pushed and pulled across the surface. Embeds coarse aggregate, removes screed marks, smooths the surface. Done while concrete is still plastic.
3. Wait for bleed water to evaporate. Do not begin further finishing while bleed water is on the surface; floating it back into the surface produces a weak surface layer that scales later.
4. Edging: round the perimeter and any control joints with an edging tool.
5. Grooving: cut control joints to the design pattern (typically 1/4 of slab thickness deep, spaced at slab-thickness × 24–36 in. for a 4-inch slab; that is, every 8–12 ft).
6. Floating: a steel or magnesium hand float. First pass closes finer surface voids. May be done with a power float (bull float on rotating disk) on larger slabs.
7. Troweling (if a hard troweled finish is wanted): one or more passes with a steel trowel, increasing pressure with each pass. Final trowel pass at concrete that has just lost surface sheen.

For exterior or wet areas where slip resistance is wanted, a broom finish replaces final troweling. Drag a stiff broom across the surface in straight passes after the second float pass.

**Professional: over-finishing and the air-void system**

Steel troweling applies pressure and shear to the surface paste, and repeated or aggressive passes work the entrained air bubbles out of the near-surface zone. The slab interior keeps its air; the surface skin loses it exactly where freeze-thaw exposure is most direct. The result is scaling on a slab whose delivered mix met its air specification at the truck. A second mechanism compounds the first: entrained air slows bleed-water rise, so the surface can look ready to trowel while bleed water is still coming up, and troweling then traps that water under the closed surface as a weak, delaminating layer.

The practice rules that follow:

- Exterior freeze-thaw-exposed flatwork is not hard-troweled at all. The float or broom finish specified above is the correct surface for the project's exterior slabs.
- Initial passes on air-entrained concrete use no steel tools; the bull float and a magnesium hand float disturb the void structure less than steel.
- Power troweling, where used, runs under roughly 120 rpm, in one or two passes, only after bleed water has fully evaporated.
- The finishing method follows each element's air content and exposure per the call-out table in §7.15. One uniform finishing technique across all slabs is the error these rules prevent.

**Professional: finishing behavior of SCM mixes**

A fly ash mix behaves differently under the float than a straight portland mix, in three ways the crew plans for. Color: the ash's unburned carbon, iron, and lime content shifts the cured shade, darker with high-carbon ash and lighter with high-lime ash. Color consistency requires one ash source for the whole project. Any exposed architectural surface gets a sample panel of the actual source before the pour; exposed finishes are in the Aesthetics And Architectural Expression chapter. Workability: the spherical ash particles reduce water demand at a given slump, roughly 7 percent less at a 30 percent replacement; batch-water calibration for owner-mixed elements starts from that lower baseline. Set and bleed: fly ash mixes bleed less and set later than a straight portland control. Published set extensions run at least 30 percent, and up to 50 percent, at 30-60 percent replacement. The finishing window opens later than straight-portland timing suggests, later still on a cool day, and the crew reads the surface rather than the clock. This project's 15-25 percent replacement sits below the high-volume range; the same effects apply at reduced size.

**Applied: flatwork equipment, rent or buy**

| Tool | Rate (2026) | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| Walk-behind power trowel (36-48 inch, gas) | $90/day, Salem rental listing; $95-$105/day national reference by size class | Rent for any large slab pour; hand-finish small pours that fit inside the finishing window |
| Vibratory power screed | $100/day self-pickup, Salem; roughly $550/day where delivery and pickup are included | Rent with self-pickup for the largest slab pours; the hand straightedge and bull float stay the baseline for room-scale pours |

The deciding variable is the finishing window. On a slab of roughly 1,000 square feet and up, a hand crew risks losing the surface before finishing completes, and the trowel's day rate is small against a failed surface at that size. On a few hundred square feet, a two-person crew finishes by hand within the window, and the rental adds transport time and an unfamiliar machine. No published square-footage crossover exists; pour size and crew speed decide each case. The screed's delivered price against self-pickup is a transport decision: a truck or trailer that carries the machine saves roughly $450 per rental day. The saw's 21-day breakeven ($2,673 against $125 per day) overstates the case for buying. Control joints, block cuts, and patch cuts cluster into a handful of sessions on a single house, and rental charges accrue only on the days the saw is used. Confirm current rates with the rental company serving the site at pour time.

**Professional: why slabs crack, and what the joints control**

Four mechanisms produce slab cracks, each with a countermeasure already specified in this chapter:

- Plastic shrinkage. Surface water evaporates faster than bleed water rises while the concrete is still plastic; the surface shrinks over a wet, dimensionally stable mass and tears into shallow, map-patterned or parallel cracks within the first hours. The drivers are wind, low humidity, heat, and direct sun. The countermeasure is the hot-weather protocol below: fog spray, evaporation retarder, off-peak timing.
- Drying shrinkage. The hardened slab loses moisture over weeks to months and the paste contracts, surface first. Tensile stress cracks the section wherever it is weakest, and that line is what a control joint predetermines. The saw cut reduces the effective depth along a chosen line, so the crack forms there instead of at random. A higher water-cement ratio increases total shrinkage, so the project's 0.45 maximum is crack control as well as durability. Weak curing sharpens the surface-to-core drying gradient that the curing durations below flatten.
- Curling. The same top-down drying gradient lifts slab edges and corners into a slight curve. Curling is not itself a crack; it concentrates load stress at joints and unsupported corners, and it produces corner cracking where joint spacing runs too wide or subgrade support is uneven.
- Restraint. Subgrade friction, embedded items, and re-entrant corners (interior corners at columns and openings) prevent free shrinkage and concentrate stress. The compaction and base-course work in §7.5-7.6 is crack control as well as bearing, and a re-entrant corner gets diagonal reinforcement or a diagonal joint even where the field spacing rule passes.

Joint timing is bounded on both sides. A cut made too early ravels the green joint face at the blade. A cut made too late arrives after shrinkage stress has passed the early tensile strength, a random crack has already formed, and the joint controls nothing. Conventional wet-cut sawing works within roughly 6-12 hours of finishing; early-entry dry-cut saws move the window to 1-4 hours after finishing and fit hot, fast-drying conditions.

**Applied: curing methods**

| Method | Description | Best fit |
|---|---|---|
| Wet burlap | Burlap layered over the surface, kept saturated with water | Slabs, walls; the cleanest method for verifying continuous moisture |
| Plastic sheeting | Polyethylene sheet over the concrete; moisture preserved by trapping bleed water | Slabs, especially in cold weather; low maintenance |
| Curing compound | Membrane-forming compound sprayed on the surface; locks in moisture | Slabs where wet curing is impractical; many ready-mix suppliers offer compatible compounds |
| Wet-cured forms | Wood or steel forms left in place, kept wet | Walls, columns; minimal additional effort |
| Sealing forms | Sealed forms with no water loss | Walls; less common for residential |

Curing duration: 7 days minimum at 50 °F or warmer; 14 days for SCM mixes (fly ash, slag); longer in cold weather.

ACI 308R covers curing in detail. The general rule: maintain a temperature above 50 °F and concrete moisture above the dry-out threshold for the curing period. Failure to cure produces concrete that is weaker, more permeable, and more crack-prone.

**Professional: curing failures**

Skipped curing leaves the surface zone permanently weaker, more porous, and more permeable than the same mix cured properly, because the surface loses its mix water before hydration completes. The surface zone carries the exposure (abrasion, freeze-thaw, chloride and carbonation entry), so under-curing damages durability out of proportion to its effect on cylinder strength. A passing cylinder break does not certify a cured surface; the cylinders cure in the laboratory's moist room, not on the slab. Curing started late or ended early loses a proportional share of the same protection. Intermittent moisture is worse than either error: burlap that dries between waterings, or sheeting with gaps, re-opens the same surface microcracking with every drying cycle. Each method in the table above works only as a continuous method: saturated burlap stays saturated, sheeting stays sealed, a compound film stays unbroken.

The compound-versus-wet-cure tradeoff: a membrane-forming compound retains the mix's own water rather than adding any, requires no daily attendance, and is the practical choice where no one is on site every day. Wherever the film is thin, uneven, or damaged before full cure, that spot reverts to the no-curing condition. Continuous wet curing adds external moisture and produces the best documented durability outcome, at the labor cost of never letting it lapse. An inconsistent attempt at wet curing produces a worse surface than a well-applied compound.

**Professional: extended curing for SCM mixes**

The 14-day SCM minimum exists because fly ash replacement reduces the paste's calcium hydroxide reserve and adds a pozzolanic reaction slower than cement hydration. With curing cut short, an SCM mix carbonates faster and stays more permeable than the plain mix it replaced. Given moisture and time, the same reaction densifies the pore structure past what plain cement reaches. Longer moist curing measurably lowers both carbonation rate and permeability, on a curve that flattens: the gain from day 21 over day 14 exceeds the gain from day 35 over day 28. No published table converts added days into a stated percentage for this project's 15-25 percent replacement. The project practice, a reasoned target rather than a laboratory-verified threshold: 21-28 days of continuous wet curing on below-grade and structurally critical SCM elements (footings, foundation walls, water-retaining elements), coordinated with the backfill strength schedule in §7.20. The carbonation and chloride mechanisms behind this practice are in the Designing For Centuries chapter.

**Cold-weather concreting (ACI 306R)**

When ambient temperature is below 40 °F or expected to drop below 40 °F during the curing window:

- maintain concrete temperature at minimum 50 °F (40 °F for thicker sections) for the protection period (typically 3 days for normal mix, longer for SCM mixes)
- protect from freezing (concrete that freezes before reaching 500 psi compressive strength loses much of its design strength)
- options: heated enclosure, insulated blankets, hot water in the mix (but limited by ACI 306 max temperature), accelerator admixtures
- verify the 500 psi threshold with one of two field methods: the maturity method (ASTM C1074, a maturity sensor cast into the pour), or field-cured cylinders stored beside the work and broken early

Two additions to the protection list:

- Subgrade, forms, and reinforcement are frost-free and above 32 °F at placement. Warm concrete on a frozen subgrade loses heat downward quickly and cures unevenly through the thickness, and a subgrade that thaws later settles under the set footing. Ground thawing treats the subgrade directly: insulating blankets or a heated enclosure over the excavation the day before the pour. Air temperature alone does not describe subgrade condition.
- Accelerators near reinforcement are non-chloride. Calcium chloride, the traditional cold-weather accelerator, promotes corrosion at the steel interface and is excluded from this project's reinforced elements, which is every structural element. Calcium nitrite (§7.14.4) or another non-chloride accelerator provides the set acceleration without the corrosion cost.

The plant-side cold-weather order combines a higher early-strength cement content or Type III cement, warmed batch water, and the non-chloride accelerator, all batched at the plant rather than added on site. Type III's faster, hotter hydration raises early-age thermal-cracking risk in thick sections, and a high-early-cement approach and a high-SCM-replacement approach pull early strength in opposite directions. The cold-weather mix is decided as a single coordinated decision covering cement type, SCM fraction, and accelerator.

Concrete that freezes before reaching roughly 500 psi loses design strength permanently: the water in the not-yet-hydrated paste expands as ice and disrupts the developing cement matrix in a way later curing does not repair. The documented loss reaches half the design strength. That mechanism is why the protection threshold is 500 psi rather than the freezing point alone.

**Professional: the fly ash cold-weather penalty**

The pozzolanic reaction is slower than cement hydration, and cold slows it further; the two effects compound rather than add. Under the ACI cold-weather definition (three or more consecutive days averaging below 40 °F, with temperature above 50 °F for no more than half of any 24-hour period), a fly ash mix gains strength minimally through the affected period. The delay to the 500 psi protection threshold and to stripping or backfill strength concentrates in the first 3-7 days, and the mixes generally converge by 28 days. No verified day-count exists for a 25 percent replacement at Oregon winter temperatures; the delay depends on the ash source's reactivity, the water-cement ratio, and the concrete temperature held under protection. Cold-weather scheduling therefore runs on field verification rather than the calendar: the maturity method or field-cured companion cylinders confirm each threshold before forms strip, protection lifts, or backfill proceeds. Where a date is fixed, the options are a reduced fly ash fraction for that one cold-weather element, accepted as a deliberate durability trade, or the non-chloride accelerator plus a heated enclosure.

**Hot-weather concreting (ACI 305R)**

When ambient temperature exceeds 80 °F or concrete temperature would exceed 90 °F:

- shade aggregate stockpiles
- chill mix water (or substitute ice for part of the mix water)
- limit concrete temperature at placement to 90 °F maximum
- mist or fog the surface to prevent rapid drying
- use retarder admixtures to extend setting time
- pour during cooler parts of the day (early morning or evening)

## 7.20 Backfill And Foundation Protection

Backfill loads the foundation walls. Bracing has to be in place, the wall has to have reached design strength, and the backfill material has to be appropriate.

The Soil, Ground Prep, Drainage, And Foundation Risk chapter contains the backfill material classes, the wet-season excavation protection rules, and the site-water management this work depends on. This section states the structural timing and the against-the-wall sequence.

**Applied**

Before backfill:

- foundation drains installed and inspected
- waterproofing or dampproofing membrane installed and inspected
- dimple drainage board over the membrane (where used)
- protection board or insulation over the drainage board (where used)
- foundation has reached at least 7-day strength (design strength typically targeted before backfill)
- floor system or temporary bracing installed at the top of the foundation wall; without top bracing, backfill loads can rotate or crack the wall

Backfill material:

- free-draining granular material is preferred (sand, gravel, crushed stone)
- avoid clay or silty soil that traps water against the foundation
- avoid debris, organics, or oversized rocks that can damage the membrane
- compact in lifts of 6–8 inches, at low energy near the foundation (do not over-compact and load the wall horizontally)

Backfill sequence:

1. Place free-draining material at the perimeter footing drain to the level of the drain rock.
2. Place granular backfill in 6–8-inch lifts, compacted lightly with a hand-tamper or small plate compactor near the wall.
3. Use heavier compaction equipment only beyond the influence zone of the foundation wall (at least 4–5 ft away from the wall, depending on wall height).
4. Slope the upper lifts away from the foundation per ORSC R401.3 (6-inch fall in 10 ft).
5. Final lift: topsoil for landscaping (placed only after final grading and erosion control are accepted).

**Foundation protection during construction**

- protect the dampproofing or waterproofing membrane from puncture during backfill (the protection board is for this purpose)
- protect the top of the foundation from weather during the wall-construction window
- prevent equipment from striking the wall or driving over the perimeter drain
- maintain temporary erosion-control around the foundation perimeter until permanent landscaping and drainage are in place

**Code**

```
Backfill waits for anchorage or bracing.
  Backfill is not placed against a foundation wall until the wall is anchored to the floor above or sufficiently braced.
  Source: ORSC R404.1.7.

The finished grade must fall away from the wall.
  6-inch fall within 10 feet of the foundation.
  Source: ORSC R401.3.

Backfill material is classified and compacted to standard.
  Classification by the soil standards; density specifications from the Proctor tests.
  Source: ASTM D2487 and D2488; ASTM D698 and D1557.
```

## 7.21 Pour Records And Foundation Acceptance

Foundation acceptance requires the documentation chain to be complete. Each pour generates an operational record; the records together demonstrate that the foundation was built per the design.

**Applied: operational record per pour**

For each major pour, the project binder receives:

- the batch ticket (ready-mix) or batch records (owner-mixed) for each load
- the field-test record: slump, air, temperature, cylinder ID, time of test
- the placement record: time of placement start and finish, ambient and concrete temperatures, weather, crew, equipment
- the curing record: method, start and end of curing, ambient temperature during curing, any protective measures used
- photographs before, during, and after the pour
- the inspection record: pre-pour inspection approval, any during-pour observations, any post-pour observations
- the laboratory cylinder test results when received from the testing lab
- the field-equipment verification records: slump-cone dimension check, air-meter calibration, thermometer certificate (§7.17), each dated within its 12-month window at the time of the pour

**Foundation acceptance**

Foundation acceptance requires:

- footing inspection passed
- foundation wall inspection passed
- foundation drainage and dampproofing/waterproofing inspection passed (where applicable)
- under-slab inspection passed (where slab on grade)
- slab inspection passed (some jurisdictions; not always)
- cylinder test results within ACI acceptance criteria
- engineer of record letter of acceptance (where engineered foundation; not always required for prescriptive)

Once the foundation is accepted, work proceeds to the structural shell. Wall and floor construction follows the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter; the inspection schedule from this point forward is in the Inspection And Approval Sequence chapter.

**Records to save through occupancy and beyond**

The foundation records are kept for the life of the building. They support:

- final inspection and certificate of occupancy
- insurance underwriting
- future repair, alteration, or addition
- resale disclosure
- defect-claim defense

The Documentation, Maintenance, And Future Upgrades chapter contains the retention strategy, digital backup, and long-term archive practices.
