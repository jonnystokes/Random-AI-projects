## 9.1 The Idea: Make It, Test It, Use It

**Simple**

Can you legally build a house from blocks you made yourself? In Oregon, yes. The residential code accepts materials two ways. The first way is prescriptive: a factory block that meets the standard specification (ASTM C90) is accepted without argument. The second way is the alternative path: the building official may approve any material shown to be as good as what the code prescribes. The proof is testing. A lab crushes samples of your material and reports the strength. That report, with an engineer's design built on it, is the evidence. This chapter is the manual for both halves: making units in quantity, and producing the proof.

One more fact sets the stage. Since October 1, 2023, Oregon's residential code includes Appendix AU, a prescriptive path for cob (monolithic adobe, an earthen wall material). Oregon now accepts at least one owner-made earthen wall system by standard code tables, with named tests and pass values. That appendix is the clearest evidence of what Oregon already accepts as proof for an earthen material.

**Professional**

The two acceptance layers structure every decision in this chapter:

1. Prescriptive acceptance. A unit meeting ASTM C90, laid per ORSC R606 within its seismic and detailing limits, needs no special approval. An owner-made unit can target C90 itself: the specification does not require a factory, only compliance. Meeting C90 means meeting its net-area compressive strength minimum (1,900-2,000 psi depending on edition; the testing lab confirms the current value), its dimensional tolerances, and its absorption and shrinkage limits, demonstrated by ASTM C140 testing.
2. Alternative acceptance. A material that does not fit C90 (a compressed earth block, a geopolymer unit, a rammed-earth wall) is proposed under ORSC R104.11. The official evaluates equivalency in quality, strength, effectiveness, fire resistance, durability, and safety, and may require tests as evidence under R104.11.1. Approval is per project and per jurisdiction. The statewide route (a Building Codes Division alternate-method ruling that binds every Oregon jurisdiction) is described in the Oregon Code And Compliance Map chapter; no current statewide ruling addresses residential masonry or earthen walls.

The material families in this chapter, mapped to their acceptance route:

- dry-cast concrete block: C90 directly, or R104.11 with test data
- cement-stabilized earth block and rammed earth: R104.11, with Appendix AU, the New Mexico earthen code, and the Pima County standard as precedent frameworks
- cob: prescriptive under Appendix AU within its limits
- geopolymer, lime-pozzolan, and the other alternative binders: R104.11 only
- papercrete, hempcrete, and other bio-aggregate materials: non-structural roles only, on the strength evidence in this chapter

**Code**

```
The building official may approve an alternative material, design, or method.
  Approval requires equivalency to the prescribed code provisions in quality, strength, effectiveness, fire resistance, durability, and safety; a denial is issued in writing with reasons.
  Source: ORSC R104.11 (2023 edition).

The building official may require tests as evidence of compliance for an alternative material.
  Testing is at no expense to the municipality; test reports come from an approved agency or individual, retained per the code's records provisions.
  Source: ORSC R104.11.1, R104.4 (2023 edition).

Cob construction has a prescriptive residential path in Oregon.
  Appendix AU, Cob Construction (Monolithic Adobe), adopted effective October 1, 2023, with its own material test protocols and five samples per required test per material lot.
  Source: ORSC Appendix AU (2023 edition, effective 2023-10-01).
```

## 9.2 Making Versus Buying Units: The Full Weighing

The Wall System chapter specifies the wall; the decision recorded here is whether the units in that wall are purchased or owner-made. This project plans owner-made units. This section states what that decision costs and buys, across five dimensions: cost, labor and schedule, quality, code risk, and what making buys.

**Cost.** Delivered commercial 8x8x16 gray block runs roughly $1.60-$2.25 per unit, retail $1.25-$3.00 (2026), with the plant's C90 compliance already documented (the Materials, Suppliers, Procurement, And Substitution chapter contains the supplier detail). Owner-made compressed-earth block has a documented materials-plus-labor cost of roughly $0.05-$0.40 per block (2026), the range spanning 0-10 percent cement stabilization and labor priced from unpaid owner time to $25 per hour. Equipment sits between those numbers. Documented owner-scale options run from a welded lever press built from published plans for a few hundred dollars in steel, up to the open-source Liberator hydraulic press at roughly $8,000 (2026). Commercial machines of comparable output run $45,000-$65,000 (2026). Manual press purchase prices require current vendor quotes; the research found no reliable 2026 figures. The testing program is a real cost with no published total: lab compression testing is priced per specimen by quote, and engineering support runs $100-$220 per hour (2026). A per-unit cost advantage on the order of $1-$2 only becomes a project advantage after it has repaid the press, the molds, the mixer, and the testing program.

**Labor and schedule.** A purchased load of block arrives in one truck. Owner production at the target rate of roughly 100 units per day requires 2.5-3.5 hours of press time on a manual press (30-50 blocks per hour), plus mixing, handling, curing-yard work, and quality checks around it. The documented benchmark for the reference manual press is roughly 200 blocks per day from a three-person crew. Against that benchmark, 100 per day for one operator is an ambitious rate; the pacing evidence is in the throughput section of this chapter. A house-quantity run is measured in weeks to months of production calendar, and that calendar sits in front of wall-laying, not parallel to the first course.

**Quality.** A block plant ships units inside C90's tolerances (dimensions within 1/8 inch) with plant QC behind them. An owner line reaches consistency only through the discipline in this chapter: a calibrated moisture target, a fixed compaction routine, weigh-a-block checks, and periodic lab verification. The failure cost differs too: a bad purchased lot is the supplier's problem; a bad owner lot is discovered by the owner's own testing or, worse, by the inspector's.

**Code risk.** Purchased C90 units carry no acceptance risk. Owner-made units targeting C90 carry testing risk only: the units either pass C140 testing or they do not. Owner-made units outside C90 carry approval risk: R104.11 acceptance is discretionary, per official and per project, and the acceptance sections of this chapter exist to manage exactly that risk.

**What making buys.** Four returns:

- unit cost per block after equipment payback
- control of the mix and the face: color, texture, and format options priced at pigment cost rather than architectural-unit premiums
- a unit dimensioned to this project's module
- a documented production capability that outlasts the house project

The decision holds only with the production discipline, the testing program, and the schedule margin stated above; a reader who wants the wall without the production program buys the block and keeps the rest of this chapter as reference.

## 9.3 The Test-To-Prove-It Method

**Simple**

How does a homemade material become a number an engineer can design with? By the same loop a lab uses. Choose a candidate mix. Make samples the standard way. Cure them the standard time. Have a lab crush them and report the strength. Change one variable and repeat. The loop ends when samples pass the target with margin, batch after batch. The lab report is the product. Every path in this chapter, standard or strange, ends at the same kind of report.

**Applied**

Run the proving loop in this order. Record every batch in the production log (the record format is in the curing-yard section).

1. Characterize the inputs first. For an earth-based mix: commission a Proctor compaction test (ASTM D698) on the actual site soil to establish optimum moisture content and maximum dry density. Cost is a standard geotechnical lab item; get a quote with the soil report work in the Soil, Ground Prep, Drainage, And Foundation Risk chapter. For a concrete mix with local aggregate: run the 16-day ASTM C1260 mortar-bar screen for alkali-silica reactivity before committing to the source. The two-year C1293 prism test is the research-grade alternative; the 16-day screen fits a build schedule.
2. Fix the specimen format to the claim. A concrete or geopolymer mix is proven on cylinders under ASTM C39. A soil-cement mix is proven on cylinders under ASTM D1633. A finished masonry unit is proven under ASTM C140, reported against the ASTM C90 specification. Cob under Appendix AU is proven on 4x4x4-inch cubes and 6x6x12-inch beams. Test the format the submission will cite.
3. Batch a matrix, not a single mix. Vary one variable per batch: cement percentage, moisture, compaction energy. Three to five levels per variable locates the response curve; published curves from other soils and other fly ashes do not transfer.
4. Cure specimens by the standard schedule and log the dates. The 28-day benchmark governs reported strength for portland-cement mixes; earlier breaks (7-day) predict the trend but do not replace the 28-day number.
5. Add durability tests where the material is earth-based or the exposure is wet: ASTM D559 (wetting-and-drying) and D560 (freezing-and-thawing) on the stabilized mix. Each program takes about a month; schedule it with the strength matrix, not after it.
6. Set the production target above the passing value. The margin covers batch-to-batch variation an owner line produces and a plant does not. The margin size is a judgment made with the engineer of record against the measured scatter of the trial batches.
7. Calibrate the field checks last. Weigh passing specimens and record target unit weight; the daily weigh-a-block check (curing-yard section) then guards the lab-proven density without breaking units.

**Professional**

The test standards divide by what they prove. ASTM C39 measures compressive strength of a cast cylinder: it proves the material. ASTM C140 measures a finished unit: compression, absorption, dimensions, density. It proves the product, and the C90 specification is enforced through it. ASTM D1633 is C39's counterpart for molded soil-cement cylinders, compacted per D558 at the D698-established moisture. D559 and D560 measure mass loss and volume change under 12 cycles of wetting-drying and freezing-thawing. The Portland Cement Association criteria built on them set minimum cement content for weathering. That durability minimum commonly exceeds the strength minimum. Steel or polymer fibers change flexural toughness, measured under ASTM C1609 or C1399. Their documented effect on the C39 compression number is minimal; fiber additions are not a route to a higher reported compressive strength. Water-retaining structures are proven by a different instrument entirely: a hydrostatic tightness test held at full fill (ACI 350.1 class), covered with ferrocement later in this chapter.

Lot-based unit testing under C140 is how a whole production run stays proven, not just the trial batch. The program structure: a defined lot (a stated quantity or production period under one mix design), samples from each lot per the standard, and a test frequency tied to production quantity. Appendix AU's five-samples-per-test-per-lot rule and the Pima County earthen standard's frequency-by-square-footage rule are the two code-anchored models. The sampling program this project proposes to the official is specified in the curing-yard section; its acceptance role is in the Oregon acceptance path section. The Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement chapter's concrete cylinder program is the same discipline applied to placed concrete; the two programs share the lab and the record format.

**Code**

```
Loadbearing concrete masonry units must meet the C90 specification.
  Net-area compressive strength per the edition in force (1,900-2,000 psi across recent editions; the lab confirms the current value); dimensional tolerance 1/8 inch on width, height, length; absorption and linear-shrinkage limits per the specification tables, all demonstrated by ASTM C140 test methods.
  Source: ASTM C90; ASTM C140.

Soil-cement for structural evaluation is molded and tested by standard methods.
  Moisture-density relations per ASTM D558 with compaction targets from ASTM D698; compressive strength on molded cylinders per ASTM D1633; durability by 12-cycle wetting-drying (ASTM D559) and freezing-thawing (ASTM D560) mass-loss tests.
  Source: ASTM D558; ASTM D698; ASTM D1633; ASTM D559; ASTM D560.

Cob material under the Oregon prescriptive path is proven by cube and beam tests.
  4x4x4-inch cube compressive strength at least 60 psi for cob walls and 85 psi where used in braced wall panels; 6x6x12-inch beam modulus of rupture at least 50 psi for braced panels; five samples per required test per material lot.
  Source: ORSC Appendix AU (2023 edition).
```

## 9.4 Standard Dry-Cast Zero-Slump Block

**Simple**

Factory concrete block is pressed, not poured. The mix contains so little water that it cannot flow at all. A machine packs it into a mold under pressure and vibration, and the mold lifts away immediately. The packed particles grip each other and the block stands on its own, minutes old. The cement then hardens over the following weeks inside a shape that already exists. This is why one mold can make hundreds of blocks a day. The whole production system in this chapter is built around that one property.

**Applied**

Batch the dry-cast mix inside this window, then prove it on cylinders per the test-to-prove-it method.

1. Hold the water-cementitious ratio near 0.30-0.38. Precast practice cites values down to roughly 0.22 for specialized products. Below the window the mix will not consolidate; above it the block slumps at demold.
2. Keep total cementitious content at or above roughly 470 pounds per cubic yard, the common dry-cast specification floor.
3. Use small, rounded coarse aggregate (pea gravel is the industry default) with a well-graded sand fraction. Large angular rock wears molds and resists the packing behavior immediate demold depends on.
4. Check moisture every batch with the hand-squeeze test (mixing section), calibrated against the mix design, not against feel alone.
5. Treat compaction energy as part of the recipe. The same batch pressed harder makes a stronger block; a correct mix without adequate compaction makes no block at all.

**Professional**

The mechanism is particle packing, not fluid behavior. At near-zero water content the mix is governed by inter-particle friction; added water past the window does not raise the ASTM C143 slump reading, it destroys green cohesion. Commercial block machines combine a static pressing force on the order of 75 kg/cm2 (roughly 1,070 psi) with mold-box vibration at 25-50 Hz. Higher-output machines run a second, independently controlled vibration system on the pressure head, with components cited up to 75-120 Hz to work the fine paste fraction. Compressive strength rises with compaction energy to a point of diminishing return.

Two production traditions descend from that physics, and the split organizes the equipment sections that follow. The high-static-pressure tradition (the compressed-earth-block press) applies 7-15 tons to a small solid block face with no vibration. The vibration-plus-modest-pressure tradition (the commercial block machine) substitutes vibration energy for static tonnage across the larger hollow CMU face. Reaching the 75 kg/cm2 benchmark on a full 8x8x16 face (roughly 826 cm2) by static force alone requires on the order of 60 or more tonnes; the same face consolidates under a vibrating table plus a few-tonne head. That arithmetic, from the National Precast Concrete Association's dry-cast guidance and block-machine industry data, decides which press an owner needs for which unit.

## 9.5 Fast Demold And Throughput: The 100-Units-A-Day Problem

This project's production target is roughly 100 units per day from one or two people. The press is not the constraint. At a documented manual-press rate of 30-40 blocks per hour (50 with an electric vibrator assist), 100 units is 2.5-3.5 hours of press time. The constraint is everything around the press: keeping a forced-action mixer ahead of it, moving each green unit to the curing yard without damage, and cycling molds and pallets back to the head of the line. No published source documents a complete one-or-two-person 100-unit-per-day line; the components are documented separately in this chapter, and their assembly is this project's own design work.

Documented operations bracket the target from both sides:

| Operation | Output | Crew |
|---|---|---|
| Ghana hand-molding baseline | 400 bricks/day | 30 molders |
| Same yard, one semi-automatic machine | 3,200 blocks/8-hour shift | machine crew |
| Nigeria, three manual machines | 2,200 blocks/day average | 15 workers |
| Hydraform static manual (V1SE) | 60 mold cycles/hour | 6-8 staff |
| Hydraform light electric (V2LE) | 35 mold cycles/hour | 5-7 staff |
| Auram-class manual lever press | 30-50 blocks/hour | 1 operator |
| AECT Impact 2001A (7 HP diesel hydraulic) | ~300 blocks/hour | small crew |
| Egg-layer mobile machines | 3,840-16,800 blocks/day | machine crew |

Two conclusions follow. First, 100-plus units per day with minimal hand labor requires mechanized compaction; the pure hand-molding baseline is roughly 13 bricks per person per day. Second, every machine class above the manual lever press is over-scaled for this project: the smallest egg-layer cited makes this project's entire daily target in about 40 minutes. The manual lever press tops out near 40-60 blocks per hour even with vibration assist and a second operator; sustained production beyond that rate is where powered hydraulic machines stop being optional. This project's target sits inside manual-press range on cycle rate, and above the documented solo workload benchmarks.

The workload evidence: the reference manual press (CINVA-Ram class) is documented at roughly 200 blocks per day from a three-person crew that rotates tasks, roughly 65-70 blocks per person per day. A solo operator at 100 per day is running half again above that benchmark with no rotation, every production day, for the weeks to months a house-quantity run takes. Industry sources state the qualitative consequence plainly: manual machines produce operator fatigue and injury exposure that hydraulic systems remove. At solo scale the hydraulic-press decision is a body-preservation decision as much as a throughput decision; the same trade appears in the Owner-Builder's Body And Mind chapter. No source documents a sustainable solo rest-day cadence for this work. The project treats 100 per day as a burst rate until its own practice-structure phase (practice-structures section) measures a sustained rate, and sizes the production calendar from the measured rate, not the target.

## 9.6 Presses And Compaction At Owner Scale

The press decision is the largest equipment decision in the production line. This section states the classes, the force arithmetic, and the documented owner routes; purchasing detail (vendors, quotes, shipping) is in the Tools, Equipment, And The Owner-Builder Shop chapter.

**The three press classes.** The manual lever press descends from the CINVA-Ram, designed by Raul Ramirez at the Inter-American Housing Center (CINVA), Bogota, in 1952. Roughly 80 pounds of hand force on a toggle lever is multiplied to a compaction pressure in the 2-7 MPa class on a small solid block (roughly 290x140x100 mm). The machine weighs 140 pounds, and a local welder can fabricate it from plate steel. Documented cured strength from that class is 200-500 psi. Its modern descendant, the Auram Press 3000, applies up to 15 tons through a lever-hydraulic mechanism at 30-40 blocks per hour (roughly 50 with an added vibrator). The mid-range manual machine class (Hydraform static models) raises cycle rate to 35-60 mold cycles per hour but needs a 5-8 person crew. The powered hydraulic class (AECT Impact 2001A, 7 HP diesel) reaches roughly 300 blocks per hour, with larger models to 480.

**The open-source tier.** Open Source Ecology's Liberator is a fully documented, freely downloadable hydraulic CEB press design: 6 full-size bricks per minute at 12.5 gallons per minute of hydraulic flow, or 16 per minute at 24 gpm in the automatic configuration. Power comes from OSE's Power Cube or a tractor's hydraulic take-off. Cost roughly $8,000 (2026) built or purchased from OSE, against commercial machines of comparable throughput at $45,000-$65,000 (2026); documented production cost 5-40 cents per block (2026) across 0-10 percent cement and $0-$25/hour labor. At 360-960 blocks per hour it is over-scaled for this project's target; its value here is as a reference design: open, dimensioned hydraulic press engineering to study or scale down.

**Owner-built presses.** Multiple documented plan sets and completed builds exist. Manual tier: the Charmaine Taylor CINVA-Ram plan set (dimensioned drawings), the original CINVA drawings in circulation, and a documented owner build with write-up on the permies forum; fabrication requires basic welding and plate work, per the original 1952 design intent. Hydraulic tier: a documented build around a 12-ton air-hydraulic bottle jack in a shop frame, a documented minimal rig at roughly $30 (2026) in parts, and the Liberator's full open plan set. No controlled study compares home-built against commercial block quality at equal force; the block responds to force, mix, and moisture, and the owner's own test cylinders verify whatever press is built.

**The force arithmetic that separates the classes.** Required force equals target pressure times mold face area. At the commercial 75 kg/cm2 benchmark, a CEB-class face (roughly 276 cm2) needs about 20 tonnes; a 15-ton lever press produces serviceable blocks at slightly lower pressure. A full CMU face (roughly 826 cm2) needs 60-plus tonnes, beyond any lever press and any ordinary shop press. The consequences:

- A manual lever press is physically limited to small-face solid block; it cannot make full-size structural CMU by static force.
- Full-CMU production at owner scale runs vibration plus a modest weighted head instead of raw tonnage.
- Bottle jacks (8-20 ton), shop H-frame presses (10-50 ton), and log-splitter cylinders (20-30 ton class, undocumented for this use but the same ram category) all serve the small-face route. No documented case exists of salvaging a commercial block-plant press; those presses are integrated into larger hydraulic-vibration systems and do not circulate as standalone units.

**Hand tamping.** The lowest-cost compaction route is a flat steel tamper plate on a handle roughly four feet long, the same tool the early-1900s block industry used. Compaction energy measurably changes block density and strength, and no published blow-count or energy standard exists for hand-tamped block; consistency is the method's documented weakness. Where hand tamping is used (trial batches, the bootstrap rig below), the weigh-a-block check in the curing-yard section substitutes an outcome standard for the missing process standard: tamp a test unit, weigh it against the target density, adjust.

**The historical precedent.** Harmon S. Palmer's cast-iron hand-tamp machine (patented around 1900; his Hollow Building Block Company was producing roughly 400 machines a year by 1904) founded the American block industry on exactly this chapter's process: a semi-dry mix, hand-tamped in a mold, demolded immediately. Sears sold its Wizard block machine to owner-builders at $57.50 (early-1900s catalog price). Rock-faced block buildings from those machines remain in service across North America. The historic form factor offers no compaction advantage over a modern steel mold with a press or vibrating table; its relevance is proof that owner-scale block production built lasting houses for decades.

**The bootstrap rig.** Before committing to any machine, the mix and method can be proven on a minimum rig assembled from this chapter's own findings:

- one well-fabricated steel or steel-faced mold with correct draft and a release agent
- a compaction source matched to a small CEB-class face: a hand tamper for first trials, or a bottle-jack press in the 8-20 ton class for consistent results
- a moisture routine: the hand-squeeze test calibrated by one real Proctor test on the actual soil
- a flat curing surface with a moisture cover applied within the first hour

Documented cost for the components runs from a few hundred dollars to the low thousands (2026). Test cylinders made on this rig and broken under ASTM C39 or D1633 remain valid evidence after a faster press arrives, because the tests measure the mix, not the machine. A hydraulic press of any size gets a positive mechanical stop under the platen and the other guards in the safety section before first use.

## 9.7 Molds: Design, Materials, And Service Life

**Simple**

The mold determines more than the block's shape. It determines whether the green block releases without tearing. It sets how true the finished wall lays up. It caps how many units a day one station can cycle. Three design facts govern every mold in this chapter. Every internal face needs a slight taper so the block releases. The face needs to be smooth, because a rough face grips the weak green block and tears it. And the mold wears; the design question is what wears and how cheaply it is replaced.

**Draft and face finish.** Dry-cast practice specifies a total taper of 1/8 to 1/4 inch on mold faces for immediate release; general concrete casting uses 5-15 degrees of draft, and shallow paver molds 1-2 degrees. The taper goes on every internal face, including core formers, not only the visible ones. Face finish controls strip friction: ground steel, HDPE, or a cast urethane liner releases a green unit that a rough or rusted face would spall at the edges. A documented release agent raises casting success materially; a sealed surface still gets one.

**Mold materials, compared.**

| Material | Release | Stability under vibration | Documented service | Relative cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Welded steel (A572 class) | best with release agent | no flex, no moisture movement | 10-year daily-use commercial claim | highest; welding required |
| HDPE (solid or facing) | low-friction, near-Teflon | rigid, no moisture uptake | no cycle count published | mid |
| Cast polyurethane rubber | purpose-made for concrete | flexible; needs rigid backing | industry standard for precast faces | mid |
| Sealed melamine / coated plywood | workable; sticks lightly | edges chip; wood swells | lowest tier; frequent replacement | lowest |

Commercial production molds are nitrided tool steel (surface hardness HV 800-1100, rated 100,000-150,000 cycles, roughly $200-$800 per cavity set for the hardening, 2026). A house-quantity run is a few thousand to low tens of thousands of cycles; the commercial hardening amortizes over multi-shift industrial service an owner never reaches. The owner-grade answer is mild steel with a replaceable wear liner (bolt-on steel, HDPE, or urethane at the contact faces): wear becomes a scheduled cheap part, not a mold replacement. Sheet-metal facing over a plywood mold body buys the same trade at the lowest fabrication skill.

**Cast rubber molds from a master.** A reusable rubber mold can be cast in liquid polyurethane around one master block. Polyurethane is the industry recommendation over silicone for concrete. It costs roughly half as much, offers harder grades (Shore A60 general purpose, up to D45 rigid), and cures faster (pot life 15-60 minutes, full cure about 16 hours). It also produces fewer efflorescence and color problems on the cast face. Its role is faces and textures; a rubber cavity still needs a rigid mold body behind it to take press force.

**Gang molds.** A multi-cavity mold multiplies units per cycle without multiplying operators; it also multiplies the ways a cycle jams. Rules from commercial practice:

- identical draft on every face of every cavity, because one binding cavity stops the whole strip
- a follower plate (the commercial pressure head) that pushes all units through together on the strip stroke
- vibration sized to the full loaded gang mass, mounted centrally or split across two smaller vibrators so near and far cavities compact alike

**Dimensional tolerance.** ASTM C90 allows finished units to vary 1/8 inch in width, height, and length (1/16 inch on molded features). A standard 3/8-inch mortar bed absorbs that class of variation course to course. A dry-stack or interlocking system has no bed to absorb error and needs unit-to-unit repeatability far tighter than C90, near the mold's own machining tolerance. An owner-built mold for mortared block targets C90's numbers; an owner-built mold for dry-stack is a precision-fabrication project.

**Interlocking and dry-stack molds.** Interlocking compressed-block molds add tongue-and-groove or peg-and-socket geometry, grout perforations, and more faces needing correct draft. Structural testing tempers the appeal: an otherwise-similar mortared assembly showed 46 percent higher flexural bond strength and 23 percent higher shear strength than the dry-stacked version of the same material. Dry-stack buys laying speed and lower skill demand at the cost of a harder mold and a documented bond penalty; this project's default is mortared units, and the Masonry Craft chapter states the laying consequences.

**Fair-face versus rendered runs.** For fair-face architectural units, the mold face is kept polished and the release agent is confirmed compatible with any later sealer, because release residue can interfere with coatings. For units that will be stuccoed, the mold finish matters little: render adhesion comes from etching or mechanically roughening the cured face (a dilute muriatic-acid etch is standard practice), not from a rough mold.

**Release schedule and dimension drift.** Conventional (petroleum or water-based) release agents are applied every cycle. Semi-permanent (wax or polymer) products last a documented 5 to 30-plus cycles between applications and suit a 100-unit day better than an every-cycle product. Either way, release buildup in corners and core formers acts as added material thickness: over a multi-week run it shifts unit dimensions and blurs face detail. The production routine pairs a semi-permanent release with a scheduled full strip (wire brush, pressure wash) and re-coat, on an interval set by tracking unit dimensions against the mold's nominals in the production log.

## 9.8 Mixing, Moisture Control, And Vibration

**Simple**

A zero-slump mix breaks the tool most people already own. An ordinary rotating-drum mixer works by gravity: the drum lifts wet concrete and lets it fall back on itself. A mix stiff enough to hold its shape does not fall back on itself. It rides the drum in a lump, unmixed. Stiff mixes need a machine that forces paddles through the material. This section specifies that machine, the water control that makes the mix pressable, and the vibration that packs it.

**Applied**

Set up the batching station around these rules:

1. Use a forced-action mixer: a pan mixer, or a horizontal-shaft paddle (mortar/plaster) mixer. Do not batch zero-slump mix in a rotating-drum mixer; the failure is categorical, not partial. A paddle mortar mixer shares the forced-shear mechanism and is the lower-cost, widely sold class. Confirm it on a small test batch before buying. No source documents paddle mixers on zero-slump block mix.
2. Size by consumption. At 30-50 blocks per hour of small CEB-class units (about 2.76 liters each), the press consumes roughly 83-138 liters of mix per hour. A small pan or paddle mixer in the 40-250 liter class feeds it with batches to spare. Buy the mixer used before fabricating one. Shafts, bearings, and drives are a harder build than any frame in this chapter.
3. Batch on a cadence, not in bulk. Dry-cast mix loses pressability as it sits; keep batches sized so the press consumes each one promptly. No published number fixes the sit-time window; establish it by observation and write it into the production log.
4. Check moisture on every batch with the hand-squeeze test. A correct handful squeezes into a firm ball without crumbling and without wetting the palm. Crumbling means too dry: the press cannot reach density. Oozing means too wet: the unit slumps at demold, and the extra water lowers cured strength. The test checks today's batch against a moisture target set by a real Proctor test. It does not replace that calibration.
5. Weigh pigment and admixtures per batch on a scale; scoop dosing produces visible batch-to-batch color and behavior shifts (appearance section).

**Professional**

Vibration tuning for stiff mixes runs opposite to wet-cast practice. Low frequency and high amplitude consolidate a zero-slump mix. High-frequency, low-amplitude energy only works the paste fraction. The commercial reference band is 25-50 Hz at the mold box. Internal poker vibrators are unsuited to stiff mixes (the mix cannot flow closed behind the head) and appear nowhere in documented block production. The two working formats are the vibrating table (one powered station serving a rotation of molds) and the clamp-on form vibrator (the vibrator stays with one mold). The choice is workflow, not physics.

A working table is a documented owner build. The parts: a motor with deliberately unbalanced (eccentric) mass on its shaft, a stiff top plate, and a spring suspension isolating plate from frame so the energy goes into the mold. Repurposed washing-machine motors are the documented budget route. Purpose-sold vibrator motors run 0.28-0.75 kW at 110 V; a cited 0.28 kW unit delivers roughly 2.9 kN of force. Commercial 12x24-inch tables run $800-$1,000 (2026); documented builds save most of that. Sizing is by eccentric moment (the unbalance mass times its offset, in kg-mm) against the combined mass of table plus loaded mold, not by motor kilowatts. An undersized motor buzzes at low amplitude and consolidates nothing. The practical owner route is a motor rated above the estimated need, verified by result. Vibration time for stiff mixes runs toward the long end of the cited 30-65 second range. Under-vibration, not over-vibration, is the realistic risk at zero slump. The commercial machines add a second vibration system on a weighted pressure head. A weighted follower plate resting on the mix during table vibration approximates that vibro-compaction combination at owner scale. The combination is mechanically sound and undocumented at owner scale; its gain is measured with paired test cylinders, not assumed.

Dust control for daily dry batching is a regulated exposure question, stated in the safety section: production-scale mixing does not qualify for the small-quantity exclusion in the silica rule.

## 9.9 Stabilized Earth: Compressed Earth Block And Cement-Stabilized Rammed Earth

**Simple**

Stabilized earth is site soil mixed with a measured percentage of cement, pressed at the right moisture, and cured like concrete. It is the oldest owner-made structural material in this chapter and the most studied. The same material takes two forms. Rammed in place between forms, it is a rammed-earth wall. Pressed in a mold and demolded immediately, it is a compressed stabilized earth block (CSEB). The pressed-block form is the more common one worldwide. The strength comes from three inputs together: how much cement, how close to the right moisture, and how hard the press compacts. Oregon adds a fourth requirement the dry Southwest does not. The mix must survive wet winters. The cement needed for that usually exceeds the cement needed for strength alone.

**Professional**

Form. Pressed CSEB demolds immediately by the same particle-packing mechanism as dry-cast concrete (standard dry-cast section); green blocks are handled gently to a curing surface and stacked only after days of cement hydration. Monolithic rammed earth moves the compaction into the wall form; its engineering precedent in Oregon is the Benton County house in the precedents section. Everything below applies to both forms; the block form is this project's production path.

Cement content against strength. Published soil-cement data scatter widely by soil, and the shape is consistent: strength rises with cement content, roughly monotonically. Representative published points:

- 1.77, 2.85, 4.79, and 7.61 MPa at 0, 5, 10, and 15 percent cement on one soil
- gains of 41, 80, and 92 percent over an unstabilized 1.15 MPa reference at 6, 8, and 10 percent on another
- 2.68 to 9.30 MPa at 9 percent on a 30-percent-clay soil
- 5-10 MPa reported across studies at around 10 percent cement

Blocks at 6 percent or more cement consistently cleared 2 MPa (about 290 psi) in the study confirming that band, with 7.5 percent reaching 2.48 MPa. For scale, the New Mexico earthen code's structural floor is 300 psi (2.07 MPa), and most published mixes at 5-7 percent cement reach it on reasonable soils. No literature percentage transfers to an untested soil: the project number comes from D1633 cylinders on the actual site soil, with the trial ladder below.

Moisture. Optimum moisture content comes from a standard Proctor test (ASTM D698): the soil is compacted at about five water contents, and the peak of the dry-density curve is the target. Density and strength fall on both sides of the peak. Too dry, the press cannot consolidate the mix at rated force; too wet, the block will not stand at demold and the press force is fighting water instead of closing voids. No universal field tolerance band is published; the hand-squeeze test tracks the target day to day once a real Proctor test has set it.

Compaction. Density and strength rise with compaction energy, and the effect compounds with cement content rather than substituting for it. The three delivery tools rank: hand tamper (lowest energy, operator-dependent), pneumatic rammer (more energy, more consistency, compressed-air logistics), press (highest and most repeatable force, the standard route for production block). No published table converts tool choice into strength on a common soil; a controlled comparison runs on the owner's soil with parallel D1633 cylinders.

Durability in Oregon. Wet-climate suitability is the governing criterion, tested under ASTM D559 (wetting-drying) and D560 (freezing-thawing); each program takes about a month. The durability-driven cement minimum commonly exceeds the strength minimum. Published wet-exposure markers:

- roughly 8 percent cement is the commonly cited threshold for water-resistant CEB
- cement-stabilized blocks measured 8.7-12.2 percent water absorption, against a 20 percent ceiling in the Brazilian standard NBR 8491
- wet strength after 24-hour submersion held above 33 percent of dry strength (the Heathcote criterion)

No published minimum is calibrated to western Oregon's wet, mild-freeze profile; the project number comes from running D559/D560 on the actual stabilized mix or from the engineer of record's exposure judgment.

Additives. Hydrated lime alongside cement extends long-term strength through a slow pozzolanic reaction with the soil's clay. Fly ash serves as a partial binder at doses up to primary-binder scale in some systems. Sodium silicate at 0.25-1.5 percent by weight increases block ductility (a more gradual failure mode); strength gains plateau above roughly 1.5 percent, so higher doses waste material. Short fibers at 0.25-1.5 percent raise toughness and crack resistance more than compressive strength. None of the four is documented to interfere with immediate demold at the cited doses.

**Applied**

Run the CSEB trial ladder before any production commitment:

1. Commission the Proctor test (ASTM D698) on site soil; record optimum moisture and maximum dry density.
2. Press trial batches at 4, 6, 8, and 10 percent cement at optimum moisture, one press routine throughout.
3. Break D1633 cylinders at 7 and 28 days; plot strength against cement content.
4. Send the leading mix to D559/D560 durability testing; the month it takes runs parallel to further strength trials.
5. Set the production cement percentage at the durability-passing content plus the engineer-agreed margin for owner-scale batch variation. Record the target unit weight for the daily weigh-a-block check.

## 9.10 Alternative Binders And Concretes

Beyond portland cement and stabilized earth, a real field of alternative binders exists, each with documented strengths and documented disqualifiers. This section states each candidate's mechanism, numbers, and standing for this project. All of them route through the same R104.11 test-to-prove path; none has a prescriptive residential code path. Handling hazards for the caustic and hot-process materials in this section are consolidated in the safety section.

**Geopolymer (alkali-activated fly ash and slag).** A geopolymer replaces cement with an industrial aluminosilicate powder (fly ash, ground slag, metakaolin) reacted with an alkali activator. The documented strengths are high: ambient-cured fly-ash/slag brick programs report 16.7-25.3 MPa and 30.5-51.7 MPa at 28 days across studies, above ordinary CMU targets. Slag content is the lever that removes the heat-cure requirement of pure fly-ash systems: at 30 percent slag in one mortar study, 3-day strength rose from 1.5 to 33.5 MPa and 28-day strength from 34.7 to 73.4 MPa. Two activator formats exist. The two-part route dissolves sodium hydroxide (10-14 molar in surveyed studies) with sodium silicate (silicate-to-hydroxide ratios roughly 1.0-3.0; a 1.6-2.3 band is reported best for durability; activator modulus around 0.8-1.2): a corrosive, exothermic liquid chemistry with real handling requirements. The one-part route pre-blends a solid activator so the binder mixes like cement, with lab strengths of 47.4-70 MPa at 28 days documented; no retail owner-scale supply of pre-blended one-part binder was found, so sourcing is a build-time supplier question. Pressing suits the chemistry unusually well: pressure-compacted geopolymer reached 114.2 MPa compressive and 29.9 MPa flexural at 28 days against 60.0 and 6.2 for the cast equivalent. The open problems for this project: efflorescence from unreacted alkali (mitigated by slag content and a lower-silicate activator, not eliminated), and no published long-term durability record in a wet temperate climate. A geopolymer unit is provable on cylinders like any other material here; its climate record would be this project's own.

**Lime-pozzolan and natural hydraulic lime (NHL).** Lime binders set and strengthen more slowly than portland cement; their documented returns are longevity, vapor permeability, and self-healing (the mortar consequences are in the mixes section). NHL grades map to 28-day strength bands under EN 459-1: NHL 2 at 2-7 MPa, NHL 3.5 at 3.5-10 MPa, NHL 5 at 5-15 MPa, with set speed and wet-exposure suitability rising with grade. Historic lime-pozzolan ratios (2 parts pozzolan to 1 lime for hydraulic work; roughly 1:3 for general mortar) produced a replicated strength of 9.49 MPa at 60 days in one modern study. A second replication reached about 4.6 MPa (670 psi) at six months. That timescale is the conflict: lime-pozzolan strength arrives in months, and the production line in this chapter demolds a unit in seconds and stacks it in days. A pressed lime-pozzolan block holds its green shape (compaction, not chemistry, holds it), and no source documents anyone running lime-pozzolan through a fast production line. The materially sound hybrid direction, unproven at any published dose, adds a small portland or slag fraction for early handling strength while the lime matures underneath; proving it is a test-cylinder program, not a literature lookup.

**Hot-mixed Roman-style lime concrete.** The 2023 Science Advances study of Roman concrete (Seymour et al., MIT/Harvard) identified hot mixing with quicklime as the source of embedded lime clasts. When water enters a crack, the clasts dissolve and reprecipitate as calcium carbonate, and the crack seals. The team reproduced the self-healing behavior in modern lab mixes made with ordinary commercial quicklime. The mechanism is real and reproducible. What does not yet exist is a structural mix design built on it and taken through a compression-test acceptance path; the researchers' own framing is future development. For this project it is a legitimate experimental track for non-critical elements, behind the quicklime hazards stated in the safety section.

**Sulfur concrete.** Elemental sulfur is melted at 130-140 C, blended with preheated aggregate, and cast; hardening is cooling, not hydration. Documented proportions run 12-30 percent sulfur by weight (a cited working mix is 20 percent sulfur, 32 fine aggregate, 48 coarse, with about 5 percent silica flour). Strength is high and fast: high mechanical strength within roughly 24 hours, with industry figures of 90 percent of ultimate in 6-8 hours and 40-70 MPa ultimate reported but not traced to a primary study. The material is fully remeltable, so scrap recycles. Modifiers (dicyclopentadiene or styrene at 1-5 percent of the sulfur) temper its brittleness. The disqualifiers for this project: the working temperature sits 10-20 C below the temperatures that generate toxic SO2 and H2S and the roughly 232 C ignition point (safety section), and no wet-climate freeze-thaw durability record was found either way. A hot-pour production line is a different and more hazardous operation than everything else in this chapter; the material is included here as a documented option, not a recommendation.

**Gypsum and anhydrite block.** Both set fast and demold fast, and both are disqualified for exterior structural use in a wet climate: gypsum composites swell, warp, and support mold under sustained moisture, and coatings mitigate without solving it. Every source found treats them as interior or dry-climate materials. Interior partition block is their remaining role here.

**Magnesium oxychloride (Sorel) cement.** Magnesia plus magnesium chloride solution sets fast and hard (documented above 50 MPa within hours). Its defining flaw has stood since 1867: the chloride phase is water-soluble, and immersion testing shows 50-70 percent compressive-strength loss after 28 days in water. Renders protect the surface without stopping the mechanism. Documented uses are interior flooring and fire-protection products. For an exterior wall in Oregon it is a no at the current state of the art.

**Cast-stone analogs.** True cast basalt is a 1,200-1,400 C industrial melt process, out of reach at owner scale. The achievable version of "owner-made stone" is the pressed geopolymer above, optionally with basalt powder as filler: basalt substitution is documented to raise density and cut alkali-activated-material cost by up to 80 percent against a pure-metakaolin formulation. Same press, same molds, same test path as the rest of this chapter.

**The Oregon exposure ranking.** No source ranks these materials against western Oregon's exposure directly; the ranking below is assembled from each material's own record:

| Standing for exterior structural use here | Materials | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Proven, standards-based | Portland dry-cast block; cement-stabilized earth at durability-tested cement content | C90/C140, D559/D560 frameworks |
| Documented wet-exposure route | Lime-pozzolan at NHL 5 grade class | EN 459-1 grade suitability for severe exposure |
| Unverified for this climate | Geopolymer; sulfur concrete | strong lab record, no wet-temperate field record |
| Unsuitable exterior | Gypsum/anhydrite; Sorel cement | documented moisture disqualifiers |

Materials in the unverified row require this project to generate the climate evidence itself; the engineer of record decides whether the exposure-class framework in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter accepts that evidence for a given element.

## 9.11 Cellulose, Bio-Aggregate, And Craft Concretes

**Simple**

This section states the strength record of the most asked-about materials. They are paper concrete, hemp concrete, earth-and-straw walls, seashell lime, and cement shells troweled over mesh. Each is real, documented, and useful somewhere. None of the first three carries a house wall. The strength numbers below state why, and the roles each one still fits on this property.

**Papercrete.** Re-pulped waste paper mixed with cement (and sometimes sand or clay) casts light, workable, insulating blocks. Documented compressive strength is 140-160 psi in most reports, up to about 260 psi, an order of magnitude below loadbearing masonry targets. Documented practice has carried roofs on low buildings using foot-thick, rebar-pinned papercrete walls, and no US code-path validation of that use was found. Its role here is non-structural: garden structures, infill, sculptural work. One permitting anecdote about papercrete classified as adobe appears in the precedents section.

**Hempcrete.** Hemp hurd bound with lime casts an insulating infill with typical compressive strength around 0.3 MPa (reported range 0.03-1.22 MPa; roughly 4-45 psi). Structural candidacy is cited at 3-5 MPa, which conventional hempcrete does not approach; ordinary structural concrete runs 20.7-34.5 MPa. Its documented role is deliberate: insulating infill within a structural frame, finished with vapor-permeable lime render. In this project's wall system the exterior insulation layer is specified in the Envelope chapter; hempcrete competes for that role only as infill, never as structure.

**Cob.** Monolithic hand-formed earth and straw now has a prescriptive Oregon path: Appendix AU, with cube-compression minimums of 60 psi (walls) and 85 psi (braced wall panels) and a 50 psi modulus-of-rupture beam test. Cob is the one material in this section that can legally bear residential loads without an R104.11 case, inside the appendix's own limits. The appendix detail and its use as precedent are in the earthen codes section.

**Tabby (lime-shell concrete).** The historic coastal method routed here from the Designing For Centuries chapter: oyster shell (over 90 percent calcium carbonate) burned above roughly 900 C yields lime, which is mixed with water, sand, ash, and whole shell as aggregate. The regional supply question has real Oregon candidates: Oregon Oyster Farms on Yaquina Bay (operating since 1907) and Netarts Bay Oyster Company on 66 acres of leased tidelands both generate shell as a processing byproduct. Whether either sells or gives away shell, in what quantity, at what cost, is unconfirmed; the next step is a direct inquiry to the farms. As a structural material tabby has no test record here; its first use here is a garden or yard element, proven (if the owner wants more) through the same cylinder path as everything else in this chapter.

**Ferrocement.** A ferrocement shell is cement-rich mortar hand-plastered over layers of mesh and reinforcing wire. Its documented economics: for water tanks, sources report 15-20 percent cost savings against conventional reinforced concrete and a lower skill requirement, with far less material in the thin wall. The documented context is largely non-US construction where the two-person trowelling skill is learned inside the project; no modern Oregon-labor cost comparison exists. For this property its natural application is the cistern (sized and sited in the Water Independence chapter). A water-retaining structure is proven by tightness, not compression. The ACI 350.1-class test fills the tank and measures water level at intervals (a cited protocol reads every 12 hours for 3 days) with an evaporation control. Reported acceptance thresholds: 0.1 percent of volume per 24 hours for general water structures, and 0.05 percent per 24 hours for prestressed tanks under the AWWA D110/D115 standards. The exact figures come from purchased standards; the lab or engineer administering the test confirms them.

## 9.12 Mixes, Ingredients, And Admixtures

**Simple**

Every additive in this section changes one property, and none is free. Fibers change how a block breaks, not how much load it takes. Accelerators buy early strength and can cost steel corrosion. Pozzolans trade early speed for later strength. The entries below state what each ingredient does, the working dose, and the trap. Doses come from published studies and product literature; the final dose for this project's mix always comes from its own test cylinders.

**Steel fibers.** Dose 20-50 kg/m3 (0.25-0.75 percent by volume), hooked-end geometry; the hook anchors each fiber and bridges cracks. Fibers raise flexural toughness, impact resistance, and post-crack capacity, measured under ASTM C1609/C1399. Their documented effect on the ASTM C39 compression number is minimal. The trap: adding fiber expecting a higher reported compressive strength for the permit file; the permit number will not move.

**Bendable concrete (ECC).** Engineered cementitious composite replaces coarse aggregate with fine mortar and about 2 percent specialty polymer fiber, reaching tensile strain capacity of 3-7 percent against roughly 0.01 percent for ordinary concrete. The documented cost driver is the fiber: local PVA fiber around $8 per kg against $28 per kg imported (2026), the highest-cost common fiber class. Its documented applications are seismic elements (coupling beams, joints, repair overlays); no residential owner-scale use is documented. If the engineer of record wants ECC anywhere in this seismic design, that is an engineering specification, not a production-line default.

**Natural fibers (sisal, jute, coir).** Dose 0.25-1.5 percent. Untreated cellulose fiber degrades in cement's alkaline pore water; studies report treated sisal improving compressive strength 21.75 percent (pozzolan treatment) versus 12.83 percent (alkali treatment) over untreated. Treatments: alkali soak, thermal treatment at 150-200 C, or calcined-bentonite coating. A lower-alkalinity matrix (lime-pozzolan) is a plausible, untested pairing; no side-by-side durability comparison exists.

**Accelerators.** Calcium chloride at 1-2 percent of cement weight (2 percent practical ceiling) is the low-cost route and the wrong default here. Chloride drives corrosion of embedded steel, and this project's loadbearing block program includes rebar dowels and bond-beam steel per the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter. Above the ceiling it also adds shrinkage and creep. Non-chloride accelerators (calcium nitrate, calcium formate class) dose at roughly 0.5-2 percent or 10-45 fluid ounces per 100 pounds of cement per product literature, cost more, and carry no corrosion penalty. Project rule: non-chloride wherever steel is present; calcium chloride only in genuinely unreinforced elements. Exact dose comes from the chosen product's current data sheet.

**The early-strength stack.** Three levers speed demold-to-stack strength through separate mechanisms and combine: ASTM C150 Type III cement (finer grind, faster early hydration), a lower water-cement ratio (less capillary porosity), and a warmer mix (faster reaction kinetics, the same principle steam curing exploits). Precast practice stacks all three routinely. No published study isolates each lever's contribution; the combined curve for this project's mix comes from its own 1-, 7-, and 28-day cylinder breaks.

**Supplementary cementitious materials.** Fly ash and ground-granulated blast-furnace slag fit a zero-slump press mix without raising water demand, and slag additionally serves the geopolymer ambient-cure role stated earlier. Silica fume and metakaolin are poor fits: their fineness raises water demand, exactly what a dry-cast mix cannot absorb without losing green strength. That transfer is reasoned from wet-cast behavior; no dry-cast-specific study was found.

**Integral water repellents.** The commercial reference system (DRY-BLOCK) is sold as a matched pair by requirement: an admixture in the block and a matching admixture in the mortar. A repellent unit laid in an ordinary wicking joint concentrates water and efflorescence at the joint line, and the reverse pairing fails the same way. Any owner-formulated repellent system inherits the same rule and would need its own paired testing.

**Mortar for owner-made units.** The controlling principle from ASTM C270 practice and National Park Service preservation guidance: mortar equal to or softer than the unit, never harder, so the joint remains the sacrificial, repointable element. The common inversion is over-specifying Type M or S mortar (or a cement-rich field mix) on a softer unit, which forces movement and moisture stress to crack the block instead of the joint. For owner-made block the mortar type is selected against the block strength the test program measured, not a catalog default. Natural hydraulic lime mortar adds documented properties portland mortar lacks: free lime that re-crystallizes across hairline fissures (self-healing), higher vapor permeability than any portland-bearing mix, and reported immunity to sulfate attack. The costs are slower strength gain and longer moist-cure protection; grade selection (NHL 2/3.5/5) sets that trade. Joint execution and repointing practice are in the Masonry Craft chapter; the century-scale repointing logic is in the Designing For Centuries chapter.

**Crystalline waterproofing admixtures.** Commercial brand formulations are proprietary; the chemistry is published. Active silicates and carbonates react with free lime in the pore system to grow crystals that block capillaries. One published research formulation names calcium carbonate, sodium silicate, glycine, tetrasodium EDTA, and sodium acetate: a testable starting recipe for an owner-chemist trial under the alternative-materials path. Matching a brand product's performance is not assumed; it is measured with a permeability or self-healing test against a control batch.

**Pigments** are dosed and batched in the appearance section; their only mix-side rule is weighed dosing and a fixed water ratio, because water content is the largest documented driver of color variance.

## 9.13 Curing For Fast Turnaround

**Simple**

A demolded block has its final shape but almost none of its final strength. Compaction holds it together; the cement chemistry that gives it strength runs for weeks and needs water the whole time. Curing is keeping that water in. The production question is narrower: how soon can a block be stacked, and how soon can it be laid? Stacking comes days in; the standard strength report comes at 28 days. Heat speeds the chemistry, and heat past a known ceiling causes a delayed internal failure. The schedules below state both.

**Applied**

Run the default (unheated) cure this way:

1. Cover every green unit with plastic sheeting or a moist tarp within the first hour after demold. Uncovered green block loses surface water to wind and sun and crazes.
2. Hold damp conditions for the first 3-7 days; that window develops the bulk of early strength. Re-wet on a schedule tuned to the cover and the weather; the surface never dries visibly.
3. Stack only when a unit demonstrably carries the stack. The field check is handling behavior plus the weigh-a-block record, within the first week, not a fixed calendar day.
4. Treat 28 days as the reporting benchmark for lab strength. Units are commonly laid before 28 days; the lay decision runs on the task-specific strength the engineer accepts, not the report date.

**Professional**

Accelerated curing options, with their documented parameters:

Atmospheric steam or hot-box curing follows the four-phase precast schedule. The delay period runs roughly 2-5 hours, never less than 30 minutes; steam before initial set degrades final quality. The ramp is limited to 20-40 F (11-22 C) per hour; faster risks thermal-shock cracking. The hold sits at 60-82 C, and the cool-down is gradual. One optimization study found a 4-hour delay plus an 8-hour hold at 60 C performed best; sources place the onset of later-age strength loss at hold temperatures above 65-70 C. Handling strength in 8-12 hours is documented for steam-cured product. The industry ceiling that governs everything: concrete temperature not above 150 F (65 C) unless delayed-ettringite countermeasures are in place. No complete owner-scale hot-box design (insulation, steam source, humidity control) is published; the schedule transfers, the enclosure is this project's own fabrication.

Delayed ettringite formation (DEF) is the failure the ceiling guards against: sulfate phases suppressed by early heat re-form later in the hardened, moist concrete and crack it from inside (mechanism in the Designing For Centuries chapter). Thresholds across sources: unstable above roughly 70 C, with specifications capping at 65-80 C. A single small block sheds hydration heat through its high surface-to-volume ratio and does not approach those temperatures on its own; DEF enters this production line only when deliberate heat does. The monitoring instrument is a metal-probe thermometer rated to at least 250 F in the core of the hottest unit of the batch, with 65 C as this project's working ceiling.

CO2 carbonation curing adds documented strength and speed at industrial scale: roughly 15 percent compressive gain at 7 days and 28 percent at 28 days over non-carbonated references, with CO2 uptake near 23 percent of cement mass. The process needs units preconditioned to 50-60 percent relative humidity. The documented caveat: carbonation consumes pore water, and without a post-carbonation water spray the 28-day strength can fall below an ordinarily cured reference. No owner-scale carbonation chamber is documented anywhere; bottled CO2 into a sealed box is the controllable version of the concept, combustion exhaust the uncontrolled one. This is an experiment with a real basis, run only alongside a proven default cure.

Chemical acceleration (Type III cement, non-chloride accelerators, warm mix water) is specified in the mixes section and combines with any of the above.

## 9.14 The Curing Yard: Handling, Racks, And Field Quality Control

**Simple**

The yard is where blocks spend their first weeks. A good press run is preserved or ruined there. Three things run at once. Weak young blocks get moved gently. A daily check confirms production still holds the tested standard. A written record ties every block to its batch and its test. The yard's location and shelter are planned in the Developing The Site chapter. This section is what happens inside it.

**Applied**

Handle green units by these rules:

1. Set each demolded unit down gently on a flat, debris-free surface; never drop or slide it. A green unit is held together by compaction friction and hours-old chemistry.
2. Do not re-handle a unit in its first days except to cover it. Every extra pickup in that window is a crack risk.
3. Move units on their rack or pallet, not by hand, wherever possible. Press to curing rack to near-wall staging is the whole intended path. Each added transfer costs one lift-and-carry per unit across thousands of units; the Owner-Builder's Body And Mind chapter accounts for that body budget.
4. Protect edges: level supports, no point contact, no leaning stacks.

Run the daily field QC loop:

1. Weigh one unit per batch at a consistent point after demold. Compare it against the target unit weight recorded from lab-passing specimens. A unit meaningfully light for its mold volume is under-compacted: more voids, less density, less strength, no lab test needed to know it.
2. Check dimensions against the mold nominals on a sampling basis. Dimensional drift catches mold wear, release-agent buildup, and press misalignment, failures the scale misses.
3. Check batch moisture by the hand-squeeze test before pressing, per the mixing section.
4. Set the reject thresholds with the engineer from this project's own trial data: the weight band and dimension band that correlated with passing lab tests. No published reject percentage exists to borrow. A batch outside its band stops the line until the cause (moisture, mold, press) is found.
5. Apply the visual accept/reject line from the C90 specification, stated in the Code block below.

Keep the production record, per batch:

- date
- mix proportions and material sources
- operator
- weather and cure conditions
- unit count and lot assignment
- weigh-a-block and dimension results
- dated photographs

For every lab specimen, record chain of custody: which batch, which lot, and which wall location that lot serves. File lab reports with the standard each was run to. This is the record format the acceptance sections rely on. It matches the fabricator-certification detail the Pima County earthen standard requires: units molded, sizes, mortar volume, fabrication dates, test results.

**Professional**

The lot-based sampling program is routed here from the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement chapter; that chapter's cylinder program is the placed-concrete counterpart. Production is divided into lots. Each lot is a stated quantity or production period under one unchanged mix design. Each lot is sampled and tested under ASTM C140 against the C90 criteria (or D1633 for soil-cement units against the engineer's specified strength). Two code-anchored frequency models exist. Appendix AU requires five samples per required test per material lot. Pima County ties test frequency to constructed quantity, with a certified lab comparing field density against the pre-construction Proctor baseline. This project's proposed program, subject to the building official's conditions under the acceptance path: every lot is tested early in production, and a reduced frequency is negotiated once consecutive lots pass with margin. A mix change, a soil-source change, or a press change starts a new lot and resets the frequency.

Yard equipment, from the documented record. Ground transport needs wheel diameter of 13-16 inches (325-400 mm) in solid rubber or pneumatic tires; standard warehouse dollies carve into soft ground under load and are unsuited to a rural yard. Pallets under green block: wood absorbs moisture, swells, and needs replacement roughly annually in production service, and steel rusts and needs refinishing. Glass-fiber-reinforced plastic (GMT) pallets run roughly $3-$8 each in bulk (2026) and are reported to serve up to 20 years, the durability match for a wet-season Oregon yard. No owner-scale curing-rack design is documented; the commercial principle scales down directly. Racks sized to hold a full pallet load, moved as a unit by cart or pallet jack, make the rack the pallet; the transfer count stays at press-to-rack, rack-to-staging.

## 9.15 Appearance: Color, Texture, And Face Options

**Simple**

Because the owner batches the mix, the face is a production decision. Color costs pigment, not a manufacturer's architectural premium. Texture costs a mold liner. The limits come from the dry-cast process itself: a mix with no slump takes shallow patterns, not deep relief. This section states what the process can and cannot do to the visible face, and how to keep color consistent across a hundred small batches.

**Integral color.** Dry mineral-oxide pigment doses at 2-8 percent of cement weight; most saturated colors land at 5-6 percent, and doses above roughly 8 percent add cost without adding depth. Consistency across a production run is a batching discipline:

- pigment weighed per batch; pre-weighed single-batch packets rather than scooping
- one cement, sand, and aggregate source held for the whole run
- a fixed, measured water quantity per batch (water content is the largest documented driver of visible color variance; drier is darker and more consistent)
- at least about 10 minutes or 100 drum revolutions of mixing after pigment goes in

Integral color runs through the full unit: chips, cuts, and future drilled holes expose the same color. Painted or stained faces cost less pigment and become a maintenance item, and every future chip or patch shows gray unless refinished. Bulk iron-oxide pigment is a commodity priced by the pound; current pricing needs a supplier quote (Solomon Colors and Davis Colors are the reference suppliers). No published account documents pigmenting a small-press block operation; the dosing guidance above transfers from wet-mix practice and gets confirmed on this project's own trial batches.

**Texture from the mold face.** Ground steel gives the flattest architectural face. Rubber or urethane liners reproduce shallow, repeatable patterns; patent literature on dry-cast block states the process supports only relatively shallow texture, because compaction pressure and mix stiffness prevent deep detail. A rough-sawn board liner leaves a shallow grain impression (and wears and swells faster than steel or bonded rubber). Deep board-form relief and sharp negative detail belong to flowable wet-cast work and are not available to this process.

**Face mix and exposed aggregate.** Commercial architectural block places a thin decorative face layer and a structural backup mix in one press cycle through a second hopper and a purpose-built facing shoe. Without that hardware, the owner substitute is hand-placing a face layer in the mold before the body fill. That step is workable, untested for how well decorative aggregate survives compaction in position, and slow enough per unit to pull output below the 100-a-day target. A face-mix program is a deliberate product line with its own cycle time, not a free option.

**Slump block.** The Southwest slump-block look (soft, sagged, adobe-reading edges) is manufactured by the opposite process: a wetter mix, pressed again on ejection so the unit deliberately sags before set. Running this project's press wet enough to slump sacrifices immediate demold and green stackability, the properties the whole line depends on. Slump units are achievable as an occasional separate run with their own mix and their own slower schedule.

**Choosing uniformity or variation.** Soil and pigment sources shift color across a long production run. Both outcomes are available on purpose: blend and homogenize a large stockpile before batching for a uniform run, or stockpile and batch by source lot and lay the resulting color bands deliberately by course or elevation. A wider appearance-acceptance band also mechanically lowers the rejection rate and its cost; the structural accept/reject line (C90 defect limits, curing-yard section) does not widen with it. The choice is the owner's; the stockpiling decision has to precede production, not follow it.

## 9.16 Safety In The Block Yard

**Simple**

Four hazards in this chapter go beyond ordinary construction risks. Fine silica dust comes off daily dry batching. Lye and quicklime burn skin and eyes. Molten sulfur gives off toxic gas if overheated. Hydraulic presses store crushing force. Each has a known control.

**Silica dust at production scale.** OSHA's respirable crystalline silica rule (29 CFR 1926.1153) excludes "mixing small amounts of mortar" performed in isolation. A 100-unit-a-day batching operation repeated daily is neither small nor isolated, and this project treats it as a covered task. Controls, from the rule's own Table 1 principles:

- pre-moistened or wet batching rather than dry-dumping bagged cement
- exhaust ventilation for any enclosed mixing station
- the station oriented so prevailing wind carries dust away from the operator
- a properly rated respirator worn for daily production mixing

The wet-batch step and the respirator habit go into the production routine from the first trial batch.

**Alkali activators (sodium hydroxide, sodium silicate).** Both are corrosive above pH 13, and dissolving solid NaOH is strongly exothermic. Handling rules:

1. Add the solid slowly to water, never water to the solid, in a ventilated space.
2. Let the solution cool before it meets the precursor.
3. Wear chemical splash goggles plus a face shield, chemical-resistant gloves rated for caustics (ordinary nitrile is not sufficient), and protective clothing.
4. Read the purchased product's current safety data sheet before the first batch, and keep an eyewash source at the mixing station.

**Quicklime.** Slaking quicklime releases roughly 3.54 megajoules per 3.1 kilograms reacting with a liter of water; concentrated reactions approach 370 C, hot enough to burn skin and ignite nearby combustibles. NIOSH lists calcium oxide as causing severe eye damage on contact and respiratory-tract injury from dust. Respirator selection steps up with airborne concentration; a particulate respirator of the N95 class covers the low-exposure band. Slaking rules:

1. Never add water to a bulk mass of quicklime in a closed or poorly ventilated container.
2. Keep combustibles clear of the slaking station.
3. Wear splash goggles, caustic-and-heat-rated gloves, and a rated dust respirator.
4. Keep an eyewash or quick-drench source at hand before the first slake.

**Molten sulfur.** The working window (130-140 C) sits 10-20 degrees below the temperatures that generate harmful SO2 and H2S (above roughly 149 C), and within reach of the roughly 232 C ignition point. A sulfur fire produces gases that form sulfuric acid in moist air. The controls are continuous temperature monitoring (not a one-time check), fume extraction, and hot-material burn protection. This chapter's standing instruction: no sulfur-concrete melting on this property until an industrial-hygiene professional or the sulfur supplier's safety documentation has reviewed the specific setup.

**Hydraulic presses.** Two rules from hydraulic practice: no body part goes under a load the operator could not lift unassisted, and a suspected high-pressure oil-injection injury (a pinhole leak through skin) is a medical emergency regardless of how minor it looks. An owner-built press gets a positive mechanical stop (a pin or block, not hydraulic pressure) preventing platen drop, and a fabricator's review of its guarding before first use. No published guarding standard exists for owner-built block presses; the review substitutes for the missing standard.

## 9.17 Problems And Solutions

**Simple**

Almost every production failure traces to one of four inputs: water, compaction, the mold, or the cure. The table below maps symptom to cause to fix. Each fix names the section with the full procedure.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Unit crumbles at demold | Mix too dry, or compaction short of target | Re-check moisture against the squeeze test and Proctor target (mixing section); raise compaction energy (presses section) |
| Unit slumps or bulges at demold | Mix too wet | Cut water back to the calibrated target; verify the w/cm window (dry-cast section) |
| Edges tear or spall on the strip stroke | Mold friction: rough face, missing draft, spent release agent | Polish or line the face, verify taper on every internal face, renew release (molds section) |
| Surface crazing within the first day | Green unit left uncovered | Cover within the first hour; hold damp through day 3-7 (curing section) |
| Units run light on the daily weigh check | Under-compaction; vibration undersized (buzzing without consolidating) | Check press force and routine; size vibration by eccentric moment, not watts (mixing section) |
| Laitance or segregated faces | Excess water, or prolonged vibration of a wet batch | Fix the water first; vibration duration follows (mixing section) |
| Unit dimensions drift over weeks | Release-agent buildup or mold wear | Scheduled strip-and-recoat; replace wear liners (molds section) |
| White deposits on cured units (portland mix) | Moisture moving soluble salts to the face | Control curing moisture path; for walls, match repellent admixtures in block and mortar as a pair (mixes section) |
| White deposits (geopolymer) | Unreacted activator alkali migrating | Raise slag fraction, lower activator silicate modulus (alternative binders section) |
| Color shifts batch to batch | Scooped pigment, floating water content | Pre-weighed pigment packets, fixed measured water (appearance section) |
| Cracked units found in the stack | Stacked too early, or point-contact supports | Delay stacking until the handling check passes; level supports, no leaning stacks (curing-yard section) |
| 28-day results fall below trial-batch results | Cure lapse, mix drift, or an unlogged input change | Audit the batch log; a soil, cement, or press change starts a new lot with full testing (curing-yard section) |
| Strength loss months after a heated cure | Cure temperature exceeded the 65 C ceiling | Thermometer in the core unit every heated batch; hold the ceiling (curing section) |

**The two invisible failures.** Two failures in the table leave no sign on the day they happen. A cure-temperature excursion (delayed ettringite) shows up as cracking long after the wall is built; the only defense is the logged thermometer reading. An unlogged mix change breaks the lot system: the test report on file no longer describes the units in the wall. The production log is the fix for both. That is why the record format in the curing-yard section is not optional paperwork.

## 9.18 Practice Structures And Site Uses First

**Simple**

The first thing built from owner-made block should not be the house. Oregon exempts small non-habitable structures (roughly 200 square feet, walls up to 10 feet) from permits. The first one or two projects are free practice: real walls, real weather, no approval risk. Each practice structure also becomes acceptance evidence. The material stands as a real wall, two winters old, with its production log and test reports on file.

**The sequence, and what each step proves.** No published source validates an order; this project's sequence follows ordinary construction logic. A garden or site wall comes first: no roof load, no attachment to anything occupied, removable without consequence. It proves the unit itself: dimensional consistency across a real run, mortar behavior, color, and a first exposure season. A woodshed base or similar low loaded element comes second: a real foundation interface and sustained load on the mortar bond. A small roofed enclosure (a generator or pump shed) comes third: openings, lintels, roof anchorage, and the base and head moisture details the house will scale up. The deliberate version of this strategy (building the permit-exempt structure specifically to precede the house application) has no documented precedent anywhere in the research. Nothing prevents it, and its cost is only the practice structure this program builds anyway.

**Site walls and retaining walls.** For a low, non-critical site wall, a dry-stacked interlocking system would remove mortar labor and skill, at the cost of a second unit geometry and press tooling alongside the house units. Using the same mortared unit stream the house program already produces keeps one mold set, one mix design, and one skill curve. A retaining wall is a different exposure, not a shorter house wall: saturated backfill on one face, lateral soil and water pressure, and freeze-thaw cycling while damp. The house-wall test package (compression, absorption, shrinkage) does not cover that case by itself. The relevant basis is the wet-exposure evidence in the stabilized-earth section: the roughly 8 percent cement water-resistance threshold and wet-strength retention testing. Whether a county requires engineering below Oregon's 4-foot unengineered retaining threshold is confirmed with that county. Unreinforced mass elements (a gravity garden wall, a root cellar, a well house) are the one class where owner-made material can sidestep the reinforcement-corrosion failure mode entirely. The design logic is in the What Concrete Uniquely Enables chapter; sizing any specific element unreinforced is the engineer's call.

**Openings in outbuilding walls.** A 10-foot-class door in an owner-made CMU outbuilding wall takes any of the three standard lintel routes: a steel angle, a fabricated beam, or reinforced grouted lintel block (U-shaped units carrying rebar and grout). Which one, and its bearing on each jamb, is a structural calculation from the actual wall height, loads, and seismic demand. Practitioner practice suggests not less than 4 inches of bearing per jamb as a starting point. For owner-made units the calculation runs on the tested strength of this project's block, not a catalog value. The Structural System And Concrete Design chapter owns the engineering.

## 9.19 The Oregon Acceptance Path: R104.11 In Practice

**Simple**

One person holds authority over this whole program: the local building official. The rule sets no checklist and no deadline. Approval is per project and per jurisdiction. The official may condition it on testing before and during construction. The working strategy is early contact, with the kind of evidence commercial producers generate. That evidence lets the official approve on the same basis used for commercial products.

**The evidence package.** No statewide checklist exists; R104.11.1 sets only the floor (the official may require tests, at no cost to the jurisdiction). The package this project prepares mirrors commercial quality control:

- a plain description of the material, process, and where it goes in the structure
- the engineer of record's design built on the tested values
- the written testing program: which standards, the lab, the lot definition, and the sampling frequency (curing-yard section)
- ASTM C140 test reports from a laboratory evaluated per ASTM C1093 (the evaluation standard C140 itself points to)
- mortar and grout specified per ASTM C270 and C476
- the production log format and, where the official wants it, periodic third-party observation accepted under R104.4

Scoping the submission by component (unit, mortar and grout, assembly, foundation connection) mirrors how the code itself divides them, and lets the official approve each part as its evidence lands. No documented Oregon case establishes either scoping practice for owner-made block, so the scoping question goes on the pre-application agenda.

**Who counts as an approved agency.** Oregon publishes no definition for R104.4 purposes. The credible default is a lab evaluated per ASTM C1093; whether a given official also wants formal accreditation (A2LA, IAS) is the official's discretion. Two lower-cost routes are plausible readings of R104.4's "agencies or individuals," and both are undocumented in Oregon practice. One is a university or community-college civil-engineering materials lab running the compression tests. The other is a licensed PE witnessing tests on the owner's own press and certifying the results. Both cost one phone call each to check before the testing budget is set; neither is assumed in this project's plan. Cost anchors, in the absence of published totals: structural-engineering time runs $100-$220 per hour, single site visits $150-$1,000, residential engineering averages near $550 per engagement (2026); commercial lab testing is quoted per specimen against the project's actual specimen count.

**Ongoing conditions.** The documented pattern where jurisdictions have formalized earthen or alternative-material QA is testing tied to production, not one approval. Oregon's Appendix AU embeds five samples per test per material lot; the Pima County earthen standard requires construction-phase density testing against the pre-construction Proctor baseline. An Oregon official holds the authority to impose the same shape of condition on an R104.11 approval, and this project's proposed sampling program is written so the official can adopt it rather than invent one. A voluntary structural-observation arrangement with the engineer of record (scheduled visits at defined stages, independent of any code trigger) is ordinary professional practice and a further confidence lever, priced by the visit figures above.

**The pre-application meeting.** What goes to the first meeting: the material description, photographs of a real demonstration batch (or the standing practice structure), and the testing-program outline framed as a proposal. What waits for the formal submission: final mix proportions, finished calculations, and the fully bound QC manual. Those represent investment that follows the official's first reaction, not precedes it. An invitation to see the press, the curing yard, and the practice structure in person is undocumented as Oregon practice and costs nothing to extend. Whether volunteering the full bound manual at the first meeting shortens review is unmeasured anywhere; this project treats it as a judgment call made in the room.

**Routes this project does not lead with.** An ICC-ES evaluation report is built for manufacturers selling across jurisdictions: unpriced by published schedule, tied to hearing cycles, and out of proportion for one house when R104.11 exists. A formal deferred-submittal agreement (trusses-style) is a commercial-code mechanism (IBC 107.3.4.2) with no named ORSC equivalent; phased review happens only at the official's discretion, and the plan does not assume it. The statewide route is a Building Codes Division alternate-method ruling (application per OAR 918-008-0095), binding in every Oregon jurisdiction once issued. No such ruling has ever issued for an earthen or owner-manufactured masonry material; pursuing one would be a first-of-its-kind proceeding with no published timeline. The mechanism and its published rulings are described in the Oregon Code And Compliance Map chapter (Statewide Alternate Methods).

**The scoped-down fallback.** If unit acceptance stalls, the middle path scopes the R104.11 request to a non-structural role: owner-made block as veneer or infill on a conventionally engineered concrete or CMU frame. Every earthen code in the precedent record (Appendix AU, New Mexico, Pima County) treats veneer as a separately scoped, less demanding case than a bearing wall, with Pima County publishing veneer-specific thickness and anchorage rules. The fallback keeps the material's appearance and sourcing benefits, moves the structural burden to the engineered frame, and converts the acceptance question from novel to routine. Its cost against the all-conventional and all-alternative cases is unpriced and depends on the frame design.

**Code**

```
A statewide alternate method ruling approves a method for use in every Oregon jurisdiction.
  Application to the Building Codes Division per the division's alternate-method process; the relevant advisory board recommends, the division rules; local officials must allow an approved method within the ruling's limits.
  Source: OAR 918-008-0095; OAR 918-008-0110.
```

## 9.20 Earthen Codes, Standards, And Precedents

**Simple**

Four published rule sets already state numbers for earthen walls. They are Oregon's cob appendix, New Mexico's earthen code, Pima County Arizona's standard, and New Zealand's earth-building standards. None of them governs an Oregon CEB or rammed-earth wall directly. Together they give an engineer and an official shared numbers: allowable stresses, test protocols, height tables. This section states what each contains and what stands built in Oregon already.

**Professional**

Oregon Appendix AU (in force). Adopted into the ORSC effective October 1, 2023, covering cob (monolithic adobe) specifically. It contains material test protocols (the cube and beam values in the test-method section) and the five-samples-per-test-per-lot rule. Its scope permits cob as infill within another structural frame as well as within its own prescriptive limits. It does not govern CEB or rammed earth. Its significance for this chapter is precedential: a current Oregon code text already states what evidence Oregon accepts for an earthen material, and a CEB/CSRE submission modeled on its test-and-lot structure speaks the reviewing official's own language.

New Mexico 14.7.4 NMAC. The longest-standing US earthen code: adobe, burned adobe, CEB, rammed earth, and terron for one- and two-family dwellings, with a 300 psi unit-strength floor. Its prescriptive tables are built on a two-story maximum and a Seismic Site Class D1 basis; rammed-earth walls over 24 inches thick are treated as self-buttressing. Written for lower seismic demand than western Oregon carries.

Pima County, Arizona earthen standard. The most usable numbers in the precedent record, read in full for this project. Table R614.4:

- allowable compressive stress 30 psi (gross section), against a required 300 psi unit compressive strength
- modulus of rupture 50 psi
- shear 8 psi with special inspection, 4 psi without
- modulus of elasticity 60,000 psi

Wall height and parapet tables run by actual thickness (10-24 inches) and site seismic Sds band (0.00-0.50), with bond-beam reinforcement tabulated per combination. Cement-stabilized rammed earth must reach 95 percent of maximum dry density (ASTM D698) and exceed 300 psi (ASTM D1633) at 14 days. Its stated scope is Seismic Design Category A-C and basic wind speed 90 mph or less; much of western Oregon sits in D0-D2, above that scope, so its tables inform an Oregon design without covering it.

New Zealand NZS 4297, 4298, 4299. The high-seismic country's earth-building standards, revised 2024 and available as free downloads from Standards New Zealand. NZS 4297 carries explicit seismic engineering (a stated h/150 elastic deflection limit), and the 2024 revision removed unreinforced earth buildings from scope on seismic-vulnerability grounds, a decision directly relevant to any Oregon earthen design. Whether its values map onto Oregon's Seismic Design Category D0-D2 parameters is an engineer's reading of the full text. No Pacific Northwest engineer is documented having adapted the NZS method for a US project; the named firms below are the people to ask.

ASTM E2392/E2392M. A design guide for earthen wall systems (adobe, rammed earth, cob, cast earth), not a test method: it points to other standards and engineering judgment and contains no allowable-stress tables. It is unlikely to satisfy R104.11.1's test-evidence clause on its own, and no official is documented accepting it as such. Confirmed edition E2392/E2392M-10(2016); text paywalled.

**What stands built.** The strongest documented Oregon case: the Benton County rammed-earth house (owner-builder Dave Wills, structural engineering by PSE Consulting Engineers, reported 2017 as the county's first permitted rammed-earth home). Documented facts: earth from a nearby quarry with a small percentage of portland cement, pigments, and an acrylic sealer; 2x2-foot poured concrete footings with rebar extending up into the earth walls for seismic anchorage. Reported wall design strength is 2,000 psi at 2 feet thick. The reported permitting posture: the county went along with the design because a licensed engineer had stamped it; Oregon had no earthen code text at the time. Engineering and testing costs and the application-to-approval timeline are not in the public record. In the wet Pacific Northwest more broadly, EarthDwell (Washington) builds stabilized rammed earth commercially. Its published climate rule matches what this project's Envelope and Wall System chapters state in their own domains: a wall detailed for Arizona fails in a few wet Pacific Northwest winters. Overhang depth, drainage slope, base height above grade, and sealer selection are set for local rainfall, not copied from a dry-climate design. EarthDwell's published permitting expectation list: structural calculations, material testing documentation, energy compliance data, and fire-rating information. (One correction to earlier project notes: Root Down Designs is a South Carolina firm, not a Pacific Northwest precedent.)

**County track record, and its limits.** Benton County holds the documented rammed-earth approval. Coos County hosts the Cob Cottage Company (founded 1993, the organization credited with the North American cob revival), with no publicly documented permit history for its structures. A forum-era account of Douglas County refusing cob permits predates Appendix AU and is out of date at the state-code level regardless of county practice. The current posture of Douglas, Klamath, Coos, and Deschutes counties toward earthen and owner-made masonry is confirmable only by direct present-day contact with each building department. That contact is a stated dependency of this project's siting and permitting plan; no forum thread substitutes for it. The Northwest EcoBuilding Guild's Code Innovations Database (documented, successfully permitted alternative systems in the region) is on point if it remains maintained; its current accessibility is unconfirmed. One widely repeated anecdote (a papercrete building approved after the official classified the clay-rich mix as adobe) could not be verified to a jurisdiction. The reclassification logic it illustrates runs stronger for cement-stabilized earth, which is compositionally adjacent to the soil-cement materials existing earthen codes already regulate.
