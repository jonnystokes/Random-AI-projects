# Chapter 3: Land, Site, And Feasibility

This chapter documents the evaluation of a parcel before a purchase contract becomes binding. Most items here are questions, not answers, because the answers depend on the specific parcel. The goal is to know what to ask, what to verify in writing, and what should make you walk away.

The house design itself works across a wide range of Oregon climates and terrain. Insulation thickness, snow load, foundation depth, and drainage strategy all scale to the site. This chapter identifies which end of each range applies, and whether a given lot can support the project at any reasonable cost.

Four other chapters contain the follow-on work. The soil chapter contains the geotechnical work for a parcel under serious consideration. The legal chapter contains title, ownership structure, and the owner-builder rules. The water independence chapter contains the design of wells, cisterns, storage, and treatment; this chapter contains the water questions that decide a purchase. The site-development chapter contains the shop, block-yard, and outbuilding layout in full. This chapter contains the early screening; the buyer uses it to decide whether a parcel justifies that further work.

## 3.1 The Feasibility Filter

Before making an offer, or during the inspection period after one is accepted, sort every issue into a severity band.

| Result | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Dealbreaker | A core requirement is missing or legally blocked | Walk away, or make the contract contingent on the issue being resolved before closing |
| Expensive risk | The parcel remains possible, but the fix is costly or uncertain (for scale: a $20,000-$50,000 alternative septic system) | Price the risk, add written contingencies, get agency or contractor answers in writing |
| Design constraint | The parcel works if the house adapts to it | Carry the constraint into the site plan and the budget |
| Lifestyle tradeoff | The parcel works, but daily life changes | Decide whether the tradeoff fits how the household wants to live |
| Future-flexibility check | The parcel supports the initial build but may block later plans | Check outbuilding allowances, accessory-dwelling rules, work-area limits, access |
| Strong candidate | Land, utility, access, septic, water, zoning, and title all pass | Move toward design with the site facts in hand |

Answer the dealbreaker questions first: septic, water, legal access, zoning, and title. Any of them can end the project. Climate, drainage, topography, and distance are usually design or lifestyle questions.

The dealbreaker band, from the table above, contains five conditions:

- no legal dwelling path under the zoning
- a failed septic evaluation with no alternative system that fits
- no legal access to a public road
- no usable water source
- a recorded restriction that prohibits residential use

## 3.2 Finding The Parcel

**Simple**

Rural parcels sell through more channels than the national listing sites. Some parcels change hands before any public listing appears. This section lists the channels, what each costs, and the price data that anchors an offer.

**Applied**

Work the channels in parallel rather than one at a time:

1. Interview land-focused brokers for the target region before signing anything. Since the 2024 National Association of Realtors settlement, buyer-side compensation is a negotiated item in a written buyer-representation agreement, not an automatic seller payment. Land-sale commissions run roughly 5-10% of price (2026), split or allocated by agreement.
2. Check the Accredited Land Consultant directory (rliland.com) when the search needs water-rights, timber, or resource-zoning depth. The ALC designation requires 104 hours of coursework plus a transaction record; an ALC charges an ordinary negotiated commission, not a separate fee.
3. Search the county assessor and GIS portals directly for absentee and inherited-land owners. Assessor tax-roll data, including owner mailing addresses, is public record in Oregon, and the requester's purpose is not grounds for denial. Confirm any bulk-export conditions and fees with the specific county assessor.
4. For direct-mail outreach to absentee owners, plan on the response rates the investor-training industry documents: roughly 1-3% response, on the order of 300-500 pieces mailed per closed acquisition. Those figures come from house-focused campaigns, not rural-land campaigns; no land-specific dataset exists.
5. Monitor the USDA Farm Service Agency resales portal (properties.sc.egov.usda.gov/resales) and BLM Oregon/Washington disposal announcements. Both channels are intermittent; treat them as background monitoring, not a primary path.
6. Check regional forums and groups at search time: the permies.com real-estate forum hosts recurring Oregon land threads, and county-specific homesteading groups form and dissolve continuously. Activity levels change too fast to name a reliable list in advance.
7. For a forested parcel, order a timber cruise (a field inventory of species, sizes, and merchantable volume) before pricing the offer: $8-$15 per acre with a $300-$500 practical minimum for small tracts, commonly $300-$2,000 total (2026).

**Professional**

Price anchors. Oregon State University Extension publishes county-level farmland values from USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service surveys: 2025 statewide figures of roughly $8,000 per acre for irrigated cropland, $3,000 for non-irrigated cropland, and $1,080 for pastureland. Every county assessor publishes real-market value (RMV) by tax lot; RMV is not a listing price, but it is a maintained, zoning-aware baseline for sanity-checking an asking price. Closed-sale comparables are recorded in the regional MLS, reached through a licensed broker or appraiser.

Two Oregon-specific brokerages with documented regional focus: The Whitney Land Company (Pendleton, operating since 1970, concentrated in Umatilla County and northeast Oregon wheat, ranch, and recreation land) and Oregon Farm and Home Brokers (rural and farm properties, with stated water-rights, soils, zoning, and timber expertise). Mossy Oak Properties is a national franchise; its strength in any Oregon county depends on the local franchisee.

Timber-company land moves in bulk institutional transactions, not through standing individual-buyer disposal programs. A documented example: Weyerhaeuser's 2020 transactions acquired roughly 85,000 acres in mid-coastal Oregon while selling roughly 149,000 acres in southern Oregon to Hancock-managed funds. Isolated inholdings, when sold, reach the market through ordinary land brokerages; there is no list to join.

A confirmed, transferable water right raises a parcel's market value, but no controlled Oregon study isolates the premium; current water-right property listings average roughly $3,993 per acre across dissimilar parcels, which is an asking-price average, not a matched comparison. A parcel's enrollment in farm or forest tax deferral does not reliably discount its asking price; sellers price on market potential, and the rollback exposure is the buyer's to quantify (the special-assessment section below).

The timber cruise result converts to dollars through a stumpage price (the price paid for standing timber). The Oregon Department of Forestry historically published quarterly price reports but now relies on a subscription log-price service for its own appraisals; a consulting forester supplies current stumpage figures. A buyer who will not harvest prices standing timber as a reason to counter below asking; a buyer who will harvest prices it as a near-term asset.

No documented account fixes a typical search-to-closing timeline for a project with this one's requirements; the search runs in months to years, and the resource-zoning confirmation steps below are usually the slowest part.

## 3.3 Climate And Design Loads

Oregon spans coastal, valley, mountain, and high-desert climates. The design loads change substantially across that range, and permit drawings need parcel-specific values.

**Simple**

Cold, heat, snow, and wind become numbers in the design. So do earthquake load, rain, and frost depth. The same house plan needs more insulation in the mountains, deeper footings in freezing ground, and a stronger roof under mountain snow. Where the lot sits sets the numbers.

**Applied**

Collect these values for the candidate parcel before finalizing the structural design:

- winter design temperature (the outdoor low the heating design uses)
- summer design temperature (the outdoor high the cooling design uses)
- ground snow load
- roof snow-load method (flat or sloped, with shape coefficients)
- basic wind speed and exposure category
- seismic design category
- frost depth or frost-protection requirement
- annual rainfall pattern
- elevation and exposure
- hazard-zone designations, a separate category set by maps and local adoption rather than by climate: whether the jurisdiction has adopted the wildfire-hardening code for the area, and floodplain status where applicable

The Oregon Building Codes Division runs a Design Criteria Hub with jurisdiction-level values where the jurisdiction participates. The OSU/SEAO snow-load tool (snowload.seao.org) gives site-specific ground snow loads and is the source the code itself points to. Save each answer, its source, and the date in the project binder.

Two public datasets supply parcel-specific climate inputs once a candidate is chosen. NOAA's U.S. Climate Normals (the 1991-2020 thirty-year normals at ncei.noaa.gov) list monthly temperature and precipitation for every long-term station. The PRISM Climate Group at Oregon State University (prism.oregonstate.edu) interpolates the same variables for parcels far from any station. Three design calculations use these inputs: the driest consecutive-month stretch that sizes rainwater storage (the water independence chapter), a multi-year drought margin read from the U.S. Drought Monitor's historical series (droughtmonitor.unl.edu), since Oregon's 2020-2022 drought ran below-normal for consecutive wet seasons, and the masonry weathering index, the product of freezing-cycle days and winter rainfall that classifies brick exposure severity (the durability engineering chapter). The exact ASTM C216 weathering-index category boundaries are stated in the purchased standard; the calculation itself waits on a chosen parcel and its nearest station.

The values below are early planning ranges, useful for first sizing and first conversations. They are not permit values.

| Condition | Early planning range across Oregon |
|---|---|
| Winter design temperature | -10 °F (high elevations east of the Cascades) to 25 °F (Willamette Valley) |
| Summer design temperature | 85 °F (coast) to 105 °F (high desert) |
| Ground snow load | 15 psf (valley lowlands), 25-50 psf (rural hills), 100-300+ psf (mountain elevations above roughly 3,000-4,000 ft) |
| Basic wind speed | 95-115 mph in most of Oregon; higher near the coast and in the Columbia River Gorge |
| Seismic design category | Most of western Oregon is SDC D0, D1, or D2 (Cascadia Subduction Zone); eastern Oregon is largely SDC C |
| Frost depth | 12-18 inches in most valleys; 24-36 inches in cold mountain zones |
| Annual rainfall | 15-20 inches east of the Cascades; 60-100+ inches on the coast and west slopes |

**Professional**

The structural design needs these site values:

- ground snow load
- roof snow load
- basic wind speed
- exposure category
- seismic design category
- flood hazard
- frost protection

The envelope and mechanical designs add outdoor and indoor design temperatures, humidity and solar assumptions, rain exposure, and wildfire-smoke assumptions where they apply.

For a concrete house, these load effects must close into one permitted design:

- gravity
- lateral load
- uplift
- sliding
- overturning
- foundation demand

Added wall mass increases several of those loads. Flat concrete roofs add design questions that do not arise in wood-framed construction: snow drifting, ponding instability, and parapet detailing.

**Code**

```
Every dwelling must be designed for its site's loads.
  The prescriptive provisions key off the climatic and geographic design table the local jurisdiction adopts; the values are local, never statewide.
  Source: ORSC R301 and Table R301.2(1) (2023 edition).

Wind design uses mapped speed plus an exposure category.
  Exposure B (suburban, wooded), C (open terrain), or D (open water, flat smooth terrain); special wind regions on the coast and in the Columbia Gorge can exceed 130 mph design speed.
  Source: ORSC R301.2.1.

Seismic design category sets the reinforcement rules.
  SDC D0, D1, and D2 (most of western Oregon) trigger expanded reinforcement for foundations, walls, and diaphragms; BCD publishes a how-to guide for determining SDC.
  Source: ORSC R301.2.2; oregon.gov/bcd.

Ground snow load comes from the state lookup tool.
  The code refers snow design to the OSU/SEAO site-specific lookup; roof snow load is then calculated with shape, exposure, and thermal coefficients.
  Source: ORSC R301.2.3; snowload.seao.org; ASCE 7.

Flood-hazard areas have their own construction rules.
  Foundation, elevation, and venting requirements differ inside Special Flood Hazard Areas as mapped by FEMA and adopted locally.
  Source: ORSC R322; msc.fema.gov.

Wildfire-hardening construction applies only where the local jurisdiction adopts it.
  The 2025 legislature repealed the statewide wildfire hazard map; ORSC R327 (Class B roof minimum, ember-resistant vents, ignition-resistant walls, protected eaves and decks, tempered glazing) now applies only in jurisdictions that adopt it by ordinance for designated areas, and only to new construction. The applicant confirms in writing with the local building department whether R327 applies to the parcel. A concrete-walled house satisfies the wall requirement outright.
  Source: ORSC R327 (amended January 1, 2026); SB 83 (2025), Oregon Laws 2025 chapter 590.
```

## 3.4 Zoning And Allowed Use

Zoning is the legal classification on the parcel. It controls allowed use, lot dimensions, setbacks, height, accessory uses, and the review path. Zoning failures are dealbreakers. Building design cannot cure a zoning failure, and a building permit cannot override zoning.

**Simple**

Every piece of land has rules about what can be built on it. Some land allows a home with an ordinary permit. Some requires special approval. Some does not allow a home at all. Zoning confirmation comes before committing money; building design cannot change zoning.

**Applied**

Before making an offer, contact the planning department directly. Identify the parcel by address, map number, or tax lot, never by description. Confirm in writing:

- whether a primary single-family residence is allowed outright, by conditional approval, or not at all
- whether the parcel meets the minimum lot size for its zone
- the front, side, rear, stream, road, and resource setbacks
- whether those setbacks leave room for the house, driveway, septic, well, drainage, and staging
- the maximum building height
- the maximum lot coverage
- whether outbuildings are allowed, at what size and count
- whether an accessory dwelling unit (ADU, a second smaller home) is allowed, and under which rule
- whether home occupations or home-based businesses are allowed
- whether the jurisdiction has adopted the wildfire-hardening code (ORSC R327) for the area
- any limits on outdoor storage, equipment, animals, farming, or exterior work areas

Save the planner's name, the date, the parcel identifier they confirmed against, and the written answer. A verbal answer in passing is not a basis for a purchase decision.

**Professional**

Common rural Oregon zone families, ranked by typical fit for an owner-built single-family residence:

| Zone family | Typical posture | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Rural Residential (RR-2, RR-5, RR-10, RR-20) | Often the cleanest target | The number is the minimum lot size in acres. Primary residence typically allowed outright. ADUs, home occupations, and outbuildings often permitted with limits. |
| Mixed Use Agricultural (MUA-10, MUA-20) | Often workable | County-dependent. Often allows a primary residence outright alongside farm and timber uses. Douglas County uses MUA zones widely. |
| Farm-Forest (F1, F2) | Conditionally workable | Usually more review than RR zones. County-dependent. |
| Non-Resource Rural Lands | Workable when present | Rare designation. Tends to be flexible. |
| Exclusive Farm Use (EFU) | Restrictive | A residence usually requires qualifying as a farm-income parcel, meeting template-dwelling criteria, or another defined exception. Second dwellings often prohibited. The highest-risk zone family for this project. |
| Urban residential (R1, R2) | Workable but constrained | Small-lot rules. May not fit the testing-space, outbuilding, and privacy goals of this project. |

Land-use rules determine what may be built on the parcel, and their review comes before building-code review. Zoning, overlays, resource-land rules, setbacks, access rules, floodplain rules, and home-occupation limits can each reshape the project before the first footing is drawn.

This project needs more than a house on the lot, so several ordinary-looking items require specific verification:

- long driveways and equipment delivery routes
- staging areas for materials, formwork, and equipment
- outbuildings: workshop, garage, storage, equipment shed
- testing space: compaction tests, concrete-mix trials, block-press operation if it happens
- material storage: rebar, block, lumber, sealants, insulation
- any future business use under the home-occupation rules

A parcel that allows a residence does not automatically allow all of that. Each item is confirmed in writing before the budget and design depend on it.

Accessory dwelling law splits by location. ORS 197.312 requires cities and counties to allow at least one ADU per detached single-family dwelling, but only inside an urban growth boundary, in a zone that already allows detached single-family dwellings. Outside a UGB, on Rural Residential land, Senate Bill 391 (2021) gives counties the option, not the obligation, to allow one rural ADU: capped at 900 square feet of usable floor area, on a parcel of at least 2 acres with exactly one existing dwelling, and located no farther than 100 feet from that dwelling. A county may restrict further but may not expand past those limits. On EFU or forest land, a second dwelling is not an ADU question at all; it is a separate farm-dwelling or nonfarm-dwelling approval (the next section). The buyer confirms with the county planning department whether SB 391 has been adopted locally.

MUA and RR zones are county-created districts, not statewide statutory zones; permitted uses, outbuilding counts, and home-occupation standards come entirely from the county's own ordinance. The statutory home-occupation caps of ORS 215.448 apply by their own terms only in EFU, forest, and mixed farm-forest zones; a county may allow a home occupation in any zone on its own locally set terms. Whether a specific county's MUA or RR ordinance permits a small manufacturing operation, a block press among them, distinct from an ordinary home workshop, is answered only by that county's ordinance text.

Non-resource-land designations exist where a county has shown land meets neither the farm nor the forest definition. DLCD confirms several counties have adopted such zoning but publishes no list. The Southern Oregon Regional Pilot Project (Douglas, Jackson, and Josephine counties, 2012-2016) attempted a joint non-resource framework and ended without consensus. A non-resource zone can add review layers a plain RR zone lacks, because Goal 5 (habitat), Goal 6 (carrying capacity), and Goal 7 (hazards) findings still apply; the county's own ordinance sets the burden. New rural-residential exception areas carry a 10-acre minimum parcel floor under the Goal 14 rule.

Parcel facts are public: county and city GIS portals list parcel identity, the zoning layer, tax lot, overlays, mapped constraints, and contours, without account creation. DLCD administers the statewide planning goals behind the restrictive rural zones and publishes explanations of them (oregon.gov/lcd).

**Code**

```
Counties control rural land use under state framework law.
  County comprehensive plans and zoning, the EFU framework, template-dwelling criteria, and resource-land rules all sit under this chapter.
  Source: ORS chapter 215.

Cities control urban zoning under a parallel framework.
  Incorporated areas plan and zone under city authority.
  Source: ORS chapter 227.

The local development code contains the actual rules.
  Zoning district map, allowed uses, setbacks, conditional uses, home-occupation rules, ADU rules, overlays, and the review path, adopted under ORS 215 or 227.
  Source: the county or city comprehensive plan and development code.

ADUs are mandatory only inside urban growth boundaries.
  One ADU per detached single-family dwelling must be allowed in single-family zones within a UGB; the mandate does not reach rural land.
  Source: ORS 197.312.

Rural ADUs are a county option with fixed statutory limits.
  Where adopted: 900 square feet maximum usable floor area, parcel of 2 acres or more with one existing single-family dwelling, ADU within 100 feet of that dwelling.
  Source: SB 391 (2021); the adopting county's ordinance.

New rural-residential exception areas have a minimum parcel size.
  A new rural residential exception area takes a 10-acre minimum lot size, with clustering allowed; pre-existing exception areas keep the county's smallest minimum in effect on October 4, 2000, where 2 acres or larger.
  Source: OAR 660-004-0040.
```

## 3.5 Dwelling Paths On Farm And Forest Land

**Simple**

On farm and forest zoning, the right to build a house is not automatic. Oregon law defines a short list of dwelling paths. Each path has exact tests: income, soil class, parcel dates, acreage, or the neighborhood's existing pattern. A parcel that fits no path cannot legally hold a dwelling. The purchase price has no effect on path eligibility. The buyer confirms a path before any design work starts.

**Applied**

Before an offer on EFU or forest-zoned land:

1. Identify which dwelling path the parcel could qualify under, using the tests in the Professional block below.
2. Pull the parcel-creation date and the full deed chain. The nonfarm-dwelling path requires a parcel created before January 1, 1993. The lot-of-record path requires acquisition by the present owner before January 1, 1985, or by inheritance from such an owner. A title company or a county deed-records search proves both dates.
3. For the template path, pull the parcel's soil productivity class first from the NRCS Web Soil Survey (free); where the county review will demand more, hire a consulting forester ($8-$15 per acre, $300-$500 minimum, 2026) for an on-the-ground determination. The Oregon Department of Forestry's own guidance puts NRCS soil survey data first when it is available.
4. Draw the 160-acre template square on county GIS, centered on the tract centroid, and count qualifying lots and dwellings as of January 1, 1993. Treat the count as a screen; only the county's formal review settles it, and only lawfully created lots count.
5. Search the parcel's land-use file for recorded conditions from any earlier partition; a recorded condition can bar further dwellings regardless of everything else.
6. Request a pre-application meeting with the county planning department, and get the path determination in writing before the contingency period ends.
7. Treat any answer of "probably" as a dealbreaker until it becomes a written "yes."

**Professional**

Five statutory paths govern nearly every new dwelling on EFU or forest land.

**Farm dwelling (income test).** ORS 215.283(1)(e) permits a dwelling "in conjunction with farm use." The income thresholds are set in administrative rule, not the statute: gross annual farm-product sales of at least $40,000 on land not identified as high-value farmland, or $80,000 on high-value farmland. The qualifying schedule, fixed by ORS 215.279: the income earned in each of the last two years, in three of the last five, or as the average of the best three of the last five. A separate large-tract alternative (160 acres non-rangeland, 320 acres rangeland, or a county capability table) uses no dollar test. The current rule text contains no $2,500 or $20,000 threshold.

**Nonfarm dwelling.** ORS 215.284 requires findings on three points. The dwelling must force no significant change in accepted farm or forest practices nearby. The parcel must have been created before January 1, 1993. Area land-use stability must hold. The soil test splits by region. In the Willamette Valley, the parcel must be predominantly Class IV through VIII soils. Those soils must not, when irrigated, rate as prime, unique, Class I, or Class II. Elsewhere, a "generally unsuitable land" functional test applies (terrain, drainage, vegetation, tract size and location). A parcel may not be called unsuitable solely for its size or location if it could serve farm or forest use in conjunction with other land.

**Lot-of-record dwelling.** ORS 215.705 allows a dwelling where the lot was lawfully created and acquired by the present owner before January 1, 1985. Acquisition by devise or intestate succession from such an owner also qualifies. The tract must hold no existing dwelling. High-value farmland limits apply. Remaining portions of the tract consolidate into a single parcel as a condition of approval.

**Large-tract forest dwelling.** ORS 215.740: 160 contiguous acres in western Oregon, 240 in eastern Oregon. An owner of non-contiguous forest-zone tracts in the same or adjacent counties may combine acreage to 200 acres (western) or 320 (eastern). The combination requires recording nonrevocable deed restrictions barring any future dwelling on every counted tract.

**Template dwelling.** ORS 215.750 qualifies a small forest parcel by the surrounding development pattern inside a 160-acre square centered on the tract's centroid (the tract's geometric center point). Where the tract abuts a pre-1993 road, a one-mile by quarter-mile rectangle aligned with the road replaces the square. The lot-count tiers rise with soil productivity, in cubic feet of wood fiber per acre per year. Western Oregon: soils of 0-49 need 3 other pre-1993 lots, 50-85 need 7, above 85 need 11. Eastern Oregon: 0-20, 21-50, and above 50, with the same 3, 7, and 11 tiers. Every tier also requires at least three dwellings existing on those other lots on January 1, 1993. The case law is active and strict. Only lawfully created lots count toward the template (Reeves v. Yamhill County, 53 Or LUBA 4 (2006)). A county must show its centroid methodology (Eng v. Wallowa County, 79 Or LUBA 421 (2019)). A property-line adjustment made after January 1, 2019 cannot be what qualifies the parcel (ORS 215.750(5)(g), further amended in the 2025 session). The current wording gets a recheck at application time.

No state statute caps the size of the primary farm or forest dwelling itself. The statutory square-footage caps apply to other categories: a family-forestry accessory dwelling at 900 square feet (ORS 215.757) and guest-ranch lodging under its own limits (ORS 215.461). A county ordinance can still add a local size or siting cap; the applicant confirms with the county planning department.

Parcel assembly has a formal step. Combining ownership of adjacent parcels does not, by itself, make their combined acreage count toward a dwelling threshold. The county requires consolidation into a single lot or parcel through the property-line-adjustment process. Only after consolidation is the assembled land treated as one qualifying unit (Marion County's published practice under ORS chapter 215; the partition section below).

Denial patterns to screen against, all from published LUBA cases:

- an overlay zone raising the effective minimum far above the base zone: an 80-acre deer-winter-range minimum defeated a split of a 41-acre parcel (ODFW v. Klamath County, 66 Or LUBA 92 (2012))
- a recorded condition from a decades-old partition barring further division (Woods v. Wallowa County, LUBA No 2021-064 (2021))
- a road-improvement exaction across the full length of the access road, upheld under rough proportionality (Dudek v. Umatilla County, 42 Or LUBA 427 (2002))
- a failed suitability showing on a nonresource partition (Newsome v. Clackamas County, 27 Or LUBA 578 (1994))

DLCD's biennial Farm and Forest Land Use Report tabulates approved dwellings by county; it is not confirmed to track denials. Most denials are never appealed, so the county planning department is the only source for a local approval-versus-denial picture.

A block-making operation is not farm use. ORS 215.203(2)(a) defines farm use around crops, livestock, and related agricultural activity; manufacturing concrete masonry units falls outside it at any scale. On EFU land the operation fits only the home-occupation category (ORS 215.448; OAR 660-033-0130(14)), under four statutory conditions:

- operated by a resident or a resident's employee
- no more than five full-time or part-time on-site employees
- operated substantially within the dwelling or other buildings normally associated with permitted uses
- no unreasonable interference with permitted uses

ORS 215.448(3) bars the county from authorizing any new structure the zone would not otherwise allow, so a purpose-built production building fails on that point. The controlling case rejected a purpose-built structure for a paying-events home occupation (1000 Friends of Oregon v. Clackamas County, 81 Or LUBA 664 (2020)). Event and operation staff count toward the five-employee cap regardless of who hires them (Green v. Douglas County, 63 Or LUBA 200 (2011)). The phrase "clearly secondary to the agricultural character of the property" is not statutory text. "Clearly subordinate" tests and square-footage caps are local-code creations that differ by jurisdiction (1,000 square feet in Clackamas County, 600 in Island City, from the published cases). The owner-made-blocks chapter contains the production-scale planning; the legal chapter contains the permit process.

**Code**

```
A farm dwelling on EFU land takes a gross-income test.
  $40,000 in gross annual farm-product sales (non-high-value farmland) or $80,000 (high-value farmland), earned in each of the last two years, three of the last five, or the best-three-of-five average.
  Source: OAR 660-033-0135(3)(a), (4)(a); ORS 215.283(1)(e); ORS 215.279.

A nonfarm dwelling takes soil, date, and stability findings.
  Willamette Valley: parcel predominantly Class IV-VIII soils, not prime, unique, Class I, or Class II when irrigated; all regions: parcel created before January 1, 1993, no forced change to accepted practices, no material change to area stability.
  Source: ORS 215.284.

A lot-of-record dwelling turns on the acquisition date.
  Lot lawfully created and acquired by the present owner before January 1, 1985, or by devise or intestate succession from such an owner; no existing dwelling on the tract; remaining tract portions consolidated.
  Source: ORS 215.705(1).

A large-tract forest dwelling takes fixed acreage.
  160 contiguous acres (western Oregon) or 240 (eastern); non-contiguous combination to 200 or 320 acres with recorded nonrevocable no-dwelling deed restrictions on all counted tracts.
  Source: ORS 215.740.

A template dwelling counts neighbors inside a 160-acre square.
  3, 7, or 11 other lots existing January 1, 1993 (tiered by soil productivity: western 0-49, 50-85, over 85 cubic feet per acre per year; eastern 0-20, 21-50, over 50), plus at least 3 dwellings existing on those lots; square centered on the tract centroid, or a 1-mile by 1/4-mile road-aligned rectangle.
  Source: ORS 215.750.

A post-2019 property-line adjustment cannot create template eligibility.
  Any property-line adjustment to the lot or parcel after January 1, 2019 must not have had the effect of qualifying it for a dwelling under the template statute.
  Source: ORS 215.750(5)(g) (amended, Oregon Laws 2025 chapter 38, section 41).

Home occupations in resource zones have statutory caps.
  Resident or resident's-employee operator; five or fewer on-site employees; operated substantially within the dwelling or buildings normally associated with permitted uses; no new structure not otherwise allowed in the zone.
  Source: ORS 215.448; OAR 660-033-0130(14).

Farm use is defined by statute, and block production is outside it.
  "Farm use" is the current employment of land primarily to profit from crops, livestock, dairying, and listed related uses.
  Source: ORS 215.203(2)(a).
```

## 3.6 Measure 49 Authorizations And Rezoning Notice

**Simple**

Some rural parcels have a recorded Measure 49 authorization. That is a state order allowing home sites the current zoning would otherwise prohibit. The order transfers with the land, but a later owner faces a deadline the original claimant never had. What is allowed is stated in the order document, not in the listing description.

**Applied**

For any parcel whose value rests on a Measure 49 claim:

1. Open the Measure 49 Analyzer (hub.oregonexplorer.info, DLCD-funded). Search by election number, claim name, or by clicking the parcel where the county shares tax-lot data.
2. Download the Final Order and Home Site Authorization for the claim. Read the authorized counts (dwellings, parcels, home sites) and every condition. Do not assume a generic one-dwelling outcome.
3. Establish the completion clock. There is no deadline for the original claimant. A subsequent owner must complete the development within 10 years, and the clock starts at the first transfer after the claimant, not at each later transfer.
4. Confirm the trigger date for the specific chain of title: transfer into a revocable trust or to a spouse does not start the clock; the trust turning irrevocable at the claimant's death does; a claimant death after the final order starts a 10-year clock at death. A claimant who died before December 6, 2007 passed no right at all.
5. Have the title company confirm the order's recording, and have an attorney review any old or unusual claim. No documented case invalidates an authorization bought in good faith by a later owner; the review confirms the specific claim's validity, conditions, and clock.

**Professional**

Measure 49 (2007) replaced Measure 37 relief with transferable home-site authorizations under ORS 195.300 to 195.336. "Owner" for clock purposes means the fee title holder, the purchaser under a recorded land sale contract, or the settlor of a revocable trust. Most Measure 49 determinations are not land-use decisions, which keeps them outside LUBA's ordinary jurisdiction and narrows the paths for later challenge. Separately, Measure 56, approved by voters in November 1998, created the rezoning-notice duty now codified for counties at ORS 215.503. The duty: individual mailed notice when a proposed ordinance would rezone property or limit a previously allowed use. The statute prescribes the notice form down to boldfaced warning language. The parallel city statute is ORS 227.186.

**Code**

```

  Development must be complete within 10 years, measured from the first ownership transfer after the original claimant; later transfers do not restart the clock.
  Source: ORS 195.300-195.336; OAR chapter 660, divisions 029 and 041; DLCD Measure 49 FAQ (2023).
```

## 3.7 Property Rights, Title, And Recorded Restrictions

Buying land does not automatically buy everything on it, under it, or leading to it.

**Simple**

The trees, the minerals underground, and the water can each be owned separately from the land. So can the right to drive across a neighbor's road. Old restrictions recorded decades ago can still control what gets built. These conditions appear in the title report (a legal document from a title company listing what the property legally includes). That report is read in full before the purchase closes.

**Applied**

Before closing, and before the title contingency expires, review the preliminary title report and the recorded property records for each item:

| Right or restriction | What to verify |
|---|---|
| Access easements | Legal and physical access from a public road; who maintains the access |
| Utility easements | Routes, widths, limits on planting or building over them |
| Road maintenance agreements | Cost-sharing rules, responsibility, dispute mechanisms |
| Mineral rights | Whether oil, gas, and hard-rock rights come with the surface |
| Timber and forestry rights | Whether standing timber and future cut rights transfer |
| Water rights | Surface, spring, and some groundwater rights are separate legal rights in Oregon |
| Riparian rights | Rights and obligations along creeks, rivers, ponds, and lakes |
| Hunting and fishing rights | Sometimes deeded separately, especially near timber-company holdings |
| Air rights and height limits | Restrictions on building height, antennas, wind equipment |
| Grazing rights | May attach to parcels adjacent to federal land |
| Conservation easements | Recorded permanent use restrictions; they bind every future owner |
| Covenants, conditions, and restrictions (CC&Rs) | Private subdivision or HOA rules that can control style, materials, or use |
| Liens, judgments, recorded claims | Anything affecting transfer, financing, or use |

If the parcel does not touch a public road, resolve access before anything else. Legal access and physical access are separate checks: the recorded easement must exist, and the road must carry a loaded concrete truck in winter. Confirm maintenance responsibility, emergency access, and delivery access at the same time.

Walk the access route yourself. Drive it in bad weather if you can.

**Professional**

Title review and site feasibility are one exercise. A parcel can be physically buildable and still be blocked by easements, severed rights, covenants, conservation restrictions, road-maintenance obligations, water-rights limits, or a survey gap. The working documents are the preliminary title report, assessor records, water-rights records, seller disclosures, any survey, any road agreement, and the county records. The preliminary title report is the working index: it lists the recorded matters affecting ownership or use.

Issues that show up regularly:

- **Severed mineral rights.** Older parcels in former timber and mining areas may have mineral rights owned by someone else. The surface owner cannot prevent reasonable access to extract them.
- **Severed timber rights.** Standing timber may have been sold separately. Harvesting can proceed unless those rights expired or were bought back.
- **Water-rights ambiguity.** Oregon water law uses prior appropriation (first to use the water, first in right). A domestic well is usually available, but irrigation requires an allocation, and a spring on the land may legally belong to a downstream holder. Domestic well permits, irrigation rights, and groundwater registrations are researched in Oregon Water Resources Department records (oregon.gov/owrd), not in the county records.
- **Road-maintenance disputes.** Cost allocation is the common failure on shared private roads, most often over snow removal. The agreement assigns maintenance shares and an enforcement mechanism (the access section below).
- **Conservation easements.** Often signed by a past owner for tax benefits. They run with the land forever, and some prohibit any new construction.

The severed mineral estate follows the general common-law pattern: the mineral estate is dominant, and its holder may use as much of the surface as is reasonably necessary to explore and extract, without the surface owner's consent. The "reasonable" limit is real but undefined by any Oregon case squarely adopting the more protective accommodation doctrine some states apply; no such Oregon holding was located, and the manuscript does not assume one. Two statutes frame the edges: ORS 517.180 gives a surface owner a notice-and-recording procedure to extinguish a genuinely dormant mineral interest, and ORS 308.115 keeps severed mineral interests off the separate tax rolls unless mined. The severance deed's own language matters most; some deeds confine the mineral owner to deep extraction methods that never touch the surface. An attorney reads the recorded severance document before the offer prices the risk. Parcels adjacent to federal land or with historic federal ownership may also be subject to federal mineral reservations, researched in BLM records (blm.gov).

A severed timber right is confirmed the same way it was created: through the recorded chain of title. Oregon has no timber-rights registry; a full title-company search surfaces a recorded timber deed, timber sale contract, or reservation clause the same way it surfaces a mineral reservation. A seller's verbal assurance and a tax-lot lookup miss a decades-old recorded right that never expired.

Conservation easements are statutory nonpossessory interests (ORS 271.715 to 271.795) held by land trusts or public bodies, individually drafted, with purposes spanning natural, scenic, agricultural, and historic protection. No published Oregon case was located in which one blocked an owner-builder's construction after purchase; the recorded restrictions bind whether or not they have ever been litigated. The recorded easement text controls, restriction by restriction, and it is read in full before an offer.

A long-standing misplaced fence line is a narrower risk in Oregon than the popular framing suggests. ORS 105.620 requires ten years of actual, open, notorious, exclusive, hostile, and continuous possession, plus an honest belief of ownership with an objective basis, held from entry through the full ten years, all proven by clear and convincing evidence. A neighbor who always knew the fence was wrong cannot satisfy the honest-belief element, and grazing alone is statutorily insufficient. The residual risk concentrates in fences placed in good faith on a bad survey; a current boundary survey resolves it.

**Code**

```
Recorded interests control, in recorded order.
  Recording gives deeds, easements, covenants, and liens their legal effect and priority.
  Source: ORS chapter 93; county clerk or recorder records.

Timber harvest and riparian activity are regulated under the forest practice rules.
  Harvest restrictions and riparian setbacks apply under the forest practice rules, administered by the Oregon Department of Forestry.
  Source: OAR chapter 629.

  Oregon's recreational immunity framework applies if neighbors or the public cross the land recreationally.
  Source: ORS 105.700-105.795.

  Source: ORS 105.620.
```

## 3.8 Access Rights And Road Standards

**Simple**

Some mapped roads were platted decades ago and never built. A mapped road also does not prove a legal right to use it. Fire code sets minimum driveway width, clearance, and turnarounds. A driveway that fails those numbers gets rebuilt at the buyer's expense. The fix costs (regrading, widening, easement negotiation) are priced before the offer, not after.

**Applied**

Verify access in three independent records, then on the ground:

1. Check the county's accepted-public-road inventory (many counties publish an "All Public Roads" layer; a statewide layer sits on Oregon GEOHub). This shows what the county maintains.
2. Check the recorded plat or county surveyor records for what was legally dedicated. A road can be dedicated and never accepted: Jefferson County names this category the Local Access Road, public on paper, built and maintained by no one.
3. Drive the route. A road can be legally dedicated and physically absent.
4. Where access crosses private land, confirm a recorded easement exists. An unrecorded easement gives no notice to future owners of the burdened parcel and is close to unenforceable in practice.
5. For a private shared road, require a recorded, written maintenance agreement before closing, with four terms in it: a numeric cost-sharing formula, named snow-removal and drainage responsibility, a named dispute-resolution mechanism (mediation or arbitration), and an enforceable right to compel a non-paying party. Without the enforcement term, compelling a non-paying party requires a lawsuit with no fee-shifting.
6. Ask the fire district, in writing, which access standard applies to the parcel's driveway and which fire-code edition and amendments the district enforces.
7. Walk any culvert crossing before and after storms once under contract, and check inlet debris and headwall condition seasonally after purchase; a blocked inlet is the standard washout mechanism.

**Professional**

Establishing a new easement across a neighbor's land is a private negotiation with no fixed price or clock. The mechanical costs are known ranges: attorney drafting commonly runs a few hundred to a few thousand dollars (2026). A boundary survey applies where the easement line needs monuments; the surveyor files the record of survey with the county surveyor within 45 days of setting them. The county recording fee is on the order of tens of dollars. The negotiation itself is the unbounded cost: the neighbor owes nothing, can demand compensation, or can attach use limits. A residential-only clause would matter later if block-yard delivery traffic uses the easement.

Where negotiation fails and the parcel is landlocked, the statutory way of necessity applies (ORS 376.150 to 376.200). The procedure starts with a petition to the county governing body and a bond for the county's investigation. Multnomah County's local rule sets $500 as one documented bond figure. The case can transfer to circuit court. ORS 376.180 lists twelve conditions, including least-damage routing and a 30-foot width cap without county authorization. The petitioner bears the burden of proving no other legal access path exists (Tyska v. Prest, Oregon Court of Appeals, 1999). The procedure's own answer-and-appeal windows put even an uncontested case at several months. The court may also award the burdened owner costs and attorney fees.

The fire-access dimensional standards below come from the Oregon Fire Code as quoted in a fire district's published applications guide (Clackamas Fire District #1, 2021 revision). The quoted basis is the 2018 International Fire Code as adopted. In the quoted code text, the reduced 12-foot driving surface serves three or fewer dwelling units, not two. ORS 368.039 makes locally adopted county or city road standards supersede the fire code's road provisions. The controlling document for a specific driveway is therefore settled only by the county road department and fire district together. The gap between a driveway's as-built condition and whichever standard controls is the remediation cost. Regrading, widening, and turnaround construction are priced by a civil engineer or road contractor against the specific site.

Base rock depth for concrete-truck loading (a loaded ready-mix truck runs roughly 66,000-80,000 pounds gross) has no lookup value. It is a geotechnical design against the subgrade soil, the axle loads, and the drainage. Culvert sizing at a watercourse crossing follows the county road standard (commonly a 25-year or 50-year design storm, county-specific). A crossing that disturbs a regulated streambed adds Department of State Lands removal-fill permitting. The build-sequence chapter contains the rationale for building the durable driveway first. Every later delivery uses the driveway, and a driveway that ruts under the first loaded truck delays the rest of the build. A second emergency access route (a graded secondary route, a formalized neighbor easement) costs least when arranged at design time. No documented dollar comparison exists, and the resilience chapter contains the redundant-access planning.

**Code**

```
Fire apparatus access reaches every dwelling served by a driveway.
  Driving surface 20 feet unobstructed (26 feet adjacent to a hydrant); 12 feet permitted serving three or fewer dwelling units, inside a 20-foot cleared corridor, with turning radii of 44 feet inside and 56 feet outside; vertical clearance 13 feet 6 inches; grade 12 percent maximum, 15 percent where the dwelling is sprinklered; surface rated for a 13,500-pound wheel load and 75,000-pound gross vehicle weight.
  Source: Oregon Fire Code 503.2.1 and Appendix D, D103.1 (2018 IFC-based adoption; the local district's adopted edition and amendments control).

Dead ends and long driveways take turnarounds and turnouts.
  Dead-end access over 150 feet requires an approved turnaround (96-foot cul-de-sac or 120-foot hammerhead among the approved shapes); over 500 feet, a 26-foot driving surface; any access road over 400 feet requires 10-foot by 30-foot turnouts at intervals of 400 feet or less.
  Source: Oregon Fire Code Appendix D.

Locally adopted road standards supersede the fire code's.
  Where a county or city adopts road specifications and standards by charter, plan, or ordinance, those standards prevail over the fire code's road provisions.
  Source: ORS 368.039.

A statutory way of necessity is capped and conditioned.
  Maximum 30-foot width without county authorization; twelve statutory conditions, including least-damage routing, no traffic-hazard connection, and no other available legal access; the petitioner bears the burden of proof.
  Source: ORS 376.180; ORS 376.150-376.200.
```

## 3.9 Utilities And Access

Does the parcel already have power, water, sewer or septic potential, internet, and a road that works year-round? Extending the missing ones can cost more than the land.

**Simple**

Every utility the parcel lacks becomes a project cost, and the numbers are large. A new transformer alone runs $5,000-$25,000 (2026). A written quote from each provider comes before the offer.

**Applied**

Verify each system directly with the provider. Coverage maps and listing descriptions are not quotes.

| System | Confirm in writing |
|---|---|
| Electrical service | Distance to the nearest transformer or service point; extension cost estimate; which utility (PGE, Pacific Power, a cooperative); service-entrance and metering requirements; available voltage and ampacity |
| Water | Municipal water available, or a well required; for an existing well: yield, recovery, and water-quality results; nearby well depths and yields; legal water-use limits |
| Wastewater | Sewer available; if not, whether the parcel has passed or can pass a septic site evaluation |
| Internet | Which providers serve the exact address; the deliverable speed, not the advertised speed; installation cost |
| Road access | Public or private; who maintains it; year-round truck access; bridge and culvert weight limits; overhead clearance |
| Natural gas | Available and at what cost; this design may not need it, but the answer affects future options and resale |
| Cellular service | Coverage at the building site, not the property line; multiple carriers |

Save names, dates, and the written answers in the project binder.

Planning ranges for electrical extensions, in 2026 Oregon dollars: roughly $15-$30 per foot overhead, $20-$50 per foot underground. A new transformer, if one is needed, starts at $5,000-$25,000. Internet drops vary wildly; some providers extend for free, others quote $1,000-$10,000 or more. Always get the written quote.

For the electrical estimate itself, use the utilities' own tools rather than a rule of thumb. PGE and Pacific Power each publish a free online residential line-extension estimator; both apply a tariffed allowance credit against the extension cost, and both exclude trenching, conduit, and vault costs for underground service from the allowance entirely. At least one investor-owned utility's terms exclude designated high-risk wildfire areas from certain new-home extension cost provisions, which matters on exactly the forested parcels this search may consider. Cooperatives set their own board-adopted policies: Pioneer Electric Cooperative extends up to a quarter mile at no cost, then charges $6.50 per foot (2026); a first-length-free pattern of 200-500 feet is common across cooperatives. Get the specific cooperative's policy in writing.

Screen solar access with two tools before any paid survey. Run NREL's PVWatts calculator (pvwatts.nrel.gov, free) for the location's baseline production. Bring a Solar Pathfinder (a reflective-dome instrument readable in any weather, in any season) or an equivalent phone application to the site visit; it maps exactly which parts of the sun's path the trees, terrain, and buildings block. Evaluate any phone application at time of use; the market changes too fast to name one.

**Professional**

Utility feasibility sets early geometry. Service routes, transformer location, and trenching paths shape the site layout. The water source drives pump, pressure, storage, treatment, and backup-power planning. Wastewater feasibility can move the house before the floor plan exists.

A grid-extension quote and an off-grid solar-plus-battery system compare on lifecycle cost, and the comparison needs two inputs that exist only after a parcel and an appliance-and-heating design are chosen: the utility's written extension quote, and the household's itemized load calculation. The method: the grid path sums the net extension cost plus the discounted stream of monthly service and energy charges over the comparison period; the solar path sums the system capital cost (sized in PVWatts against the site's real solar resource and the real load) plus battery and inverter replacements at their service intervals. The solar and energy independence chapter contains the sizing method in full.

Western Oregon's winter solar resource caps what any siting decision can deliver: the sun shines roughly 33% of the time from November through February, and Pacific Northwest passive-solar design guidance documents a 20-30% heating-load reduction as the realistic ceiling, against 40-60% in sunnier cold climates. The 5-6-hours-of-winter-sun rule of thumb in general passive-solar literature is not verified for Oregon's latitude and cloud pattern; the working standard for photovoltaics is the measured shading-loss percentage from the site reading, not an hours count. The passive design chapter contains the design response.

The site must also function as a construction site. The build-sequence chapter contains the staging detail; at the feasibility pass, the checks are:

- a concrete truck can enter, position, and turn around without backing into traffic
- a pump truck can set up where its boom reaches the work
- a lowboy trailer can deliver an excavator and compaction equipment
- staged material (block, rebar, roofing, tanks, windows) has a safe spot that does not block the road
- emergency vehicles can reach the house

A parcel that looks excellent from the air can still be a poor construction site.

Permit filing is online in much of the state: participating jurisdictions process land-use, building, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical permits through Oregon ePermitting (oregon.gov/bcd/epermitting).

**Code**

```
Utility extensions follow each utility's published policy.
  The investor-owned utilities and the cooperatives each publish extension policies and cost-recovery formulas; PGE Schedule 300 and Pacific Power's line-extension policy are the two big ones.
  Source: the serving utility's published line-extension schedule.

Driveway connections to public roads need a permit.
  Sight distance, grade, and drainage requirements apply at the connection; county road departments handle county roads, ODOT handles state highways.
  Source: the county road department or ODOT approach-road permit program; ORS chapter 374.
```

## 3.10 Septic And Water Feasibility

No sewer means the parcel needs a septic system. No municipal water means it needs a well or another approved source. Either question can end the project; these two checks are the earliest hard gates in a rural purchase.

**Simple**

Can a septic system legally go on this land? Some soils drain too slowly, some drain too fast, and some parcels cannot support any approved system. A failed evaluation means no dwelling can legally be built on the parcel. The test that answers the question costs about $500-$1,200 (2026). At the range extremes the outcome swings $42,000: an $8,000 standard system against a $50,000 engineered alternative. The real spread is set by system type, site conditions, and installer pricing. This answer comes before any rural land purchase.

**Applied**

Screen for free before paying for the evaluation:

1. Open the NRCS Web Soil Survey (websoilsurvey.nrcs.usda.gov, free, statewide). Draw the parcel boundary and read each mapped soil unit's septic-absorption limitation rating. A "severe" rating is a strong warning before spending evaluation money; "slight" or "moderate" is encouraging, not a guarantee, because the mapping scale spans acres and misses local conditions. The same data reaches the Oregon GEOHub SoilWeb layer; Polk County publishes its own county soils layer, and other counties vary.
2. Walk the site for the surface indicators an evaluator reads first: standing water or saturated ground in the wet season, water-loving vegetation, rock outcrops, shallow bedrock, steep slope.
3. Check for existing septic records. DEQ's online records tool holds them for Curry, Jackson, and Josephine counties (plus pre-June-2025 records for Baker, Union, and Wallowa); everywhere else, request records from the county environmental-health or community-development office.

If sewer is unavailable, make the offer contingent on a satisfactory septic site evaluation by the local onsite-program authority. The evaluation answers:

- whether the soil drains at acceptable rates
- whether there is enough usable area for the primary system
- whether there is a reserve area for a future replacement system
- the system type required: standard gravity, pressure-distribution, sand filter, or an alternative treatment technology (ATT)
- the required setbacks from wells, surface water, slopes, property lines, and structures
- whether the system fits alongside the planned house, driveway, drainage, and future outbuildings

If municipal water is unavailable, investigate the well path before closing:

| Item | Source |
|---|---|
| Existing well log | OWRD records (oregon.gov/owrd) |
| Well yield and recovery | The log, or a new drawdown test |
| Static water level and drawdown | The log, or a new test |
| Water quality | A recent test from an accredited Oregon lab |
| Nearby well depths and yields | The OWRD database for adjacent parcels |
| Legal water-use limits | Oregon water-rights records and OWRD guidance |
| Setback conflicts | Local septic, well, and building setback rules |

Draw the house, driveway, septic primary area, septic reserve area, well, utilities, and drainage on one site sketch before committing the building location. Do not place the house first and force water and wastewater into the leftover space.

**Professional**

Septic feasibility is a layout problem. The soil facts (texture, percolation, the limiting layer of clay, water table, or rock, and seasonal high groundwater) have to fit together with the slope, the drainfield size, the reserve area, and the setbacks on one plan. Water feasibility joins the same plan: well yield, pump curve, static level, drawdown, storage, treatment, pressure, freeze protection, and backup power.

The site evaluation's output is quantitative. The evaluator's soil group (A, B, or C) and effective soil depth read directly into the trench-length tables: shallower soil and finer texture mean more linear feet of absorption trench per unit of daily flow, up to 175 feet per 150 gallons at the worst table position, down to 50 at the best. A permanent water table within 4 feet of the absorption facility's bottom (24 inches in DEQ-designated areas with a supporting groundwater study) rules out the standard trench designs entirely.

A high water table or bad percolation changes the required system, in order of escalation: from gravity trenches to pressurized distribution (dosed, even distribution across the trench), then to capping fill or a mound (imported fill that adds vertical separation), then to a sand filter (an engineered pretreatment bed) or an aerobic treatment unit. Construction cost rises through that same order, and DEQ's permit fee schedule tiers its fees in the same order: standard trenches and their variants lowest; alternative treatment technologies, capping fill, and pressurized distribution next; sand filters highest. DEQ's onsite program fees rise 33% on July 1, 2026; the applicant prices permits from the current OAR 340-071-0140 fee table, not from older published figures.

Graywater diversion does not shrink the drainfield. Oregon permits graywater reuse as its own DEQ program (three permit tiers, from a single-family subsurface-irrigation general permit for under 300 gallons per day upward), and DEQ's program materials require the property to keep a permitted full-size disposal system connected; the diversion is parallel plumbing, not a design-flow credit. Whether a specific county evaluator will credit a reduced design flow at evaluation time, with a graywater system or a composting toilet in the design, is an evaluator-level question this project treats as unanswered until the county's onsite program answers it in writing for a specific parcel. The nonwater-carried path exists in rule (separate, smaller separation distances for self-contained systems), which shows the rules contemplate toilet-type differences; the pre-purchase credit question remains the county's to answer.

Cost anchors in 2026 Oregon dollars:

| System | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Septic site evaluation | $500-$1,200 | The contingency test; the range extremes above differ by $42,000 |
| Standard gravity septic | $8,000-$15,000 | Suitable soils, gravity drainfield |
| Pressure-distribution septic | $12,000-$25,000 | Marginal soils needing even distribution |
| Sand filter or ATT | $20,000-$50,000 | Difficult soils or shallow groundwater |
| Standard well (Willamette Valley) | $8,000-$20,000 | Shallow water table, moderate depth |

Two ongoing obligations affect the purchase decision. Oregon's onsite rules require an operation-and-maintenance contract with a certified provider for the life of any new septic system, with annual inspections. An alternative treatment technology system requires manufacturer-certified maintenance in addition. The plumbing and documentation chapters contain the detail. The relevant fact at the feasibility pass: a difficult-soil parcel obligates the household to a permanent service contract, in addition to the construction cost.

A low-yield well has a standard fix: a storage cistern (commonly 300-5,000 gallons; roughly 1,100 gallons circulates in industry sources as a lending-standard minimum) fills slowly from the well and a booster pump pressurizes the house from the cistern, decoupling recharge rate from peak demand, with run-dry protection on both pumps. Component sizing is a design task in the water independence chapter. A precast concrete cistern adds a delivery constraint: tanks of roughly 1,500-5,000 gallons ride a standard flatbed and a truck crane needing a firm, level pad; single tanks past roughly 5,000-10,000 gallons need a larger crane and a wider, taller route, which a narrow rural driveway can rule out in favor of cast-in-place or several smaller manifolded tanks. The crossover is quoted by a regional precast supplier against the measured driveway, not estimated.

The septic primary and reserve areas are protected infrastructure, not spare ground for outbuildings or landscaping.

## 3.11 Water Rights And The Exempt Well

**Simple**

Oregon water belongs to the public, and the right to use it comes from the state. An ordinary house well fits an exemption: no permit, no water right, up to 15,000 gallons per day. The exemption has categories, and some basins restrict it. Rainwater off a roof is exempt entirely. Springs and ponds follow their own rules. The four source types are four separate legal questions, not one.

**Applied**

1. Check the candidate parcel against OWRD's Groundwater Administrative Areas map and table before the offer. The map is a live document (the Harney Basin designation is recent and contested); check it fresh at search time, not from memory.
2. Pull nearby well logs from OWRD's Well Report Query (apps.wrd.state.or.us, free, wells since 1955): depth, casing, reported flow, static level. Compare drilling dates against depths. Newer wells drilled deeper than older neighbors signal a declining water table; take a worrying pattern to a hydrogeologist before waiving contingencies.
3. Confirm in the drilling contract that the driller's scope includes the statutory exempt-use filing: the well log and tax-lot map filed with OWRD within 30 days of completion, with the $300 recording fee. The statute assigns this duty to the driller; keep copies of the filed log and map in the project binder.
4. For a spring, establish whether its water leaves the property under natural conditions at any time of year. That single fact changes its legal status.
5. For a pond, check the permit thresholds before any design work: under 9.2 acre-feet (water covering one acre to a depth of one foot) of storage and under a 10-foot dam height qualifies for OWRD's simplified Alternate Reservoir Process; at or above either figure, the standard process requires engineered plans through OWRD dam safety review.

**Professional**

ORS 537.545 exempts seven groundwater uses from any registration, permit, or certificate:

- stockwatering (a 12,000-gallon-per-day cap at new confined animal feeding operations is repealed September 15, 2027, leaving stockwatering uncapped)
- watering a lawn or a noncommercial or commercial garden of a half acre or less, up to 3,000 gallons per day
- school grounds up to 10 acres inside critical groundwater areas
- single or group domestic use up to 15,000 gallons per day
- down-hole heat exchange
- any single industrial or commercial purpose up to 5,000 gallons per day
- a narrow reused-water land-application category

A commercial garden plus an industrial or commercial use together may not exceed 5,000 gallons per day. "Commercial garden" excludes cannabis-family plants unless the grower is licensed under ORS 571.281. A temporary carve-out bars the commercial-garden exemption in the Lower Umatilla Basin Groundwater Management Area until its scheduled repeal on January 2, 2028.

The domestic and industrial categories are legally separate, and the split matters to this project. A household, including one with a future accessory dwelling, draws on the 15,000-gallon domestic allowance ("group domestic" applies to multiple dwellings on one well). A personal workshop classifies as domestic-incidental use. A block operation that becomes a commercial manufacturing activity plausibly falls in the separate industrial-commercial category, capped at 5,000 gallons per day and legally distinct. OWRD publishes no guidance drawing that line for a home workshop. The owner confirms the classification with OWRD before finalizing well design if block production goes commercial. The owner-made-blocks chapter contains the production-water estimate.

Inside a designated Critical Groundwater Area, the exempt-well assumption can fail: each area's own administrative rule can restrict, condition, or close new exempt uses. The designated inventory includes four Umatilla Basin areas (Ordnance Gravel, Ordnance Basalt, Butter Creek, Stage Gulch), Cow Valley, The Dalles, and Cooper-Bull Mountain. The Harney Basin designation passed through an actively contested rulemaking. The buyer confirms the current designation list and the specific area rule with OWRD. Mitigation programs run separately in the Deschutes, Grande Ronde, and Klamath basins. In the Deschutes program (OAR 690-505), a new groundwater right proceeds by acquiring mitigation credits sold through the Deschutes River Conservancy. No published price list exists; current pricing comes from the Conservancy and OWRD directly.

The documented decline record justifies the screening. Harney Basin groundwater has fallen more than 100 feet in places, dropping up to 8 feet per year in the worst areas. Roughly 70 residential and stock wells there are already dry, and irrigation takes roughly 97% of the basin's groundwater use. An OWRD memorandum counts roughly 1,220 Oregon wells that went dry or fell below pump capacity between June 2021 and December 2023. Oregon has no mandatory dry-well reporting, so the absence of reported failures near a parcel proves nothing. A WaterWatch of Oregon analysis (an advocacy source, read as such) names the Deschutes, Umatilla, Klamath, and Harney basins as the over-allocation pattern.

Rainwater has the simplest legal status of the four sources. ORS 537.141(1)(h) exempts the collection and use of precipitation from any artificial impervious surface, with no cap and no structure-purpose limit in the text. A purpose-built collection roof (a carport-style catchment structure) sits inside the exemption's plain text. No OWRD interpretation addresses that exact configuration, so the owner confirms OWRD's reading before building one. Combining sources adds no water-rights filing, but it does add a plumbing requirement. An auxiliary source beside a well triggers backflow protection (a reduced-pressure-principle assembly) under the plumbing code and the OHA cross-connection rules, so the sources cannot contaminate each other. The water independence chapter contains the treatment train, storage sizing, and cross-connection design.

A spring's legal status turns on its outflow. Where spring water, under natural conditions, forms no channel and never leaves the property, secondary legal sources report development for use as exempt from standard permitting. No controlling statute section is verified for that exemption, so the buyer confirms the specific spring's status with OWRD before pricing it into the offer. Where the water leaves the property, it is public surface water requiring a standard permit, with first-in-time priority applying. A spring above the house delivers by gravity through any power outage. Whether that resilience outweighs other site factors is the owner's decision, and the solar and energy independence chapter's battery planning reduces the margin. No sourced Oregon figures price spring development (cutoff wall, collection bed, spring box, overflow, service line); a spring-development contractor and a well driller quote the comparison for a real parcel.

A newly built pond requires a reservoir permit (ORS 537.400); the pre-1995 registered-pond exemption is closed to new construction. Below 9.2 acre-feet and a 10-foot dam, the Alternate Reservoir Process applies, with OWRD's guidebook (June 2024) as the application manual. A pond remains a workable supplemental source for irrigation, stock, and fire reserve; the resilience chapter contains the fire-reserve sizing.

| Source | Legal treatment | The check before purchase |
|---|---|---|
| Drilled well | Exempt categories under ORS 537.545; permit beyond them | Administrative-area map; neighboring well logs |
| Developed spring | Exempt if no natural outflow off the property (unverified statute; OWRD confirms); surface-water permit if it flows off | The outflow question, answered on the ground and by OWRD |
| Roof or impervious-surface rainwater | Fully exempt, no cap | None on the rights side; plumbing rules apply at the house |
| Pond or reservoir | Reservoir permit required | The 9.2 acre-foot and 10-foot thresholds |

No located Oregon source documents neighbor-to-neighbor water sharing in a dry-well emergency; any such sharing depends on proximity and existing relationships. A remote parcel has fewer potential helpers within hauling distance; the resilience chapter treats stored water as the dependable answer.

**Code**


## 3.12 Hazard And Environmental Screening

**Simple**

Free public maps screen a parcel for six risks:

- landslide
- flood
- wildfire
- tsunami
- wetland
- contamination

Every screen costs nothing and takes minutes. The maps under-report by their own publishers' statements. A clean map result is a screen, not a clearance; the paid follow-up settles anything the screen flags.

**Applied**

Run every screen on every serious candidate:

1. **Landslide.** Open DOGAMI's SLIDO map (via Oregon HazVu or Oregon GEOHub). Read the two layers separately: the inventory layer records mapped past landslides; the susceptibility layer models future potential in four tiers (Low, Moderate, High, Very High). Either an inventory hit or a High or Very High susceptibility rating sends the parcel to a licensed geotechnical engineer or engineering geologist before closing. No consultation price is published; get quotes, and match the scope (desktop review, borings, or slope-stability analysis) to the flag.
2. **Flood.** Search the address at FEMA's Map Service Center (msc.fema.gov), free and immediate. Where the result is ambiguous or a lender wants proof, order an elevation certificate from a licensed surveyor: roughly $600-$2,700, 5-10 business days (2026). An elevation showing the site above the base flood elevation supports a Letter of Map Amendment removing the parcel from the mapped zone.
3. **Wildfire.** Open the Oregon Wildfire Risk Explorer (Oregon GEOHub, OSU-maintained). It replaced the repealed statewide hazard map as an awareness tool, not a regulatory assignment. Check whether the county or fire district has adopted the State Fire Marshal's voluntary defensible-space model code at defensiblespace.oregon.gov; the adoption list changes jurisdiction by jurisdiction.
4. **Tsunami, for any coastal or estuarine candidate.** Pull the DOGAMI Tsunami Inundation Map plates for the segment (local Cascadia and distant-source scenarios) and the companion evacuation walk-time map. Read the parcel against inundation depth and the on-foot time to mapped high ground, then field-check the mapped walking route against the real terrain. In a local Cascadia event the first wave can arrive in minutes, before any warning issues.
5. **Wetlands.** Check the National Wetlands Inventory and Oregon's Statewide Wetlands Inventory. Budget a professional wetland delineation as routine on any parcel with forest cover, seasonal wet areas, or drainage features, whatever the map shows; the map's own limitations document states that on-the-ground delineation is the only verification.
6. **Contamination.** Search DEQ's Your DEQ Online records portal for the parcel and its neighbors, since plumes and tank leaks cross lot lines. A hit or an ambiguous old record sends the parcel to a Phase 1 Environmental Site Assessment (a consultant's records-and-site review) before closing.
7. **Orchard legacy, in fruit country.** In the Rogue Valley around Medford, Central Point, Phoenix, Talent, and Ashland, former orchards commonly hold lead, arsenic, DDT and its breakdown products, and dieldrin in soil; Hood River Valley has the same history at smaller scale. Former-orchard ground gets consultant soil sampling before purchase.
8. **Walk the ground for what no database holds:** old dump sites in gullies and fence corners, a tank filler pipe or vent stub near an old barn with stained or dead vegetation, unnaturally regular pits or tailings from old prospecting, and third-party dumping near road frontage. Any of these also points to the Phase 1 assessment.

Where the target county offers an integrated GIS (Deschutes County's DIAL is a documented example, overlaying parcels, zoning, wildfire, and wetland layers with downloadable data), start there; otherwise assemble the state and federal tools above individually.

**Professional**

The screening tools' own error statements limit what a clean result proves. DOGAMI states SLIDO is not for site-specific planning and that its first release located between 0% and 25% of actual landslides at any location; later releases improved coverage unevenly. DSL states the wetlands inventory under-represents smaller, seasonal, and forested wetlands, excludes certain farmed wetlands by federal-definition policy, and rests mostly on mid-1980s imagery outside the coastal zone. County GIS parcel data is maintained for tax administration and is screening-grade, not survey-grade. DEQ describes Your DEQ Online (which replaced the ECSI cleanup database on April 16, 2024) as working information, with unconfirmed and incomplete records.

Wetland buffers are local law. OAR 660-023-0100 requires local protection of significant wetlands but sets no statewide buffer width; adopted widths in documented examples run from 50 feet (Talent) to 200 feet on wetlands and fish-bearing streams in the Columbia River Gorge National Scenic Area, with Eugene's own overlay standards in city code. The buyer gets the specific jurisdiction's adopted buffer from its planning department, or files a Wetland Land Use Notification with DSL, which answers within 30 days.

Wildfire insurability is a purchase factor separate from construction cost. The Oregon FAIR Plan is the insurer of last resort and pays actual cash value only, not replacement cost; qualifying for it means the standard market declined the property. Oregon insurers must disclose whether they consider mitigation in underwriting; no current rule requires a discount, and a 2026 legislative proposal (LC 182) would mandate mitigation credits if enacted, a recheck item. What is documented today: the IBHS Wildfire Prepared Home certification supports carrier discounts of roughly 10-20%, with the certification inspection at $150-$300 and 2-3 hours (2026), and independent-agent reporting describes specialty carriers pricing documented defensible-space and hardening work 15-30% below unmitigated baselines. This project's planned construction (noncombustible concrete walls, a Class A roof assembly, ember-resistant detailing) maps onto the scored hardening categories, but no located source quantifies a credit for concrete construction as its own line item; real quotes against a real parcel and design settle it. No Oregon study quantifies wildfire-insurance difficulty's separate effect on resale price.

Tsunami regulation does not reach this project's building type. ORS 455.447 restricts only "major structures" (over six stories with 60,000 square feet or more, or over ten stories) and buildings designated Tsunami Risk Category III or IV; a detached single-family dwelling is Risk Category II under OSSC Table 1604.5 and stays outside the statute under any home-occupation or accessory-structure scenario short of a genuine Category III or IV occupancy. ORS 455.446 directs DOGAMI to map the inundation zone; the TIM plates and walk-time maps are the siting tools.

Former-orchard cleanup for redevelopment runs through DEQ's published pathways (January 2025 guidance): the Independent Cleanup Pathway with technical consultation at a $1,500 deposit and a 45-day review of a complete application; the Voluntary Cleanup Program at a $5,000 deposit and up to 180 days for project-manager assignment where contaminated soil is managed on site; a Solid Waste Letter Authorization at a $500 fee and roughly 45 days for off-site soil disposal outside a permitted landfill. Per-sample laboratory pricing depends on the consultant and sampling plan; it is quoted, not looked up.

**Code**

```
Tsunami construction limits apply to large and high-risk structures only.
  ORS 455.447 reaches "major structures" (over six stories and 60,000 or more square feet, or over ten stories) and Tsunami Risk Category III and IV buildings; a detached single-family dwelling is Risk Category II.
  Source: ORS 455.447(1); OSSC Table 1604.5.

Wetland protection is delegated to local governments.
  Local governments protect significant wetlands by an individualized Goal 5 process or a safe-harbor ordinance restricting grading, excavation, fill, and vegetation removal; the buffer width is set locally, not by state rule.
  Source: OAR 660-023-0100.

Defensible-space code adoption is voluntary and tracked by rule.
  A city, county, or fire agency adopting the State Fire Marshal's model defensible-space code notifies OSFM through the local-adoption process; adoption status is jurisdiction-specific.
  Source: OAR 837-040-0030; defensiblespace.oregon.gov.
```

## 3.13 Topography, Drainage, And Buildability

Slope and drainage decide the water management, the truck access, the foundation type, and the erosion-control obligations.

**Simple**

Flat, dry land costs the least to build on. Slope and wetness add cost at each step: excavation, retaining walls, drainage, and the driveway. Slope changes the design and the cost rather than blocking a purchase.

**Applied**

Walk the site after rain if possible. Look for:

- where surface water flows and where it pools
- soft ground or springs
- erosion along driveway routes, swales, and slopes
- low spots near the planned building area
- uphill water aimed at the future house
- evidence of old grading or fill: different soil colors, buried debris, abandoned terraces, rip-rap
- room for excavator setup, concrete delivery, pump-truck position, and staging
- root systems that suggest seasonal saturation
- vegetation patterns: water-loving plants in low spots, drought-stressed plants on dry slopes

Use topographic maps and county GIS before the visit, and trust the walk more than the map. A contour line does not show standing water. For early terrain work, use the free USGS 1/3 arc-second elevation models for Oregon (usgs.gov); their resolution is adequate for early feasibility.

**Professional**

Topography sets the cut-and-fill volume, the drainage strategy, the retaining-wall demand, and the driveway grade. It also sets the erosion-control plan, the staging areas, the waterproofing demand, and the foundation complexity. A simple rectangular footprint on a contour map can become stepped footings, tall retaining walls, imported fill, difficult compaction, and a driveway that fails every winter.

Patterns that require attention:

- **Slope orientation.** A south-facing slope receives winter solar gain. A north-facing slope is colder, darker, and slower to dry.
- **Cold-air pooling.** Valley and basin sites pool cold air on still winter nights, running 5-15 °F colder than the regional design value. That affects heating sizing and freeze protection.
- **Wind exposure.** Ridgelines get more wind, which raises lateral loads and infiltration. Valleys get less wind but pool cold air and smoke.
- **Flood exposure.** Land outside the mapped flood zone can still flood locally in extreme events. Neighbors hold the local flood history; the buyer asks them.
- **Old grading.** Terraces, rip-rap, and odd contours usually mean prior earthwork; the geotechnical investigation resolves what sits at footing depth before footings bear on it.

Clearing and grading the building pad has planning-range pricing, all national contractor-guide figures (2026), none a bid: lightly forested clearing at roughly $733-$2,333 per acre and heavily forested at $3,395-$6,155 (a Portland-area report spans $610-$5,000); light grading at $1,000-$3,000 per acre, moderate grading at $3,000-$7,000, steep ground above $7,000; building-pad preparation itself at roughly $15,800-$44,535 per acre. A moderately forested, moderately sloped combined house, shop, and block-yard pad of one to two disturbed acres points to a rough total of $10,000-$40,000 or more, driven by tree density, slope, rock, and haul distance. An excavation contractor's bid against the real pad layout replaces every figure above.

Retaining walls enter the permit system at a statutory threshold. The 4-foot line is measured from the bottom of the footing to the top of the wall, not from grade, and two conditions pull even a shorter wall back into permit territory: an ascending slope above the wall steeper than 3:1, or a nonsoil surcharge (any load beyond the retained earth itself: a driveway's vehicle and pavement load, a building pad, stacked block-yard material, parking). The code text does not quantify the surcharge trigger; the local building official applies it case by case, and the applicant confirms the jurisdiction's ordinance language at permit time.

Staging is part of feasibility for a concrete house. Trucks, pump setup, excavation, reinforcement, and waterproofing all require physical room that a steep or constrained site may not have. The soil chapter contains the geotechnical work.

**Code**

```
Surface water must drain away from the foundation.
  Grade falls 6 inches in the first 10 feet; where 10 feet is not available, swales or drains carry the water.
  Source: ORSC R401.3 (2023 edition).

Footings near slopes need setbacks.
  Footings adjacent to a descending slope keep a setback measured on a 45-degree plane from the toe; soil conditions can require more.
  Source: ORSC R403.1.7.

Flood-hazard construction has its own rules.
  Foundation, elevation, and venting requirements change inside Special Flood Hazard Areas.
  Source: ORSC R322; FEMA flood maps, msc.fema.gov.

Grading and erosion control are locally permitted.
  Each jurisdiction adopts its own grading, erosion-control, driveway, and stormwater permits; ground disturbance over one acre triggers the state stormwater permit.
  Source: the local grading and erosion-control ordinance; DEQ 1200-C permit under OAR 340-045 and ORS 468B.050.

Short retaining walls sit below the local permit threshold.
  A municipal ordinance may not require a permit for a retaining wall of 4 feet or less, measured from the bottom of the footing to the top of the wall, except where the wall supports an ascending slope steeper than 3:1 or a nonsoil surcharge.
  Source: ORSC R101.2.2, item 2 (2023 edition; recheck under the 2026 ORSC).
```

## 3.14 The Site Plan: Order Of Placement

**Simple**

The buildable area shrinks fast once the fixed elements are placed. Septic soil, well separations, hazard zones, and the driveway entrance are site facts. The elements lock onto the plan from least movable to most movable. When two fixed elements need the same ground, the parcel fails this screen.

**Applied**

Lock the site-plan elements in this order. No agency publishes a mandatory sequence; this order follows from which constraints are externally fixed.

1. **Septic primary and reserve areas.** The evaluator's qualifying soil is wherever it is, and the reserve (an equal second footprint, mandatory) locks at the same time. Setback rings radiate from this footprint.
2. **Well.** It must land where water is likely and hold its separations from the septic placement (100 feet from the absorption and reserve areas, 50 from the tank). Work well and septic as one locked pair; drilling first risks losing good soil, and siting septic first risks losing good water.
3. **Hazard and buffer exclusions.** Floodplain, wetland buffer, and landslide-susceptibility ground subtract from the buildable envelope before anything else is drawn.
4. **Driveway, grade, and fire turnaround.** The entry point, any culvert crossing, and the achievable grade are parcel facts, and construction traffic reaches nothing until the driveway exists.
5. **House footprint.** It fits inside what remains, holding 10 feet from the absorption and reserve areas, 5 feet from the tank, and the well clearances, with the south wall oriented for solar gain.
6. **Solar array shade-free zone.** Reserve the sun-path envelope south of the array location now; a later tree or outbuilding placed without checking shading degrades the array for its whole life.
7. **Shop, block-yard, and outbuildings.** The most movable elements fill the remaining ground last. The site-development chapter contains their layout in full.

Where a future ADU is a live possibility on RR-zoned land, keep the ground within roughly 100 feet of the house clear of outbuildings, because an SB 391 ADU is legally confined to that radius, and run a spare utility stub (power, water, a septic connection point) toward that zone while trenches are open; retrofitting utilities to a spot chosen years later costs more than a stub run during construction. On EFU or forest land the 100-foot logic does not apply; a second dwelling there is its own approval, resolved before any layout depends on it.

On a farm- or forest-deferred parcel, draw the homesite envelope (house, driveway, septic, disturbed yard) as compactly as the county's minimum-homesite rules allow; the smaller the carve-out, the less acreage leaves special assessment (the rollback-tax section below). Where a severed mineral estate exists, the same compact, central envelope is the defensive siting.

**Professional**

The setbacks that control the plan, gathered:

- from the septic absorption and reserve areas: 10 feet to any building foundation line (garages and outbuildings included), 10 feet to property lines
- from the septic tank: 5 feet to foundations and property lines
- from the well: 5 feet to any permanent structure as the legal floor, with substantially more kept in practice for drilling-rig and pump-service access (the rule's own 20-foot power-pole recommendation signals the workable clearance)
- from well to absorption area: 100 feet

A block-yard is an open pad, not a building, so the foundation setback does not bind it numerically. The real constraint is keeping its traffic and material loading off the absorption and reserve soil. Accessory-structure setbacks from property lines are zoning, county by county.

The acreage arithmetic below is an estimate with stated assumptions, not a code value. A typical primary-plus-reserve septic footprint runs a fifth to a half acre before its setback rings, per general septic guidance. General homesteading guidance puts 0.75-1.5 acres as the floor for septic plus well alone, with the reserve requirement pushing toward the high end. Below roughly 2 acres, fitting house, septic primary and reserve, well separations, and a block-yard without overlap gets genuinely tight on rolling terrain. Five acres and up gives comfortable working room for this project's full footprint. Soil quality, wetland buffers, and slope move the number parcel by parcel.

The block-yard needs contiguous gentle ground. A working convention for hand-and-wheelbarrow production pads is a grade under roughly 5-8%; the threshold has no published source, and the site-development chapter contains the pad-grade decision. At the screening level, the test is what level ground remains after the higher-priority subtractions. A parcel with none left is a warning for the block-yard plan even without an exact area target.

One permit exemption shapes early outbuilding planning: a nonhabitable one-story detached accessory structure up to 200 square feet and 15 feet tall needs no building permit. The allowance doubles to 400 square feet on a parcel of 2 acres or more when the structure keeps 20 feet from property lines and other regulated structures. The exemption waives the building permit only. Zoning setbacks and any electrical, plumbing, or mechanical permits still apply.

**Code**

```
Small accessory structures are exempt from the building permit.
  Nonhabitable one-story detached accessory structures up to 200 square feet and 15 feet high; up to 400 square feet on a parcel of 2.0 acres or more with the structure at least 20 feet from all property lines and regulated structures. Zoning setbacks and trade permits still apply.
  Source: ORSC R105.2, item 1 (2023 edition; recheck under the 2026 ORSC).
```

## 3.15 Distance And Logistics

Distance shapes how the project gets built and how daily life works during and after construction.

**Simple**

Distance adds daily time costs that continue long after the purchase. Every mile adds time to material runs, deliveries, inspections, and emergency response. The bands below state what each distance range changes.

**Applied**

Drive the route to the site before committing. Check it for:

- hardware and material runs
- grocery and basic-services trips
- medical access
- emergency response time
- winter conditions, ice, and snow
- steep grades, sharp curves, and bridge or culvert weight limits
- delivery-truck clearance: overhead, width, weight
- concrete-truck and pump-truck access
- internet and cellular coverage along the route

Drive it in poor weather if possible; summer condition does not predict winter condition on rural roads.

Call the building department that would serve the parcel before making an offer. Ask for typical permit turnaround, how the office is staffed, and how inspections are scheduled around a concrete pour. The Building Codes Division's Local Building Department Directory (oregon.gov/bcd/lbdd) identifies the serving department and its administration type.

**Professional**

Distance affects labor productivity, subcontractor willingness, delivery cost, inspection scheduling, emergency response, utility options, the resale pool, and owner fatigue. The more remote the site, the more staging discipline matters: spare fittings, backup tools, weather protection, temporary power and water, fuel storage, and tightly scheduled deliveries.

| Distance to hardware and services | Effect on the build |
|---|---|
| Under 15 minutes | Easy runs; fast emergency response; higher land cost; less privacy; tighter zoning is typical near towns, though not caused by the distance itself |
| 15-30 minutes | Reasonable runs; medium land cost; reasonable privacy; usually buildable zoning |
| 30-60 minutes | Every missing fitting costs a half-day; cheaper land; real privacy; more flexible zoning |
| Over 60 minutes | Plan around scarce trips and keep a deep inventory of fasteners and consumables; emergency response can pass 30 minutes; cheapest land; most flexibility |

Building-department service varies with the serving office. Oregon jurisdictions administer the building program locally, through BCD directly, or through a contracted private firm, and no published dataset compares turnaround times or staffing across rural counties; a single official covering a large county is a plausible, unconfirmed pattern. A concrete pour needs an inspector on a specific day, so the department's scheduling flexibility is a site-selection fact, learned by the pre-offer phone call above. The compliance-map chapter contains the administration structures.

For an owner-builder on a fixed schedule, distance changes staging and inventory planning rather than blocking a purchase.

## 3.16 Farm And Forest Special Assessment And The Rollback Tax

**Simple**

Many rural parcels are taxed under special assessment (a reduced property-tax valuation tied to farm or forest use). The reduction ends when the qualifying use ends, and back taxes come due. The lookback is five or ten years, depending on the program and zone. The bill lands on the owner at the time of the disqualifying act. The seller who collected the benefit owes nothing.

**Applied**

1. Ask the county assessor, before the offer, for the parcel's current potential-additional-tax figure. It is a maintained roll notation, not a mystery.
2. Do not assume the deferral discounted the asking price; sellers price on market potential, and no dataset shows enrolled parcels selling at a rollback-sized discount.
3. Plan the homesite carve-out (the site-plan section above): the dwelling disqualifies the homesite acreage at minimum, and a compact envelope keeps the remainder qualified.
4. Where the seller should bear the rollback, negotiate it expressly: the transaction discloses the assessment on the standard Special Tax Assessment Addendum (OREF Form 2.22 in practice), and a seller indemnification or price adjustment shifts the economic burden by contract. The county still collects from the owner of record at the triggering act; the clause creates a private reimbursement right, not a change of taxpayer.
5. For Small Tract Forestland parcels, confirm the program mechanics with the assessor before modeling the exposure; the STF option has its own two-track rule.

**Professional**

Disqualification triggers, per the Department of Revenue's farm-use guidance and county assessor practice: a sale or transfer where qualifying use ends, the land ceasing qualifying use (including failing an applicable income test), recording a subdivision or partition plat for the platted portion, and the owner's own request. A transfer alone does not disqualify while qualifying use continues; the assessment and its potential-additional-tax notation run with the land. Building a dwelling disqualifies at minimum the homesite.

The homesite mechanism is statutory, not assessor grace: ORS 308A.253 provides the homesite's own special valuation, and ORS 308A.259 keeps the remaining qualifying portion under special assessment when the homesite is disqualified, with no additional tax in most homesite cases; the rollback under ORS 308A.700 to 308A.733 attaches where a nonfarm dwelling is established under ORS 215.236 in an EFU zone. A partition that carves the building site off a larger deferred tract works the same way, remainder qualifying use continuing (the partition section below).

Program economics differ. Designated forestland is assessed at 100% of forestland special value; Small Tract Forestland is assessed at 20% of that value with an 80% severance tax due at harvest instead. The rollback exposure doubles for EFU farmland that stays outside a UGB after disqualification (ten years) versus every other category (five years); STF splits by destination, ten years when the land moves to another qualifying program and five on a full exit. The additional tax equals, for each lookback year, the difference between the special-assessment tax and the ordinary tax, for the lesser of the statutory maximum or the years the land qualified.

**Code**

```
The farm-use rollback lookback is ten or five years.
  Ten years for exclusive farm use zone farmland remaining outside an urban growth boundary after disqualification; five years for non-EFU farmland, Western and Eastern Oregon designated forestland, and EFU farmland inside a UGB; the additional tax is the yearly difference between assessed and special-assessment tax for the lesser of the maximum or the years qualified.
  Source: ORS 308A.703(3).

Small Tract Forestland has its own two-track lookback.
  Ten years where the disqualified land remains in another qualifying special-assessment program; five years where it exits special assessment entirely.
  Source: ORS 308A.707(2)(b), (3)(a)(B).

A homesite disqualification does not take down the tract.
  When a homesite is separated or disqualified, the remaining qualifying portion of the parcel continues to be valued under its special assessment; in most homesite cases no additional tax is imposed at disqualification.
  Source: ORS 308A.259; ORS 308A.253.
```

## 3.17 The Purchase Contract And Contingencies

**Simple**

The contingency clauses (contract conditions that let the buyer exit before a stated deadline) are the walk-away rights. Oregon courts enforce a satisfaction contingency on the buyer's genuine dissatisfaction alone. The buyer does not have to prove the dissatisfaction reasonable. The right disappears if its deadline passes without written action. A renegotiation right exists only if the contract provides it.

**Applied**

1. Tie each contingency to a named report or event: "subject to purchaser's approval of the septic site evaluation report," not "subject to purchaser's satisfaction" at large. The tested Oregon case enforced exactly that pattern.
2. Calendar every contingency deadline the day the contract signs. Failure to terminate by the deadline waives the contingency and accepts the condition; that is the standard Oregon mechanism, with no grace built in.
3. Write the price-renegotiation option in expressly if wanted: a stated window to request a credit or seller-funded remediation on a named report's result, with the walk-away right preserved if the seller declines. Without that text, a marginal septic or well result leaves two options only, proceed at the agreed price or terminate.
4. Set earnest money (the deposit the buyer forfeits or recovers under the contract terms) at the market pattern: 1-3% of price generally, or a flat $500-$2,500 on lower-priced rural land (2026). In competition, raise earnest money and offer closing-date flexibility before shortening the inspection period; a shorter inspection period is less protection by definition, not a free signal. Oregon's standard form defaults to 10 business days, and competitive offers commonly cut to 5-7.
5. Have an Oregon real estate attorney draft the contingency and any tiered structure (a short initial walk-away window, then staged deadlines for financing, title, and named reports). The tiered pattern is in current use; no rural-land-specific tested precedent exists to copy.
6. On a resource-zoned parcel, make the contract contingent on the county's written dwelling-path confirmation, and on any Measure 49 order's verification, with the deadlines set to the county's real turnaround times.

**Professional**

The tested case is Zygar v. Johnson, 169 Or App 638, 10 P3d 326 (2000). A handwritten condition, "subject to purchaser's approval of a pest and dryrot report," let the buyer terminate on genuine dissatisfaction with the report even though he also had unrelated reasons to exit. The court applied a subjective good-faith standard, holding that once one contractual source of dissatisfaction exists, other motives are immaterial. Secondary summaries describing an objective reasonable-basis standard are contradicted by the primary case text; the subjective standard is the more buyer-protective rule. The case also applied the rule that handwritten terms control conflicting printed boilerplate, which converts into drafting practice: the specifically negotiated, named-report condition outperformed the form language. "Sole and absolute discretion" clauses circulate in current templates, a Deschutes County purchase-and-sale template among them, and remain untested in published Oregon decisions. No published dataset documents what price cut or credit an adverse septic, well, or wetland finding wins in practice; the outcome is whatever the contingency text and the negotiation produce.
**Code**

```
Handwritten contract terms control printed terms.
  Where a contract mixes handwritten and printed provisions and they conflict, the handwritten language governs.
  Source: ORS 42.270.
```

## 3.18 The Due-Diligence Window

**Simple**

A typical Oregon raw-land inspection period runs 30 to 60 days. Every free screen runs in the first days. A bad parcel is eliminated at zero cost. The slow, paid items start immediately in parallel, not in sequence. The septic evaluation and the zoning letter are the slowest items. A 30-day window works only when both are ordered on day one.

**Applied**

Run the window on this schedule:

1. **Days 1-3.** Order the preliminary title report. In parallel, run every free screen: county GIS (zoning, tax lot, overlays), the Measure 49 Analyzer where relevant, DOGAMI SLIDO, the FEMA flood lookup, the wetlands inventory, the OWRD well-log and administrative-area search, the NRCS soil survey, and Your DEQ Online for the parcel and neighbors.
2. **Days 3-10.** Request the county zoning confirmation letter (often a paid, multi-week item; start it early). Schedule the septic site evaluation with the onsite-program authority; it is the longest-lead paid item and gates everything.
3. **Days 7-20.** Order the boundary survey where the legal description or access is at all ambiguous. Commission a wetland delineation where the parcel holds forest cover, seasonal wet ground, or drainage features, whatever the map showed.
4. **Days 15-30.** Get the well drawdown and yield test on an existing well, or a driller's opinion for a new one. Send every hazard flag to its professional: geotechnical for a SLIDO hit, elevation certificate for a flood-zone hit, Phase 1 assessment for an environmental hit.
5. **Days 30-45+.** Read every returned report against the severity bands, renegotiate where the contract allows, and exercise or waive each contingency before its deadline.

For a tax-foreclosure or auction parcel with only the roughly four-week notice window, the compressed version: pull the recorded-document chain yourself at the county clerk's index, run the OWRD well query (immediate, statewide), request county septic records (online for Curry, Jackson, and Josephine; by direct request elsewhere), and get a planner's phone confirmation of zoning, while treating all of it as screening short of title insurance and a real site evaluation.

**Professional**

The sequencing rule is fixed-and-free first, with paid-and-slow items started in parallel. Every screening tool in the list above is free and same-day. Any one of them can end the deal before a dollar is spent. The free tools have the reliability limits stated in the hazard-screening section above. A clean free pass therefore clears the parcel for paid follow-up, not for closing. No single published checklist combines these steps. Each step above is individually sourced earlier in this chapter, and the order shifts toward whichever risk is most likely to end the specific deal.

## 3.19 Financing And Alternative Purchase Structures

**Simple**

Loans on raw land cost more than home loans. Down payments run higher, rates run higher, and fewer lenders offer them. Some purchase structures add a second problem. A land sale contract leaves legal title with the seller, and construction lenders need title. The structure chosen at purchase decides how the build gets financed years later.

**Applied**

1. Get land-loan quotes before the offer, from lenders that work rural Oregon: AgWest Farm Credit markets land and construction products for rural parcels, including agricultural-zoned and bare land (farmcredit.com/states/oregon locates the association for a county), and local credit unions and community banks handle most of the rest. Expect a passed septic evaluation to function as a lending gate.
2. For seller financing, verify the seller can deliver title: the preliminary title report shows any underlying mortgage; require written disclosure of its lender, balance, and due-on-sale status; route payments through a licensed escrow or collection agent that verifies the underlying loan stays current; and record the contract or a memorandum of it immediately.
3. In any land sale contract, include a written prepayment right without penalty, a stated tax-and-insurance payment procedure, and a default-cure procedure that references and does not shorten the statutory notice-and-cure timeline. The statute overrides contrary contract text anyway; the clause states the binding timeline inside the contract itself, where both parties read it.
4. Prefer a note and trust deed over a land sale contract when construction financing is a near-term goal; the trust deed conveys title at closing, and the construction lender can take its position.
5. Before bidding at any tax-foreclosure or sheriff sale, engage the title company and the construction lender and ask two questions: will a policy issue on this quitclaim chain after the sale, and is a quiet-title action required first. Budget the answer.
6. For a lease-with-purchase-option, put every pre-option site activity (soil test pits, septic evaluation, any ground disturbance) in writing as landowner consent; an ordinary lease does not imply the right to dig.
7. For co-ownership, sign a written agreement before the deed records: buyout formula (appraisal plus first refusal), pro-rata expense sharing, a default remedy for a non-paying co-owner, and an exit process. Attorney-drafted agreements of this kind run roughly $10,000-$15,000 (2026, a general figure). Without one, any co-tenant can force a judicial partition or sale at any time.
8. For a family transfer, bring the donor's original purchase price and date, any prior gift-tax filings, and a current appraisal to a CPA; the questions are the Form 709 filing threshold, carryover basis on a future sale, and whether an appraisal documents the transfer.

**Professional**

Institutional land-loan terms (secondary-source snapshots, 2026, not quotes): down payments of 20-50%, with raw unimproved land at 35-50%. Rates run 1-3 percentage points above conventional mortgages, roughly 7.0-8.5% against a 5.875% conventional benchmark early in 2026. No source documents a rate premium keyed to zoning class. Development readiness (confirmed septic, utilities, access) drives terms more than the zone label. Resource-zone dwelling restrictions plausibly affect underwriting through buildability. No dataset of closed Oregon seller-financed transactions exists, and seller terms are not reliably cheaper than institutional terms. Balloon payments (a large final payment due when the term ends) and vague terms are the documented hazards.

A land sale contract (contract for deed) leaves legal title with the seller until payoff. The buyer holds equitable title and receives a fulfillment deed at the end. A construction lender secures against recorded legal title, so an outstanding land sale contract blocks or complicates construction lending until paid off or restructured. The forfeiture protections are statutory and non-waivable: ORS 93.910 permits forfeiture only after the ORS 93.915 notice, notwithstanding contrary contract text. The notice is served on the buyer, occupants, and recorded notice-requesters. The cure period runs 60 to 120 days, keyed to how much of the price is paid. No receiver may be appointed over an occupied principal residence. The second risk is the seller's own mortgage: a land sale contract typically triggers its due-on-sale clause. A seller default leaves the buyer's later-recorded equitable interest junior to the lender's lien, with the land and payments both losable. Title verification, escrowed payments, and immediate recording are the working protections.
Tax-foreclosed county land sells "as is, where is" by quitclaim deed (a deed conveying only the county's interest, with no title warranty). The ORS chapter 312 foreclosure vests title in the county free of liens except assessments. Sales run under ORS chapter 275, with a published list roughly four weeks ahead of auction. Unsold parcels move to a standing surplus list. What survives the foreclosure: governmental assessments (irrigation and special districts) by the statute's own carve-out, and recorded easements and covenants, which run with the land.

A title company can insure a quitclaim chain after its own search, sometimes behind a quiet-title action with its own time and legal cost. No documented owner-builder financing case exists either way. Redemption turns on the sale type, not on vacancy. A nonjudicial trustee's sale (the common mortgage foreclosure) has no post-sale redemption. A sheriff's execution sale after judicial foreclosure allows redemption within 180 days for the judgment debtor and 60 days for lien claimants. A construction lender's title policy also addresses Oregon's 75-day construction-lien recording window once work begins; the legal chapter contains lien law.

A lease-with-purchase-option requires a writing signed by the seller. An option without its own stated duration expires with the lease. Rent credits toward price are forfeited when the option lapses. Tax and insurance responsibility during the lease is a negotiated term with no default. No documented Oregon rural-land lease-option transaction supplied model terms; all four core terms (price-setting method, rent-credit percentage, option period, tax and insurance) are open negotiation.

Co-ownership defaults to tenancy in common (ORS 93.180). Survivorship exists only by express deed language. Absent a signed agreement, ORS 105.205 lets any co-tenant maintain a partition suit, with court-appointed referees dividing or selling the property. A buyout mechanism applies where division would cause great prejudice, and costs are allocated under ORS 105.405. No aggregate Oregon cost or duration data exists for partition suits; both scale with the dispute.

Family transfers follow federal gift-tax mechanics (2026 figures, legislatively changeable). Gifts above the $19,000 annual exclusion per recipient per donor require a Form 709 filing, applied against the $15,000,000 lifetime exemption before any tax is owed. Gifted land takes the donor's carryover basis, preserving the built-in gain, where inherited land steps up at death. A below-market sale is a part-gift for the discount and a cost basis at the price paid. A life-estate-and-remainder arrangement leaves present possession with the life tenant, and the remainderman builds only with the life tenant's written consent. Whether a remainder interest satisfies "owns" in the owner-builder exemption (ORS 701.010(7)) is undefined in the statute. An attorney reviews the deed language, the consent, and the occupancy plan together. The county building department confirms who the acceptable permit applicant is. The legal chapter contains the owner-builder exemption in full; the project-finance chapter contains construction-loan sequencing.

**Code**

```
Land-sale-contract forfeiture requires statutory notice, always.
  A seller may enforce forfeiture only after the statutory notice of default, notwithstanding any contract provision to the contrary.
  Source: ORS 93.910.

The default notice and cure period are fixed by statute.
  Written notice served on the buyer, property occupants, and recorded notice-requesters; the cure period runs 60 to 120 days, keyed to the share of the purchase price already paid; no receiver over an occupied principal residence.
  Source: ORS 93.915.

Sheriff's execution sales carry redemption windows; trustee's sales do not.
  A judgment debtor may redeem within 180 days of an execution sale and a lien claimant within 60 days, vacant or occupied alike; a completed nonjudicial trustee's sale has no statutory redemption.
  Source: ORS 18.964 (ORS 18.960-18.983); ORS 86.705-86.815.

Tax foreclosure vests title in the county nearly clean.
  The county takes title free from liens and encumbrances except assessments; subsequent sale runs under the county-property statutes, with published notice before auction.
  Source: ORS chapter 312; ORS chapter 275.

Co-ownership defaults to tenancy in common.
  A conveyance to two or more unmarried persons creates a tenancy in common unless the deed clearly states survivorship intent.
  Source: ORS 93.180.

Any co-tenant can force partition or sale by suit.
  A tenant in common may maintain a suit for partition, or for sale where partition would cause great prejudice, with court-determined buyout and cost allocation provisions.
  Source: ORS 105.205; ORS 105.210; ORS 105.405.
```

## 3.20 Partitions, Property-Line Adjustments, And Parcel Assembly

**Simple**

A partition creates new legal parcels. A property-line adjustment moves a shared boundary without creating one. The adjustment is faster and cheaper; the partition is a full land-use review. Combining parcels to qualify for a dwelling takes a formal consolidation, not just common ownership.

**Applied**

1. Search the parcel's land-use file and title chain for conditions from any earlier partition before counting on a future one; a recorded no-further-division condition is binding until proven released.
2. Budget the survey as the dominant partition cost and schedule driver: Oregon surveyors commonly run about six weeks to start fieldwork and roughly eight more to a recordable plat.
3. Plan an uncontested minor partition at about four to six months start to finish; county processing fees commonly run on the order of $100 per new parcel plus a base application fee, county-specific. Confirm the fee schedule, hearing requirement, and current timeline with the county planning department and surveyor's office.
4. Use a property-line adjustment where the change only moves a boundary the two parcels share; expect administrative processing (Portland's published example runs six to eight weeks) with a surveyor's legal description, at county-specific fees.
5. When assembling adjacent resource parcels toward a dwelling acreage threshold, acquire common ownership first, then complete the consolidation application; the combined acreage counts only after the consolidated parcel records.

**Professional**

A minor partition divides land into three or fewer parcels. Four or more in a calendar year is a subdivision. The plat must be made by a registered professional land surveyor. The jurisdiction decides a complete application within the 120-day statutory ceiling (the city statute; counties run under an analogous deadline). Staffing sets the real pace. A property-line adjustment relocates or eliminates a boundary common to abutting parcels and by definition creates no new unit of land. LUBA enforces the distinction strictly. Adjusting only shared boundaries qualifies (McKay Creek Valley Assoc. v. Washington County, 24 Or LUBA 187 (1992)). Moving whole parcels or non-common lines is a partition or replat even when the parcel count never changes (Goddard v. Jackson County, 34 Or LUBA 402 (1998)). Consolidation for dwelling eligibility runs through the same adjustment process. Where lots are relied on together for a minimum-acreage dwelling standard, county practice under ORS chapter 215 requires their consolidation into a single parcel. The consolidation is a condition of approval. A homesite partition off a farm- or forest-deferred tract pairs with the homesite assessment mechanics in the rollback-tax section. The remainder keeps its special assessment while it keeps its qualifying use.

**Code**

```
Partition plats are surveyed documents.
  The partition plat is made by a registered professional land surveyor and recorded with its legal descriptions.
  Source: ORS 92.050.

A property-line adjustment creates no new parcel.
  An adjustment relocates or eliminates a common boundary between abutting properties without creating an additional unit of land; parcel sizes after adjustment answer to the zoning ordinance.
  Source: ORS 92.010; ORS 92.192.

Land-use applications have a decision deadline.
  A final decision issues within 120 days of a complete application (the city statute; counties operate under an analogous deadline).
  Source: ORS 227.178.
```

## 3.21 The Purchase-Decision Review

Before the offer becomes binding, run the severity sort one more time.

| Severity | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Dealbreaker | Do not buy unless solved before closing | No legal dwelling path; failed septic evaluation with no workable alternative; no legal access; no usable water; prohibitive recorded restriction |
| Expensive risk | Possible, but priced into the offer | Long utility extension; major driveway work; difficult drainage; uncertain title issue; marginal septic needing a $20,000-$50,000 system |
| Design constraint | The house adapts | Slope, snow load, wind exposure, solar orientation, staging limits, floodplain |
| Lifestyle tradeoff | The land works, daily life changes | Long drive, weak internet, winter access, cellular dead zones |
| Future-flexibility check | Initial plan supported, later plans maybe not | Outbuilding limits, ADU rules, home-occupation limits, equipment storage |

Then confirm every item on this list before signing. Weigh each item by its severity band: the zoning, size, septic, water, access, and title items are the dealbreaker class; the rest are risks, constraints, and tradeoffs to price rather than reasons to walk.

- zoning allows the intended primary residence
- on farm or forest zoning, the specific dwelling path confirmed by the county in writing
- any Measure 49 authorization verified against its final order and completion clock
- parcel size meets the zoning minimum
- setbacks leave room for house, driveway, septic, well, drainage, and staging
- height limits fit the intended house
- septic feasibility confirmed, if sewer is unavailable
- water source confirmed
- groundwater administrative-area status checked with OWRD
- electrical service available, or the extension cost accepted in writing
- road access legal, physical, usable year-round, and achievable under the controlling fire-access standard
- internet acceptable for the project and for daily use
- title report free of dealbreakers
- easements, conservation restrictions, and CC&Rs understood and acceptable
- mineral, timber, and water rights understood
- floodplain and drainage exposure acceptable
- wetland screen run, and a delineation done where the ground suggested one
- environmental history checked for the parcel and its neighbors
- climate design values obtainable for the parcel
- wildfire-hardening status confirmed with the jurisdiction
- an insurance quote in hand for any wildfire-zone parcel
- special-assessment rollback exposure quantified by the assessor
- financing pre-qualified for the land itself, with the purchase structure chosen for the later construction loan
- topography supports excavation, drainage, staging, and delivery
- solar orientation works, if solar is in the plan
- distance to town, services, and emergency response acceptable
- outbuilding, ADU, and home-occupation rules fit the long-term plan

Treat a risk that cannot be priced, permitted, engineered, insured, accessed, or inspected as a dealbreaker until a written answer resolves it.
