# Chapter 20: Materials, Suppliers, Procurement, And Substitution

This chapter turns the design into material orders without letting a sales counter, a shortage, a substitute product, or an old price quietly change the house. It contains the verification principle that keeps a specification durable, the substitution-authority framework that governs who may approve a change, a material-standards reference by trade, the owner-manufactured-block decision, the product-listing and documentation chain, jobsite chemical safety, energy incentives, and the supplier and procurement process.

The content divides into the verifiable and the volatile. Material standards, product-listing rules, substitution authority, batch-ticket requirements, and hazard-communication rules are stable enough to specify in writing. Prices, lead times, supplier availability, rebate dollar amounts, and current product-listing numbers change too quickly to fix in the manuscript and are re-verified at the estimating pass and again at order time.

## 16.1 The Verification Principle

The specification names the standard the material must meet, not the brand that meets it today. A standard is durable across the design-to-build window; a brand and part number can become wrong with a single reformulation.

**Simple**

Every block, pipe, wire, membrane, valve, and fastener becomes part of the house. A wrong material can create a leak, a crack, or a failed inspection. Three written facts keep an order from drifting: the standard the material must meet, the reason it was chosen, and who may approve a change. The actual product and price get verified at the time of purchase.

**Applied**

Sort every material decision into three reference tiers:

- Permanent references (do not revisit per build): the consensus-standard families (ASTM, ACI, AWS, NSF/ANSI, IAPMO, AHRI, NEMA, AAMA/WDMA/CSA, UL, ICC-ES). These do not move quarter to quarter.
- Slow-moving references (revisit per code cycle): the Oregon code adoptions and their amendment tables. Revisit when the next adoption is announced.
- Volatile data (verify per build, never fixed in the spec text): unit prices, supplier minimums, lead times, rebate amounts, and current product-listing numbers. Treat any older estimate as a historical note until replaced by a dated quote.

Write the standard into the spec ("concrete conforming to ASTM C94/C94M, designed per ACI 318 and ACI 301, with batch tickets per ASTM C94 Section 14"), not the brand and mix number.

**Professional**

The material system connects design intent, code compliance, product listings, manufacturer instructions, supplier capability, installation method, warranty, inspection evidence, and future maintenance. A concrete house with a long-service goal depends on compatibility across the concrete, reinforcement, waterproofing, insulation, drainage, and mechanical systems, so a cheaper substitute is evaluated against the assembly it joins, not in isolation. The specification anchors to the standard so the document stays defensible while the market underneath it changes.

**Code**

```
Referenced standards are enforceable code text.
  A standard referenced in the ORSC is part of the code to the prescribed extent of the reference.
  Source: ORSC R102.5 (2023 edition).
```

## 16.2 Substitution Authority

A change to a specified material is approved only by the authority whose scope covers it, and there are five distinct authorities. Confusing them is the most common procurement error on an owner-builder job.

**Simple**

Five different parties can say yes or no to a material change, and they are not interchangeable. The building official rules on code alternatives. The engineer rules on anything under their stamp. The manufacturer rules on what keeps a warranty alive. The inspector only checks what was already approved. The seller at the counter is none of these.

**Applied**

Match every substitution to its authority:

- The building official approves an alternative material, design, or method that departs from the prescriptive code (ORSC R104.11), on evidence of equivalence.
- The inspector enforces what the building official approved and cannot approve a field substitution; a change to an approved detail goes back through the building official.
- The design professional in responsible charge approves substitutions within the scope of any PE-stamped work before the building official accepts them.
- The manufacturer's warranty controls its listed assembly: substituting a fastener, primer, or accessory not in the installation instructions can void the warranty even where the substitute meets the standard.
- The installer carries independent professional liability for what they install for pay, even when the owner or designer approved the product.

Tag each material spec with its substitution tier: no substitution (Tier 1), substitution with written designer and manufacturer approval (Tier 2), equivalent product with documentation (Tier 3), or no approval required (Tier 4).

**Professional**

ORSC R104.11 makes the building official the only authority who can accept an alternative that departs from the prescriptive code. The alternative must be at least equivalent in quality, strength, effectiveness, fire resistance, durability, and safety, with test data under R104.11.1 or a documented history of successful use. A denial can be escalated to the Building Codes Division for a statewide alternate-method ruling under OAR 918-008-0095, which binds every Oregon jurisdiction but takes months. Prescriptive paths and the code's alternative appendices are used first, and statewide petitions are reserved for what the design genuinely requires. ORSC R102.4 gives the manufacturer's listing precedence where a code provision would violate the listing, which is the rule that makes warranty-critical assemblies (waterproofing, fenestration, roofing, manufactured veneer) name the listed accessory by part number and document any substitution with the designer and the manufacturer's representative.

**Code**

```
The building official rules on alternatives to the prescriptive code.
  Equivalence in quality, strength, effectiveness, fire resistance, durability, and safety; evidence by test data or documented history of use; tests at no expense to the municipality.
  Source: ORSC R104.11 and R104.11.1 (2023 edition).

A denied alternative can be escalated to a statewide ruling.
  BCD alternate-method ruling binds every Oregon jurisdiction once issued; expect months of process.
  Source: OAR 918-008-0095; OAR 918-008-0110.

Stamped work keeps its design authority.
  Substitutions within the scope of PE-stamped work are approved by the design professional in responsible charge before the building official accepts them.
  Source: ORSC R106.3.4 (2023 edition).

The manufacturer's listing outranks a conflicting code provision.
  Where a code provision would violate the listing, the listing and the manufacturer's instructions control.
  Source: ORSC R102.4, Exception (2023 edition).
```

## 16.3 Material Standards Reference By Trade

Each trade's materials anchor to a family of consensus standards. The specification cites these; the specific product and lot number live in the submittal log, not the manuscript. This reference supports writing a durable spec and checking a submittal without opening each standard.

**Simple**

Every material in the house conforms to a published standard. The table below lists the governing standards for each trade. The specification cites the standard; the actual product and lot number get recorded at purchase.

## 16.4 Owner-Manufactured Block: The CMU Make-Versus-Buy Decision

An owner-builder can manufacture concrete masonry units on site with a block press, and Oregon permits it when the units are proven to the masonry-unit standard. There is no prohibition and no shortcut: a block is a block if it tests as one, and the owner builds the same paper trail a commercial plant maintains. The economics rarely justify it, and this section states both the path and the price.

**Simple**

A machine that presses concrete blocks can be bought for a few thousand dollars. The law does not care who made a block. What the law cares about is proof: lab tests showing the blocks meet the same standard as factory block. A factory block delivered to the site costs about $1.60-$2.25 (2026). Making your own means buying equipment, developing a mix, curing thousands of blocks, and paying a lab, all to replace a $2 item. It makes sense only where delivery is nearly impossible.

**Applied**

If owner-manufactured CMU is considered:

- Hold a pre-application meeting with the building official for the specific county before committing, and get the accepted testing program, lot definition, and sampling frequency in writing.
- Propose the commercial-style acceptance package proactively: a written production and quality plan (mix design, material sources, lot definition, curing regime, record forms), pre-production qualification tests, per-lot acceptance testing, and records delivered with each lot.
- Test at an accredited laboratory (evaluated per ASTM C1093; ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation is the practical marker): unit sampling and testing to ASTM C140 (about six units of identical configuration per lot in industry practice, split between compression and absorption/density), plus the ASTM C426 drying-shrinkage test on the developed mix.
- Develop the dry-cast (zero-slump) mix with trial batches: no authoritative published recipe exists, machine-maker proportions are starting points, and the mix is proven only by the lab results.
- Use a pan mixer for the zero-slump mix (a drum mixer cannot mix it), and plan the power supply: many machines want three-phase power, which on a rural site means a generator, a phase converter, or a diesel-engine machine.
- Cure at owner scale: protect green block from rain, sun, wind, and frost, cover and mist, do not move or stack for about 7 days, and count strength gain toward 28 days before load-bearing use.
- Keep production a full cured, lab-passed lot ahead of the masons; a failed lot discovered after laying means demolishing the wall it went into.
- Compare honestly against commercial block before deciding: delivered 8x8x16 gray block runs roughly $1.60-$2.25 and retail $1.25-$3.00 (2026), with the plant's compliance already documented.

**Professional**

ORSC R606.2.1 requires load-bearing concrete masonry units to conform to ASTM C90, and the standard is product-neutral: nothing in R606 restricts who manufactures the unit. Units that test as C90-compliant are C90 units and enter the normal R606/TMS 402 design path; the alternative-method section (R104.11) is the fallback only where acceptance is sought on some basis other than full compliance, which is a weaker position. The building official's lever is R104.11.1, the authority to require tests as evidence of compliance at no expense to the municipality, plus R104.4 acceptance of certified third-party reports; a formal special-inspection program is an OSSC concept that does not apply under the ORSC.

The controlling values: C90 sets a minimum net-area compressive strength of 2,000 psi (raised from 1,900 in the 2014 revision), maximum water absorption by density class (lightweight under 105 pcf, medium 105-125, normal 125 and over), and dimensional tolerance of plus or minus 1/8 inch on width, height, and length. C90 also caps linear drying shrinkage at 0.065 percent, measured by ASTM C426. The shrinkage test is the hidden hurdle: it is a weeks-long laboratory test that commercial producers run once per mix design, and an owner either commissions it on the developed mix or persuades the official to waive it. C90 contains no freeze-thaw test and no exposure classes; durability rides on density, absorption, cement content, and wall detailing, so an east-of-Cascades project specifies normal-weight units with low absorption results as a performance argument, not as a C90 class citation. Height is the variable dimension on vibro-compaction machines (fill and compaction control it), and under-compacted owner block trends light, porous, and high-absorption, which is the most common test failure after low strength.

The equipment and campaign: residential-scale machines are import-class, from manual vibrating machines (roughly $1,000-$4,500) through mobile egg-layer hydraulic machines (about $3,500-$4,500, which lay blocks on a cured production slab) to automatic plants ($11,500 and up) that exceed single-house scale. A pan mixer (about $4,000-$10,000) is required for the mix. A single-story CMU dwelling needs on the order of 2,500-5,000 units, and a defensible owner production rate is 150-300 blocks per day with 7-day cure holds, so block production is a multi-week to multi-month campaign that leads the masonry schedule. Laboratory acceptance runs from a few hundred dollars to low four figures per lot. West of the Cascades, rain protection governs the campaign; east of the Cascades, green block freezes, so production shrinks to roughly late spring through early fall or moves under heated cover. No published case documents an owner-manufactured structural-CMU dwelling clearing plan review in Oregon, so the owner taking this path is breaking procedural ground with the front-loaded testing package above. The per-lot economics stated in the Simple block are why this project prices commercial block first and treats the press as an edge-case tool.

**Code**

```
Load-bearing concrete masonry units conform to the unit standard, regardless of manufacturer.
  ASTM C90: minimum net-area compressive strength 2,000 psi; absorption limits by density class (lightweight under 105 pcf, medium 105-125, normal 125+); dimensions within 1/8 inch; linear drying shrinkage not more than 0.065 percent (ASTM C426).
  Source: ORSC R606.2.1 (2023 edition); ASTM C90; ASTM C426.

Compliance is demonstrated by accredited-laboratory testing.
  Sampling and testing per ASTM C140 (about six units per lot in industry practice; per-lot counts confirmed against the purchased current edition); laboratory evaluated per ASTM C1093.
  Source: ASTM C140; ASTM C140 Note 1 (ASTM C1093).

The building official may require the testing and accepts the reports.
  Tests as evidence of compliance at no expense to the municipality; written certified reports of approved agencies accepted; program confirmed at a pre-application meeting for the project county.
  Source: ORSC R104.11.1 and R104.4 (2023 edition).

The masonry assembly follows the structural standards regardless of unit source.
  Design per ORSC R606 and TMS 402; mortar ASTM C270, grout ASTM C476; specification per TMS 602 via the OSSC.
  Source: ORSC R606 (2023 edition); TMS 402/602.
```

## 16.5 Product Listing, Labels, And The Documentation Chain

A material's compliance is proved by an unbroken document chain from specification to closeout. The label disappears into the work, so the photograph taken before cover is often the only remaining evidence.

**Simple**

Every material arrives with proof on it: a listing mark, a batch number, a label. The proof gets buried in the wall with the product. So the label is photographed before the work is covered, and the photo joins the permanent record. Years later, that photo is the answer to "what exactly is in there?"

**Applied**

Carry each material through the chain:

- At specification: cite the standard and the listing (for example, "Type X gypsum, ASTM C1396, UL-listed for the U419 assembly").
- At submittal: submit the product cut sheet showing the standard, listing mark, and any ICC-ES number; the designer or building official approves.
- At delivery: verify the product matches the submittal and photograph the batch numbers and listing labels.
- At install: keep the packaging label until the relevant inspection is signed off; photograph it before it is covered.
- At inspection: record the date and the products the inspector verified.
- At closeout: assemble the binder.

**Professional**

The closeout binder is the defensible record of how the house was built, and it holds the approved construction documents, the submittal approvals, the ICC-ES reports current at install, every concrete batch ticket, the product cut sheets and label photographs, the AHRI and NFRC labels, the equipment startup reports, the warranty registrations, the alternate-method approvals, the inspection records, and the required test reports (concrete strength, soil bearing, blower door, and the energy-compliance forms). Batch tickets are retained in full because they are the substantive evidence of what was delivered if a structural question arises; the paper copies fade, so each is photographed on site and stored digitally. The binder is kept as a paper copy in a fireproof location and as a digital copy in two independent locations, and it is the evidence the business-and-brand and documentation chapters rely on.

**Code**

```
Concrete delivery tickets contain the specified content and are retained.
  Batch-ticket content per ASTM C94 Section 14; slump, air, and temperature verification per ACI 301 within the discharge window.
  Source: ASTM C94/C94M, Section 14; ACI 301.

The closeout record is assembled and duplicated.
  Approved documents, submittals, evaluation reports, batch tickets, label photographs, AHRI/NFRC labels, startup reports, warranties, alternate-method approvals, inspection records, and test reports; paper in a fireproof location plus digital in two independent locations.
  Source: project documentation standard (see the documentation chapter).
```

## 16.6 Jobsite Chemical Safety: SDS And Hazard Communication

The moment a paid worker steps onto the site, Oregon's hazard-communication rules attach, and several common construction chemicals carry real exposure hazards. For the owner's own labor the rules are not a regulatory requirement, but the safety-data-sheet information is still how the exposure is managed.

**Simple**

Every chemical product on site comes with a safety data sheet: foam, sealant, acid, epoxy, admixture. The sheet states what the product does to a person and how to handle it. The sheet is free and the habit is cheap: read it before opening the container. Once anyone is paid to work on site, keeping those sheets available stops being a habit and becomes law.

**Applied**

- Where any paid helper, employee, or subcontractor is on site, maintain a written hazard-communication program, a chemical inventory, and a safety data sheet for every hazardous chemical, accessible during all shifts (a digital copy is acceptable if it opens without an internet connection).
- Read the safety data sheet for storage temperature, shelf life, ventilation, personal protective equipment, and disposal before using a product.
- Use the manufacturer-specified respiratory protection and reentry time for spray polyurethane foam.
- Control respirable silica when cutting, drilling, or grinding concrete or masonry with water suppression or local exhaust, and use the specified respiratory protection.
- Use eye protection and acid-resistant gloves for masonry acids and skin protection for two-part epoxies.

**Professional**

OR-OSHA's Hazard Communication standard (OAR 437 Division 2/Z and Division 3/D, GHS-aligned since 2012) requires the written program, inventory, safety data sheets, labels, and worker training whenever workers other than the owner are on site. A federal 2024 update to GHS Revision 7 is being evaluated by OR-OSHA, so the 2012 baseline remains the enforced training level as of writing. The respirable-crystalline-silica standard (29 CFR 1926.1153, enforced by OR-OSHA under OAR 437 Division 3) requires Table 1 controls for concrete and masonry cutting and grinding. The safety data sheet's sections on handling, exposure controls, and disposal drive the practical decisions (foam reentry time, admixture alkali-burn protection, coating ventilation and ignition control, and epoxy skin sensitization).

**Code**

```
Hazard communication attaches when any paid worker is on site.
  Written program, chemical inventory, safety data sheet access during all shifts, GHS labels, and training.
  Source: OAR 437, Division 2/Z and Division 3/D (OR-OSHA Hazard Communication).

Respirable silica from concrete and masonry work is controlled.
  Table 1 controls (water suppression or local exhaust with specified respirators) for cutting, drilling, and grinding.
  Source: 29 CFR 1926.1153, enforced by OR-OSHA under OAR 437, Division 3.

The safety data sheet is the product's handling specification.
  Storage, shelf life, ventilation, personal protective equipment, and disposal per the 16-section sheet (Sections 7, 8, 9, 13, 16).
  Source: OR-OSHA Hazard Communication (GHS-format SDS).
```

## 16.7 Energy Incentives

Incentive programs and their dollar amounts are the most volatile content in the chapter, so the specification captures the eligibility framework and defers every dollar figure to the estimating pass. Equipment is specified to keep the incentive doors open rather than committed to one program.

**Simple**

The federal tax credits that paid for heat pumps and solar ended for equipment placed in service after 2025. A build finished later cannot claim them. State and utility programs continue, and their amounts change yearly. The safe pattern: buy certified equipment that qualifies for whatever exists, and check the current programs the season you buy, not the year you design.

**Applied**

- Specify equipment that is AHRI-listed and, where the category has it, ENERGY STAR-certified, which keeps most incentive paths open at a small cost premium.
- Build an equipment-decision spreadsheet at estimating with three columns: AHRI/ENERGY STAR status, current incentive eligibility per program (pulled from current program documents), and net post-incentive cost.
- Do not budget any specific rebate dollar amount into a build until the administering agency confirms the owner-builder install pattern qualifies in writing.
- Confirm program availability at the time of the build; funding tranches can close mid-quarter.

**Professional**

The federal Section 25C and 25D residential energy credits ended for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, so a build completed in 2026 or later cannot claim them, and no older source that assumes otherwise is relied on. The IRA-funded HOMES and HEAR rebate programs are state-administered through the Oregon Department of Energy with a phased launch and generally require an enrolled contractor, which makes the owner-builder pattern awkward for some measures; agency confirmation in writing precedes any budgeting. The state Heat Pump Purchase Program and the utility-funded Energy Trust of Oregon incentives continue independent of the federal sunset, with Energy Trust offering measure-based incentives that update at least annually. The durable rule is to specify to the standards and pull the current incentive documents at estimating; the frameworks are stable, the dollars and funding flags are not.

**Code**

```
The federal residential energy credits ended for later-completed builds.
  Sections 25C and 25D unavailable for property placed in service after December 31, 2025.
  Source: Internal Revenue Code Sections 25C and 25D, as amended (sunset date).

The state-administered rebate programs carry an enrolled-contractor pattern.
  HOMES and HEAR through the Oregon Department of Energy; owner-builder qualification confirmed with the agency in writing before budgeting.
  Source: Oregon Department of Energy HOMES/HEAR program documents (current at estimating).

State and utility incentives continue and update.
  Oregon Heat Pump Purchase Program; Energy Trust of Oregon measure-based incentives, updated at least annually; amounts verified at estimating.
  Source: current program documents at the estimating pass.
```

## 16.8 Suppliers, Procurement, And Buy-Versus-Rent

Supplier names, lead times, and minimums change too quickly to fix in the manuscript, so the chapter captures the procurement process and the buy-versus-rent framework. Anyone paid to do licensed work on the site carries the corresponding license and workers' compensation.

**Simple**

Suppliers are verified fresh for each build. Who serves the site, what they stock, the minimum order, and the delivery time all change. Long-lead items like windows and fabricated steel get ordered months ahead. Tools follow one question: will it earn its keep across several phases, or is it cheaper to rent for the one week it works?

**Applied**

- At estimating, confirm for each material category: which plant or distributor serves the site, the standard compliance held, the minimum order and delivery terms, and the current lead time.
- Order long-lead items (fenestration, custom steel, special-order masonry, fabricated reinforcement) early and stage the balance to the pour and rough-in schedule.
- Buy tools used across more than one phase with low storage cost; rent equipment used once or twice with high storage and maintenance cost; hire-with-operator for crane, large excavation, and concrete pumping, where the operator carries the certification and insurance.
- Ensure anyone paid to perform CCB-licensed work holds a CCB license, and route unskilled labor through a temp agency that carries workers' compensation.

**Professional**

Ready-mix, aggregate, masonry, reinforcement, and structural steel are served by regional producers and fabricators; the balance of trades is served by national wholesalers with Oregon branches. The verification at estimating is the plant or distributor that serves the specific site, the mix designs or gradations they hold compliance for, the minimums and delivery cadence, and the return policy for unused stock. Rental yards that deliver and operate equipment are providing a service, so the operator's employer carries the workers' compensation and OR-OSHA training; hiring a friend to help without an agency is the workers'-compensation exposure documented in the legal chapter. The equipment selection for owner-operated machines is detailed with the build-sequence chapter.

**Code**

```
Paid construction work is licensed work.
  A person undertaking work as a contractor holds a CCB license; the owner exemption covers the owner's own labor, not paid helpers doing licensed work.
  Source: ORS 701.021(1); ORS 701.010(7) (see the legal chapter).

Paid labor carries workers' compensation.
  Temp-agency labor rides the agency's coverage; direct hires create employer obligations.
  Source: ORS chapter 656 (see the legal chapter).
```
