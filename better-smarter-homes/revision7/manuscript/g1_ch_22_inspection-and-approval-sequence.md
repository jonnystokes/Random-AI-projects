# Chapter 22: Inspection And Approval Sequence

This chapter specifies the inspection and approval process: the statutory basis for inspections, the full list of inspections a build faces, which authority handles each one, how to schedule and be ready, the rule that work stays exposed until it is approved, what each major inspection verifies, how corrections and appeals work, and the records the process produces. It is the inspection-readiness reference; the trade chapters state the technical detail of what is being inspected, and the build-sequence chapter states the build-order coordination.

The Oregon inspection system has one statutory backbone but several parallel inspecting authorities: the building department, separate plumbing and electrical inspectors, county environmental health for septic, the water resources department for wells, the road authority for the driveway, the serving utility for the meter, the fire authority for fire access, and approved third-party testers for the energy tests. The build has to be scripted around all of them, and the closeout package assembles the inspection records into one audit-ready binder before the certificate of occupancy can issue.

## 18.1 What Inspections Are And The Statutory Framework

An inspection is a third-party code check at a defined moment, before the work is covered. The law requires the work to stay exposed until it is approved and puts the duty to provide access on the permit holder.

**Simple**

An inspection is a pause point. An inspector checks the work before it gets covered, buried, energized, or occupied. A problem is caught while it is still reachable. The inspector is not the project's quality control; the owner-builder still owns daily quality, sequencing, and correction. The inspection is a code check at specific gates.

**Applied**

- Keep every element of the work accessible and exposed until it is approved; do not pour, backfill, insulate, drywall, energize, or occupy ahead of the required inspection.
- Provide the inspector with access, the approved documents, and any test setup.
- Plan the schedule so a failed inspection is a correction, not a crisis; do not stack the next phase so tightly that a re-work blows the schedule.

**Professional**

ORSC R109.1 requires that work for which a permit is required stay accessible and exposed until approved; work covered, backfilled, energized, or occupied before approval is presumptively non-compliant, and the building official may require it uncovered at the permit holder's expense. R109.3 puts the duty to provide access on the permit holder. R109.2 lets the building official accept reports from approved agencies (third-party testers, ICC-ES evaluation reports, and special inspectors), which is how the blower-door and duct-leakage results and engineering-intensive items enter the record without the official witnessing each test. Which jurisdiction administers the program is set by ORS 455.148 and 455.150; most populated Oregon jurisdictions run their own programs while the Building Codes Division provides direct service in some counties and runs the statewide programs.

**Code**

```
Permitted work stays accessible and exposed until approved.
  Work covered, backfilled, energized, or occupied before approval is presumptively non-compliant; the building official may require uncovering at the permit holder's expense.
  Source: ORSC R109.1 (2023 edition).

The permit holder provides access and means for inspection.
  Access, the approved documents, and the test setup are the permit holder's duty.
  Source: ORSC R109.3 (2023 edition).

Approved-agency reports can substitute for witnessed inspection.
  Third-party test reports, ICC-ES evaluation reports, and special-inspection reports accepted at the building official's discretion.
  Source: ORSC R109.2 (2023 edition).

Program administration and appeals have statutory anchors.
  Municipal inspection programs per ORS 455.148 and 455.150; appeals per ORS 455.690.
  Source: ORS 455.148; ORS 455.150; ORS 455.690.
```

## 18.2 The Inspection List

The statutory minimum inspection list is short, but a real build faces a much longer list across several authorities. The project's specific list is confirmed with the controlling jurisdiction at the design pass.

**Simple**

The code names only a handful of required inspections. The real build faces two dozen or more checkpoints. The septic, well, driveway, utility, fire, and energy approvals all count. The project keeps one list of all of them, with the authority and phone number for each.

**Applied**

Expect these inspection groups:

- Site and pre-construction (before the building permit issues): land-use sign-off, septic site-evaluation approval, well log on file, driveway approach permit, erosion-control approval, fire-access verification, and address posting.
- Foundation and below-grade: setback and staking, footing excavation, foundation reinforcement, under-slab, foundation drainage, waterproofing, and pre-backfill.
- Masonry and structural: masonry reinforcement and grout, bond beam and lintel, floor and wall and roof framing and sheathing, shear-wall nailing, and engineered-item documentation.
- Rough trades: rough plumbing with its pressure tests, rough mechanical, rough electrical, firestopping, and the combined frame inspection after the rough trades pass.
- Pre-cover and energy: insulation, air sealing or the blower-door result, vapor retarder, duct leakage, and the energy-compliance documentation.
- Final and occupancy: plumbing, electrical, and mechanical finals, the building final, and the temporary or full certificate of occupancy.

**Professional**

The statutory minimum under ORSC R109.1 is foundation, rough plumbing/electrical/mechanical, frame and masonry, insulation and vapor retarder, and final. The practical list adds the site and utility approvals, the separate energy tests, and the several authorities' inspections. The exact grouping (which inspections are combined or split) is a local-practice matter, so the project builds a specific inspection list at the design pass rather than assuming a standard sequence. This list and the build-sequence chapter's rough-in cascade describe the same sequence from two sides: that chapter orders the work, this chapter the approvals.

**Code**

```
The code's minimum inspection list is five stages.
  Foundation; rough plumbing, electrical, and mechanical; frame and masonry; insulation and vapor retarder; final.
  Source: ORSC R109.1 (2023 edition).
```

## 18.3 The Jurisdictional Matrix

Which authority handles which inspection depends on the jurisdiction, and the arrangement varies across Oregon. The project maps every inspection to its agency, contact, and scheduling method at the design pass.

**Simple**

There is no single inspector. The building department checks the structure, and a separate electrical inspector may work for the state. The county health office handles the septic. The power company will not set a meter without the electrical sign-off. The project's map of who inspects what is built once, early, and kept by the phone.

**Applied**

Build a project matrix naming the authority for each inspection:

- Building department (city, county, or the Building Codes Division where it administers): structural, framing, foundation, energy, fire-rated assemblies, final building, and occupancy.
- Plumbing inspector and electrical inspector: their own trade inspections, often on separate staff; the electrical program is run directly by the Building Codes Division in many parts of Oregon.
- County environmental health: septic site evaluation, install, and operation, under delegated DEQ authority.
- Water resources department: the well log and water rights (a documentation gate, not an on-site inspection).
- Road authority or ODOT: the driveway approach.
- Serving utility: the meter set and energization, after the electrical inspector signs off the service equipment.
- Fire authority: fire-access verification and any sprinkler work.
- Third-party tester: the blower-door and duct-leakage tests, and any special inspection.

**Professional**

The building department employs Oregon-certified building inspectors, and the plumbing, electrical, and mechanical disciplines may be on the same staff (cross-certified) or separate. The electrical program is frequently run by the Building Codes Division's regional offices in counties without their own program, so a build can have county plumbing and electrical inspectors and a state building inspector, or the reverse. County environmental health carries delegated DEQ authority for septic, and the serving utility energizes only after the electrical inspector's approval. Because the arrangement varies, the project matrix names the agency, contact, and scheduling channel for each inspection and is verified at the design pass.

**Code**

```
The inspecting authority varies by program and jurisdiction.
  Building department (local or BCD-administered); trade inspectors (electrical often BCD); county environmental health (septic, delegated DEQ); OWRD (well log); road authority (approach); serving utility (meter); fire authority (access, sprinklers); approved third-party testers (energy tests).
  Source: ORS 455.148 and 455.150 (program administration); project matrix verified at design pass.
```

## 18.4 Scheduling And Being Ready

An inspection is requested through the jurisdiction's channel and passes only when the work is complete, accessible, and backed by the documents the inspector needs on site. Being ready avoids a reinspection fee and a schedule stall.

**Simple**

An inspection passes when the work is done, reachable, and provable. The approved plans and permit card are on site. Any pressure test is already holding on the gauge, and someone who can answer questions is present. Calling before the work is ready buys a reinspection fee and a lost week.

**Applied**

- Request the inspection through the jurisdiction's channel (the statewide ePermitting system, a local portal, or phone), at least a day ahead unless same-day is reliably offered.
- Have on site: the approved construction documents, the posted permit placard, the inspection card, any test setup (gauges holding for a pressure test, ladder access to elevated work), and the documentation the item requires (an engineer's letter, an ICC-ES report, or the manufacturer's instructions where the listing controls).
- Be present or have someone competent to answer questions; inspectors defer or fail when no one can speak to the work.
- Confirm in writing what the inspection is for, photograph the signed inspection record after a pass, and read the inspector's notes after a failure for the specific correction.
- Do not call for an inspection until the work is complete and accessible.

**Professional**

Most Oregon jurisdictions accept requests through the Building Codes Division's statewide ePermitting system or a local portal, with same-day requests typically due by early morning and next-day requests by late afternoon the prior business day. Rural inspectors cover large areas on route schedules that compress availability, so the route schedule for the build's area is confirmed rather than any-day availability assumed. ORSC R106.3.1 and R105.7 require the approved documents and the permit placard on site, and R109.4 the signed inspection card where issued. A reinspection fee is charged when the work is not ready, not accessible, or a failed item is not corrected, so the discipline is to call only when the work is complete.

**Code**

```
The approved documents, placard, and card are on site.
  Approved construction documents until completion (R106.3.1); permit placard posted (R105.7); signed inspection card where issued (R109.4).
  Source: ORSC R106.3.1, R105.7, R109.4 (2023 edition).

Requests run through the jurisdiction's channel on its clock.
  Statewide ePermitting, local portal, or phone; same-day typically by early morning; rural route schedules confirmed for the build's area.
  Source: BCD ePermitting program; local jurisdiction scheduling practice.
```

## 18.5 The Cover-And-Conceal Rule

The single rule that governs the build flow is that no work is covered before it is approved. Where a temporary cover is unavoidable, it is documented with photographs, but structurally critical work is never covered without the inspector's approval.

**Simple**

Nothing gets covered before it passes. Concrete does not pour over unapproved rebar, backfill does not go against an unapproved wall, and drywall does not close an unapproved cavity. Covered-early work is treated as failed work: the official can order it opened at the owner's expense.

**Applied**

Do not, before the relevant inspection passes:

- Pour concrete over reinforcement, anchor bolts, holdowns, the vapor retarder, in-slab piping and conduit, or radiant tubing.
- Backfill against foundation walls until the foundation, drainage, and waterproofing inspections pass and the walls are braced.
- Insulate until the rough trades and framing inspections pass.
- Drywall until the insulation and any pre-cover inspections pass.
- Energize the electrical service until the electrical inspector signs off the service equipment.
- Pressurize plumbing or hydronic systems for service until the pressure test passes.
- Occupy any portion of the building until at least a temporary certificate of occupancy is granted.

Where a temporary cover is unavoidable (an overnight trench closure against weather), photograph the work from several angles with a date stamp and tell the inspector when scheduling; acceptance of photos in lieu of uncovering is the inspector's discretion, and rebar and anchor-bolt placement are never covered without approval.

**Professional**

ORSC R109.1 is the legal hook for the entire build flow, and the sequence in the build-sequence chapter exists to respect it. Most inspectors will accept clear, date-stamped photographic evidence for routine items covered out of necessity, but this is discretionary and not a right, and anything structurally critical stays exposed until approved. Temporary construction power is limited to 180 days and does not become permanent without the trade inspector's specific sign-off.

**Code**

```
No work is covered before approval.
  The binding build-flow rule; photographic evidence for necessity covers is discretionary; structurally critical work never covered without approval.
  Source: ORSC R109.1 (2023 edition).

Temporary power has its own clock and sign-off.
  Construction power limited to 180 days; permanent energization only on the electrical inspector's approval of the service equipment.
  Source: OAR 918-261-0038; OESC (2023 edition).
```

## 18.6 Foundation And Structural Inspection Readiness

The foundation inspection happens after the reinforcement and embeds are set and before the pour, and it verifies the excavation, forms, reinforcement, and every embedded item at once. The structural inspections verify the framing or masonry against the approved plans.

**Simple**

The foundation inspection is the one that can never be redone, because concrete makes it permanent an hour later. Everything buried in the pour (rebar, anchor bolts, the grounding electrode, sleeves, the vapor barrier) is checked in that one visit. The pour is scheduled so a failed item can be fixed without losing the concrete truck.

**Applied**

Have ready for the foundation inspection:

- The excavation to undisturbed bearing soil at the required depth, and the forms set square, level, and braced.
- The reinforcement (bar size, spacing, cover, splices, chairs), anchor bolts, and holdowns per the structural plan.
- The concrete-encased electrode (the grounding tail) in place per the electrical chapter.
- The under-slab vapor retarder, insulation, radon provisions where required, and every plumbing, electrical, and mechanical sleeve.
- The foundation drainage and the waterproofing, and the wall braced for the pre-backfill inspection.

Have ready for the structural inspections the framing or masonry per the approved plans: member sizes, spacing, sheathing and its fastening pattern, wall bracing, anchorage, and the engineered-item documentation.

**Professional**

ORSC R109.1.1 sets the foundation inspection after excavation, forms, and reinforcement and before concrete. The verified items (excavation depth to undisturbed soil, footing dimensions per Table R403.1, reinforcement cover, anchor bolts per R403.1.6, holdowns with their ICC-ES report, the concrete-encased electrode per NEC 250.52(A)(3), the vapor retarder, slab-edge insulation, sleeves, waterproofing per R406, and drainage per R405) are detailed in the foundation, structural, and electrical chapters. The framing inspection under R109.1.4 verifies the wall bracing per R602.10, the sheathing fastening (the most common framing-inspection failure is fasteners too far apart), and the anchorage, plus a moisture-content notice that framing is at or below 19 percent before interior finishes. For masonry, the reinforcement is inspected before grouting and the grout pour is observed, with cleanouts verified for high-lift pours.

**Code**

```
The foundation inspection precedes the pour and checks every embed.
  Excavation to undisturbed soil; forms; reinforcement and cover; anchor bolts (R403.1.6); holdowns with their evaluation report; concrete-encased electrode (NEC 250.52(A)(3)); vapor retarder; sleeves; waterproofing (R406); drainage (R405).
  Source: ORSC R109.1.1 (2023 edition).

The frame and masonry inspection follows the rough trades.
  Wall bracing per R602.10; sheathing fastening pattern; anchorage; framing moisture at or below 19 percent before interior finishes.
  Source: ORSC R109.1.4 and R109.1.4.1 (2023 edition).

Masonry reinforcement is verified before grout.
  Reinforcement inspected before grouting; grout pour observed; cleanouts for high-lift pours.
  Source: TMS 602; ORSC R606 (2023 edition).
```

## 18.7 Rough-Trade Inspection Readiness

The rough plumbing, mechanical, and electrical inspections happen after the systems are installed and before they are concealed, with the plumbing and hydronic systems held under a pressure test. The frame inspection cannot pass until all three rough trades do.

**Simple**

The rough systems are proven under pressure, not by eye. The drain pipes hold a column of water, and the supply pipes hold pressurized air or water on a gauge. The gas line holds its test for the inspector to see. The gauge is already holding when the inspector walks in.

**Applied**

- Rough plumbing: hold the drain-waste-vent system under a water or air test and the water supply under a pressure test with the gauges holding when the inspector arrives; verify cleanout locations, gas-piping test where applicable, water-heater seismic strapping, and backflow devices.
- Rough mechanical: have the ducts, exhaust runs, refrigerant line-set (in place, not brazed), condensate routing, and makeup-air paths in and sealed.
- Rough electrical: have the service, panel, branch circuits, boxes, grounding and bonding, GFCI and AFCI circuit identification, smoke and CO alarm rough, and low-voltage separation in per the electrical chapters.
- Call the rough-trade inspections before the frame inspection, which is gated on them.

**Professional**

Rough plumbing under ORSC R109.1.2 and the OPSC tests the drain-waste-vent system (a 10-foot water head or a 5 psi air test, held 15 minutes) and the water supply (1.5 times working pressure, not less than 50 psi, held 15 minutes). Gas piping is tested under the Oregon criteria stated in the HVAC chapter's fuel-gas section, and the water heater's seismic strapping is required (OPSC 507.3). The rough electrical verifies the service and panel (working space per NEC 110.26), the conductor-to-breaker match, grounding and bonding, the GFCI and AFCI protection (per the Oregon-amended list in the branch-circuit chapter), and the alarm and low-voltage rough. The mechanical rough verifies duct sealing (mastic, not fabric tape), duct and dryer-exhaust runs, and the refrigerant line-set before brazing. The technical detail for each is in the plumbing, electrical, and HVAC chapters; here the concern is that the work is complete, tested, and accessible.

**Code**

```
Rough plumbing is inspected under test.
  Drain-waste-vent: 10-foot water head or 5 psi air, held 15 minutes. Water supply: 1.5 times working pressure, not less than 50 psi, held 15 minutes. Water-heater seismic strapping per OPSC 507.3.
  Source: ORSC R109.1.2 (2023 edition); OPSC 712, 609, 507.3 (2023 edition).

Gas piping is inspected under the Oregon test criteria.
  Rough and final gas piping inspections; 10 psi held 15 minutes for standard systems.
  Source: ORSC G2417.1.1.1, G2417.4.1 (2023 edition; see the HVAC chapter's fuel-gas section).

The rough electrical and mechanical are complete before concealment.
  Service and panel working space (NEC 110.26), grounding and bonding, GFCI/AFCI identification, alarm rough, low-voltage separation; ducts sealed with mastic; refrigerant line-set in place before brazing.
  Source: OESC/NEC (2023 edition); ORSC M1601.4 (2023 edition).
```

## 18.8 Insulation, Energy, And Blower-Door Inspection

The insulation and air-sealing are inspected before cover, and the energy tests (blower door and, where ducts are outside the envelope, duct leakage) are run by an approved third-party tester and submitted before the certificate of occupancy. The target depends on the compliance path chosen.

**Simple**

The insulation inspection checks that what the energy paperwork promised is in the walls, at full thickness, with no gaps. The blower door then measures the whole house's leakage with a calibrated fan. The smart order is to run that test before drywall, while leaks can still be found and sealed cheaply.

**Applied**

- Pass the insulation inspection with the insulation and any vapor retarder installed to the energy documentation, before drywall.
- Have the blower-door test performed by an approved third-party tester, before final and ideally before drywall so a failure can be remediated, and submit the result on the state form.
- Have the duct-leakage test performed where ducts run outside the conditioned envelope.
- Have the crawl-space ground cover, eave baffles, and firestopping in for the pre-cover inspection.

**Professional**

The insulation inspection under ORSC R109.1.5.2 verifies the type, thickness, and R-value against the compliance documentation and the installation quality (full cavity fill, no gaps or compression), plus the vapor retarder where the climate zone requires it. Air sealing under N1104.8 is met by the air-barrier criteria or a blower-door result at or below the path target; the blower-door test (ASTM E779 or E1827, by an approved tester) is reported on the state form, and the target depends on the path chosen in the envelope chapter. Duct leakage under N1105.3 applies to ducts in unconditioned spaces. The building official's acceptance of the specific tester is confirmed before scheduling, since the code names approved agencies rather than specific credentials. The envelope and HVAC chapters state the detailed values.

**Code**

```
Insulation is inspected against the energy documentation before cover.
  Type, thickness, R-value, installation quality, and the vapor retarder where required.
  Source: ORSC R109.1.5.2 (2023 edition).

Air sealing is proven by criteria or by test.
  Blower-door test per ASTM E779 or E1827 by an approved third-party tester; result on the state form before the certificate of occupancy; tester acceptance confirmed with the building official.
  Source: ORSC N1104.8 and R109.2 (2023 edition); ASTM E779; ASTM E1827; BCD residential blower-door form.

Ducts outside the envelope are leak-tested.
  Duct-leakage test for ducts in unconditioned spaces.
  Source: ORSC N1105.3 (2023 edition).
```

## 18.9 Final Inspection, Temporary Occupancy, And Occupancy

Each trade has its own final, the building final confirms the life-safety and energy items, and the certificate of occupancy is the legal gate to moving in. A temporary certificate is available at the building official's discretion when the life-safety items are complete.

**Simple**

Three trade finals and one building final stand between the finished work and the certificate of occupancy. The building final checks the safety items a family depends on: alarms, exits, guards, shock protection, water, waste, and heat. Moving in before the certificate is illegal occupancy, even in a house you built yourself.

**Applied**

- Complete the trade finals: plumbing (fixtures connected, water heater commissioned, pressure under load), electrical (devices live, panel labeled, GFCI and AFCI and alarms tested, service energized), and mechanical (equipment installed, startup documented, controls operational).
- Complete the building final: all trade finals signed off, energy documentation filed, life-safety items operational, required testing complete (well water, septic startup, radon where applicable), address numbers in place, and conditions of approval satisfied.
- Where a temporary certificate is used, physically separate and label any unfinished area as not for occupancy, and complete the written remaining-work list by the deadline.

**Professional**

The building final under ORSC R109.1.6 and R110 confirms the trade finals, the energy-compliance documentation (blower door, duct leakage, AHRI certificates, NFRC labels, and the lighting form), the life-safety items, and the required testing. A temporary certificate of occupancy under R110.4 is discretionary and requires the life-safety items complete, at least one functional bathroom, a kitchen with a sink and counter, one bedroom with egress, permanent power, water, waste, and heat, and a written remaining-work list with a deadline. It can be denied even when the minimum items are met if a condition of approval is unmet. Where finish work continues after move-in, a clean lockable separation between the occupied and in-progress areas is designed in. Selling before the certificate of occupancy is a disclosure risk covered in the legal chapter.

**Code**

```
The building final and certificate of occupancy close the permit.
  Trade finals signed; energy documentation filed; life-safety items operational; required testing documented; conditions of approval satisfied.
  Source: ORSC R109.1.6 and R110 (2023 edition).

The temporary certificate is discretionary and conditional.
  Life-safety items complete; minimum habitable spaces (bathroom, kitchen sink and counter, bedroom with egress); permanent utilities; written remaining-work list with a deadline; unfinished areas separated and labeled.
  Source: ORSC R110.4 (2023 edition).
```

## 18.10 Corrections, Appeals, And Revisions

When work fails, the inspector issues a correction notice that is addressed before reinspection; disputes escalate through a local or state appeal; and a change to the approved design goes through a field revision, an engineer's letter, or a formal plan revision depending on its significance.

**Simple**

A failed inspection is normal, not a scandal; the notice names the item and the code section. The item gets fixed, photographed, and reinspected. A disagreement about what the code means has a formal appeal path. What never works is arguing at the site or covering the disputed work.

**Applied**

- Address each item on a correction notice, document the correction with photos and a written response, and request reinspection through the same channel.
- For a change during construction: a minor field revision that does not affect life safety can be approved in the field and documented on the field set with the inspector's initials; a structural deviation from stamped engineering needs a PE-stamped engineer's letter; a substantive change to structure, egress, or energy compliance needs a formal plan revision.
- For a dispute over a code interpretation, use the local board of appeals first (typically within 30 days of the decision), and the state appeal or a statewide alternate-method ruling only for items that genuinely matter.
- Stop work immediately on a posted stop-work order; continuing is a separate violation.

**Professional**

A correction notice identifies the failed item and code section, and reinspection carries a fee (commonly $50 to $200, 2026). The building official may issue a stop-work order under ORSC R114 for work in violation, without permit, or creating a hazard, with daily civil penalties under ORS 455.770 for continuing. Appeals run through the local board of appeals under R112 or a state appeal under ORS 455.690 and OAR 918-008-0120; a statewide alternate-method ruling under OAR 918-008-0095 is the alternate path that binds every jurisdiction. A field revision is documented on the field set; an engineer's letter clears a structural deviation and is retained in closeout; and a plan revision (added or removed structural elements, a changed egress or energy-affecting schedule, or an owner-driven design change) is resubmitted, re-reviewed, and can take weeks, so anticipated revisions are scheduled early.

**Code**

```
A stop-work order stops the work immediately.
  Issued for work in violation, without permit, or creating a hazard; continuing draws daily civil penalties.
  Source: ORSC R114 (2023 edition); ORS 455.770.

Disputes have a two-level appeal path plus the statewide ruling.
  Local board of appeals (R112, typically within 30 days); state appeal (ORS 455.690; OAR 918-008-0120); statewide alternate-method ruling (OAR 918-008-0095) binds all jurisdictions.
  Source: ORSC R112 (2023 edition); ORS 455.690; OAR 918-008-0120; OAR 918-008-0095.

Design changes route by significance.
  Field revision on the field set with the inspector's initials; PE-stamped letter for structural deviations; formal plan revision for substantive changes to structure, egress, or energy compliance.
  Source: ORSC R106.4 (amended construction documents, 2023 edition); local plan-revision practice.
```

## 18.11 Inspection Records

The inspection process produces a legal record and a larger project record. The legally required items are kept on site and filed; the recommended closeout binder assembles the full audit-ready history that the documentation and legal chapters rely on.

**Simple**

Every inspection produces paper, and the paper outlives the memory. The signed record of each pass, the correction notices, and the test results all go into one binder. That binder is what proves, years later, that the house was built inspected and legal.

**Applied**

Keep the legally required records:

- The approved construction documents on site until completion, the posted permit placard, and the signed inspection card.
- The energy-compliance forms (blower-door and duct-leakage results, AHRI certificates, NFRC labels, and the lighting form) before the certificate of occupancy.
- The manufacturer's installation instructions on site for any product where the listing controls, and the contractor information form for any contractor work.
- Any special-inspection reports and floodplain elevation certificate where applicable.

Assemble the recommended closeout binder: every inspection sign-off and correction notice with its correction, the concrete batch tickets, the engineer's letters, the product cut sheets and label photographs, the ICC-ES reports, the AHRI and NFRC labels, the equipment startup and commissioning reports, the warranty registrations, the test results, the pre-cover photographs of every concealed condition, the plan revisions, and the permits and final certificate.

**Professional**

The legally required records (ORSC R106.3.1 approved documents, R105.7 placard, R109.4 inspection card, and the energy forms before occupancy) are the minimum. The closeout binder built in the materials chapter and referenced by the documentation and legal chapters is the substantive as-built record and the evidence behind the seller-property-disclosure form if a defect claim arises after a sale. It is kept as a paper copy in a fireproof location and as digital copies in two independent locations, including one off-site.

**Code**

```
The legally required inspection records are kept and filed.
  Approved documents on site until completion (R106.3.1); permit placard (R105.7); inspection card (R109.4); energy-compliance forms before occupancy; manufacturer instructions on site where the listing controls; contractor information form.
  Source: ORSC R106.3.1, R105.7, R109.4, Chapter 11 documentation (2023 edition).

The closeout binder is the audit-ready project record.
  Inspection sign-offs and corrections, batch tickets, engineer letters, product data and label photographs, evaluation reports, AHRI/NFRC labels, startup and commissioning reports, warranties, test results, pre-cover photographs, plan revisions, permits, and the final certificate; paper (fireproof) plus digital in two independent locations.
  Source: project documentation standard (see the documentation chapter).
```

| Rough electrical | Before walls close | Boxes, conductors, panel rough, grounding, bonding, low-voltage where included |
| Rough mechanical | Before walls or ceilings close | Equipment, ducts, gas piping, ventilation, exhaust, refrigerant lines |
| Insulation and air sealing | Before drywall | Insulation type, depth, continuity, air-sealing penetrations |
| Energy and blower door | Where the compliance path requires testing | Blower-door result, duct test result if applicable |
| Final building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical | Each discipline finals separately | Each discipline's final inspection record |
| Site-development final | Before CO | Permanent erosion control, stormwater, landscape, address |
| Certificate of occupancy | All disciplines final + Form 4823 | CO issued; binder closes |

If a TCO is needed before the full sequence completes, the official defines the minimum life-safety items. The TCO does not waive the remaining items. The remaining items still have to close before the full CO is issued and within the TCO's time window or its extensions.
