# Chapter 4: Legal, Permits, And Owner-Builder Rules

Legal requirements accompany the physical build from permit application until the certificate of occupancy is issued and the records are closed. This chapter identifies who may perform which work in Oregon and what the owner-builder path requires: the homeowner statement, the resale-timing rules that exist (and the ones that do not), the ownership structures that preserve the path, the permit exemptions for small and agricultural structures, the alternate-method path for a material outside the prescriptive code, contract building departments, insurance and financing for an unconventional build, and the inspection sequence at the legal level.

The detailed inspection-readiness criteria for each phase are in the Inspection And Approval Sequence chapter. The Oregon Code And Compliance Map chapter contains the overview of all regulatory layers. This chapter contains the legal detail.

This chapter is general information, drawn from public Oregon statutes, administrative rules, agency forms, and case law as of the dates in the source paths. It is not legal advice for any specific project. The right structure for a particular owner depends on facts that require an Oregon construction attorney, a CPA familiar with multi-entity small business, and, where stamped work exists, a licensed Oregon professional engineer.

## 4.1 Legal Requirements Are Construction Tasks

Permits, inspections, occupancy approvals, and the compliance records are construction tasks, scheduled with the physical work.

**Simple**

Every regulated piece of work has a legal side: the framed wall, the concrete pour, the wire, the pipe, the installed appliance. Someone has to pull a permit, sign for the work, schedule an inspection, save the result, and prove later that it was done right. A skipped legal step causes problems at four points: cover inspection, the certificate of occupancy, insurance, and sale.

**Applied**

For each phase of work, know:

- which permit covers the work
- who is legally allowed to do the work
- what documents must be submitted at application
- what the inspector must see before the work is covered
- which work cannot be covered until inspection is approved
- what correction process applies if the work fails inspection
- what record enters the project binder
- when the permit expires if work pauses, and how to extend it

Each city or county administers the state codes through its own permit, inspection, and approval workflow. Local amendments, fee schedules, and scheduling vary; the specifics come from the local building department's adopted procedures.

The worst time to discover a permit problem is after concrete is poured, a trench is backfilled, insulation is installed, drywall is up, or a finish material is applied. Resolution becomes destructive once work is covered.

A permit is not open-ended. Under ORSC R105.5, a permit expires if the work does not start within 180 days of issuance, or if the work stops for 180 days. Written extension requests of up to 180 days each are available for justifiable cause, with no stated limit on the number of extensions. The code contains no reinstatement procedure for an expired permit; what happens after expiration is local policy, so request the extension before the deadline instead of testing the local policy after it.

An appeal pauses only the plan-review clock. ORS 455.475(3) extends the plan-review deadline by the number of days an appeal takes; no rule tolls the 180-day expiration clock after the permit issues. If a post-issuance dispute stalls the whole project (for example, over a correction notice on an approved alternative material), the 180-day clock keeps running. Ask the building official in writing whether ongoing work on other parts of the permit counts as activity, and file extension requests in parallel with any dispute.

**Code**

```
State law authorizes the building codes and the permit system.
  ORS chapter 455 contains the authority for the specialty codes, the uniform permit, inspection, and certificate-of-occupancy requirements (ORS 455.055), the owner-built structural exemption (ORS 455.320), and the rural-county exemption framework (ORS 455.330-455.350).
  Source: ORS chapter 455.

Contractor licensing and the owner-occupier exemption come from a separate statute.
  ORS chapter 701 contains the CCB licensing framework, the owner-occupier exemption with its intent-to-sell prima facie test (ORS 701.010(5)), and the homeowner-applicant statement requirement (ORS 701.325).
  Source: ORS chapter 701.

State rules administer the residential code and the certificate of occupancy.
  OAR chapter 918 division 480 adopts the ORSC (918-480-0005) and contains the certificate-of-occupancy rule (918-480-0140).
  Source: OAR chapter 918 division 480.

The residential code's first chapter contains the process rules.
  Permits, required inspections, certificate of occupancy (R110), temporary certificate of occupancy (R110.4), and the correction-notice framework.
  Source: ORSC Chapter 1 (2023 edition).

A permit expires after 180 days of inactivity.
  Two triggers: work not commenced within 180 days of issuance, or work suspended or abandoned for 180 days. Written extensions of up to 180 days each are available for justifiable cause; the code contains no reinstatement procedure, so post-expiration recourse is local policy.
  Source: ORSC R105.5; ORSC R105.3.2 (the same clock for pending applications).

Plan review for a simple dwelling runs on a statutory clock.
  10 business days after a complete application in a jurisdiction under 300,000 population, 15 business days at 300,000 or more; a complex structure requiring additional review is exempt from the clock; an appeal extends the deadline by the number of days the appeal takes.
  Source: ORS 455.467; ORS 455.475(3).

The hazard-zone restricted-construction statute does not reach a single-family dwelling.
  ORS 455.447 restricts major structures (over six stories with 60,000 square feet aggregate floor area, over 10 stories, or parking structures defined by rule) and buildings in Tsunami Risk Category III or IV under OSSC Table 1604.5; a single-family dwelling is Risk Category II and below every threshold.
  Source: ORS 455.446-455.447; OSSC Table 1604.5.
```

## 4.2 Owner-Builder Responsibility And The Homeowner Acknowledgement

The owner-builder path is a defined legal role with attached duties. The owner-builder becomes the permit holder, the document keeper, the inspection scheduler, the correction manager, and the first person responsible for proving the work stayed inside the approved path.

**Simple**

Oregon lets a property owner build their own home without a contractor license, under specific rules. The owner has to own the property, intend to live in the finished home, and sign a state form acknowledging responsibility. All the code, permit, and inspection requirements that bind a licensed builder still apply. The exemption removes the licensing requirement only.

**Applied**

If using the homeowner path:

- apply as the property owner where allowed by the local jurisdiction
- read the homeowner statement before signing it
- sign the required Property Owner Statement before regulated work begins
- keep the permit and approved plans on the project site
- schedule each inspection before the work is covered
- resolve correction notices before moving the next phase forward
- hire CCB-licensed contractors when required by the work or beyond the owner's competence
- if hiring subcontractors, the form requires that they be CCB-licensed (and for plumbing or electrical permits, also BCD-licensed)
- keep subcontractor licensing, insurance, permit, and inspection records in the binder

### What The Property Owner Statement Says

The statewide BCD Property Owner Statement (Information Notice to Property Owners About Construction Responsibilities, ORS 701.325(3), most recently revised January 2, 2025) is a single form covering residential building, electrical, mechanical, and plumbing permits, published at oregon.gov/bcd/epermitting. The form is named in this book, not displayed.

The form requires the applicant to check that they will be performing work on property they own and reside in or will reside in. It requires acknowledgement that the permit is non-transferable. Two optional checkboxes apply when the applicant intends to perform their own electrical work (under ORS 479.540) or their own plumbing work (under ORS 693.020).

The form does not contain a "minimum holding period before sale" attestation. The sale and rental restrictions come from the substantive exemption statutes, not from the form.

The page-one disclosures the applicant is acknowledging include:

- that homeowners using unlicensed paid labor may be considered employers subject to Oregon withholding tax, unemployment insurance, BIN registration, workers' compensation insurance, federal Social Security, and federal income-tax withholding
- that the homeowner is responsible for code compliance and inspection scheduling
- that the homeowner should obtain liability and property-damage insurance covering accidents and injuries to non-employees on the site

The closing certification requires the applicant to certify that they are the homeowner applying for the permit and that the information on the homeowner statement is true and accurate.

The statement process contains no verification step beyond the signature. ORS 701.325(2) requires the jurisdiction to supply the information notice and to withhold the residential building permit until the applicant signs the statement identifying whether a licensed general contractor will be used; neither the statute nor the current form adds a callback, a competency confirmation, or an interview.

**Professional**

The 2008 amendment to ORS 701.010 changed the rules for owners hiring subcontractors directly. An owner who directly hires subcontractors for permit-required work loses the homeowner-GC exemption unless the work is performed under the direction of a licensed general contractor. The full map of which licensing regime controls which trade is in the who-can-do-which-work section of this chapter.

**Code**

```
The homeowner statement is a statutory requirement.
  The applicant must acknowledge construction responsibilities before the permit is issued.
  Source: ORS 701.325(3).

Permit issuance waits on the signed statement and adds no other applicant test.
  The jurisdiction supplies the information notice and may not issue the permit until the applicant signs; the statute and the current form contain no verification, competency, or interview step.
  Source: ORS 701.325(2).

The structural exemption defines who counts as an owner-builder.
  An owner-built dwelling has structural components constructed entirely by the owner who intends to occupy, or by the owner with friends and relatives on an unpaid basis; an owner who used the exemption within the prior five years does not qualify.
  Source: ORS 455.320.

The electrical exemption is limited to non-sale property.
  Owner or immediate-family work on residential or farm property "not intended for sale, exchange, lease or rent"; permit, inspection, and code compliance still required.
  Source: ORS 479.540.

The plumbing exemption applies to the owner's own building.
  No sale-or-lease restriction in the statute itself, and no immediate-family extension; permit, inspection, and code compliance still required.
  Source: ORS 693.020.

The CCB defines family for the contracting-side exemption.
  Parents, spouses, sisters, brothers, daughters, sons, daughters-in-law, sons-in-law, and grandchildren.
  Source: OAR 812-002-0275.

The CCB defines speculative construction.
  A structure constructed in anticipation of, or with the intent of, selling to another entity during or after construction; the basis for implied-warranty exposure.
  Source: OAR 812-002-0680.

Coordinating construction is itself contractor work.
  Selecting contractors, obtaining permits, scheduling, and purchasing materials for a project constitutes construction management requiring CCB licensure.
  Source: OAR 812-002-0160.
```

## 4.3 The Oregon Resale-Timing Question

There is no statewide fixed waiting period before an owner-built home may be sold. Claims of one conflate four separate regimes, or repeat a city's local interpretation as if it were state law. Oregon uses an intent test under contractor-licensing law plus several separate timing rules, each controlling a different thing.

**Simple**

Oregon law contains no fixed waiting period before an owner-built home may be sold. What the law contains is a test of whether the owner intended to occupy the home or intended to sell. Living in the home is the strongest evidence of intent to occupy. There are also a few separate rules about how often certain exemptions can be reused. A 75-day notice requirement applies when a recently completed home is sold. The federal government has its own rule too. It sets how long a person must live in a home to avoid capital-gains tax on a sale. None of these add up to a state-mandated waiting period.

**Professional**

The four overlapping regimes:

| Regime | Authority | What it controls | What it does not control |
|---|---|---|---|
| CCB contractor-licensing intent test | ORS 701.010(5) | Whether construction is treated as owner-occupier work or as contracting for sale | No fixed time period; non-occupancy after completion is prima facie evidence of intent to sell |
| Structural-exemption five-year cooldown | ORS 455.320 | How often an owner can use the structural-code exemption (room sizes, ceiling heights, temperature) on a new permit | Not a resale waiting period |
| Rural-county exemption five-year limit | ORS 455.335 | How often a qualifying exempt owner-built-dwelling permit can be issued in participating rural counties | Not a resale waiting period |
| Homebuyer-protection 75-day notice | ORS 87.007 / ORS 87.093 | A lien-disclosure obligation when a residential structure is sold within roughly 75 days of substantial completion | Not a prohibition on sale |

Federal capital-gains treatment under IRC §121 (the personal-residence exclusion) requires ownership and use of the home as a principal residence for two of the five years before sale. The exclusion is up to $250,000 for an individual and $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. The §121 timeline often becomes the practical floor for resale, but it is a federal tax rule, separate from Oregon construction law.

A common local point of confusion: Portland's residential permitting page states that a homeowner may pull their own electrical permit only if they own and occupy the home and are not planning to sell or rent within six months. That six-month threshold is Portland's working interpretation of the statutory phrase "not intended for sale, exchange, lease or rent" in ORS 479.540. Other Oregon jurisdictions use different thresholds or none at all.

The Tandem Properties LLC v. Construction Contractors Board decision (184 Or App 28, 2002) is the controlling Oregon Court of Appeals case on the intent test. Owners who arrange residential construction with the intent of later offering the structure for sale are excluded from the owner-occupier exemption. Intent to sell without occupancy makes the owner a contractor by operation of law.

A separate disclosure duty attaches at any resale, on a different subject: property condition rather than resale timing. ORS 105.462 through 105.490 require the seller of residential property to deliver the statutory Seller's Property Disclosure Statement to every buyer who makes a written offer. The duty covers the seller's actual knowledge at the time of disclosure (ORS 105.464); no hazard-zone study is required. The form's line items include floodplain status and designated slide or other geologic hazard zones, and the OREF-020 form in statewide use lists wildfire-risk status. The seller confirms the current form's line items at sale, including how a tsunami-inundation designation is treated. A voluntary construction standard (an IBHS FORTIFIED-class design) changes no disclosure obligation; its documented market effect is insurance pricing, confirmed with the specific insurer.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: clean owner-builder posture.**
Owner buys land in their personal name. Pulls permits as the homeowner. Builds the home over two years while living elsewhere. Receives certificate of occupancy. Moves into the home as their primary residence. Maintains utility accounts, mail, voter registration, tax filings, and insurance at the address. After three years of actual residence, sells and moves to a new property.

Result: the intent-to-sell prima facie evidence (non-occupancy after completion) is rebutted by actual occupancy. The federal §121 exclusion applies (more than two of the last five years occupied as principal residence). No CCB exposure.

**Example 2: borderline posture, fact-dependent.**
Owner buys land in their personal name. Pulls permits as the homeowner. Builds the home and receives certificate of occupancy. Moves in for nine months, then accepts an out-of-state job offer and lists the home for sale.

Result: a genuine post-completion change of circumstances is a defense to the intent-to-sell test, not a violation. The federal §121 exclusion may not yet be available (less than two of five years), so capital-gains tax may apply. Lien-notice obligation under ORS 87.007 applies if sale falls within roughly 75 days of substantial completion. CCB exposure is fact-dependent and depends on whether the original intent was occupancy or sale.

**Example 3: speculative posture, hard exposure.**
Owner buys land in an LLC name. LLC pulls permits as a developer. Construction begins. The owner never moves in; the property is listed for sale before the certificate of occupancy is issued.

Result: the LLC is a contractor by operation of law (Tandem Properties pattern). The owner-occupier exemption is unavailable because the LLC is not a natural person. CCB enforcement risk under ORS 701.098, civil penalties under ORS 701.992, loss of standing to bring claims before the CCB, and potential implied-warranty exposure as a speculative builder under OAR 812-002-0680.

**Applied: defensible owner-builder posture in Oregon**

1. Hold title in the correct personal ownership before relying on the homeowner path.
2. Pull permits as the homeowner where allowed.
3. Build because of an intent to occupy.
4. Do not list, market, or solicit offers while owner-builder work is in progress.
5. Move in only after the building official issues the certificate of occupancy or temporary certificate of occupancy.
6. Maintain ordinary occupancy records: utility bills, mail forwarding, voter registration, tax-residency address, insurance address, and daily-life records.
7. If circumstances change after move-in, document the actual reason (job change, family change, health change).
8. Before any sale, lease, trust transfer, LLC involvement, or tax-planning move, obtain professional advice.

The strongest position is genuine occupancy with records that match the facts. The federal §121 timeline (two of the last five years) is usually the binding resale-timing constraint, not a state-construction rule.

**Code**

```
The licensing exemption turns on intent, with no fixed time period.
  The exemption does not apply to a person who builds or arranges construction with intent to offer the structure for sale before, upon, or after completion; non-occupancy after completion is prima facie evidence of intent to sell.
  Source: ORS 701.010(5).

The structural exemption has a five-year reuse limit.
  An owner who used the exemption may not use it again for five years. The exemption reaches only ceiling heights, room sizes, and maintenance of specific temperature levels (ORS 455.320(2)); the county-clerk recording is required only where the dwelling does not comply with those requirements (ORS 455.320(3)); a missing recording does not affect any conveyance of the property (ORS 455.320(4)). A limit on exemption frequency, and separate from resale timing.
  Source: ORS 455.320.

Rural counties may adopt a broader exemption, with its own five-year limit.
  Participating counties issue exempt owner-built-dwelling permits to a person at most once every five years, with purchaser-notice obligations and buyer remedies.
  Source: ORS 455.330; ORS 455.335; ORS 455.345-455.350.

A sale within about 75 days of substantial completion requires a lien-protection notice.
  The seller serves the Homebuyer Protection Act notice; the trigger is substantial completion as defined in the lien statutes. An obligation on the sale, and separate from any prohibition.
  Source: ORS 87.007; ORS 87.093; ORS 87.045.

A resale requires the statutory property-condition disclosure.
  The Seller's Property Disclosure Statement is delivered to every buyer who makes a written offer; the duty is limited to the seller's actual knowledge; line items include floodplain and designated geologic hazard zones.
  Source: ORS 105.462-105.490; ORS 105.464.

Federal tax sets its own occupancy clock.
  The personal-residence capital-gains exclusion requires ownership and use as a principal residence for two of the five years before sale; $250,000 excluded for an individual, $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. A federal tax rule, and separate from Oregon construction law.
  Source: IRC section 121.

The certificate of occupancy gates occupancy, and only occupancy.
  The CO and TCO control when the home may be lived in; neither starts a resale clock.
  Source: ORSC R110; OAR 918-480-0140.

The controlling case on intent is Tandem Properties.
  An entity that owns property and arranges construction with intent to sell is a contractor by operation of law; the owner-occupier exemption requires a natural person with occupancy intent.
  Source: Tandem Properties LLC v. Construction Contractors Board, 184 Or App 28 (2002).
```

## 4.4 Ownership Structure And The Owner-Builder Posture

The owner-builder path depends on who owns the property and who is doing the work.

**Applied**

Confirm the ownership structure before relying on the homeowner path:

- individual ownership
- spouse or co-owner on title
- revocable trust
- irrevocable trust
- LLC ownership
- future transfer plan
- financing or insurance requirements

Fixing title after a project starts is harder than choosing the clean structure at purchase.

**Professional**

The safest owner-builder posture is a natural person who owns the property, signs the homeowner statement, intends to occupy, performs or properly contracts the work, and keeps the records.

| Structure | Effect on owner-builder posture |
|---|---|
| Individual title | Cleanest. Direct fit with ORS 455.320 and ORS 701.010 owner-occupier framing. |
| Spouses on title (tenancy by the entirety, ORS 93.180) | Generally accepted. CCB family-member definition under OAR 812-002-0275 is broad (parents, spouses, sisters, brothers, daughters, sons, daughters-in-law, sons-in-law, grandchildren). For trade-permit purposes (electrical), Portland's enumeration is narrower (mother, father, sister, brother, daughter, son) and notably omits spouses; verify the local jurisdiction's working list. |
| Revocable living trust where the trustee is also the beneficiary-occupant | Typically accepted by jurisdictions when the occupant clearly controls the trust. Verify locally before relying on this. |
| Irrevocable trust with the occupant as beneficiary only | Fact-specific and shakier. No statewide bright-line rule; local building departments make the call. |
| LLC ownership of construction-intent property | Presumptively a developer structure under ORS 701.005. The LLC owner-builder posture is treated as contracting, prima facie. This is the Tandem Properties pattern. |
| Married couple titling the project in a jointly held LLC | Worst posture: looks speculative and loses the natural-person owner-occupant defense. |
| Tenancy in common between unmarried co-owners | Oregon's default form for two or more owners without a survivorship declaration (ORS 93.180). The owner-occupier posture follows the co-owner who signs, occupies, and keeps the records; a written co-ownership agreement sets the exits. |
| Life estate with the builder holding the remainder | Unresolved under the owner-builder statutes, which presume an applicant with present possession. Requires attorney review of the creating instrument plus the building department's written answer on who signs as owner, before any application. |

If title will be held by a trust, an LLC, or any arrangement other than direct individual ownership or tenancy by the entirety: obtain confirmation before relying on the homeowner path. Ask local planning, the building department, and an Oregon construction attorney.

**Code**

```
The statute defines who is a developer.
  A developer is a contractor that owns property with the intent to sell; LLC ownership with construction intent fits the definition.
  Source: ORS 701.005.

The owner exemption requires a natural person with occupancy intent.
  The exemptions from contractor licensure center on personal ownership and intent to occupy.
  Source: ORS 701.010.

Spousal title defaults to tenancy by the entirety.
  Transfers to spouses take this form unless stated otherwise.
  Source: ORS 93.180.

The CCB family-member list controls the contracting-side exemption.
  Parents, spouses, sisters, brothers, daughters, sons, daughters-in-law, sons-in-law, grandchildren.
  Source: OAR 812-002-0275.

Speculative construction is defined by intent to sell.
  A structure constructed in anticipation of, or with intent of, selling to another entity during or after construction.
  Source: OAR 812-002-0680.

The controlling case is Tandem Properties.
  LLC ownership combined with intent to sell makes the entity a contractor by operation of law.
  Source: Tandem Properties LLC v. CCB, 184 Or App 28 (2002).
```

### Co-Ownership Between Unmarried Buyers

**Simple**

Buying land with a sibling, a friend, or an unmarried partner creates a tenancy in common (separate, transferable ownership shares with no survivorship) unless the deed says otherwise. Each share passes separately at death. Any co-owner can sue to force a division or sale of the whole parcel. A written agreement, signed before closing, is what keeps those exits out of court.

**Applied**

Before closing on co-owned land, sign a co-ownership agreement drafted or reviewed by an Oregon real-estate attorney. Oregon attorney rates average $325 per hour, in a $206-$504 per hour range across practice areas (2026). Include, at minimum:

- a buyout formula: an appraisal-based price, or a shotgun clause (one owner names a price at which the other must either buy or sell)
- a right of first refusal before any sale of a share to an outsider
- expense-sharing terms for taxes, insurance, and site work, with a deadline and a remedy for a co-owner who stops paying
- a decision rule for day-to-day use of the land
- an exit timeline

Generic co-ownership templates exist; practitioner guidance is direct that a raw-land purchase gets custom drafting or attorney review, because the failure mode is a partition lawsuit.

**Professional**

The failure mode is the judicial partition action under ORS 105.205 through 105.290, a circuit court suit. Any tenant in common may demand division of the property by three court-appointed referees. Where division would cause great prejudice to the owners, the court orders a sale instead. ORS 105.210 also permits a court-valued buyout of one owner's interest by another. The statute provides no administrative shortcut, no fee schedule, and no timeline. Cost and duration are litigation facts that depend on whether the action is contested. The joinder rules (ORS 105.215 through 105.230) require lienholders and unknown claimants to be joined as parties, which adds parties and time.

**Code**

```
A conveyance to two or more persons defaults to tenancy in common.
  Tenancy in common unless the conveyance clearly and expressly declares a right of survivorship; transfers to spouses instead default to tenancy by the entirety.
  Source: ORS 93.180.

Any tenant in common may force partition through circuit court.
  Division by three court-appointed referees, sale where division would cause great prejudice, or a court-valued buyout of one owner's interest; lienholders and unknown claimants are joined as parties.
  Source: ORS 105.205; ORS 105.210; ORS 105.245.
```

### Life Estate And Remainder Arrangements

**Simple**

A life estate lets an older family member keep a property for life, with the builder holding the remainder (ownership that begins when the life estate ends). Oregon law defines the arrangement. It does not state whether the remainder holder can pull a building permit and build before the life estate ends.

**Professional**

ORS 112.345 vests a life estate in the devisee and the remainder in the named heirs. ORS 93.050 governs creation by conveyance. The life tenant holds the present right of possession and the duty to maintain the property. The permit framework presumes an applicant with present possession, and the owner-builder statutes (ORS 455.320; ORS 701.010) do not address divided present and future interests. No statute, rule, or documented account resolves the construction mechanics. Before any permit application under this structure, an Oregon real-estate or estate-planning attorney reviews the instrument that created the arrangement (including whether it addresses the life tenant's consent to construction). The building department answers in writing whether the life tenant, the remainder holder, or both sign the application as owner.

**Code**

```
A life estate divides present possession from future ownership.
  A devise for the term of a person's life vests a life estate in the devisee and the remainder in the heirs; creation by conveyance is governed separately. The owner-builder statutes contain no provision for a remainder holder as permit applicant.
  Source: ORS 112.345; ORS 93.050.
```

### Brand And LLC Separation Pattern

If a separate LLC carries education, content, plan licensing, or research activity (for example, "Better Smarter Homes LLC"), the LLC must be kept out of the construction-and-sale stream. The risk-control structure has two layers.

**Activity separation:**

- the LLC owns intellectual property (plans, content, branding, trademarks, domain names, video, photographs, written material)
- the LLC sells educational material, runs advertising, accepts sponsorships, and provides purely advisory consulting
- the LLC does not buy, build, or sell homes
- the LLC does not supervise or coordinate construction on the owner-builder's homes
- the natural-person owner holds title to the land, pulls the permit, signs the homeowner statement, and occupies
- a licensed Oregon professional engineer is hired per project where stamped engineering is required

**Money separation:**

- LLC revenue stays in the LLC bank account
- personal home-build expenses come from personal funds
- the LLC does not pay for materials that go into the personal home
- LLC accounting and personal accounting do not commingle

Marketing language matters. Oregon's professional engineering statute (ORS 672.007) treats any verbal claim, sign, advertisement, letterhead, or business card that implies the speaker is a professional engineer as the practice of engineering. ORS 672.020 makes that practice without registration unlawful. The Oregon State Board of Examiners for Engineering and Land Surveying (OSBEELS) has imposed civil penalties on individuals who held themselves out as engineers without registration.

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| "Engineered by Better Smarter Homes" | "Designed by Better Smarter Homes" |
| "Better Smarter Homes-engineered design" | "Built using Better Smarter Homes design principles" |
| "Engineering services by Better Smarter Homes" | "Based on research by Better Smarter Homes" |
| "Stamped by Better Smarter Homes" | (do not use any visual that resembles a PE seal) |
| "Better Smarter Homes-certified" | "Built to Better Smarter Homes' published methodology" |
| "Approved by Better Smarter Homes engineering" | (only where a licensed PE is involved: "Structural engineering by [PE name and Oregon license number], in collaboration with Better Smarter Homes") |

The architecture statute is gentler. ORS 671.030(1) exempts plans and specifications for single-family residential dwellings, farm buildings, and certain small structures from the practice of architecture. A design-and-research LLC can produce single-family home plans without an architect license. The words "architect," "architecture," and "architectural" still cannot appear in marketing without registration.

**Code**

```
Claiming engineer status is itself the practice of engineering.
  A verbal claim, sign, advertisement, letterhead, card, or any title or representation implying engineer status constitutes practice.
  Source: ORS 672.007.

Practicing engineering without registration is unlawful.
  An Oregon PE license is required to practice, with seal requirements for stamped documents; aiding or abetting unlicensed practice and false representation are separately prohibited.
  Source: ORS 672.020; ORS 672.045.

The engineering board publishes its penalty record.
  Civil penalties for unlicensed engineering practice typically run $1,000-$7,000 per violation.
  Source: OSBEELS disciplinary actions, oregon.gov/osbeels; OAR chapter 820.

Architecture has a registration bar and a residential exemption.
  Practicing architecture or using the title without registration is prohibited; plans and specifications for single-family dwellings, farm buildings, and structures up to 4,000 square feet and 20 feet tall are exempt from the practice definition. "Home designer" is permitted wording; "residential architect" is not.
  Source: ORS 671.020; ORS 671.030(1).
```

## 4.5 Who Can Do Which Work

The contracting-side exemption (CCB licensing) is independent from the trade-side exemptions (electrical and plumbing) and from the licensing regimes for special systems (LP gas, septic, well, refrigerant, boiler).

**Simple**

Oregon lets an owner do most of the work on their own house: the building, the plumbing, the wiring, the heating equipment. Special owner permits even allow installing your own septic system and drilling your own well. A few jobs are off limits at any price. LP gas work requires a state-licensed installer. Anything involving refrigerant (connecting a heat pump's lines, charging it) requires a federally certified technician. Every job, owner-done or hired, still gets a permit and an inspection.

**Applied**

Use this table before deciding to self-perform any regulated work.

| Work area | Owner-builder posture | Permit and inspection still required |
|---|---|---|
| General residential building | Often allowed under the homeowner path | Yes |
| Plumbing | Owner work allowed on owner's own building (ORS 693.020). No sale or lease restriction in the statute itself. No immediate-family extension. | Yes |
| Electrical | Narrower owner-or-immediate-family exemption (ORS 479.540), tied to property "not intended for sale, exchange, lease or rent." | Yes |

### Permit-Required Work By Trade

**Electrical:** Permit-required electrical work includes any permanent wiring, new circuits, panel upgrades, fuse-to-breaker conversions, garage-door opener receptacles, and low-voltage systems (alarms, audio-video, network). Permit-exempt electrical work is narrow: like-for-like replacement of outlets, switches, and fixtures with GFCI installed; lamp replacement; resetting fuses or breakers; replacing 30-amp-or-less garbage disposals or dishwashers; and low-voltage garage-door wiring.

**Plumbing:** Permit-required plumbing work includes new water-supply piping, drain-waste-vent piping, fixtures requiring concealed work, water heaters, sewer lines, water service, and backflow devices. Permit-exempt plumbing replacements are limited to like-for-like replacement of fixtures without altering concealed piping (faucets, toilets, sinks, hose bibbs, water filters, garbage disposals).

**Mechanical:** Permit-required mechanical work includes installing, altering, or repairing vented heating or cooling systems, woodstoves and fireplace inserts and pellet stoves, ductwork, gas piping, bath fans, dryer exhausts, kitchen range exhausts, heat pumps, mini-splits, and AC condensers. There is no blanket exemption for residential equipment replacement; a like-for-like furnace swap still requires a mechanical permit and final inspection.

**Professional**

The CCB licensing exemption (ORS 701.010) governs the contracting side. The plumbing exemption (ORS 693.020) and the electrical exemption (ORS 479.540) are independent statutes. LP gas (ORS 480.432), septic (DEQ rules under ORS 454 and OAR 340-071), water wells (ORS 537.747 and OAR 690-205), refrigerant handling (EPA Section 608), and boilers and pressure vessels (ORS 480.630) are separate licensing regimes that do not flow from the residential-permit framework.

Tenants do not have an owner-occupier exemption. ORS 90.145 prohibits a tenant from doing electrical or plumbing installation, maintenance, or repair without proper licensure even with a rent-reduction agreement. Property managers, landlords' employees, and business entities cannot use the owner-occupier exemption for someone else's residence.

Fire-sprinkler and rural water-supply rules interact. Oregon adopted NFPA 13D (2022 edition) as the Oregon One- and Two-Family Dwelling Fire Sprinkler Installation Code, and BCD's interpretation applies it to voluntarily installed systems in one- and two-family dwellings. NFPA 1142 (2022 edition), the rural water-supply sizing standard, permits the fire authority to reduce the minimum required water supply where the dwelling is sprinklered throughout. Published examples show a base 1,000-gallon-per-minute, one-hour fire flow for a dwelling under 5,000 square feet dropping to roughly 500 gallons per minute with an approved sprinkler system. No Oregon statute or rule compels the reduction; it is the fire district's discretionary call. The applicant confirms the credit and its size with the rural fire protection district before sizing any cistern or fire-flow supply; the sizing method is in the Water Independence chapter.

## 4.6 Paid Help, Family, And Subcontractors

ORS 455.320(1)(b) defines an owner-built dwelling as one whose structural components are constructed entirely by the owner who intends to occupy, or by the owner and friends and relatives of the owner assisting on an unpaid basis. The operative words are *entirely* and *unpaid*.

**Simple**

Friends and family can help build the house for free with no legal complications. The moment anyone gets paid, the rules change. A paid helper who is not a licensed contractor can make the homeowner an employer, with tax withholding, workers' compensation, and federal obligations attached. The two clean options are unpaid help and properly licensed contractors.

**Applied**

Before anyone touches the project, write down the labor plan:

- who will work
- whether they are paid
- what they will do
- what license they hold, if any
- which permit covers the work
- who supervises the work
- what insurance covers the jobsite

If anyone is paid and is not a properly licensed contractor, stop and resolve the employer obligations in the table below before work continues. Paid labor can make the homeowner an employer.

If a licensed subcontractor is hired:

- verify the CCB license before work starts (ccb.state.or.us license search)
- check the complaint and disciplinary history (search.ccb.state.or.us, the CCB's public per-license record search)
- verify the trade license (BCD-licensed for plumbing or electrical) where applicable
- put any agreement above $2,000 in writing; ORS 701.305 requires the contractor to deliver a written contract at that threshold, on the CCB's standard terms
- know the cancellation window: written cancellation is available until midnight of the next business day after signing (ORS 701.310)
- collect the certificate of insurance with the homeowner as additional insured if requested
- confirm workers' compensation coverage for the sub's employees
- file the contract, license record, insurance certificate, permit, and inspection result in the binder

Document unpaid help while it happens:

- keep a dated log for each work day naming each helper, the relationship (friend or relative), and the work performed
- collect a short signed statement from each unpaid helper, at or near the time of the work, that no payment or in-kind exchange was made or expected
- keep paid licensed subcontractors' records in a separate file from unpaid-helper records
- photograph the crew on structural work days, with names and dates noted

ORS 455.320(1)(b) makes the unpaid basis and the relationship the two facts to prove if the CCB or an insurer questions the help after the fact. No CCB or insurer test of this documentation practice is on record; how much documentation is legally sufficient is an attorney question.

**Professional**

The duties created when paid help is not properly licensed:

| Duty | Authority | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Workers' compensation coverage | ORS 656.027 | All workers are subject workers unless an exception applies |
| Householder/repair exception | ORS 656.027(2) | Applies to maintenance, repair, remodeling, or similar work in or about a private home; *Caddy v. SAIF*, 110 Or App 353 (1991) limited this to work on an existing private home, leaving new construction from scratch on raw land likely outside the exemption |
| Casual-labor exception | ORS 656.027(3) | Applies when total labor cost in any 30-day period is less than the indexed threshold (originally $500, $1,000 in the 2023 ORS edition, indexed annually) and the work is not in the employer's trade or business |
| Income-tax withholding and unemployment insurance | ORS 316 | If workers are not licensed contractors, the homeowner is treated as their employer; BIN registration may be required |
| Liability insurance | BCD form recommendation | Sufficient coverage for non-employee injuries on site |
| Federal employer obligations | IRS rules | Social Security and federal income-tax withholding |

The defensible posture: unpaid family or friends (no wages and no in-kind compensation) helping with structural work fits ORS 455.320; everyone else triggers employer obligations under ORS 656, ORS 316, and federal employment law. The only safe sub-case for paid help is using actual licensed CCB-endorsed subcontractors, with the licensed-GC-supervision rule from the 2008 amendment to ORS 701.010 applied where it applies.

A written contract is a statutory requirement above $2,000. ORS 701.305(1) prohibits a contractor from performing residential work for a property owner without a written contract where the aggregate price exceeds $2,000. Where a price starts lower and grows past $2,000 during performance, the contractor delivers the written contract within five days of knowing it will. A missing written contract does not void the contract. The threshold governs the contractor-to-owner contract and does not reach a subcontractor's agreement with a general contractor. Written subcontractor agreements remain this book's record-keeping practice on liability-management grounds, without a statutory trigger.

The CCB investigates activities, not categories of person. Under ORS 701.225, an authorized inspector may require any person engaged in regulated construction activity to demonstrate license compliance. A person contracting directly with the property owner who cannot demonstrate compliance receives a written notice of noncompliance, served on that person and on the property owner. The notice allows 10 days to comply before a stop-work order. A person who was never licensed at all may be ordered to stop immediately. The board holds subpoena and hearing power and may investigate jointly with city or county inspectors at board expense (ORS 701.225(2)). The CCB licensing track and the building department's code-compliance track are legally separate. Whether one triggers the other in practice is local, and the applicant confirms the local practice at pre-application where it matters to the plan.

CCB field investigators also run proactive sweeps of active construction sites; the 2024 statewide sweep activity produced 226 proposed violations. Active, visible construction is itself a trigger category, independent of any complaint. No published CCB statistic ranks complaint sources; the known trigger categories (neighbor complaint, building-department referral, workers'-compensation-carrier flag, sweep) are unranked.

The stacked exposure when paid help is unlicensed:

| Exposure | Amount or grade | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| CCB civil penalty | Up to $5,000 per offense | ORS 701.992(1) |
| Unlicensed contracting (the helper personally) | Class A misdemeanor | ORS 701.990(1); ORS 701.021 |
| Workers'-compensation noncoverage, first offense | Twice the premium that should have been paid, $1,000 minimum | OAR 812-005-0800; Workers' Compensation Division penalty schedule |
| Continued noncoverage after an order | Up to $250 per day | Same sources |
| Back withholding and unemployment tax | Assessed with penalties and interest, case-specific | ORS chapter 316; federal employment-tax rules |

No documented real-case aggregate total is public. Whether voluntary self-disclosure of a mid-project employer-status mistake produces different treatment than discovery is likewise unpublished. The penalty rule's first-offense amounts are smaller than its continued-noncompliance amounts, so prompt correction reduces exposure. The self-report route (which agency to contact first, and through whom) is a question for an Oregon employment or construction attorney before any contact is made.

## 4.8 Permit Exemptions For Small And Agricultural Structures

Two exemptions remove the structural permit for specific outbuildings: the accessory-structure exemption in the residential code and the agricultural-building exemption in statute. Both exemptions remove only the structural permit requirement. The residential code still applies, and the electrical, plumbing, and mechanical permit requirements are unchanged.

**Simple**

A small shed or shop can be legal to build without a building permit. Oregon exempts a one-story detached accessory structure (a building serving the main use of the property: a shed or a small shop) up to 200 square feet. On parcels of two acres or more, the limit rises to 400 square feet with a 20-foot setback. Farm and forest parcels can also qualify a barn or equipment building as an agricultural building. That exemption removes the structural permit entirely. Neither exemption skips the wiring, plumbing, or heating permits. Neither lets anyone live in the structure.

**Applied**

Before planning any structure around the accessory-structure exemption:

1. Confirm the limits: 200 square feet of building area and 15 feet of height, measured from grade plane (the average finished ground level against the building) to the average height of the highest roof surface.
2. On a parcel of 2.0 acres or greater, use the larger limit where it fits: 400 square feet, with the structure at least 20 feet from all property lines and all regulated structures. The setback clears both.
3. Pull the trade permits anyway. Electrical circuits for a press or mixer, plumbing, and mechanical work each need their own permit inside a permit-exempt building.
4. Build to code. The structure still meets the residential code's structural, material, and safety requirements.
5. For multiple exempt structures, get the county's written position first. The code text contains no aggregation limit; whether several exempt-sized structures near each other get treated as one larger structure is the building official's call.
6. Recheck the section against the 2026 residential code edition once adopted (anticipated near October 1, 2026); the figures above are the 2023 edition.

**Professional**

The 400-square-foot exception is an Oregon amendment; the base IRC contains no 2.0-acre expansion. The exemption removes the building permit only.

The residential code applies to detached one- and two-family dwellings and townhouses (Group R-3), not more than three stories, and their accessory structures. The code defines an accessory structure at up to 3,000 square feet of floor area and two stories, used incidentally to the dwelling on the same lot. A home business changes the classification analysis by use, not by rental status. A home office under 10 percent of the dwelling's floor area stays fully residential under the live/work provisions the scope references (Section 508.5). A workshop converted to a business with public or employee occupancy raises a change-of-use question that can cross into the commercial structural code (OSSC). The applicant puts the planned use to the local building official as a written classification question before finalizing any side-business use. No primary source establishes that renting a completed dwelling by itself changes its residential-code classification; classification turns on physical use and configuration.

Detection of unpermitted structures is a real exposure category. County assessors nationally use aerial and satellite imagery to find unassessed improvements, and code-enforcement use of the same tools is growing. No Oregon building department is documented doing so. A staged-construction plan rests on written exemption confirmations and the thresholds above, with detection from imagery treated as an existing practice rather than a hypothetical one.

A permit-exempt test structure in the planned wall material is a build-sequence option with no disclosure obligation attached. The alternate-method rules require no listing of unrelated exempt structures. An exempt structure was never reviewed, so no official is bound by it in either direction. How a specific official weighs a weathered, standing example of the material is that official's judgment. Raising the structure proactively at pre-application presents it as the demonstration it is. Whether to build a test structure first is the owner's sequencing decision. The Owner-Made Blocks And Alternative Concretes chapter contains the test-structure strategy itself.

### The Agricultural-Building Exemption

**Simple**

On farm or forest land, Oregon exempts a true agricultural building (a barn, equipment shed, or crop storage building) from the structural permit entirely. Qualification turns entirely on the building's use, against a closed statutory list. A dwelling never qualifies. A shop that manufactures building materials does not fit the listed uses.

**Applied**

1. Check the use against the statute's list before assuming the exemption: storage, maintenance, or repair of farm or forestry machinery and equipment; raising, harvesting, and selling crops or forest products; feeding, breeding, management, and sale of livestock, poultry, fur-bearing animals, or honeybees; dairying; and preparation and storage of farm produce.
2. Do not rely on the exemption for a block-production shop without the county's written answer. Block manufacturing appears nowhere on the list; the fallback tests (incidental and accessory to a listed use, personal to the farm owner and household, no greater hazard) are the county's judgment call, and producing blocks for sale fails the personal-use test.
3. Ask the county which qualification form it uses. BCD Form 2654 (Farm Agriculture/Equine Building Exemption Request) states it serves only the four BCD field-office counties (Coos, Umatilla, Sherman, and Wheeler), yet several other counties post the same form; the target county names its own current form, fee, and turnaround, none of which is stated in any statewide source.
4. Budget for the trade permits. The exemption form itself states the exemption applies to the structural permit only; electrical, mechanical, boiler, plumbing, and septic permits still apply.
5. Treat a later shop-to-house conversion as a full legal question. The statute excludes a dwelling from the definition; a conversion ends the qualification and exposes the never-reviewed structure to full code review as a residential structure. Obtain the building official's written statement of what a conversion triggers, and an attorney's review, before any conversion or physical connection to the house.
6. Compare the two paths in writing before choosing. The exemption path trades the permit fee and plan review for a county qualification process with its own county-set fee and timeline, plus the use-qualification risk; the ordinary accessory-structure permit path has a known fee schedule and carries neither risk. The county's two fee schedules decide the dollar difference.
7. For a production operation, describe the equipment and volume to DEQ's Air Quality program. Cement and concrete dust is a regulated particulate category; the Air Contaminant Discharge Permit threshold is exposure-and-volume-specific, with no fixed size cutoff.

**Code**

## 4.9 The Alternate-Method Path For A Material Outside The Prescriptive Code

An owner-made block wall, a double-wythe assembly, or any material outside the residential code's prescriptive tables is not prohibited. The code and the state rules contain a defined approval path, and most of it is open to a property owner directly.

**Simple**

The residential code lists pre-approved materials and methods. A material not on the lists can still be approved two ways. The local building official can approve it for one project under the code's equivalence rule. Or the state Building Codes Division (BCD) can issue an Alternate Method Ruling (a statewide decision that a material or method is acceptable). The state ruling does not expire with one project and does not depend on one official's opinion. It can even be requested before buying land.

**Applied**

To file an Alternate Method Ruling request:

1. Complete BCD Form 2936c (Alternate Method Ruling Application). The rule requires three content items: information on the material, design, or method; the specialty code section at issue; and a brief description of the technical and scientific facts behind the request. BCD may accept a substantially complete request.
2. Submit by mail (P.O. Box 14470, Salem, OR 97309-0404) or email (bcd.ptsptech@oregon.gov). No filing fee applies to the request. BCD names no natural-building or green-building contact position; the program inbox is the confirmed channel.
3. File before a land purchase where the material question should be settled first. No site address is a required field.
4. Track the advisory-board meeting where the request is discussed. Meetings are public under the Public Meetings Law; sign up with the boards coordinator to give verbal testimony, or submit written testimony at least 24 hours before the meeting.
5. Save the ruling with its stated scope and conditions in the project binder. A granted ruling is a standing statewide determination; a later project using the same material inside the ruling's scope does not relitigate the material question, and the local official still plan-reviews each specific project (foundation, connections, fire separation).

**Professional**

Three statewide tools exist under OAR 918-008, with different standing rules:

| Tool | Who may request | Scope | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Site-Specific Interpretation | A building official only | A single project; the site address is a required field | OAR 918-008-0090 |
| Statewide Code Interpretation | Any petitioner | Statewide | OAR 918-008-0085 |
| Alternate Method Ruling | Any petitioner | Statewide; no site required | OAR 918-008-0095 |

"Petitioner" is defined at OAR 918-008-0080 as any person residing, doing business, wishing to do business, or owning property in Oregon. The practical consequence: an owner facing a reluctant local official does not need that official's cooperation to start a state-level review. The official-only tool is the Site-Specific Interpretation, which is also the only one scoped to a single project.

Process facts:

- BCD's stated process: program staff work with the petitioner, local building officials, and industry advisory groups to develop the proposal; the appropriate advisory board recommends on the technical and scientific aspects; the division makes the final decision. No statutory decision deadline binds any stage, and no published figure states typical hours or review rounds.
- The reviewing board for a structural masonry or concrete request is the Building Codes Structures Board: 11 members under ORS 455.132 (member count set by the 2025 amendment). The statutory seats:
  - an architect
  - a general contractor specializing in buildings over three stories
  - a heavy-industry contractor
  - a building-trades representative
  - a utility or energy-supplier representative
  - a fire-protection-agency representative
  - a building official
  - a commercial-building owner or manager
  - an Oregon Disabilities Commission-recommended member
  - a fire protection engineer
  - a structural engineer specializing in residential buildings over three stories

  No seat is masonry-specific, and no general public or consumer seat exists on this board. The Residential and Manufactured Structures Board administers the low-rise residential program (ORS 455.135). Which board takes a residential-scale structural novelty is a question the petitioner puts to BCD at filing.
- Separate advisory boards exist per program (structures; residential and manufactured structures; electrical and elevator). A project with structural and electrical novelties files separate requests reviewed by separate boards. The stated coordination mechanism sits at the appeal stage: where more than one specialty code is at issue, the board's decision escalates to the director (ORS 455.475(1)(a)). Whether BCD coordinates related filings at intake is unstated; the petitioner asks BCD staff directly.
- Reconsideration (OAR 918-008-0115): a denied site-specific or statewide interpretation is reconsiderable on request, with no new-evidence threshold in the rule. The rule names interpretations, not Alternate Method Rulings; whether it reaches a denied ruling is unresolved on the rule text. The decision stays in effect during reconsideration unless a stay is granted.
- Appeals of a building official's decision cost $20 to the state specialty code chief and a second $20 on to the advisory board (OAR 918-008-0120). Who pays for board-commissioned technical review is stated nowhere in the rules; the petitioner asks BCD directly. No residential alternative-materials appeal outcome is documented in public sources; BCD's appeal log is a records-request item.
- An appeal during plan review extends the plan-review deadline by the appeal's duration (ORS 455.475(3)); the 180-day permit-activity clock has no parallel tolling rule, stated with the permit-expiration rules earlier in this chapter.

Precedent: Benton County permitted a rammed-earth house (reported 2017 as the county's first) after requiring architectural drawings, engineered drawings, and compression testing of the earth-cement mixture. No cost or elapsed-time figures for that approval are public; the county's Community Development office holds the permit record. No documented case is public of an Oregon owner-builder converting a favorable local position into a statewide ruling; the mechanism itself is confirmed rule text.

### Sequencing The Approval

Three orders exist. The choice is the owner's; the confirmed facts per order:

**Option A: state-level ruling first, before land purchase or permit filing.** Every supporting element is confirmed rule text: no site address required, statewide standing effect, independence from local staff turnover. The trade-off: no statutory deadline binds BCD, so the calendar is open-ended, and site-specific issues (setbacks, local amendments) surface only after a site exists.

**Option B: alternative-materials question submitted inside the local permit application.** One process instead of two. The reviewer decides whether the application counts as a complex structure exempt from the 10/15-business-day plan-review clock; ORS 455.467(2)(b) names complex structures, not alternative-material requests, so the exemption is the reviewer's call. The schedules couple: nothing on the permit gets approved faster than the material question.

**Option C: main permit first, wall system as a deferred submittal.** The deferred-submittal statutory language is commercial-only by its own words (ORS 455.467(3)). Residential deferral is local, non-statutory accommodation, with no documented rural-county practice found. No rule protects the 180-day activity clock while the deferred item is pending. The least-supported order of the three.

Where a deferred submittal is used anyway: the project designates one Design Professional of Responsible Charge (an Oregon-licensed professional engineer qualifies; no architect is required for a single-family dwelling under ORS 671.030(1)). The jurisdiction charges a deferred-submittal plan-review fee on the deferred item's own valuation. Wilsonville's published policy (BPP 111) is the named example, with 5-10 business day review targets. No jurisdiction's published rule found states what happens when a deferred item is rejected after other work has proceeded. The applicant asks the specific building official, in writing and before relying on deferral, what a rejection triggers and whether ongoing other work keeps the main permit alive.

**Code**

```

  The building official approves an alternative that complies with the intent of the code and is at least equivalent to the prescribed requirements; the approval attaches to the specific project.
  Source: ORSC R104.11 (2023 edition).

## 4.10 Contract Building Departments

**Simple**

Some Oregon cities and counties do not staff their own building department. They hire a private firm (Northwest Code Professionals is one, serving about 50 jurisdictions across Oregon, Washington, and Idaho) to run plan review and inspections under contract. Oregon law attaches extra oversight to that arrangement. An alternative-materials approval from a contract official gets a second check by the municipality's own staff. An extra local appeal board also exists.

**Professional**

ORS 455.202 defines a contract building official's "discretionary decision" to include allowing an alternative material, design, or method of construction, and attaches four controls:

- A designated qualified employee of the municipality reviews and ratifies or disapproves each discretionary decision within 30 days of notice; the contract official notifies both the qualified employee and the permit applicant, with a description of hearing and appeal options.
- The municipality establishes a local board for appeals of the contract official's discretionary decisions.
- That appeal right is in addition to, not in place of, the standard state appeal path.
- An independent auditor examines the building-inspection program's finances at least every two years.

Fee-schedule parity and inspector continuity are contract terms between the municipality and the firm, standardized nowhere in the statute. A contract can change or end mid-project. No statute or documented case states what happens to an open permit or an in-process alternative-materials approval when it does. The applicant asks the county at pre-application how long the current contract runs and what the county's succession practice is for open permits and prior determinations.

Northwest Code Professionals markets a separate paid code-consulting service to architects and developers. Its own site states consulting services are not available in Oregon (checked 2026-07-13). Where any contracted firm does sell pre-submission consulting in Oregon, the paid consultation and the later official review are legally distinct even when performed by the same staff. A favorable consultation binds nothing.

**Code**

```
A contract building official's alternative-materials call is a defined discretionary decision with mandatory oversight.
  Ratification or disapproval by a designated municipal qualified employee within 30 days; notice to the applicant with hearing and appeal options; a mandatory local appeal board; appeal rights in addition to all other appeal rights; an independent financial audit at least every two years.
  Source: ORS 455.202.
```

## 4.11 Pre-Application Contact And The Written Record

**Simple**

Counties answer questions before a permit application. Most offer a pre-application or early-assistance meeting. Statements made in those meetings bind nothing. The plan-review decision is made against the submitted application. An owner-builder timeline runs two to three years or more. Oregon adopts a new code edition on a roughly three-year cycle, and staff change. A favorable answer remains usable through those changes only as a written, dated record. A state-level ruling remains usable statewide regardless of local staff changes.

**Applied**

Before purchase, send the target county's building official a written inquiry (email is sufficient), addressed by title, requesting a written reply. Ask as separate numbered questions:

1. whether the jurisdiction has reviewed or approved any R104.11 alternative-materials request for the material, and for a copy of any approval and its conditions
2. whether the department will treat a statewide Alternate Method Ruling as binding on a future permit application, or will conduct its own independent review
3. what engineering documentation (compression testing, a stamped design, named ASTM or ACI standards) the department requires to approve the material where no prior ruling exists
4. whether any local ordinance or amendment affects alternative-materials review beyond the statewide framework
5. who the current building official of record is

File the dated reply in the project binder. A written, dated answer attributed to the office remains usable after staff turnover.

At the meeting itself, state the specific code section the material must satisfy and present the existing engineering or testing data. The state process develops proposals collaboratively with the petitioner. A meeting that opens with the code section and the supporting data starts the review from submitted evidence and shortens it.

Schedule the meeting where offered:

- Benton County offers free pre-development and pre-construction meetings (confirmed 2026).
- Portland charges $77.50 (2026) for a Time Certain Appointment and runs a paid Life Safety Preliminary Meeting program with an added fee for a structural reviewer; current figures are in Portland's fee schedule.
- Rural-county fees vary; the target county states its own fee and lead time.

**Professional**

Pre-application output format and effect are set locally. No statewide rule makes a meeting statement binding. The written-inquiry record above is the durable instrument. Meeting minutes and checklists are not designed to memorialize a yes-or-no position on a specific material question.

Meeting attendance is unstandardized, and no documented account compares bringing the engineer of record to the first meeting against bringing the engineer later. Septic and well are separate agency tracks (county environmental health or DEQ; OWRD). Those contractors attend a building-department meeting only where a cross-agency question is on the agenda. No combined multi-agency site-visit program exists. Where one visit day is wanted, the owner schedules each agency separately around a shared date. The septic site evaluation generally comes first because other site-plan elements depend on its result.

Code-edition cadence: Oregon adopts specialty-code editions on a roughly three-year cycle tied to the model-code cycle. The 2023 ORSC is current; the 2026 ORSC (2024 IRC base) is anticipated to adopt near October 1, 2026. A build that spans an adoption confirms with the building department which edition the permit vests under. No published statistic tracks building-official turnover in any county. The mitigation is the written record above and the statewide ruling, which remains effective after a local staff change.

## 4.12 Parallel Alternative-Method Processes At Other Agencies

**Simple**

The building code is not the only rulebook with an equivalence path. Wastewater rules (DEQ), well-construction rules (OWRD), and workplace-safety rules (Oregon OSHA) each contain their own version. A project combining a novel wall system, an experimental wastewater component, and a non-standard well runs three separate applications at three agencies. No joint intake exists. The owner is the only party tracking all of them.

**Professional**

| Track | Rule | Who applies | Fee | Speed provisions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Building: Alternate Method Ruling | OAR 918-008-0095 | Any petitioner | None stated | No deadline stated |
| Onsite wastewater: new or innovative technology | OAR 340-071-0135 | Any person | Review fee per OAR 340-071-0140(5), Table 9F; amount confirmed with DEQ | No deadline stated |
| Water supply well: special standards | OAR 690-200-0021 | A bonded well constructor | None stated | Oral approval permitted; written request within three working days after oral approval |
| Workplace safety: variance | OAR 437-001-0400 to 437-001-0435 | Any employer | None stated in the text read | Interim order available pending decision |

DEQ approves a new or innovative onsite technology where it protects public health, safety, and waters of the state as effectively as authorized systems. Approval may be limited to demonstrated locations or conditions. The rule also provides for study installations of unapproved technologies. The well-construction request runs through a bonded well constructor rather than the owner. It must state four items: the well location; the distances to the nearest well and septic system; the reason the standard rules cannot be met; and a design description with a site map. The proposed method must provide at least the same resource protection as the rules. The Oregon OSHA variance becomes relevant once the project has an employer (the first paid worker). A temporary variance requires proof of inability to comply on time, plus interim employee protections and a compliance timetable. A permanent variance requires showing the actual practice meets the rule's safety intent by other means. Affected employees are notified and may comment.

None of the three rules references the others, and none references the building-code process. The Water Independence chapter contains the wastewater and well sizing detail. The Owner-Made Blocks And Alternative Concretes chapter contains the production-equipment safety detail.

**Code**

```
DEQ may approve new or innovative onsite wastewater technologies.
  Approval where the technology protects public health, safety, and waters of the state as effectively as authorized systems; any person may apply; the application includes the review fee set at OAR 340-071-0140(5) (Table 9F); approval may be limited to demonstrated locations or conditions.
  Source: OAR 340-071-0135.

OWRD may approve well construction outside the standard rules.
  A bonded well constructor requests approval before completion; oral approval is permitted with a written request within three working days; the proposal must provide at least the same level of resource protection as the rules; required fields include location, distances to the nearest well and septic system, and a design description with a site map.
  Source: OAR 690-200-0021.

Oregon OSHA grants variances from rules affecting working conditions.
  Any employer may apply; a temporary variance requires proof of inability to comply by the effective date, interim employee protections, and a compliance timetable; a permanent variance requires equivalent safety by other means; an interim order may apply pending the decision.
  Source: OAR 437-001-0400; OAR 437-001-0405.
```

## 4.13 Insurance For An Unconventional Build

**Simple**

No code approval obligates a carrier to write a policy. No Oregon rule conditions the owner-builder permit on insurance. The homeowner form recommends liability coverage. Finding a carrier for an unconventional wall system runs through a specialty broker. The search starts before the design is final.

**Applied**

1. Sequence insurability before ground-breaking: secure the alternate-method approval or a clear path to it; then approach a broker with surplus-lines access (a market for hard-to-place risks outside standard carriers), presenting the engineering documentation and any fire-test data; request a non-binding indication (a preliminary read from a carrier, not a bindable quote). A bindable quote requires a completed application against finished construction documents.
2. Treat the broker's documentation list as the underwriting requirement; no published list exists.
3. Describe the house by its assembly performance rather than a bare material label: noncombustible mass-wall construction, the finish systems and their ratings, and any tested fire-resistance rating. This description practice is reasoned from the licensing-language table earlier in this chapter, without a documented underwriting account behind it; test it in the broker conversation.
4. Put three questions to the broker in writing: whether the carrier gives any weight to the code-approval letter; whether the mass wall's fire performance earns any premium credit; and what builder's-risk coverage (insurance on the structure while under construction) requires for a self-manufactured structural material, including coverage during block production before any wall exists.
5. Named starting point: Insure Pacific advertises surplus-lines access for hard-to-place Oregon homes (2026). No carrier is documented writing rammed-earth, compressed-earth-block, or unconventional-masonry Oregon homes by name, and no premium comparison against conventional construction exists in public sources.

**Professional**

No Oregon statute conditions the owner-builder permit on insurance or a bond, and no county-level requirement was found. The CCB information notice recommends liability coverage sufficient for injuries to non-employee site visitors. License bonds attach to licensed contractors, not exempt owner-builders. The applicant confirms with the target county whether any local ordinance adds a condition. Three questions have no published answers: whether an R104.11 approval letter carries underwriting weight; whether fire performance offsets the unconventional classification; and what builder's-risk terms apply to owner-made materials. Each is a carrier-specific commercial judgment, and the quote process is the only source. Resale insurability follows the record. A completed, occupied home with a clean certificate-of-occupancy history and the closeout binder presents a verifiable history that a proposed unconventional build cannot. The binder (permits, approvals, the alternate-method ruling and its conditions, inspection records) is what a downstream underwriter or appraiser verifies against.

## 4.14 Financing, Appraisal, And Property Tax

**Simple**

A construction loan for an unconventional house hits one specific problem: the appraisal. An appraiser values a house against recent sales of similar homes nearby. No nearby owner-made-block or rammed-earth sales exist. The lending industry documents a workaround (search farther away and adjust). Lender categories exist that depend less on comparables. The fallback is building from cash, which removes the appraisal and draw-inspection problems. The cost is needing the full budget without borrowing.

**Applied**

1. Ask the prospective lender, before application, how its appraisal panel handles a property with no local comparable sales.
2. Ask who performs draw inspections (the lender's progress checks that release construction funds) and whether the project budget, built around block-production milestones instead of framing-drywall-paint milestones, needs a briefing document for that inspector.
3. Include portfolio lenders (banks and credit unions holding loans on their own books instead of selling them) in the search; they set their own underwriting and can weigh the engineer's stamp and an in-person inspection over a standard appraisal. Whether a specific Oregon portfolio lender has done so for an alternative-materials home is undocumented; the phone inquiry is the source.
4. Expect owner-builder loan products to price the missing general contractor: commonly cited down payments run 25-35 percent for owner-builder products against 10-20 percent for conventional construction loans (2026).
5. For land bought at county tax foreclosure or sheriff sale, ask a title company before purchase whether it will insure, and a lender lend, on the county's no-warranty deed, or whether a curative step (title certification or a quiet-title action) comes first. The Land, Site, And Feasibility chapter contains the acquisition detail.

**Professional**

The appraisal mechanic is documented at the national level. Fannie Mae's selling guide requires the appraiser to address market resistance where improvements are incompatible with the competitive market and to explain the effect on value and marketability (Selling Guide B4-1.3-05). The standard workaround expands the comparable-sales search radius beyond normal distance guidelines and documents dollar adjustments between locations. Whether a specific construction-to-permanent lender's rules permit that workaround or decline the loan is lender-specific, with no documented Oregon alternative-materials account either way.

Property-tax assessment uses the comparables logic in the county's direction. Oregon assessors value by Real Market Value (ORS 308.205) through statistical modeling of similar-property groups, not parcel-by-parcel appraisal. A structurally unusual home fits the model badly in either direction (an under-valuation, or an over-valuation treating a lower-cost owner-built structure as a conventionally built one of the same size). The code-approval letter and the certificate of occupancy carry no special valuation weight. The remedy for a wrong assessed value is the county Board of Property Tax Appeals.

**Code**

```
Assessed value rests on Real Market Value.
  The estimated price an informed buyer would reasonably pay an informed seller in an arm's-length transaction as of the assessment date; assessors model groups of similar properties, and the appeal route is the county Board of Property Tax Appeals.
  Source: ORS 308.205; Oregon Department of Revenue real-property assessment publications.
```

## 4.15 Certificate Of Occupancy And Temporary Certificate Of Occupancy

Legal occupancy of a new dwelling requires either a certificate of occupancy or a temporary certificate of occupancy. Under ORSC R110.1, a building or structure may not be used or occupied until the building official has issued a certificate of occupancy.

**Simple**

Nobody may live in the house until the building official issues the certificate of occupancy (the CO). That includes the owner who built it. If the house is safe to live in but small items remain unfinished, the official can issue a temporary certificate (a TCO). The TCO sets a deadline for finishing. Moving in without either one is illegal occupancy, and it typically voids or suspends homeowner insurance coverage. The exact effect depends on the policy language.

**Applied**

Before the certificate of occupancy is issued, three requirements must all be met.

1. **All inspection disciplines passed.** Building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, and site-development inspections are all approved. Site development includes permanent erosion control, on-site stormwater, sanitary facilities, and any landscape requirements imposed by zoning or land-use review.
2. **Contractor information disclosure submitted.** OAR 918-480-0140(3) requires the general contractor or owner who was issued the structural permit to provide the building official with contact information and license information for the GC, the electrical contractor, the HVAC contractor, and the plumbing contractor that performed work on the dwelling. BCD Form 4823 (Notification of Contractor License Information) is the statewide form.
3. **CO statutory minimum content.** ORSC R110.3 requires the CO to include the permit number, address, owner name, use of building, building official's name, code edition under which the permit was issued, and any special stipulations or conditions of the permit.

The certificate is required for new dwellings, duplexes, and ADUs. It is not required for residential additions, alterations, or accessory structures like garages.

**Professional**

The temporary certificate of occupancy is discretionary under ORSC R110.4. The building official may issue a TCO before the permitted work completes, provided the occupied portion is safe to occupy. The official sets the TCO's validity period.

Typical TCO conditions across Oregon jurisdictions (Portland's program guide is the most-cited model):

- minimum fire and life-safety requirements complete and approved
- temporary erosion control in place
- all five inspection disciplines agree the TCO may be issued
- TCO issuance fee paid
- surety or security may be required for unfinished work (Portland sets surety at 120% of the value of remaining work)
- the TCO document lists outstanding items, completion date, and expiration date

Portland TCOs are valid 30 days from issuance. Extensions run in 30-day increments at the building official's discretion. Each extension requires a written rationale for the remaining items and a fee equal to the initial issuance fee. There is no statewide cap on extensions. Other jurisdictions (Bend, Eugene, Salem, county departments) use comparable 30-day or 60-day windows.

The CO and TCO do not start an owner-builder timing clock for resale. They are the gate to legal occupancy. The federal §121 capital-gains use clock starts when the owner occupies as a principal residence, which is the date of move-in following CO or TCO issuance, not the issuance itself.

What CO and TCO matter for, separately from the owner-builder timing question:

- legal occupancy (ORSC R110.1)
- federal §121 capital-gains exclusion (the use clock starts at move-in)
- lender disbursement (most construction-to-perm loans require CO before converting; some accept TCO with conditions)
- insurance binding (homeowner policies typically require CO or TCO before binding occupancy coverage)
- property-tax assessment (the assessor may treat substantial completion as the trigger for adding improvement value to the roll)
- address, utilities, and services (USPS, voter registration, school enrollment all require an occupiable address)

### Living On The Parcel Before The House Exists

**Simple**

Building the shop first and living in it while building the house raises a zoning question and a code question at once. Any structure used as a dwelling needs its own certificate of occupancy. A shop permitted as non-habitable was never built to dwelling standards.

**Professional**

ORSC R110.1 gates residential use of any structure, including a shop or garage used as a dwelling, regardless of whether a primary dwelling exists on the parcel. A structure permitted as non-habitable is not built to Group R-3 requirements for egress, light and ventilation, insulation, and sanitation. Occupying it without a permit revision and inspections to residential requirements is itself the code violation, separate from which structure came first. Whether the county allows any dwelling use of an accessory structure before a permitted primary residence is county zoning code. So is whether it issues a temporary occupancy permit for an RV or manufactured home during construction (several Oregon counties name that permit type). The applicant asks the county planning department, not the building department, for that permit type by name and its conditions.

**Code**

```
Occupancy requires the certificate.
  A building or structure shall not be used or occupied, in whole or in part, until the building official has issued a certificate of occupancy.
  Source: ORSC R110.1 (2023 edition).

Occupancy gating applies to any structure used as a dwelling.
  A shop or garage used as a residence requires its own certificate of occupancy and residential-code compliance, regardless of whether a primary dwelling exists on the parcel.
  Source: ORSC R110.1 (2023 edition).

A passing final inspection entitles the owner to the certificate.
  After inspection with no violations found, the CO "shall issue," containing at minimum the permit number, address, owner name, building use, building official's name, code edition of the permit, and any special conditions.
  Source: ORSC R110.3.

The temporary certificate is discretionary and time-limited.
  The building official may issue a TCO before all work completes, provided the occupied portion is safe, with a set validity period.
  Source: ORSC R110.4.

  The building official must issue a CO before occupancy; subsection (3) requires disclosure of the GC, electrical, HVAC, and plumbing contractors' contact and license information; subsection (4) permits revocation where misrepresentation is found.

## 4.16 Documents Required Before Certificate Of Occupancy

Consolidated checklist of items that must all be complete before the CO is issued.

1. final approved building inspection
2. final approved electrical inspection
3. final approved plumbing inspection
4. final approved mechanical inspection
5. final approved site-development inspection (erosion control, stormwater, sanitary, landscape per zoning or land-use review)

## 4.17 The Inspection Sequence: Overview

Inspections are the legal gates of the build: work stays exposed until the matching inspection passes, each discipline finals separately, and the certificate of occupancy waits on every final plus the contractor-disclosure form. The sequence runs from permit application through pre-construction posting, site and erosion control, footing and foundation, foundation drainage, under-slab, the trade roughs (plumbing, electrical, mechanical), insulation and air sealing, energy testing, the discipline finals, and the site-development final. The stage-by-stage table (each stage's legal trigger and the record it produces) and the readiness criteria for every inspection are in the Inspection And Approval Sequence chapter.

Where a temporary certificate of occupancy is issued before the sequence completes, the building official defines the minimum life-safety items; the temporary certificate waives none of the remaining items, which still close within its time window or extensions before the full certificate issues. The certificate requirements themselves are in the certificate-of-occupancy section of this chapter.
