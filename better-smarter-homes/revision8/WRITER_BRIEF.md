# Writer Brief

**Version 5, 2026-10-29.** This file is the instruction set for a chapter writer. It is the only
file the chapter loop edits: when a chapter comes back wrong, this brief changes, and the next
chapter tests the change. The version history is at the end.

You are writing one chapter of Stone and Wire, Revision 8. The chapter number and file are in
your dispatch message. You rewrite the chapter in place, from its Revision 7 text and the
answered research, to the Revision 8 standard. You work alone on this chapter.

## Read first, in full, in this order

1. `STYLE_GUIDE.md` (this folder). The voice, the layer contract, the bans, the targets, the judgment checks.
2. `FAULT_CATALOG.md`. The 54 named faults. You will be judged by name against them.
3. `CHAPTER_TEMPLATE.md`. The anatomy every chapter has.
4. `exemplars/anchor-bolts-sample-page.md` and `exemplars/before-and-after.md`.
5. `C:\Users\jon\Projects\writing-style-guide-v2.md`, the general prose guide, all of it.

Then your chapter's inputs:

6. Your chapter file in `manuscript/`. It holds the Revision 7 text, already renumbered. This is the baseline. Every fact in it survives or is named in Open items.
7. Your chapter's answer files, listed in `research/INDEX.md`. Every answered item is integrated, or routed to another chapter with a one-line note in your worklog saying where and why.
8. `research/OBLIGATIONS.md`: the entries that name your chapter.
9. The current text of the chapters your chapter borders (`BOOK_STRUCTURE.md`, "Boundaries to draw explicitly", and the Lands-in lines in your answer files). Read them for consistency: the same numbers, the same positions, each names the other by topic at the overlap.
10. `../revision6_code_library/README.md` before you verify or add any Code entry.

## Do

1. **Plan first.** Write a section plan and an item map (which answer items land in which section) into `worklogs/chNN.md` before any prose. Keep that file current after every section: done, doing, next. It is how the orchestrator follows the work.
2. **Write section by section** into your manuscript file, saving after each section. The anatomy is `CHAPTER_TEMPLATE.md`, exactly. Every sentence answers to `STYLE_GUIDE.md`.
   - **Code entries are telegraphic.** The value line is one sentence, the exact limit or trigger, under 35 words; the lint fails a longer line or a second sentence. When two code editions differ, state the text of the edition the permit will most likely use and put the other edition's section number in the Source line; never write both editions' texts into one value line. A table-shaped value (trench length by soil group and depth, setbacks by component) is stated as its range in the entry ("50 to 175 linear feet per 150 gallons a day, by soil group and effective depth") and the full table goes in Professional as a markdown table. Cite federal statutes by their United States Code or CFR citation ("26 U.S.C. 121", "40 CFR part 82"), never as "IRC", which in this book means the International Residential Code. Case citations keep their standard form ("Reeves v. Yamhill County, 53 Or LUBA 4, 2006"); the lint does not count a citation's year as a cost tag.
   - **No semicolons in a Code entry's requirement or value line.** The semicolon ban covers the Code layer. A value with two parts joins them with a comma or "and", or becomes two entries. A Source line may separate two citations with a semicolon, and that is the only semicolon allowed in the chapter. A Code entry's Source line names a document you read: a change summary, a fact sheet or another state's reproduction of a code is not the code, and an entry known only from one waits in Open items.
   - **A Code entry states a rule an inspector could check:** a number, a date, a named condition, a yes or no, read from the rule's own text. A pointer to a body of law is not an entry ("Counties zone rural land under state framework law. Source: ORS chapter 215"; "Timber harvest follows the forest practice rules. Source: OAR chapter 629"; "Utility extensions follow each utility's filed policy"). It is one sentence in Professional with the citation in the sentence, and the lint fails a Source line that cites only a chapter, a division or "the local ordinance". A rule known only from a fact sheet, a news story or an index goes in Professional with that source, and its entry waits in Open items. The Source line names the document the value was read from: dimensions read from a 2021 district guide built on the 2018 fire code cite that guide, and the current edition is an Open item until it is read. An entry demoted to a Professional sentence keeps its citation in the sentence.
   - **Professional does not repeat the Simple block.** Simple states the plain fact for a reader who stops there; Professional adds what Simple cannot carry (the mechanism, the parameter, the source) and never restates Simple's conclusion in other words. "Depth costs daylight and adds an exterior reveal to detail" after a Simple block that said both is cut. Read each section's Simple and Professional blocks side by side before you report.
   - **Professional repeats neither Simple nor Applied,** and every Professional paragraph, like every Simple block, ends on its last fact, never on a line built to land. A "therefore" sentence that restates an Applied step is cut. Before you report, list every value or rule the chapter states in two sections and keep it only in the section that owns it.
   - **A later chapter names an earlier chapter's entry; it does not repeat it.** Before writing a Code entry or a procedure, check whether a bordering chapter already in Revision 8 text carries it (the septic setbacks are the land chapter's; title on tax-foreclosure land is the land chapter's). If it does, one sentence names that chapter by topic and adds only what your chapter needs.
   - **A bordering chapter's decided value is the book's value.** Where a bordering Revision 8 chapter's Decisions list or Code entry records a value (a set point, a control protocol, an edition), use it and name that chapter by topic. If you think it is wrong, keep it, say so under Unsure and file a cross-chapter flag. Never write a second value for the same thing.
   - **A generic person is "they".** An owner, an inspector, a worker or a buyer is "they" or is named by role ("the owner may wire a residence the owner occupies"), never "he" or "she".
   - **Echo is any statement, not only a number.** Before you report, read each section's Professional block against its Code block. A rule or fact stated in both is cut from one of them. Professional keeps the mechanism, the consequence or the reason; Code keeps the rule. A Professional block left with nothing but restated rules is deleted, and the section goes without one.
   - **Point at the text at most once per section, and at other chapters one at a time.** When Professional leaves a rule to its Code entry, it states what the rule does to the house and lets the entry state the rule. "In the entry below" appears at most once per section. A sentence that points at another part of this chapter ("as the opening section states", "the values in the steps") is cut. A cross-reference is one sentence naming one other chapter by topic, at most once per section, only where the reader needs it on this page. Never pack two or three chapters into one sentence, and never dodge the count with "the HVAC design", "belongs to Resilience" or "Code entries for X are in the Y chapter". Routed items are recorded in your worklog, not announced in the body.
   - **Code entries live in the chapter that owns the subject.** An overview or orientation section (the owner-builder's place in the code map, a roles table) names the owning chapter by topic and carries at most the one or two entries the reader needs on this page. An answered item whose conclusion is that a rule does not apply to this house gets one sentence in Professional, not a Code entry.
   - **Numbers.** A range is already approximate: no "about", "roughly" or "approximately" before "X to Y". A planning figure from a contractor guide, a listing average or a forum is rounded to two significant figures ("$700 to $2,300 per acre", not "$733 to $2,333"; never "$3,993 per acre across 21 dissimilar parcels"); exact figures are for fees, statutory limits, test results and quotes. A count stated in prose ("five conditions", "six hazards", "seven values") is checked against the list or table it describes before the section is saved, and the same count is used everywhere in the chapter. Where no Oregon figure exists, the best figure from elsewhere is kept and labeled with its place ("Rent-All of Salem, Massachusetts"), and the Oregon figure becomes an Open item; a labeled number informs more than none (Jon, 2026-10-28).
   - **Name the source or state the fact.** Never hang a figure on a class of sources: "secondary sources report", "industry sources", "general homesteading guidance", "documented examples", "independent-agent reporting", "in the published cases". Name the source in the sentence (OSU Extension, DEQ's fact sheet, Clackamas County's code), or state the figure as a planning figure and let Open items carry the doubt. "At least one county" and "at least one utility" are banned: name the one the source names. The lint treats all of these as research narration.
   - **State the finding, never the search.** A sentence about the state of the evidence ("No study measures...", "No source gives...", "rather than a controlled trial") is research narration: the gap goes to Open items and the body states what is known. A weak source is named as what it is ("NiftyPM's project blog"), never promoted to "guidance", "the literature", "research", "national data" or "one survey". A study is named by its authors and year, or its title, never by its journal or publisher, and never as "one study", "another" or "every source". A health or safety claim names its study or agency exactly as a code value names its section.
   - **A figure keeps what its source measured.** A recommended intake is not a loss, a response-time target is not an arrival time, a permitted exposure is not a damage dose, a guideline is not a limit, a subgroup (nonmelanoma skin cancer) is not the whole. Simple may say it plainly but may not change the quantity.
   - **A threshold the plan sets is the plan's choice.** A number of days, hours or years that no source gives (two rest days, a physician after a week, 500 hours) is stated as this plan's choice, tagged (Jon to confirm) in Decisions and listed in Open items. When to see a physician is stated by the clinical sign the named source gives, not by a day count.
   - **The one-line condition before an Applied procedure is for procedures that need it.** Do not open every Applied block with a framing imperative. Where the steps stand on their own, start with step 1. A numbered list is never interrupted by a table; put the table before the list or after it.
   - **End every Simple block on its last fact about the land or the house, not on what the reader does.** "You read it in full before the title contingency expires", "You price the change before the offer", "You ask the lender before the offer" are instructions, and Applied holds them; eight of chapter 3's twenty-one Simple blocks closed that way. Not on an inverted or three-part closing line ("Below all of them sit a brochure, a video, or a habit from another state"), not on a warning in proverb cadence ("an hour after the pour nothing can be changed"), not on a colloquial flourish ("closed to you at any price"), not on a crafted triad ("everything on it, under it, or leading to it") or a cleft ("What turns a farm parcel into a building site is the county's written answer"). State the mechanism: "Once the concrete sets, the only correction is demolition." Then read consecutive Simple blocks together. A phrase that appears in two in a row ("the first step") is changed in one of them.
   - **Say a thing once.** The opening, Jon's note and the first Simple block do not repeat one another's sentences. A later section does not restate an earlier one ("a clean map result is only a screen" appeared six times in chapter 3); it refers to the earlier section by topic in a clause, or trusts the reader. A phrase that is the chapter's refrain ("in writing" 23 times, "before the offer" 19) is said in the opening and at most three more times, where the stake is highest; the lint lists the chapter's most repeated phrases.
   - **Prose carries at most four items in a list;** five or more go in a table or bullets, or are cut to the ones that matter here. "Distance affects labor productivity, subcontractor willingness, delivery cost, inspection scheduling, emergency response, utility options, the resale pool and owner fatigue" is an inventory, not a sentence. A Professional paragraph opens with a concrete subject and a verb a person could watch, not two abstractions equated ("Septic feasibility is a layout problem", "Utility feasibility sets early geometry", "Resource-zone dwelling restrictions reach underwriting through buildability", "Fixed-and-free comes first, and paid-and-slow starts in parallel"). Write what happens: "The drainfield, its reserve area, the well and the house have to fit on one plan with the setbacks between them."
   - **Every sentence follows from the one before it.** A fact that belongs nowhere (a permit portal appended to a list of truck-access checks) gets its own place, a table row or a short paragraph under a subheading, or goes to the Dropped list. Encyclopedia history that changes nothing the reader does (a company's 2020 land trades, an agency's 1977 price reports, a pilot project that ended without result) is dropped and listed in one line under Dropped.
   - **No sentence about the text itself, whatever the words.** The ban on "this chapter" covers its paraphrases: "What follows is the screening a parcel passes", "Below is the purchase on one page", "Nothing here is new, because each item is stated in full in an earlier section", "The order above follows from", "Step 2 catches". State the fact. Pointing at furniture on the same page ("the table below") is allowed.
   - **Sections run in the reader's decision order,** not in Revision 7's order. When they differ, reorder and say so in the worklog; chapter 3 kept the climate loads ahead of zoning because Revision 7 had them there.
   - **Tags and the Dropped list.** (decided) is for a choice in the record: Jon's words, an earlier chapter's Decisions list, or a Revision 7 statement of the project's choice; a sound practice the chapter recommends is (Jon to confirm). The last bullet of Open items is headed **Dropped from Revision 7.** followed by what went and why, without the Unknown, Answered by and Assumed meanwhile fields.
3. **Preserve and grow.** Every fact, value, table, Code entry and cross-reference in the Revision 7 text survives, rewritten to the standard, or appears in Open items with the reason it went. Expect the chapter to grow where the research supports it, most of all in Applied, which Revision 7 under-wrote.
4. **Fix the inherited defects** as you go: stale section references, Professional blocks that echo Code, sections without a Simple block, runs of bold run-in paragraphs, research narration, delegation endings, "(2026)" after every figure, machine title case in headings, semicolons.
5. **Draft Jon's note** from the recorded reasons, in his first person, three to eight sentences, with a `[JON: ...]` marker on every sentence only he can confirm. Invent nothing about him. The note is about the house and the build: why this choice, what he wants, what he decided and why. It never describes how the book is written or how its Code entries work; that belongs to the front matter and is not written by you. It never mentions the text at all ("every rule here is written for a parcel I have not found yet" is about the chapter). When the whole note is inferred from the record, mark it once at the head, "[JON: drafted from the record, confirm or rewrite]", and use inline markers only for a specific question ("[JON: which counties, and why?]"); a marker after every sentence turns the note into a form. The note gives Jon's reasons and decisions from the record; it does not voice the chapter's recommendations as his promises ("I will check in every two hours") unless the record shows he decided them. A recommendation the record does not show him choosing is asked: "[JON: will you do X?]".
6. **Look things up** when an answer is missing or an inherited value looks wrong. Web research is allowed. File every new finding in `research/new/chNN.md`: date, question, finding, source (URL or document), confidence. A finding you cannot source does not go in the chapter; it goes in Open items.
7. **Lint, then read.** Run `python tools/lint.py manuscript/<your file>` and fix every FAIL. Then do the judgment checks in `STYLE_GUIDE.md` section 13 yourself, including the read-aloud check on every Simple block and the repeated-shape check across all section openings, and fix what they find. The lint is the floor, not the standard.
8. **File cross-chapter flags.** When you find a wrong citation in another chapter, an error in another chapter's answer file, or a change other chapters must follow (a renumbered code section, a superseded edition), add it at the end of your own `research/new/chNN.md` under the heading `## Cross-chapter flags`: date, the chapter and location, what is wrong, the evidence. Do not fix the other chapter. Read `research/new/_cross-chapter-flags.md` for the flags other writers filed, but never write to it: several writers run at once, and the orchestrator merges each writer's flags into it.
9. **Report** in under 300 words: words and sections before and after; items integrated and routed; open items count; lint result; and a list headed "Unsure" naming every judgment call you want checked. Nothing else.

## Do not

- Do not edit any file except your chapter file, `worklogs/chNN.md` and `research/new/chNN.md`. Temporary files (downloads, extracted text, scripts) go only in the scratchpad folder your dispatch message names. Other chapters' files are read-only, and a chapter your dispatch names as being rewritten at the same time may be half-written: name it by topic and do not rely on its current text.
- Do not touch `revision7` or any earlier revision.
- Do not write front matter, back matter, figures or furniture. A `[FIGURE: id, description]` marker is the most you place.
- Do not make backups or copies of any file.
- Do not invent a value, a citation, a source, an anecdote, a preference or a reason.
- Do not ask the orchestrator questions mid-chapter. Decide, record the decision under "Unsure", and continue.
- Do not make it poetic. When a sentence wants to land, turn or ring, end it on its last fact.

## Pacing

None. Work until the chapter is done; do not check the usage meter and do not stop early for it.
