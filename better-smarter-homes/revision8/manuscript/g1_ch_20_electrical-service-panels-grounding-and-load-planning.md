## 20.1 What the pour fixes

**Simple**

Wiring in a concrete house goes where the concrete lets it, and the concrete is decided first. In a wood house an electrician drills a stud and pulls a new cable in an afternoon. Here a cable inside a wall or under a slab runs in a pipe that was cast into the concrete, and a pipe missed on pour day means a core drill through a finished wall. So the backbone is drawn before the footings are formed: where the power enters, where the panels hang, where the grounding steel in the footing comes up, and which empty pipes lead to the roof, the garage, the shop and the car. Wire and equipment can be bought years later, in whatever size the house needs by then.

**Applied**

1. Before the foundation drawings are issued, mark the transformer, the meter location and the trench route on the site plan.
2. Fix one exterior wall as the interconnect wall, for the meter-main, the surge device, the solar and battery disconnects, the generator inlet and the source directory.
3. Put the main panel on the inside face of the interconnect wall, and one subpanel on each floor near its loads.
4. Draw every embedded conduit on the foundation and wall drawings with its diameter, its route and both ends.
5. Stub a 2-inch conduit with a pull string from the panel area to the roof zone, the garage, the driveway pad, the shop and each outbuilding site.
6. Add the footing electrode and its stub to the footing drawings before the pour.
7. Photograph every conduit and the electrode stub before each pour, with a tape measure in frame, and file the photographs with the as-built drawings.

**Professional**

A conductor cast into concrete is fixed for the life of the shell, so branch wiring runs through accessible service layers, furred interior walls, ceiling chases and the suspended floor cavity, and embedded conduit is kept for the few crossings that have to pass through concrete. One exterior interconnect area holds every piece of equipment the utility, a firefighter or a future installer needs to reach: the meter, the service disconnect, the solar and battery disconnects, the generator inlet and the directory of sources. Separate listed enclosures with working space between them serve better than one crowded cabinet, because each later addition then lands in its own box. Floor-level subpanels shorten branch runs in a house where a reroute means cutting concrete, at the cost of a feeder, a separate grounding bus and a fault-current check at each. A passage cut through cured concrete later costs a core drill, a patch and a repair to whatever the drill meets, and a stub placed before the pour costs a stick of PVC and a pull string. Final conductor and equipment sizes come from the load calculation, the utility's requirements and each product's listing.

## 20.2 The electrical permit and the code edition

**Simple**

You can wire the house you own and will live in without an electrician's license, under your own permit, and the inspector holds you to the same code as a contractor. Which code? Oregon adopted a new edition of its electrical code on October 1, 2026, built on the 2026 National Electrical Code with Oregon's own list of changes. Whatever edition is in force on the day the permit application goes in is the one the house is built to, even if the state adopts another edition mid-build. A rented construction power pole can be set without a license, and any repair or change to it needs a permit of its own.

**Applied**

1. Apply for the owner's electrical permit before any conduit, box or cable is installed, in the owner's name, through the local building department or Oregon ePermitting.
2. Record the application date and the edition in force on that date on the first page of the binder.
3. State on the drawings which edition they follow, with that edition's section numbers.
4. For construction power, rent a manufactured construction service of 100 amperes or less from an electrical contractor, and have the utility connect it.
5. Pull a separate permit before altering or repairing the temporary service.
6. Keep the permit, the approved drawings and the inspection record at the panel location through construction.

**Professional**

Under ORS 479.540(1)(a) the owner's exemption exempts the person from licensing and never the work from the code, and its text stands with the other trade exemptions in the legal chapter. Because the permit issues to the owner-applicant, the owner requests each inspection and answers each correction notice in their own name. Oregon adopted the 2026 edition by BCD 16-2026 with no phase-in, so an application filed after October 1, 2026 is reviewed under the 2026 code, and Oregon's Table 1-E rewrites or strikes sections of the national text, several of them in the articles on services, grounding and standby power. Oregon's rule list also names the serving utility's published requirements among the documents that bind an electrical installation (OAR 918-305-0030(5)), which is why the utility's manual is read beside the code. Where a 2026 section number has not been read, a Source line names the 2023 edition's number, and the 2026 renumbering is an open item.

**Code**

```
Electrical work follows the 2026 Oregon Electrical Specialty Code.
  The 2026 NEC as amended by Table 1-E, with the 2023 National Electrical Safety Code alongside it, effective October 1, 2026, with no phase-in.
  Source: OAR 918-305-0100 (BCD 16-2026, effective October 1, 2026); OAR 918-305-0105.

The code in force at application governs the permit.
  The edition in effect at the earlier of a plan-review request or a permit application, or the current edition at the applicant's option.
  Source: OAR 918-305-0020.

The owner's permit is issued to the owner.
  A permit issues to an applicant for an installation on residential property the applicant or an immediate family member owns, as the license exemption allows.
  Source: ORS 479.560(1)(a).

A rented construction service is set without a license.
  A manufactured construction service of 100 amperes, 240 volts single-phase or less, used only during construction, with repairs and alterations under permit, inspection and licensed hands.
  Source: OAR 918-261-0038.

## 20.3 Utility service: the transformer, the lateral and the meter

**Simple**

Power reaches the house through the utility's transformer and its buried line, and the utility's own manual, not the electrical code, governs that stretch. The utility owns the transformer and the wire in the ground. You own the trench, the pipe in it, the concrete pad the transformer sits on and the meter base on your wall, and the manual says how each of them is built. A padmount transformer stands 10 feet from a window and 20 feet from a door, where a truck can reach it. The line comes to the house in one 3-inch plastic pipe laid 3 feet deep, and the utility's crew inspects the trench before anyone fills it.

**Applied**

Hold the utility's service-planning meeting before the foundation drawings are final.

1. Submit the new-service application with the connected load, the site plan and the load schedule.
2. Ask in writing for the available fault current at the service point, and file the answer.
3. Ask for underground service, and record the transformer location, the pad specification and the lateral route the planner marks.
4. Keep the pad 20 feet from any doorway or egress window, 10 feet from other windows, 3 feet from a noncombustible wall and within 10 feet of a drivable surface.
5. Trench to the utility's depth, 36 inches for PGE, and lay one 3-inch Schedule 40 PVC conduit with long-radius sweeps.
6. Keep the run under 150 feet and 270 degrees of bends between pull points, or get the utility's written approval for more.
7. Lay caution tape 12 to 18 inches above the conduit and a tracer wire beside it, and photograph the open trench with a tape measure in frame.
8. Call the utility for its trench inspection before backfill, and the county for the meter-base approval sticker after the socket is set.
9. Buy the meter socket from the utility's approved list, ring type, rated for the service, with a manual bypass on a 320-ampere socket.
10. Mount the socket plumb at the utility's height, with a 3-inch knockout or a swedge to no less than 2 inches, and seal the conduit ends.
11. Separate the power lateral from the communications conduit and from the water, sewer and gas lines by the distance the planner marks.
12. Set the rented construction service beside the permanent meter location.

**Professional**

The utility's manual governs from the transformer to the meter socket, and the electrical code governs from the socket inward. On the utility's side the trench and the conductor follow the National Electrical Safety Code and the utility's standards. Both Oregon investor-owned utilities supply the available fault current on request, and Pacific Power puts the usual interrupting rating of a 200-ampere residential service at 10,000 amperes while warning that a large transformer close to the house can exceed it. That one figure sets the rating of every piece of service equipment on the wall. The table below is read from the two utilities' own documents.

|---|---|---|
| Conduit for a single-family underground service | One 3-inch conduit for 200 or 400 amperes (2-inch at 125 or less), 150 feet and 270 degrees of bends maximum, Schedule 40 PVC or better above grade | Conduit required for most underground conductors, size by the service design |

## 20.4 Service equipment and the outdoor disconnect

**Simple**

One switch outside the house can shut off everything inside, and Oregon leaves it to you whether to have one. The national code added that outdoor switch for firefighters, who want to cut the power to a burning house without going in to find the panel. Oregon struck the rule in its 2023 edition and again in 2026, so an Oregon house may keep its only main switch indoors. This house puts the switch outside anyway, in the meter-main on the interconnect wall, with a red label a crew can read from the driveway and a placard beside it naming every other source: the solar array, the battery and the generator. The meter, the main breaker and the surge device share that one weatherproof box.

**Applied**

1. Choose a meter-main for the service size: a 200-ampere unit, or a 320-ampere socket rated 400 amperes with a manual bypass.
2. Mount it on the interconnect wall where a person stands in front of it without a ladder, with the working space clear.
3. Confirm that its short-circuit current rating is at least the available fault current the utility reported, and keep the utility's letter with the cut sheet.
4. Label the main breaker EMERGENCY DISCONNECT, SERVICE DISCONNECT in white letters at least 1/2 inch high on a red background.
5. Mount a placard beside it listing each source, utility, solar, battery and generator, and where each disconnect is.
6. Run a four-wire feeder from the meter-main to the main panel inside, with the neutral and the ground on separate conductors.
7. For a 400-ampere service, specify a meter-main listed with two 200-ampere mains in separate compartments, or one 400-ampere main.
8. Where the utility puts the meter at the road, treat the house as a separate structure: a disconnect at the house, its own electrode system and a four-wire feeder.

**Professional**

Because Oregon struck the dwelling items of the national service-disconnect rule, an outdoor disconnect at this house is the plan's choice, made for the reason the national rule was written: a fire crew that opens one switch from the yard does not enter a burning building to find a panel. Its red label and the source placard follow the national marking format so that crews trained on that format recognize them at a glance. Oregon's version of the location rule treats a dwelling as it treats any other building, and the meter-main's main breaker satisfies it from either side of the wall.

A meter-main combines the socket, the service disconnect and a few breaker spaces in one listed enclosure, which turns the panel inside into a subpanel on a feeder. The neutral bonds to the enclosure once, at the meter-main, and stays isolated from ground everywhere downstream. Where the utility insists on the meter at the road, the meter-main on its pedestal becomes the service equipment and the house becomes a separate structure under 250.32, with its own disconnect, its own grounding electrode system and a four-wire feeder, the footing electrode serving as the electrode at the house.

Every device at the service carries an interrupting or short-circuit rating the available fault current cannot exceed, and that current depends on the transformer's size and its distance from the wall. A padmount transformer close to the house can deliver more current into a bolted fault than a standard residential meter-main is built to interrupt, which is why the utility's figure is in hand before the equipment is ordered rather than after. Oregon's older allowance of several grouped service disconnects is what keeps a 400-ampere meter-main with two 200-ampere mains lawful, and that allowance has an expiry in the 2026 table, after which the national text applies, under which the additional disconnects sit in separate enclosures or compartments.

**Code**

## 20.5 Service size: the load calculation

**Simple**

The service is sized by adding up the loads and discounting them for the fact that they never all run at once, not by adding up breakers. The code's arithmetic counts the first slice of the connected load in full and a fraction of everything beyond it, then adds the heat pump and each car charger without discount. An all-electric house with two chargers and a shop comes out just over 200 amperes on paper. That leaves two ways to build it: a 400-ampere service with room to spare, or a 200-ampere service with a panel that pauses the car chargers while the oven and the dryer run. Costs are 2026 figures unless a different year is given. Raising a service to 400 amperes after a house is finished costs $8,000 to $12,000 in Fixr's national guide, before the utility's own charges for a larger transformer.

**Applied**

Build the load inventory before the service equipment is ordered.

| Load group | Include | Where the number comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Base house | General lighting and receptacles by floor area, the two small-appliance circuits, the laundry circuit | The code's per-square-foot and per-circuit values |
| Kitchen | Induction range or cooktop and oven, dishwasher, disposal, microwave, refrigerator | Nameplates |
| Water | Well pump, booster or constant-pressure pump, recirculation pump, heat-pump water heater, treatment equipment | Nameplates and the pump curve |
| Heating and cooling | Heat pump, air handler, ventilation unit, any backup heat | The mechanical equipment schedule |
| Shop and outdoors | Shop subpanel, welder, compressor, utility bay, irrigation, gate | Nameplates, diversified for the shop |
| Future | Two car chargers, hot tub or sauna, a second battery, an accessory dwelling | Planned nameplates |

1. Enter every load's nameplate volt-amperes in the inventory, with the floor area for the general lighting line.
2. Run the optional method: the first 10,000 volt-amperes at 100 percent, the rest at 40 percent, then the heat pump at 100 percent.
3. Add each charger at 7,200 volt-amperes or its nameplate, whichever is larger, unless a listed energy-management system limits it.
4. Add 25 percent of the largest motor, the well pump here.
5. Divide the total by 240 for amperes, and record the result with the date, the method and the inventory it came from.
6. Run the standard method as a check, and file both results.
7. Choose 400 amperes where the unmanaged result exceeds 200, or 200 amperes with a listed energy-management system that caps the managed loads below it.
8. Size the lateral conduit, the transformer pad and the meter socket for 400 amperes whichever service is installed.
9. Give the utility the calculated load and the service size at the planning meeting, and keep its transformer sizing in writing.

**Professional**

Under the standard method the general lighting and receptacle load, the small-appliance and laundry circuits and the fixed appliances each take a demand factor, the larger of heating or cooling enters in full, and the largest motor adds a quarter of itself. The optional method for a single dwelling sums every load at nameplate and discounts the sum beyond its first tier, which yields less demand for a house full of large appliances, and it is the method a dwelling with electric heat is sized by in practice. A representative all-electric program, with nameplates assumed where the equipment is not yet chosen, is in the table.

The chargers are the swing item. Unmanaged, they carry the program past 200 amperes and into a 320- or 400-ampere service. Under a listed energy-management system whose setpoint replaces their nameplates, the same house lands under 200, with the heat pump, the range and the shop still counted in full. The 2026 edition moved the calculation to Article 120 and cut the general lighting allowance by a third (EC&M, Dean Austin of NFPA, May 2026), which lowers the base-house line and changes none of the appliance arithmetic.

A 320-ampere meter socket, rated 400 amperes for noncontinuous load, is the middle step between the two services, and it takes the 400-ampere conductor set. The transformer is sized from the load the applicant reports, and a later service change can require a transformer change that the utility bills. No code sets a margin above the calculated load, so the margin is the plan's choice: either the next service step, or a managed 200-ampere service whose lateral, pad and socket already fit 400.

## 20.6 Load management: a 200-ampere service with two car chargers

**Simple**

A smart panel is a breaker panel with a computer in it that can switch off a chosen circuit when the whole house draws too much. With one, two car chargers, a heat pump, a water heater and a range share a 200-ampere service, because the panel slows or pauses the chargers for the minutes the oven and the dryer run together. The code accepts that arrangement when the panel is listed for it, and it counts the managed loads at the panel's limit instead of their full size. The price is a dependence. The panel's software has to keep working for the life of the service, or the house falls back to a plain 200-ampere service with two chargers it cannot run at once. A 400-ampere service with nothing to manage has no software in it.

**Applied**

1. Decide before the panel is ordered whether the house runs a 200-ampere managed service or a 400-ampere unmanaged one.
2. For the managed service, specify a smart panel listed to UL 67, UL 916 and UL 3141, such as SPAN's, as the main panel, or a listed load-management controller on the chargers' feeder.
3. Put both car chargers under management first, then the water heater and a hot tub, and never the well pump, the ventilation or the refrigeration.
4. Enter the system's setpoint in the load calculation in place of the chargers' nameplates, and file the setting with the calculation.
5. Confirm that every breaker in the panel works by hand with the controller dead, and test that at commissioning.
6. Record every change to the setpoint with its date, and keep the panel's listing and manual in the binder.
7. For the unmanaged service, order the 400-ampere meter-main and the 400-ampere conductor set, and skip the controller.

**Professional**

Where a listed energy-management system limits the current to a feeder or a service, the code lets the calculated load be the system's setpoint, and a charger under that system leaves the nameplate column of the calculation. Two architectures do it. A smart panel such as SPAN's replaces the main panel and switches every circuit under its own listings, UL 67 for the panelboard, UL 916 for the energy-management function, UL 869A for service equipment and UL 3141 for a power control system, the standard the 2026 code names for a system that holds currents within a rating at the point of interconnection. A charger-side controller, a current transformer on the service feeding a load-management relay or a charger's own management mode, costs less and manages only the chargers, and whether an inspector accepts it as the listed system the calculation depends on is a plan-review question, in the Who-decides table.

What the smart panel costs is a dependence on firmware over the service life of the house. A controller that stops updating still breaks over its mechanical breakers, so the fallback is a plain panel whose managed loads can no longer share the service. The unmanaged service trades that dependence for a larger transformer, a larger conductor set and a second main, and it adds no software to the thousand-year shell. Each option is priced before the choice: the managed service against the Fixr upgrade range should the controller fail, the unmanaged service against the utility's transformer charge.

## 20.7 Panels, feeders and working space

**Simple**

Every panel needs a clear space in front of it, and that space has to stay clear for the life of the house. The code sets it at the width of a doorway and the depth of a person's reach, and nothing may be stored or built in it, not a shelf, not a water heater, not a duct across the wall above. Panels cannot go in a bathroom, a clothes closet or over a stair. In this house the main panel hangs inside the interconnect wall, and a subpanel sits on each floor near the rooms it feeds, so a repair happens on the floor it concerns and the branch runs stay short. Each panel gets a light, a typed directory and spare spaces for the circuits nobody has thought of yet.

**Applied**

1. Place the main panel on the inside of the interconnect wall and one subpanel per floor, on a hallway, utility-room or garage wall.
2. Keep 36 inches of clear depth in front of each panel, 30 inches of width or the panel's width, and 6 feet 6 inches of headroom.
3. Keep the space from the floor to 6 feet above each panel free of pipes, ducts and every other system.
4. Light the panel wall to 10 foot-candles at the floor from a switch, not from an occupancy sensor alone.
5. Feed each subpanel with a four-wire feeder, and land the neutral on an isolated bar and the grounds on a bar bonded to the enclosure.
6. Size each feeder and its breaker from the load calculation for that panel, and record the calculation.
7. Reserve two spaces in the main panel for the array and the battery, and leave spare spaces in every panel.
8. Type a directory for every panel naming each circuit by its load and location, and mark unused breakers spare.
9. Choose the panel brand and model first, then the interlock kit, the surge device and the controller listed for it.
10. Open a live panel only with a second adult in the house who knows where the main switch is.

**Professional**

Working space exists so a person can open a panel, stand square to it and step back from an arc without falling over something, and the dedicated space above keeps a future plumber from running a drain across the breakers. Oregon's amendment puts a number on the national lighting rule for indoor service equipment and panels, which the national text leaves unquantified. Floor-level subpanels shorten branch runs where a reroute means cutting concrete, at the cost of a feeder, a separate neutral and ground at each panel, and a fault-current check at each: available fault current falls with feeder length and impedance, so a subpanel across the house can use ordinary 10,000-ampere breakers where the service equipment on the interconnect wall may not. A smart panel as the main panel puts the controller in the one enclosure every circuit passes through, while a conventional main panel with a managed subpanel for the chargers keeps the controller out of the service equipment.

OSHA's two-person requirement, 29 CFR 1910.269(l)(1)(i), applies to power generation, transmission and distribution work on lines and equipment energized above 600 volts, and no OSHA or electrical-code rule requires a second person at a residential panel at 120/240 volts. The second person at this house is the plan's choice, because a shock that drops the worker is survivable when someone can open the main and call for help.

## 20.8 The grounding electrode system

**Simple**

The steel in the footing is the house's connection to the earth, and it is made on pour day or never. Grounding ties the whole wiring system to the ground, so that a lightning strike or a surge on the utility line has somewhere to go and the metal in the house stays at the voltage of the soil a person stands on. Rebar in a footing lies in damp soil under a skin of concrete, which makes a better electrode than a rod driven beside the house. The rule has a catch: a slab poured over plastic sheeting does not count, because the plastic cuts the concrete off from the earth, so the footing, which sits on bare soil, carries the electrode. A bar from that footing comes up out of the concrete at the panel wall, and the inspector sees it before the truck arrives.

**Applied**

1. Mark the footing electrode on the foundation drawings: 20 feet of continuous uncoated #4 bar, 3 inches up from the bottom of the footing, in a footing poured against earth.
2. Keep plastic sheeting, foam and waterproofing out from under that length of footing, and note the bare-earth contact on the drawing.
3. Stub an uncoated #4 bar at least 12 inches above the floor plate line at the panel wall, lapped 12 inches to the footing bar and tied with at least three ties.
4. Add a second stub at the far side of the house for a future subpanel, outbuilding feed or battery, and cap both against the pour.
5. Connect the grounding electrode conductor to the stub with a clamp listed for rebar, or weld a bare #4 copper conductor to the footing steel before the pour.
6. Weld the footing laps at the major intersections where the electrode runs, and tie every other lap.
7. Keep the copper-to-steel connection above the slab where it stays dry, and keep aluminum out of contact with the concrete.
8. Measure the resistance from the stub to the farthest exposed footing bar before the pour, and record the reading, with 0.1 ohm as the pass mark.
9. Photograph the electrode run, the stub and the connection before concrete, with a tape measure in frame.
10. Call the electrical inspector for the electrode inspection before the footing pour, and keep the building inspector's footing visit the same day.
11. Bond every other electrode on the site into the same system: a metal water pipe with 10 feet in the earth, a ground ring, any rod driven for the generator or the array.
12. Where no footing touches earth, drive two 8-foot rods at least 6 feet apart, or lay a ring of 20 feet of bare #2 copper 30 inches deep around the house.

**Professional**

Concrete in contact with soil stays damp and conductive, and a footing's long contact area gives a lower resistance to earth than a rod, which depends on whatever soil it meets and dries out in August. The qualification is direct earth contact: the rule's informational note explains that a vapor barrier, insulation or similar film between the concrete and the earth ends that contact, so a slab over polyethylene is not an electrode, and in this house the footings carry it while the insulated, sheeted slab carries nothing. Oregon's residential code writes the detail into the footing itself, bar, position, stub and ties, and the electrical code sets the electrode's minimum length and cover. Where several footings qualify, bonding one of them satisfies the code. Tying the lifts together with welded connections at the major intersections is the project's practice, because it makes the whole shell one low-impedance electrode and a welded joint does not loosen in the way a clamp can.

Galvanic corrosion at the copper-to-steel junction is why the connection sits above the slab, dry, and why aluminum is kept out of concrete contact. A continuity reading between the stub and a distant bar is the project's own record of the electrode, taken because the bar cannot be seen after the pour, and no Oregon rule requires it or sets its pass mark. Below the frost line the concrete-to-earth interface stays moist through the year, and the electrode length sits there. Stray direct current from an unbonded array or battery can corrode rebar electrolytically, so the array's and the battery's equipment grounds bond to this same electrode system, under the rules in the solar chapter.

**Code**

```

## 20.9 Bonding and the one neutral-to-ground point

**Simple**

Bonding ties every piece of metal in the house together, so that a fault has a short, heavy path back to the breaker. When a hot wire touches a pipe, a tool housing or a panel box, the bond carries the current back to the source so fast that the breaker trips in a fraction of a second, instead of leaving the metal live for the next hand that touches it. The wiring's neutral touches ground at exactly one place, the main switch outside, and nowhere else. A second connection downstream lets everyday neutral current wander through the pipes and the ground wires, which heats connections and makes the safety breakers trip for no reason. Water pipes, the well casing and the generator's gas line all get a bond wire back to the panel.

**Applied**

1. Install the main bonding jumper in the meter-main only, and remove the bonding screw or strap from every panel downstream.
2. Bond the metal water piping at its entry with 4 AWG copper for a 200-ampere service, or 1/0 copper for 400 amperes.
3. Bond the generator's gas piping where it enters, with a jumper sized to the circuit that could energize it, and follow the tubing maker's instructions where corrugated stainless tubing is used.
4. Bond a metal well casing to the pump circuit's equipment grounding conductor.
5. Bond exposed structural steel and the hydronic manifolds to the grounding electrode system.
6. Mount an intersystem bonding termination outside at the meter-main, for the communications, antenna and lightning-system bonds.
7. Run a separate equipment grounding conductor in every feeder and branch circuit, and land it on the ground bar of each panel, never the neutral bar.
8. Land the array's and the battery's equipment grounding conductors on the house grounding bus, not on a separate rod alone.
9. Photograph every bond before cover, and list each one on the grounding record.

**Professional**

A fault clears only when enough current flows to trip the breaker, and the impedance of the equipment grounding path sets that current. The neutral's single bond at the service is what joins that path to the source, so that the path exists at all. A second neutral-to-ground connection downstream puts everyday neutral current onto the equipment grounds and the pipes, which corrodes connections, trips arc-fault and ground-fault devices and leaves current on a metal surface. Oregon's 250.24 adds an exception for a utility-installed ground-fault protection system ahead of the service, which a rural single-phase service does not have.

The water-pipe bond is sized to the service conductors because a fault on the service could put the whole supply on the pipe. Other metal piping, gas included, is bonded by the circuit that could energize it, so a 20-ampere igniter circuit sets a 12 AWG jumper under the equipment-grounding table. Corrugated stainless steel tubing is the exception in practice: lightning current through a weak bond has perforated its thin wall, so its makers' instructions, which govern under 110.3(B), call for a 6 AWG jumper direct to the electrode system. Oregon's residential code replaced the model fuel-gas chapter's bonding section with one sentence that sends gas-pipe bonding to the electrical code (ORSC G2411.1, 2023 edition), so that figure lives in the product instructions here and not in the code. An intersystem bonding point outside the service enclosure gives the communications protector and an antenna's discharge unit one terminal, which ties the lightning potential of every system to one reference.

**Code**

```

  An unspliced main bonding jumper connects the equipment grounding conductors and the service-disconnect enclosure to the grounded conductor within each service disconnect, and no grounded-to-ground connection exists on the load side.
  Source: OESC 250.24(C), Table 1-E (2026 edition); NEC 250.24(A)(5) (2023 edition).

  Source: NEC 250.104(B) (2023 edition); ORSC G2411.1 (2023 edition, Oregon amendment).

## 20.10 Surge protection and lightning

**Simple**

A surge protector at the main panel is a small box that swallows the voltage spike from a nearby lightning strike or a utility switching event before it reaches the well-pump controller, the heat pump's circuit board and the inverter. A rural house at the end of a long line sees more of those spikes than a city block does. The national code requires the box at every new house. Oregon left it out of its 2023 edition and makes it mandatory for permits filed from October 2027, and this house installs one from the start. The box costs $160 to $210 at a home center and clips into the panel beside the main breaker. Lightning rods on the roof are a separate system that no code requires, weighed here only for a hilltop or an open, exposed site.

**Applied**

1. Install a Type 2 surge protective device in or beside the meter-main or the main panel, on its own two-pole breaker, with the leads as short and straight as its instructions allow.
2. Choose a listed device rated for the panel it mounts to, such as Eaton's CHSPT2ULTRA, $210 at Home Depot, or Siemens' FS140, $160 to $195 in Amazon sales.
3. Protect the network, coaxial and antenna lines at their entry with protectors bonded to the intersystem bonding point.
4. Add a second-stage protector at the well-pump controller and at the network rack.
5. Check the device's status light at every panel visit, and replace the device after a known strike or when the light shows it spent.
6. On a hilltop or an exposed site, have a lightning protection system designed to NFPA 780 by a certified installer, with its down conductors bonded to the house electrodes at one point.
7. Record the device model, its installation date and each replacement in the binder.

**Professional**

A Type 1 device mounts on the line side of the service disconnect and a Type 2 on the load side, and either satisfies the national rule. Clamping voltage and lead length matter more than the type, because every inch of lead adds inductance that lets the front of a spike through before the device conducts. Low-voltage lines carry surges as readily as power conductors, which is why the network and antenna protectors bond to the same point and why the rack's battery unit is the second stage. The national rule has stood since the 2020 edition, and its 2023 companions reach feeders and outdoor circuits. Oregon's table delays all of it, so the device at this house is bought for the equipment it protects rather than for the inspector, until the date in the entry below.

A lightning protection system under NFPA 780 is a set of air terminals, down conductors and ground terminals, and the electrical code's one rule for it is that its ground terminals bond to the building's grounding electrode system. The footing electrode handles power-frequency fault current well, and a lightning impulse sees the concrete-to-earth interface at a higher impedance than steady current does, which is why NFPA 780 designs add their own ring or rod terminals and bond them to the footing electrode at one point. Exposure decides whether the system is built: a ridge, an open field, or a roof that stands above everything around it.

**Code**

```

  A Type 1 or Type 2 surge protective device at every dwelling service, under 230.67(A), (B), (C) and (E), effective October 1, 2027, with the replacement item (D) not adopted.
  Source: OESC 230.67, Table 1-E (2026 edition); OESC 230.67, Table 1-E (2023 edition, not adopted).

## 20.11 Backup power at the panel: transfer equipment and the critical-loads panel

**Simple**

A generator or a battery connected to the house wiring has to be locked out from the utility line, so that it can never push power backward through the transformer into the street. A crew repairing a dead line expects it dead, and a house feeding it from the other end makes it live at full voltage. The lock is a transfer switch or an interlock: a mechanical part that lets the house connect to the grid or to the generator, never to both. In this house the battery's inverter does the switching on its own, opening the line in a fraction of a second when the grid fails, and the generator plugs into the inverter rather than into the panel. The circuits that must keep running sit in their own small panel that the battery feeds.

**Applied**

Choose the transfer method with the panel brand, before either is bought.

1. Install the battery inverter's microgrid interconnect device between the meter-main and the critical-loads panel, per its listing.
2. Build the critical-loads panel from the outage tier list, and label it as battery-backed with the loads it carries.
3. Wire the generator to the inverter's generator input through a listed outdoor inlet, and its two-wire start contact to the inverter's start output.
4. For a portable generator connected at a panel instead, install an outdoor inlet, a backfeed breaker and the interlock kit listed for that exact panel.
5. For a permanent standby unit with its own transfer switch, use a two-pole switch that leaves the neutral solid, and remove the generator's neutral-to-ground bond.
6. Where a three-pole switch that breaks the neutral is used instead, keep the generator's bond and give the generator its own electrode.
7. Post the standby sign at the meter-main, naming the type and location of each standby source.
8. Ask the local electrical inspector in writing, before buying an interlock kit, whether they accept it for the panel brand.
9. Test the transfer at commissioning by opening the utility breaker, and log the transfer time and the loads that ran.

**Professional**

Transfer equipment makes a simultaneous connection of two sources mechanically impossible, and a listed interlock kit meets the rule when it is listed for the specific panel it mounts on. The Building Codes Division's April 2021 bulletin on generator connections states that the Oregon code requires a transfer switch or other safety device against back feeding, that the work takes a permit, and that downed lines energized by a back-fed house endanger utility workers and the public. Breaker Hunters' 2025 buyer's guide, a national retailer's figures, prices a listed interlock kit at $50 to $200 in hardware, a manual transfer switch at $1,000 to $2,200 installed and a whole-house automatic transfer switch at $1,800 to $5,500 installed.

A microgrid interconnect device built into the battery inverter senses the loss of utility voltage, opens the utility connection and lets the inverter form an island for the house. The generator then feeds the inverter, which charges the bank and carries the loads at once, and the inverter's listing covers the transfer function. Oregon's microgrid amendment puts customer-side microgrid equipment under the current code, and the generator's own siting, fuel and start rules are in the solar chapter with the outage tier table.

Neutral switching decides the grounding. A two-pole transfer switch leaves the neutral solidly connected, so the generator is not a separately derived system and its frame is grounded through the house system. Its factory neutral-to-ground jumper then comes out, or neutral current divides between the neutral and the equipment grounds. A three-pole switch that breaks the neutral makes the generator a separately derived system with its own electrode and its own bond. The critical-loads panel is a capacity decision rather than a code item. A smaller inverter and bank carry the well pump, refrigeration, ventilation, controls and lights, while whole-home backup needs continuous and surge capacity for the heat pump and the car chargers too, and spare main-panel space with a feeder stub to the critical-loads location lets the choice wait.

**Code**

```

  Transfer equipment suitable for the use, listed, and designed and installed to prevent the inadvertent interconnection of all sources of supply in any operation of the equipment.
  Source: NEC 702.5 (2023 edition).

A sign at the service equipment names each standby source.
  The type and location of each on-site optional standby power source, posted at the service-entrance equipment, for every building including a dwelling.
  Source: OESC 702.7(A), Table 1-E (2023 edition).

## 20.12 Connecting the array and the battery at the panel

**Simple**

Solar power comes into the house through a breaker in the panel, and a panel's copper bus bars can only carry so much from both directions at once. The code lets the main breaker and the solar breaker together add up to a fifth more than the bus bar's rating, as long as the solar breaker sits at the far end of the bus from the main. On a standard panel that leaves room for a modest array. A larger one connects ahead of the main breaker, on the utility's side of it, or through a listed controller that keeps the sum under the limit. The panel reserves two breaker spaces, one for the array and one for the battery, and the disconnects for both sit on the interconnect wall where the placard lists them.

**Applied**

1. Reserve two breaker spaces at the end of the main panel's bus farthest from the main breaker, for the array and the battery.
2. Size the array's backfeed breaker so that it and the main breaker together stay within 120 percent of the bus rating: 40 amperes on a 200-ampere bus with a 200-ampere main.
3. Specify a 225-ampere bus with a 200-ampere main where a larger array is planned, which allows a 70-ampere backfeed breaker.
4. For an array beyond that, connect on the supply side of the main with a listed disconnect and conductors sized to the service, or use a listed power control system.
5. Put the AC disconnects for the array and the battery on the interconnect wall within sight of the meter-main, with the source directory beside them.
6. Label the point of interconnection with the inverter output's maximum AC current and voltage.
7. Add a line to the directory for every new source on the day it is energized.

**Professional**

A busbar carries current from the main breaker at one end toward the branch breakers, and a backfed breaker at the opposite end feeds the bus from the other direction, so no point on the bus sees more than one source's full current at once. That geometry is why the sum of the two breakers may exceed the bus rating by the allowance the code gives, and only with the backfed breaker at the far end. The sum rule, which gives no allowance, lets the breaker sit anywhere on the bus. A supply-side connection taps the service conductors ahead of the service disconnect, is sized to the service, and takes the bus out of the question, which makes it the route when the array outgrows the bus. A listed power control system monitors the currents and limits them as they happen, which the 2023 edition admitted in place of the static arithmetic and the 2026 edition ties to systems listed to UL 3141. A smart panel listed as a power control system therefore serves two purposes at once, the service-side load management and the interconnection limit. The array's rapid shutdown, its grounding-electrode conductor and the storage rules are in the solar chapter.

**Code**

```

  Source: NEC 705.12(B)(3)(2) (2023 edition).

  Source: NEC 705.10 (2023 edition).

  Source: NEC 690.54 (2023 edition).
