# Chapter 7: What Concrete Uniquely Enables: Designing With Mass

The structural chapters state the rules for building the concrete and masonry shell. This chapter surveys the design options that shell opens and light framing does not:

- earth cover
- heat and cold stored in the structure
- a planted roof
- storage and furniture cast into the walls
- curved and vaulted form
- shelter and food-storage rooms built from the same mass

Each option is presented with its method, its documented costs, its known failure points, and the point where the engineer of record enters. The Structural System and Concrete Design chapter contains the engineering for the shared structural questions. The Envelope chapter contains the waterproofing and control-layer rules. This chapter contains the design choices.

One climate fact applies to every heat-storage claim in this chapter. Western Oregon winters run under persistent low overcast from November through February. Eugene's December averages roughly 76 percent cloud cover (the Passive and Bioclimatic Design chapter states the regional solar data). A monitored University of Oregon field study of four Eugene sunspaces found that conventional thermal-mass sizing rules, written for sunnier climates, require substantial revision here (Rempel et al., Building and Environment 60, 2013). The measured revision: less than half the mass those guides recommend, with positive moisture isolation from soil. In this book, thermal mass is justified by outage resilience, summer night-flush cooling, and indoor temperature stability, not by winter solar capture. Every section describing solar-charged storage restates this qualification where it applies.

## 7.1 The Design Freedoms Of Mass

**Simple**

A concrete and masonry shell supports design options that light framing cannot:

- earth bermed against the walls and over the roof
- heat and cold stored in the structure's own weight
- a roof garden, a full water tank, or a masonry heater without special framing
- curves, vaults, and cast-in furniture

This section sorts those options into two groups. The first group is mass that works as structure. The second is mass the structure must be sized to support.

**Structural mass and carried mass.** The options in this chapter divide into two categories with different cost logic.

- Structural mass: earth cover on a bermed wall or an earth-covered roof, and any dome or vault shell. Adopted earth cover becomes a code-mandated design load (dead load, plus seismic earth pressure in Oregon's seismic design categories). A shell's curved geometry is itself the load-carrying mechanism; there is no non-structural version of a vault. The cost of this mass is the cost of the structural system.
- Carried mass: green-roof growing medium, water or rock heat stores, phase-change materials, and cast-in furniture. The structure must be sized for each of these, but each exists for a separate purpose (horticulture, heat storage, storage, seating) and each can be omitted without changing the structural concept.

The research behind this chapter located no independently monitored performance data proving the winter heat-storage benefit of the carried-mass options in this climate. The thermal-storage section states that evidence in detail. Each carried-mass option is therefore evaluated individually in its own section, documented cost against documented benefit.

**Cantilevers.** Reinforced concrete permits longer unsupported projections than wood framing at the same depth. Examples: a covered entry roof, a balcony, a floating stair, a projecting bench. The working rule for early sizing is a backspan-to-cantilever ratio of at least 2:1. The anchored portion extends into the structure at least twice the projection length. The main reinforcement sits in the top of the section, because a cantilever's tension zone is at the top. The 2:1 ratio also protects the element during the pour, before the concrete cures and the section can resist its own weight. This ratio is a preliminary-sizing guideline from general engineering practice, not a code table value. The engineer of record sizes and stamps every structural cantilever. Wood-frame prescriptive tables limit joist cantilevers to a fraction of the backspan for typical lumber sizes. A reinforced section reaches longer projections because embedded steel resists the bending at the support. A floating appearance comes from concealing the top bars inside the thin projecting section and anchoring them into wall or slab mass behind the finish. Only the thin projecting section is then visible.

**Point loads.** A masonry heater, a kiln or foundry, a stone counter island, shop machinery, and a rooftop hot tub are concentrated loads. The floor live-load table in the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter lists distributed occupancy loads, not concentrated ones. General slab guidance rates a 4-inch, 3,000 psi residential slab at roughly 350 psf of distributed load over good subgrade. The same guidance flags any single concentrated load over roughly 500 lb for engineering review. Concrete's advantage is the higher starting capacity and the option to thicken or reinforce the section locally under a planned heavy object. The point-load calculation is still required, exactly as in wood framing. The owner lists every planned heavy feature with its location for the engineer of record before the floor and roof designs are final.

**Acoustic separation.** CMHA TEK 13-01D documents a direct relationship between concrete masonry wall weight and sound transmission class (STC, the standard single-number rating of airborne sound blocking). Tested comparisons show a concrete wall and an acoustically upgraded double-stud wood wall reaching the same overall rating (both STC 58 in one tested pair) while performing differently by frequency. The tested pair ran near-identical at 80-250 Hz, with the concrete wall roughly 5-10 dB better above 250 Hz and up to 27 dB better at 50-80 Hz. Low-frequency sound (a subwoofer, shop machinery, mechanical-equipment rumble) is where the mass wall outperforms a rating-matched framed wall. The STC single number underweights low frequencies. Everywhere the structural shell forms a room boundary, that separation is present at no added cost. Interior partitions that are not part of the shell default to light-frame acoustic performance. A media room, primary suite, or office that needs quiet against a shop or mechanical space is placed against structural walls at floor-plan time. Otherwise its light partitions receive a specified acoustic upgrade.

## 7.2 Earth-Sheltering And Berming

**Simple**

Earth-sheltering means putting soil against the walls or over the roof. Berming means piling and compacting that soil in place. There are three standard forms. An elevational design opens one glazed face downhill and sets the other sides into the slope. A penetrational design berms all sides, with openings cut through the cover. An atrium design sinks a courtyard into flat ground, with rooms around it. On a south hillside, the elevational form costs the least. The soil is a permanent structural load. The buried faces can never be reopened, so the waterproofing and drainage are final at backfill.

### The three forms and the site

CMHA TEK 06-07A defines the three forms. The elevational form exposes one whole building face and covers the other sides, and sometimes the roof, with earth. CMHA identifies it as the easiest and most economical earth-sheltered type to construct. The penetrational form berms earth around and on top of a structure built above grade. It needs no slope, and it costs more because the entire envelope requires full waterproofing, drainage, and structural soil-load design. The atrium form sits below grade on a flat site, arranged around a sunken courtyard, typically under 3 feet of cover. On a hillside the atrium form requires cut-and-retain work around the uphill courtyard perimeter that the other forms avoid. The elevational form matches a south-facing slope directly. The glazed face opens downhill to the south, and the remaining faces set into the uphill soil.

No comparative excavation or dollar figures per form were located in the project research. No built Oregon precedent for any of the three forms was identified. The form choice depends on the parcel's slope direction, grade, soil, and solar access, so it is made after a parcel exists. A civil or geotechnical engineer confirms excavation quantities for each candidate form.

Long-inhabited precedents inform the site planning. Interior Pacific Northwest winter pit houses (kekuli) are documented at roughly 8-10 meters diameter and 1-1.5 meters excavation depth, a shallow, wide profile near 1:6 to 1:10 depth-to-diameter. They were sited near water on elevated, well-drained ground rather than in the floodplain, with food storage built in or adjacent. The shallow-wide profile, the elevated well-drained siting, and the integrated food storage all transfer to a concrete shell. The wood-post and bark-roof structure does not. China's Shanxi and Yellow River sunken-courtyard dwellings are pits reported roughly 10 meters deep, with rooms excavated off the courtyard walls, a type still housing an estimated 30-40 million people. Tunisia's Matmata courtyard houses have rooms 4-10 meters high carved off a sunken court. Both demonstrate the atrium form's mechanism. The courtyard is the light and air well for rooms enclosed by earth on every other side, and the surrounding mass holds the interior stable against desert heat and cold. The light-well function transfers to Oregon. The desert heat-buffering problem does not. An Oregon atrium design is planned around moisture management and diffuse winter daylight, per the climate qualification at the head of this chapter.

Mike Oehler's underground-house work contributes three transferable site principles, separate from his wood-post structural method, which does not transfer. Water is handled by gravity grading before any material is relied on. Hillside sites drain by elevation difference, one reason this project favors them. An uphill-facing entry with an attached glazed space is a candidate massing option. The project research confirmed these principles only from publisher and review summaries. Any numeric grading or siting values attributed to Oehler are read from the primary book before use.

### Soil loads and seismic design

Saturated soil is the design condition for every bermed wall and earth-covered roof, because western Oregon soil spends the winter wet. The multiply corroborated planning figure is roughly 10 psf per inch of saturated soil cover. Cover of 24 inches therefore implies roughly 240 psf of soil dead load, before the structure's own weight and snow load are added. Engineered green-roof growing medium is lighter, roughly 7.5-8.0 psf per inch saturated. These are planning figures. The value in the stamped design comes from the project's geotechnical report, with the actual soil's saturated density measured, not assumed. ASTM E2397 is the referenced test method for saturated green-roof-system weight. The Structural System and Concrete Design chapter's roof and wall load tables assume no earth cover. Any bermed or earth-covered element is therefore an engineered addition to that baseline.

Oregon's Seismic Design Category D0/D1 adds a second load. A bermed wall in SDC D is designed for dynamic seismic earth pressure, not static pressure alone. The ordinary basement-wall tables in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, and Concrete Placement chapter therefore do not size it. The geotechnical engineer supplies the site's dynamic earth-pressure values. The engineer of record combines them with the other loads. Residential code manuals state the safety factor against sliding and overturning as 1.1 under the seismic load case, against 1.5 static. The likely result is a thicker or more heavily reinforced wall than the static tables produce. No generic thickness or bar-size delta exists. The exact controlling ORSC section for the trigger is verified against the adopted Oregon text before the permit set cites it.

**Code**

```
A structure in Seismic Design Category D, E, or F must include dynamic seismic lateral earth pressure in the design of its basement and retaining walls.
  The dynamic pressure values are provided by the registered design professional preparing the geotechnical investigation report.
  Source: ASCE 7 Section 11.8.3.

The design dead load of green-roof or earth-cover growing medium must be computed at saturation.
  Saturated density determined by test; code guidance sets a floor of 100 lb per cubic foot for growth media.
  Source: ASTM E2397 (referenced test method in IBC-derived green-roof provisions).
```

### Bedroom egress on a bermed wall

Every sleeping room needs an emergency escape and rescue opening. The requirement is keyed to the opening's sill height relative to grade, not to whether the wall is called a basement wall. A penetrational design therefore cuts a code-sized window well through the berm at every bermed-face bedroom opening. Each well concentrates a waterproofing and drainage detail at a hole in the earth cover. The elevational form avoids the conflict by placing all sleeping rooms on the exposed glazed face. That placement is one reason it is the least-cost form. No Oregon provision or published building-official statement confirms how a bermed above-grade wall is classified for this rule. The applicant confirms the treatment with the building department at a pre-application meeting before the floor plan is fixed.

**Code**

```
Every sleeping room and every basement must have an operable emergency escape and rescue opening.
  Net clear opening 5.7 sq ft minimum, net clear height 24 inches minimum, net clear width 20 inches minimum, opening directly to a public street, alley, yard, or court.
  Source: ORSC R310.1 (2021 IRC basis; recheck against the 2026 ORSC at adoption, expected near October 1, 2026).

An escape opening with its sill below adjacent grade must have a window well.
  Horizontal area 9 sq ft minimum, horizontal projection and width 36 inches minimum, connected to the foundation drainage system or an approved alternative, with a permanent ladder or steps where the well is deeper than 44 inches.
  Source: ORSC R310.2.3.
```

### Waterproofing that can never be reopened

A bermed wall or earth-covered roof buries its membrane for the life of the building. The membrane choice is therefore made for permanent burial, not for the ordinary re-excavatable foundation case in the Envelope chapter. Two systems are the documented choices. Hot rubberized asphalt is applied as a fluid at roughly 350-400°F and cures into one monolithic membrane with no seams or laps. Seams and laps are the most common failure point of sheet systems, and this system has none. Bentonite/HDPE composite panels (a 20-mil HDPE sheet bonded to sodium bentonite clay) work by confinement. The clay swells on water contact and self-seals around fasteners, minor tears, and penetrations for as long as it stays compressed between wall and soil. Self-adhered sheet membranes remain standard for ordinary foundations but are the weaker choice for permanent burial because of seam risk. No independently monitored 30-plus-year field study exists for any of these systems under permanent cover. The documented industry response to that gap is redundancy: a bentonite panel installed over a fluid-applied primary membrane. The project treats that pairing as practice, not as a verified service-life guarantee. Service-life mechanisms for buried membranes and the structure behind them are the subject of the Designing For Centuries durability chapter.

Electronic leak detection (ELD) addresses the buried membrane's inspection problem. An intact membrane blocks electric current. A breach passes current to a conductive layer behind it, and test equipment locates the breach position from outside. Two uses apply here. A pre-backfill survey, run by a technician immediately after membrane installation, confirms zero breaches before the wall is covered. This catches installation defects while the membrane is still exposed for repair. A permanently embedded sensor grid is installed with the membrane and wired to an accessible monitoring point. It lets a future breach be located without excavating the wall. Dye testing and point moisture sensors do not serve a never-reopened wall. Dye application requires access, and a moisture sensor reports wetness near itself, not the breach location. Documented ELD vendors (Detec Systems, Carlisle SynTec, and similar) serve commercial roofing and below-grade work. Residential-scale cost and availability are undocumented. The owner confirms cost and service with a regional ELD contractor before the embedded grid is included in the design.

### Drainage beyond the ordinary basement

A bermed wall drains through a two-tier system, beyond the single perimeter footing drain specified in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, and Concrete Placement chapter. An interceptor drain at the crest of the berm catches surface and near-surface water before it travels down the soil profile against the wall. The perimeter footing drain at the base then collects what the soil still delivers, sized for the added infiltration from the deep cover. Against the membrane itself, a drainage plane (washed gravel or a dimple-board geocomposite mat) moves water down to the footing drain. Without it, hydrostatic pressure builds against the buried membrane. This layer is the detail that most separates bermed-wall drainage from an ordinary basement's. Collected water leaves in solid pipe run to daylight at 1/16 to 1/8 inch per foot of fall. Where the site has no gravity outlet, the drain discharges to a sump. The drainage engineer sizes the sump pump for the bermed condition. Geotextile fabric wraps the drain aggregate so soil fines cannot clog a pipe that will never be dug up. The ordinary perimeter-drain code provision (ORSC R405) is the floor. The interceptor drain and drainage plane are documented good practice added above it.

### The roof-to-wall corner

Where an earth-covered or planted roof meets a bermed wall, two membrane planes meet at a corner under permanent cover. Low-slope trade literature identifies this intersection as the highest-risk leak point on the assembly, with failures that develop slowly and surface only after damage is done. The documented detail has three parts:

- a reinforcing strip of flashing membrane roughly 12 inches wide centered on the intersection, half up the wall and half on the deck
- a fillet of roughly 3/4 inch at the inside corner, so the membrane never bends sharply
- a full-width cover sheet roughly 36 inches wide over the strip, run up the vertical face to a mechanically terminated, sealed top edge

The roof membrane and wall membrane are detailed and installed as one continuous system through the corner. One crew installs the corner in one operation, not two trades meeting at a joint. This is a transfer of documented low-slope parapet practice to the buried case. No berm-specific service-life data exists, so the detail is reviewed with the membrane manufacturer before it is treated as warranted.

Making the roof soil and wall soil one continuous mass concentrates both structural load paths and both membrane systems at that same corner. Continuous mass is the configuration the annualized heat-storage literature depends on. The thermal-storage section states why that claimed benefit has no independent monitored support in this climate. The owner therefore weighs a documented high-risk detail against an unverified thermal benefit. Separating the roof assembly from the wall berm is the lower-risk default.

### Radon, moisture, and indoor air

More buried surface means more soil-gas entry area. A USGS study of nine earth-sheltered buildings in Colorado found radon averages of roughly 1-9 pCi/L, against roughly 1 pCi/L typical of conventional buildings. One poorly ventilated structure read 39 pCi/L. The study attributes the elevation as much to low air exchange as to earth contact. The passive sub-slab radon system in the Envelope chapter protects the slab area it is built under. A bermed design extends the collection layer and vent network to the wall-adjacent soil-gas entry points, a design task for the radon contractor against the county's radon-zone data. The ventilation system is sized so no room repeats the poorly-ventilated worst case.

The documented occupant complaint in wet-climate earth-sheltered houses is condensation, high humidity, and persistent mustiness. Warm interior air reaches cool earth-coupled surfaces, and the moisture has no outward drying path because the wall is buried. The documented fixes, from owner accounts:

- hold indoor relative humidity below roughly 48 percent with a monitor and a dedicated dehumidifier
- build the interior faces from mold-resistant materials (one long-term owner account excludes gypsum drywall entirely)
- dry each construction layer fully before the next layer encloses it, because trapped construction moisture cannot leave through the berm
- finish interiors vapor-open so moisture dries inward to the conditioned air, where the dehumidifier and the HRV remove it (the HVAC, Ventilation, Humidity, and Air Quality chapter states the ventilation design; the Envelope chapter states the vapor-open finish strategy)

Dehumidifier and HRV capacity for a specific bermed design is a mechanical-design calculation ordered with the HVAC design, not a rule-of-thumb selection.

### Glazing the exposed face

The general passive-solar target for south glazing is 7-12 percent of conditioned floor area, with roughly 6 sq ft of sunlit 4-inch-thick floor mass per square foot of glazing. An earth-sheltered structure's larger coupled mass tolerates glazing at the top of that range with less overheating risk than a light-frame house. A fixed overhang cannot serve both seasons. The sun's path repeats symmetrically around the solstices: hot late August shares its sun angles with cool late April. A depth sized for either month mis-serves the other. The documented fixes are seasonal exterior shading (roller shades or awnings deployed roughly late April through September) or deciduous planting on the south side. The Oregon qualification applies here directly. Reported passive-solar heating-load reductions for this region run roughly 20-30 percent, against 40-60 percent reported in sunnier cold climates. The glazing area, mass sizing, and overhang precision are therefore sized to the regional numbers, not to the sunnier-climate design guides. The Passive and Bioclimatic Design chapter states the regional method.

### Cost, financing, and insurance

General industry figures put earth-sheltered construction at roughly 15-30 percent above a standard stick-built house (undated industry figures, retrieved 2026). The premium is attributed to the heavier structural shell, the excavation, and the waterproofing system. Those figures compare against wood framing. This project already pays for a concrete and masonry shell, so its marginal cost for earth-sheltering concentrates in three narrower items. The items: added excavation volume, the permanent-burial waterproofing and two-tier drainage above, and the earth-cover structural loads. No published source prices that narrower gap. The number comes from a contractor or estimator bid comparison once a form and parcel are chosen. The same literature reports 50-80 percent annual heating and cooling energy reductions against conventional construction. Those figures come from the general literature, not from monitored Oregon homes. The winter-cloud qualification at the head of this chapter applies to the heating share.

Earth-contact homes face two documented appraisal obstacles at mortgage or resale time. Conventional underwriting needs roughly three comparable recent sales, which rarely exist for this building type. Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, VA, and FHA are documented as reluctant toward earth-contact homes. Separately, standard appraisal practice excludes any level in contact with earth from gross living area, which understates a mostly-bermed design's appraised value regardless of comparables. Documented alternatives are community and local banks underwriting by relationship, and hard-money lenders at higher rates. A self-financed, owner-occupied build avoids the construction-loan version of the problem but not the resale-appraisal or insurance-valuation versions. Before the design locks in a heavily bermed form, the owner puts the specific design in front of any lender and insurer the project will use, and records their answers. The Personal Project Finance chapter states the financing plan.

## 7.3 Passive Annual Heat Storage And Thermal-Mass Storage

**Simple**

Thermal mass is heavy material that absorbs heat and releases it slowly. A concrete house contains tons of it. In this climate, that mass delivers three dependable benefits. Indoor temperatures swing less through the day. Cool night air in summer can chill the structure for the next day. In a power outage, the house drifts toward outdoor temperature over days, not hours. A fourth claimed benefit, storing summer sun for winter heating, has no measured support here. Oregon's cloudy winters weaken the solar charging that idea depends on.

**What the mass reliably delivers.** The structure's own concrete is kept on the conditioned side of the insulation per the Envelope chapter's exterior-insulation strategy. That concrete buffers daily temperature swings and stores night-flush cooling in summer (outdoor air run through the house at night during the cooling season). It slows indoor temperature drift during a heating or cooling outage. These benefits require no added storage system. The mass is already purchased for structural reasons. Every system after this paragraph is an addition, and each is weighed against the evidence for its added benefit.

**Passive annual heat storage (PAHS) and annualized geo-solar (AGS).** John Hait's PAHS design surrounds the structure with an insulated, waterproof horizontal umbrella buried at shallow depth, reportedly extending roughly 20 feet outward in all directions. The umbrella keeps the soil beneath the house dry and thermally coupled to it. Proponent literature claims a roughly six-month time lag, summer heat surfacing in winter. It cites a 30-foot-diameter example holding between roughly 70°F and 76°F year-round with no mechanical heating or cooling, using roughly 45,000 cubic feet (about 1,800 tons) of engaged soil. AGS designs use the same stored-ground-heat premise with a separate solar collector charging the ground.

The evidence for these claims is proponent-authored. The best-documented example located in the project research is the Mica Peak residence near Spokane, Washington (1,600 sq ft, owner-built into a hillside). Its record is three winters of holding-temperature readings self-reported by its own design consultant, not independently monitored energy-use data. No university-run, utility-run, or otherwise independent multi-year dataset for any PAHS or AGS house in any climate was located. The building-science criticism on record (Robert Riversong, GreenBuildingAdvisor) states that the approach spends heavily on measures with no energy yield. It also states that soil conductivity, moisture content, and drainage make the outcome unpredictable at design time. That criticism is forum commentary, not a study. The state of the evidence is proponent claims on one side and unrefereed critique on the other, with no monitored dataset on either side.

For this region, the Rempel sunspace study stated at the head of this chapter is the one independently monitored, Oregon-specific dataset that bears on the approach. Each of its two findings runs counter to a PAHS premise. Measured Pacific Northwest performance calls for less than half the thermal mass that sunny-climate design guides recommend. Floor mass must be positively isolated from moist soil, with ordinary perimeter insulation found insufficient for that isolation. PAHS maximizes soil-coupled mass and depends on solar charging. Western Oregon's winter sky weakens the charging, and its wet soil works against the required dryness. The study does not prove a Willamette Valley PAHS cannot function, but it is monitored regional evidence against both premises. One evaluation gap remains. No engineering assessment exists of building and draining a 20-foot buried horizontal umbrella under Oregon's winter rainfall. That umbrella drainage would add to the drainage system the earth-sheltering section already requires.

This project treats PAHS and AGS as unproven for this region. An owner who pursues one anyway first commissions a modeled performance estimate from an energy modeler using the validated regional method. The owner also prices the umbrella's excavation, membrane, and drainage as an addition to the earth-sheltering drainage design.

**Sizing rules and the regional correction.** The generic passive-solar rules of thumb:

- south glazing at 7-12 percent of conditioned floor area
- roughly 5.5 sq ft of sunlit 4-inch masonry floor per square foot of south glazing
- roughly 40 sq ft per square foot for floor mass out of direct sun
- roughly 8 sq ft of 4-inch wall mass per square foot of glazing
- by weight, roughly 150 lb of masonry or 4 gallons of water per square foot of south glass

All of these trace to sunnier climates. The Rempel correction for this region: less than half the generic mass quantity, with mandatory moisture isolation. The study's full replacement sizing tables sit behind a journal paywall. The project's energy modeler therefore sizes mass from the field-validated regional models rather than from the generic ratios.

**Trombe wall.** The documented parameters:

- an 8-16-inch concrete wall (brick 8-12 inches) with a dark sun-facing surface, behind glazing with a 3/4 to 2-inch air gap
- roughly 1 inch of wall thickness per hour of heat-delivery time lag, with a 12-inch wall commonly cited as delivering from roughly 8 AM to the following morning
- optional upper and lower vents, which older designs show overheating the space in summer and losing heat by reverse convection at night unless dampered, so many current designs omit them
- a movable night-insulation panel over the glazing

A Trombe wall places its mass outside its own insulation, the reverse of this project's whole-building exterior-insulation strategy. A Trombe section is therefore a deliberately different, isolated assembly with a structural and air/vapor-control break at both jambs. No tested transition detail for that jamb exists. The envelope designer details it if the element is adopted. The winter-cloud qualification applies in full. The wall charges only when winter sun reaches it. Regional passive-solar heating reductions run roughly 20-30 percent against 40-60 percent in sunnier cold climates. Adopting one is an owner design choice.

**Water wall.** Steve Baer's drum wall (Corrales, New Mexico, built 1971-1972) stacks 55-gallon drums holding roughly 450 lb of water each in racks behind south-facing double glazing. Sun-facing drum ends are painted black, and hand-cranked insulating shutters are closed at night. Reported winter collection efficiency is roughly 60-70 percent, with roughly 50 percent added capture from the shutters' reflective interior faces laid flat outside the glazing by day. The documented maintenance problems over decades: steel-drum corrosion (no published lifespan figure), algae growth in the stored water (managed with opaque containers and periodic treatment), and the rack loads. A supporting structure is engineered for those loads. No documented version integrates the drums with a concrete structural wall. That combination needs original engineering. Modern fiberglass water-tube products sold as equivalents were not verified in the project research. Specifications come from the manufacturer before one is priced. The built example sits in high-desert New Mexico sun, and the winter-cloud qualification applies to any Oregon version.

**Rock bed.** Documented rock-bed storage uses washed rock of roughly 3/4 to 2-1/4 inches. Charging air is fed in high and drawn out low, so the airflow crosses the full bed depth instead of short-circuiting. One forum-sourced reference point: 1,500 CFM at a 60°F temperature difference yielding roughly 97,000 BTU/hr for a well-insulated 800 sq ft structure. That is an order-of-magnitude figure, not a validated design rule. The 1970s-80s installations were sealed permanently under slabs, and no documented design provides access for cleaning or rock replacement. A serviceable version would be a discrete lined vault with a removable access hatch. That is original design work carried out with the HVAC designer, not an off-the-shelf method.

**The cistern as thermal mass.** No documented precedent combines a potable rainwater cistern with a structural thermal-mass function. An owner weighs three tradeoffs before inventing one. Stored potable water that cycles warm is a documented general risk condition for bacterial growth. A thermally cycled cistern is therefore managed with filtration and disinfection, or kept non-potable. A full cistern is a real engineered load (water weighs 62.4 lb per cubic foot) on the wall and foundation design. Embedding a tank for thermal contact trades away the cleaning and inspection access that potable storage requires. A genuine cleanout and inspection point is therefore designed in from the start. The Structural System and Concrete Design chapter (water-retaining structures) states the structural standards that govern any cistern. The water-system designer and the engineer of record both review the proposal before it is adopted.

**Phase-change materials (PCM).** A 2022 peer-reviewed review places PCM mixed into structural concrete in the research-and-development stage, with no published technical guidelines for practical use. The documented open problems:

- leakage of liquid-phase PCM and its interference with cement hydration
- measured strength loss (16.39 percent at 28 days with 5 percent micro-encapsulated PCM)
- unestablished durability under lifelong melt-freeze cycling

This project adds no PCM to any structural mix. PCM-enhanced gypsum wallboard is the more mature, non-structural route. One study measured indoor-air and wall-surface temperature-swing reductions of roughly 1.4°C and 2.7°C. The wallboard changes a finish layer, not the structural mix, and can be added or removed after construction.

**The thermal chimney.** A stair tower, chimney, or service shaft already built tall and massive can also ventilate. Stack-effect airflow strengthens with shaft height. Thermal mass in the shaft walls stores daytime heat that keeps the airflow running after sunset, which matches the night-cooling schedule. One documented residential installation used a chimney cavity of 750 by 1,500 by 200 mm with a 50 mm concrete mass layer. No general minimum-height rule exists. Performance depends on height, cross-section, inlet and outlet sizing, and solar charging together. Because the element is already planned for structural reasons, venting it is a low-cost addition. The design is developed with the ventilation designer once the shaft's real geometry and sun exposure are known.

## 7.4 The Green Roof And Roof Garden On A Concrete Deck

**Simple**

A green roof is a waterproofed roof buried under drainage layers, engineered soil, and plants. This project's concrete roof deck can support one; most wood-framed roofs cannot. There are two types. An extensive roof is thin and light, planted with drought-hardy groundcover, and visited only for maintenance. An intensive roof is deep and heavy and can grow a walkable garden. The weights differ by up to ten times. The choice is locked in before the deck is designed.

**Structural loads.** Structural-engineering and insurer sources place extensive systems at roughly 15-40 psf fully saturated dead load. Intensive systems run roughly 40-150-plus psf depending on depth and water retention. FM Global's green-roof data sheet adds a minimum roof live load of 12 psf for extensive and 20 psf for intensive systems on top of the saturated dead load. It also directs that the specified saturated medium depth be increased at least 15 percent in the load calculation to cover future top-up. Both types sit on the same protected-membrane concrete deck this project already specifies. The deciding question is which load the deck is sized for. It is answered before the deck thickness and reinforcement are finalized. Growing medium is dead load, present year-round, so it adds to the site's design snow load in every governing load combination. A Cascade-foothills or high-desert site designs for the full sum. No code-recognized seasonal strategy (lighter winter medium, pre-snow drawdown) exists, and none is assumed here.

**Code**

```
Roof structural design must combine the green roof's saturated dead load with the site design snow load.
  Governing combinations include dead load and snow load together (strength design 1.2D + 1.0S; allowable stress design D + 0.7S); flat-roof snow load pf = 0.7 Ce Ct Is pg from the ground snow load.
  Source: ASCE 7, snow-load and load-combination provisions (exact section numbers to be verified against the full ASCE 7 text before the permit set cites them).

In an adopted wildfire hazard zone, the roof covering must meet the wildfire roofing standard.
  Permitted coverings: asphalt shingles, slate shingles, metal roofing, tile, clay or concrete shingles, or other approved covering equivalent to a minimum Class B roof assembly; wood shakes and shingles prohibited; noncombustible gutters with debris protection; flame- and ember-resistant vents.
  Source: ORSC R327.4.
```

The wildfire rule is written around steep-slope covering materials and names neither a gravel-ballasted membrane roof nor a vegetated assembly, in either direction. Gravel ballast is a noncombustible mineral surface and plausibly resists embers as well as a Class B shingle. No equivalency ruling, listing, or test report was located. A vegetated roof has two states with opposite fire behavior. Moist living cover resists ignition, and a browned-out unirrigated roof in an Oregon August is potential fuel. No ember-exposure test data was found for either state. In a mapped wildfire zone, the applicant resolves the question with the building official before committing to the assembly. The route is the alternative-method request process stated in the Oregon Code and Compliance Map chapter.

**Layer order and the root barrier.** The layer order, from the structural deck up:

- waterproofing membrane
- root protection
- drainage layer
- filter fabric
- growing medium
- plants

Growing medium does not replace gravel ballast one-for-one. The planted assembly adds a root-protection layer that a plain gravel-ballast roof does not include. Root protection is satisfied one of two ways. The first is a membrane independently rated root-resistant under FLL 7.1.2.5 or EN 13948, which requires no separate barrier. The second is a separate root-barrier sheet above a non-rated membrane, commonly 0.5 mm HDPE minimum (0.8 mm over bituminous membranes under more than 6 inches of medium, per FM Global). Seams are overlapped 6 inches and welded 1.5 inches minimum. Whether the membrane product specified in the Envelope chapter is certified root-resistant to FLL or an equivalent standard is a single fact. It is confirmed from the manufacturer's data sheet before the assembly is priced.

**Drainage under Oregon rain.** A discrete drainage layer between medium and membrane, roughly 0.25 to 1.5 inches for extensive systems, conveys the design storm without backing water into the medium. The common product is a geocomposite with a dimpled HDPE core and bonded filter fabric. Every roof drain and scupper sits inside an inspection chamber with a removable locking lid, reachable and clearable without digging up the medium. This is the detail that most separates a green roof's drains from a bare roof's open strainers. An emergency overflow (a scupper or secondary drain set above the primary system's capacity) is a firm requirement. Storm events exceed the medium's storage and transmission capacity more readily than a bare membrane's. The outlet count and overflow sizing against this project's roof area and the site's design-storm data are the stormwater or plumbing engineer's calculation. The bare-roof drain counts in the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter do not size a planted roof's outlets.

**Growing medium and plants.** The medium is engineered lightweight substrate (expanded shale, expanded clay, or pumice), not topsoil. Topsoil is heavier and drains more slowly, which risks winter waterlogging here even though the same trait would seem to help in August. The documented unirrigated palette is succulent groundcover: Sedum, Sempervivum, and Delosperma, all evergreen and shallow- and fibrous-rooted. Sedum sediforme is documented surviving unirrigated in pumice substrate as shallow as 7.5 cm (roughly 3 inches). Species proven on unirrigated European extensive roofs are documented performing well in the Pacific Northwest. No tested Oregon-native alternative palette was located. An owner who prefers natives consults an Oregon native-plant nursery or a regional green-roof horticulturist, and treats the substitution as untested.

**The food garden option.** Rooftop food growing is a documented deep-intensive category. The standard minimum is 12 inches of medium for most root crops, and 14-24 inches for planter-based systems. At roughly 7-8 psf per inch saturated, a 12-inch depth adds roughly 84-96 psf of dead load. A 24-inch depth adds roughly 168-192 psf, before beds, ponded water, or people. The documented alternative to filling the whole roof: open-bottomed raised beds over a lighter assembly, with shallow extensive cover elsewhere. The beds concentrate the deep-soil load into defined zones the engineer reinforces specifically. The choice is made at initial design. Retrofit literature documents that adding capacity later can require a steel frame above the existing deck. No reliable universal price per square foot exists for that retrofit. No published figure isolates the from-the-start marginal cost of the heavier deck either. The number comes from the structural engineer's comparative design pass.

**Leak detection.** The failure modes specific to a planted roof:

- membrane breaches hidden under medium and root mat
- root penetration at flashings and penetrations where the barrier is weak
- drains clogged by soil migration or roots

The countermeasures:

- an electronic leak-detection survey immediately after membrane installation and before any medium is placed, which confirms zero breaches while repair is still surface work
- an embedded sensor grid if ongoing monitoring is wanted
- a deliberate root-barrier detail at every flashing and penetration, the most-cited intrusion points
- the drain inspection chambers above

Residential-scale ELD cost is undocumented, and the owner confirms it with a regional contractor before the design relies on the embedded grid.

**Maintenance over decades.** Commercial-contract figures run roughly $0.75-2.00 per square foot per year for extensive systems, at two to four professional visits per year (undated industry figures, retrieved 2026). Intensive systems run $1.50-4.00, up to $5.00-plus with amenity planting. Those prices describe contracted commercial maintenance. An owner doing the work personally converts the cash cost into recurring labor: weeding, plant replacement, drain and scupper clearing, and medium top-up, indefinitely. The bare gravel-ballast default this project specifies has no comparable recurring line item beyond drain checks during ordinary roof inspections. That standing obligation, not the per-square-foot price, is the decision input.

**Energy accounting.** Growing medium insulates poorly per inch compared with the purpose-made insulation already specified in the Envelope chapter. On this project's fully insulated deck the added thermal benefit is marginal. No published figure quantifies energy savings against an already-well-insulated baseline. The documented reasons to build one are different. The medium and drainage layers shield the membrane from UV and thermal cycling, the two dominant membrane-degradation drivers. The assembly retains stormwater and cuts peak runoff. The fire-hardening value is possible but unresolved above. On this project, membrane protection and stormwater management are the grounds for choosing a green roof. Energy savings are not.

**The pilot roof.** The identical assembly is built first on a planned outbuilding (shop, garage, or well house) and stands through at least one full wet season. This surfaces the membrane, root-barrier, and drainage failure modes at a fraction of the house roof's area and consequence. No documented case study of this staging was located. It is a risk-sequencing choice consistent with the failure-mode evidence. It depends on the outbuilding schedule in the Build Sequence and Site Coordination chapter.

## 7.5 Cast-In Features (benches, planters, seating, shelving, niches)

**Simple**

Concrete furniture can be part of the building. Examples: a bench cast against a wall, a planter at the entry, shelves on embedded steel, a display niche in the wall. A cast-in feature cannot loosen. Its finish is the concrete itself, and it lasts as long as the wall. The cost is that it is permanent. A cast-in bench is placed once, in the drawings, before the pour.

**Integral pour or secondary pour.** A feature formed and poured with the structural wall or slab has its reinforcement tied into the structural steel. It has no cold joint to crack or leak, and it needs no second round of forms, concrete, and finishing. It is the more durable and, where the geometry and pour schedule align, the lower-labor route. Its risk is concentrated: a flawed integral feature is a flaw in the structural element itself, discovered only at form stripping. A secondary pour, cast later against a keyway with dowels cast or epoxied into the first pour, costs a second mobilization but isolates the risk. A failed bench form is re-formed and re-poured without touching the structural work. No published cost or error-rate comparison exists for the two routes. For a first attempt with no forming experience, the secondary pour is the safer sequence. The integral pour becomes the economical choice once the builder's forming skill is proven on low-stakes elements.

**The floating bench, shelf, and stair tread.** The structural arrangement behind a leg-free cantilevered element is stated in the design-freedoms section. Its parts: at least a 2:1 anchored backspan, top-placed tension reinforcement concealed in the thin projecting section, and anchorage into wall or slab mass hidden behind the finish. Any cantilevered element a person stands or sits on is structural and gets the engineer of record's design regardless of span. A light object shelf is less likely to trigger review, but no numeric span threshold separates the two cases anywhere in code. The classification is therefore confirmed with the engineer and the building official before forming.

**Cast-in shelving and storage.** An embedded steel angle, a formed ledge, or a cast hook anchored into a reinforced wall can, with designed embedment and reinforcement, exceed what stud-mounted shelf brackets hold. Residential bracket pairs are commonly rated from a few dozen to roughly two hundred pounds depending on anchors and studs. No load-tested published values exist for cast-in equivalents. The working load of each specific detail (embedment depth, angle size, backing reinforcement) is therefore the engineer of record's calculation, detail by detail. For a pantry, book wall, or tool wall, the cast-in route eliminates the secondary framed shelving system. The price is that spacing and height are fixed at the pour.

**Niches and alcoves.** A shallow niche that leaves the wall's structural section intact behind it is a local thickness reduction. It needs no jamb or lintel treatment in most cases. It does not interrupt the envelope, because the air and vapor control layers sit elsewhere in the assembly (the Envelope chapter states their location). A niche that penetrates the full structural thickness is an opening, with the jamb and lintel reinforcement and the air/vapor continuity detailing of a window. It is avoided unless a pass-through is the intent. The depth at which a specific niche starts needing added reinforcement is the engineer of record's call per niche. The call is made before the wall pour, never after. The Structural System and Concrete Design chapter (reinforced masonry walls) describes the masonry counterpart: ungrouted block cells opened as storage and display niches.

**The exterior planter.** A cast-in planter is a permanently wet box attached to a dry structure, and the detailing keeps those two conditions separate. The planter interior gets three dedicated details:

- its own waterproof lining on base and sides, never shared with the adjoining wall's waterproofing
- a drainage layer (gravel or geocomposite mat) under the growing medium
- weep holes or a piped drain at the low point, so water leaves instead of standing against the lining

At the wall interface, the planter's lining terminates independently against the wall's own membrane, bridged with a compatible sealant or flashing. A planter lining failure, the higher-probability event given the constant wet service, then stays in the planter. Product compatibility at that junction is confirmed with the waterproofing subcontractor once materials are selected.

**The fire-separation wall for the block workshop.** This project's block-production and shop areas can be separated from living space by the same masonry the project already builds. The Owner-Made Blocks and Alternative Concretes chapter states the production plan. Unlined concrete masonry has documented fire-resistance ratings with no gypsum protection (NCMA TEK 7-1B). A nominal 4-inch solid unit wall rates at roughly 1 hour, and an 8-inch hollow unit wall at roughly 2 hours. A 12-inch wall rates at roughly 3 hours hollow, and roughly 4 hours with cores completely filled with grout, perlite, or vermiculite. The rating is computed from equivalent thickness, the unit's net solid cross-section. Partial grouting therefore adds nothing over the hollow rating. Only complete filling counts. In wood framing, a rated separation is a purchased assembly of Type X gypsum layers. Here it is close to a byproduct of the wall already being built. The occupancy classification of a block-production zone, and the separation rating it requires, is confirmed with the building official. It is not assumed identical to an ordinary attached garage.

## 7.6 Cast-In Service Chases And Embeds (conduit, plumbing, blocking, anchors)

**Simple**

In a wood-framed house, new wiring is fished through hollow stud walls. A concrete house has no hollow walls. Future capacity is cast in on purpose, or added after the pour by coring through concrete. The answer is an oversized service chase (a built-in corridor for pipes and wires, larger than today's systems require). Access panels open into it on every level. It is planned once, before the pour. Future wiring and plumbing run through it instead of through new holes.

The general future-proofing strategy is documented: dedicated chases, oversized conduit runs, serviceable junction and pull boxes, and a centralized utility cabinet, all installed at construction. Spare raceways placed during the build cost a fraction of retrofitting into finished surfaces after the fact. The documented sizing:

- 1-inch conduit as the practical minimum for branch-circuit-scale runs
- 1.25-2 inches or larger for feeders, data trunks, and anticipated EV-charging or inverter runs
- intermediate pull boxes wherever a run would otherwise accumulate multiple tight 90-degree bends
- a pull string left in every empty run

In a concrete shell with a multi-century design life, the chase is built as a genuine cast-in corridor, not a pipe. The chase is a continuous vertical and horizontal void at a fixed location on every level, sized so a person can reach in through access panels. It restores the retrofit capacity a solid concrete shell otherwise lacks. This reconciles directly with the penetration rule in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, and Concrete Placement chapter: every penetration is planned before the pour. The chase is the one generously sized penetration planned in advance. Future runs pass through it, so the rule is satisfied once, at design time, rather than at every future upgrade. No published sizing standard exists for a multi-century service life. The chase dimensions are the owner's design decision, made large. The electrical and plumbing designers confirm that the day-one routing fits with margin. Individual cast-in embeds, sleeves, blocking, and anchors, and their pre-pour verification, are stated in the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter (formwork, placement defects, and cast-in embeds).

## 7.7 Curved, Sculptural, Domed, And Vaulted Forms

**Simple**

Concrete takes the shape of its form, and block walls can curve. This section surveys six routes to curved and sculptural work:

- curved block walls
- ferrocement (thin mortar troweled over steel mesh)
- fabric-formed concrete
- hypar shells built from straight boards
- corbelled vaults
- sprayed monolithic domes

They range from owner-buildable to specialist-only. Each entry states the method, its limits, and who signs off.

**The curved block wall.** A curved wall of standard rectangular block is a series of short straight chords, not a true arc. The minimum practical radius follows from unit length, unit thickness, and head-joint widths. Switching from 16-inch to 8-inch units roughly halves the achievable radius. Widening the exterior head joints (tapering them against the interior joints) tightens it further, all with ordinary uncut units. Layout is the stick-and-string method: a stake, a string cut to the radius, and the curve swept directly on the slab. This is the lowest-cost, most owner-accessible curved route, realistic for gentle, large-radius curves in either of this project's wall systems. The Wall System and Masonry Craft chapters state the assemblies and the laying technique. CMHA TEK 05-10A is the governing reference. Its numeric minimum-radius table was not extracted in the project research. Specific radius figures are therefore pulled from that document before any drawing commits to one. Pre-molded radius ICF blocks are sold in stock radii reported near 5-10 feet. Price and lead time for a small order are unverified and come from a manufacturer quote.

**Ferrocement.** Ferrocement is a steel-rod skeleton wrapped in several layers of wire mesh (0.5-1.0 mm wire at roughly 5-10 mm spacing). The skeleton is hand-troweled or sprayed with rich mortar (sand-to-cement roughly 2:1 to 3:1, water-to-cement roughly 0.40-0.45, no coarse aggregate), in coats from roughly 3 mm to a finished 10-30 mm total. The closely spaced mesh, not thickness, provides the tensile capacity, which is what permits thin, doubly curved shapes. Curing is demanding: 21 days continuously wet, or steam curing reaching 28-day-equivalent strength in roughly 12 hours where practical. Its domain on this project is non-structural and lightly loaded work: garden walls, tanks, planters, a small non-habitable roof. No code-recognized structural design values exist. Any loadbearing residential use would therefore go through the alternative-method process stated in the Oregon Code and Compliance Map chapter, with no assurance of acceptance. The engineer of record reviews any element that carries more than its own weight and light use.

**Fabric formwork.** Fabric formwork is concrete poured against tensioned or draped fabric instead of rigid plywood, documented by the CAST program (University of Manitoba) for columns, beams, panels, and walls. Fabric-formed beams are reported at roughly 40 percent less concrete than equivalent rectangular sections, because the fabric deflects into a load-following shape. Three categories: pneumatic (air-inflated molds), draped fabric hanging from a frame, and prestressed cable-net systems. Draped fabric against a simple wood frame is the owner-buildable entry point, needing only frame, fabric, and gravity. No vendor sells an owner-scale kit. The method remains research- and specialist-documented. The realistic first uses here are a bulging accent column, a wall panel, or a doubly curved bench. In those uses the skill being practiced, predicting how fabric moves under wet concrete, has no structural consequence.

**The hypar shell.** A hyperbolic paraboloid is doubly curved yet generated entirely by straight lines. Its formwork is therefore built from straight boards laid along the generator lines, ordinary carpentry producing a curved shell surface. That is why Félix Candela's large shells were buildable with local labor. The geometry scales down to a carport, pavilion, or porch canopy. The engineering does not scale down by rule of thumb, because a thin shell's strength comes from its curvature rather than from flexural reinforcement. No published small-scale span and thickness limits were located. A small hypar roof is designed by a structural engineer experienced with shells. Whether one will stamp a small residential shell economically is unverified. The answer comes from direct inquiry before the idea is drawn.

**The corbelled catenary vault.** A corbelled vault advances each masonry course slightly past the one below, keeping every course's center of mass supported at all times. That support is why it stands without any centering form during construction. A true radial arch, by contrast, is incomplete until its keystone and needs full temporary support. Building the corbel to a catenary profile (the inverted curve of a hanging chain) puts the whole vault in compression with no bending. The course offsets are calculated against the catenary's mathematical profile for the chosen span and rise, not laid by eye. No owner-builder course-by-course manual for ordinary concrete block was located. The layout is developed with a mason or engineer experienced in vault work.

**The Auroville free-spanning vault.** The Auroville Earth Institute builds formwork-free catenary vaults from compressed stabilized earth block, with glue joints as thin as 1-2 mm. Those joints are achievable because its Auram press holds block dimensions to roughly 0.5 mm. Documented built spans run 6 to 15 meters (roughly 20 to 49 feet) with crown thickness down to 7 cm. That range covers real residential rooms, not just utility spans. Two gaps block direct transfer to this project. The joint system depends on press precision that an owner-built or lower-cost press is not verified to reach. The technique's seismic behavior in Oregon's Seismic Design Category D0/D1 is unaddressed in the documentation, with the built examples concentrated in lower-seismic India. On this project the technique is a candidate for a small non-habitable structure at most. Adoption follows direct consultation with the institute or a comparable practitioner, and a structural engineer's seismic review. The owner-made block itself is the subject of the Owner-Made Blocks and Alternative Concretes chapter.

**The monolithic dome.** An inflated airform, sprayed interior foam, rebar, and layered shotcrete produce a thin-shell dome. Documented whole-home costs run roughly $100-250 per square foot (undated industry figures, retrieved 2026), with the shell and foundation at roughly 40-50 percent of total. One 2013 reference puts shell and floor alone near $65 per square foot. Overhead shotcrete placement is documented specialist work, with consolidation and rebound failures when applied by inexperienced crews. No source documents owner-builder self-performance. Hiring the specialty crew removes the owner-labor cost advantage this project's budget assumes for ordinary concrete and masonry work. Because mobilization dominates, a small accessory dome (root cellar, well house, shop) plausibly costs more per square foot than the whole-home figures, though no source confirms the scaling. The number comes from a contractor quote through the Monolithic Dome Institute's professional directory. That directory is also the route to any Oregon-licensed resources. No Oregon code provision treats a dome differently from any other engineered form, for a dwelling or an accessory building. The building official confirms the review path for a specific proposal. The Structural System and Concrete Design chapter (safe rooms and hardened-structure options) states the dome's financing, appraisal, and layout drawbacks and the single-domed-room alternative.

## 7.8 Surface Character From The Form (board-form, exposed aggregate, integral color)

**Simple**

A concrete wall's finished face is decided before the pour. Boards used as form faces print their grain into the wall. A chemical retarder lets the surface be washed off to expose the stones inside. Pigment mixed into the batch colors the wall all the way through, permanently. All three are one-shot decisions: the finish comes out of the form and there is no second try without demolition.

**Board-formed concrete.** Lumber form faces telegraph wood grain and board joints into the finished wall. Documented pricing runs roughly $30-50 per square foot, or $60-280 per linear foot, against roughly $0.25-2.00 per square foot for ordinary formwork (undated industry figures, retrieved 2026). Contractors are documented bidding board-form work at double a plain pour, because a failed result is torn down and re-poured. Success is attributed to precise carpentry: tight board fit, bracing against bowing, and joint treatment that controls leakage and grain depth. No published numeric tolerances, release-agent specification, or pour-rate limits specific to board-form work were located. An owner attempting it works from ACI 347 formwork guidance or an experienced contractor's direction, and practices on a low-stakes element first. Using lumber already on hand cuts the cash cost, not the redo probability. A first attempt fails more often than a specialist's.

One coordination gap is unresolved anywhere in the trade literature. Fully bonded waterproofing and air-control membranes assume a smooth substrate, and a board-formed face is deliberately not smooth. Whether a specific bonded membrane adheres continuously over telegraphed grain and board ridges is confirmed with the membrane manufacturer, ideally on a mockup. The confirmation precedes any board-form texture on a face the Envelope chapter requires a bonded membrane on. The default resolution is placement: board-form texture goes on faces with no membrane requirement, and membrane faces are formed smooth.

**Exposed aggregate.** Surface retarders are sold in stepped exposure depths, from a light etch to full exposure of 1-1/2-inch aggregate. A deep finish therefore removes more than an inch of surface mortar. That removed depth comes directly out of the concrete cover protecting the reinforcement. No published guidance connects the finishing trade's exposure-depth choice to the structural cover requirement. The project research confirmed this as a genuine coordination gap between two trades' literatures. The resolution on this project: wherever exposed aggregate deeper than a light etch is specified on a reinforced element, the as-poured cover at that face increases by the intended exposure depth. The net cover after washing then still meets the values in the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter. The engineer of record signs off on the finish depth and the added cover before the form is built. Decorative unreinforced elements need no such adjustment.

**Integral color.** Pigment batched into the mix colors the full section, so wear and chips never expose gray. The governing standard caps pigment at 10 percent of cement mass and requires manufacturers to test effects on setting time, air content, and compressive strength. The manufacturer's compliance data for the specific pigment and dosage is the source for those effects. A structural mix carrying pigment near the cap gets a project trial batch before the pour. Standard compliance shows the pigment is permitted, not that this project's exact mix is unaffected.

This section describes finishes cast into the concrete at the form face. Applied interior surface finishing is the subject of the interiors chapter.

**Code**

```
Pigment for integrally colored concrete must comply with the pigment standard and its dosage limit.
  Maximum dosage 10 percent of cement mass; combined pigments must not together exceed any component pigment's own cap; the standard requires testing for effects on setting time, air content, and compressive strength.
  Source: ASTM C979/C979M.
```

## 7.9 The Cold Cellar, Root Cellar, And Integrated Safe Room

**Simple**

A below-grade concrete room against native soil holds a cool, stable temperature all year with no equipment. That makes it a root cellar for storing produce. The same concrete construction is most of what a certified storm shelter requires. This section states what each use requires, and why one all-purpose room creates conflicts.

**The root cellar.** Root crops store at 32-40°F and 85-95 percent relative humidity. The documented ventilation design is a two-vent stack system: a low intake near the floor at one end, and a high exhaust near the ceiling at the opposite end. Cool air enters low, and warmer air laden with carbon dioxide and ethylene (the ripening gas stored produce releases) leaves high. Documented vent sizing is 4-6 inches of pipe diameter, with a 4-inch vent cited as adequate for a room up to roughly 6 by 8 feet. Both vents get rodent screening and rain protection, plus adjustable dampers. The dampers run open in summer and are throttled in winter to hold the target range. An interior vestibule door reduces temperature swings from access. The guidance literature comes from colder, drier climates, and no monitored Oregon performance data exists for a room of this type. The owner instruments the finished room (a $10-30 thermometer-hygrometer, 2026) through its first year and adjusts dampers from the record.

A cellar built outside the sealed thermal envelope, on its own slab against native soil, sits outside the coverage of the passive radon system specified in the Envelope chapter. A sub-slab system protects only the slab area its collection layer is built under. The project's radon professional evaluates the cellar as its own zone against the county radon data, and states whether it needs its own soil-gas venting. The room's intermittent occupancy changes the exposure picture but does not remove the question.

**The safe room.** A certified storm shelter qualifies as a complete six-sided assembly: walls, slab, ceiling, and anchorage are tested and rated together. FEMA P-361 guidance cites 4-inch concrete or 3/16-inch steel minimum walls. Door assemblies must stop a 15-lb wooden 2x4 at roughly 100 mph and stay operable afterward. Vents and every other envelope penetration must meet the same missile rating. This project's ordinary reinforced walls are specified at 8 inches nominal minimum, double the 4-inch guidance figure (the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter states the wall tables). The wall thickness therefore comes at no added cost. The real added items are the rated door, any rated vents, a rated ceiling wherever the room is not directly under the concrete roof deck, and the anchorage check. One figure is unresolved. The project research found a 3.5-inch slab value attributed to ICC 500 and a 4-inch wall value in FEMA P-361 secondary guidance. The two could not be reconciled without the full purchased standard. The discrepancy is resolved against the primary ICC 500 text before any dimension is fixed. The Structural System and Concrete Design chapter (safe rooms and hardened-structure options) states the engineering path, the marginal-cost logic, and the construction hold points.

**Code**

```
A residential storm shelter must be designed and constructed to the storm-shelter standard.
  Door and penetration assemblies tested against a 15-lb wooden 2x4 at roughly 100 mph, remaining operable after impact; walls minimum 4-inch concrete or 3/16-inch steel per federal guidance; the complete six-sided assembly and its anchorage qualify as a system, not the walls alone.
  Source: ICC 500; FEMA P-361 (slab minimum to be verified against the full ICC 500 text before design).
```

**Choosing the room.** The below-grade cellar is the strongest candidate structurally: soil-backed reinforced walls on most sides and inherent missile protection below grade. Its weaknesses are the ICC 500 systems items (an ordinary cellar has no rated door or vents) and reach time. Shelter siting guidance weighs how fast every occupant reaches the room during a short-warning event. An interior bathroom or closet is smaller and faster to reach, and cheaper in rated hardware. It needs more of its envelope upgraded wherever its walls and ceiling are not already structural concrete. The selection is made on this project's actual floor plan, with rated door and vent products priced for each candidate room.

**One room or three.** Combining root cellar, wine or cheese storage, and safe room in a single below-grade room puts conflicting specifications in one envelope. Produce storage requires continuous adjustable outside-air ventilation. A certified shelter requires a sealed rated envelope with only tested penetrations. Cheese aging requires high humidity and a dedicated mold culture kept away from produce. Canned goods require drier, cooler conditions than produce or cheese. Shared excavation and a shared structural shell are the real savings, and the savings survive the conflicts when the functions are zoned inside the shell. The zoned layout places a rated, sealed shelter sub-zone behind its own rated door. Separately vented storage zones sit outside the sub-zone, in place of one undifferentiated room with one door and one vent set. Whether the savings justify the zoning complexity on this project's plan is the owner's decision once excavation pricing exists.

**First-attempt tolerance.** Among the sculptural techniques, the ranking for a builder with no forming experience runs by error tolerance. Ferrocement is the most error-tolerant: thin coats go on one at a time, and a bad coat is visible and correctable before the next. The curved block wall is second, corrected course by course with familiar masonry skills. Fabric formwork is middling, because the pour itself is one irreversible event. The corbelled vault is the least error-tolerant: a mid-sequence error risks collapse of placed work, not a cosmetic flaw. The sequence that manages all of them: the lowest-stakes, non-structural, non-weather-critical version is built first and stands through a full wet season. Only then does the technique reach anything structural or weather-critical. The same staging applies to the green roof (pilot on an outbuilding) and to board-form finishes (practice element before a visible wall).

**Open cost questions.** Two cost figures remain unclosed in the research behind this chapter. The first is the marginal cost of earth-sheltering over this project's already-concrete baseline (every published premium compares against wood framing). The second is the marginal cost of the heavier intensive-green-roof deck. No published unit figure exists, and retrofit is documented as materially harder than designing the capacity in. Both numbers come from bid comparisons and the engineer's comparative design pass at design time. Both are requested early, because reversing either decision after construction requires structural demolition.
