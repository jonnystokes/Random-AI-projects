# Chapter 3: Land, Site, and Feasibility

Five conditions end a land purchase, and every one of them can be settled before closing: no legal path to a dwelling under the zoning, a failed septic evaluation with no workable alternative, no legal access to a public road, no usable water, and a recorded restriction against residential use. Each first screen is free. Designed for any Oregon site, the house takes its footing depth, roof snow load, insulation thickness and water source from the parcel. What follows is the screening a parcel passes before the geotechnical work in the soil chapter and the title work in the legal chapter are worth paying for. Costs are 2026 figures unless a different year is given.

## 3.1 The questions that end a purchase

**Simple**

Five facts decide whether a parcel can hold this house at all, and they come before price. Four are about the land: the zoning, the septic ground, the road in, and the water. Number five is in the deed, a recorded restriction that forbids a home. One no among the five ends the purchase. No design and no budget can repair a zoning problem or a dry well. Everything else about a parcel, its slope, its climate, its distance from town, changes the house or the budget without ending the project. You sort every finding into one of six bands, and each band has its own action.

**Applied**

| Band | Meaning | Examples | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dealbreaker | A core requirement is missing or legally blocked | No legal dwelling path, a failed septic evaluation with no workable alternative, no legal access, no usable water, a recorded restriction against residential use | Walk away, or make closing contingent on the issue being resolved in writing |
| Expensive risk | The parcel works, but the fix is costly or uncertain | A long utility extension, major driveway work, difficult drainage, an unresolved title issue, marginal soil needing a $20,000 to $50,000 septic system | Price the risk from written quotes, add written contingencies, get agency and contractor answers in writing |
| Design constraint | The parcel works if the house adapts | Slope, snow load, wind exposure, solar orientation, staging limits, a floodplain fringe | Carry the constraint into the site plan and the budget |
| Lifestyle tradeoff | The parcel works, but daily life changes | A long drive, weak internet, winter access, cellular dead zones | Decide whether the tradeoff fits how the household wants to live |
| Future-flexibility check | The first build fits, and later plans may not | Outbuilding limits, accessory-dwelling rules, home-occupation limits, equipment storage, access | Get the county's written position before closing |
| Strong candidate | Land, utilities, access, septic, water, zoning and title all pass | Every dealbreaker question answered yes in writing | Move to design with the site facts in hand |

1. Answer the five dealbreaker questions (zoning, septic, access, water, title) before any other.
2. Write every finding under its band on one page, with its source and date.
3. End the purchase on a dealbreaker, or make closing contingent on its written resolution.
4. Price each expensive risk into the offer from a written quote or agency answer.
5. Carry each design constraint into the site plan and the budget before design starts.
6. Decide each lifestyle tradeoff as a household before the contingency period ends.
7. Get the county's written position on outbuildings, an accessory dwelling, work areas and access.
8. Treat a risk that cannot be priced, permitted, engineered, insured, accessed or inspected as a dealbreaker. A written answer lifts it.

## 3.2 Finding the parcel

**Simple**

Most rural parcels that fit this house never appear on the big listing sites. Finding one means running several channels at once: land brokers, the county's ownership records, federal resale lists and the homesteading forums. Some parcels change hands before any listing appears, through a letter to an owner who lives out of state. Brokers' fees are negotiated and written into an agreement before you see a property. You check the asking price against two public numbers, the assessor's value and the state's per-acre farmland values. On a forested parcel the trees are priced apart from the ground. Counting and measuring them is a forester's job, done for a fee.

**Applied**

| Channel | What it reaches | Cost or condition |
|---|---|---|
| Land-focused brokers, interviewed before anything is signed | Listed parcels, unlisted parcels the broker knows of, and closed-sale comparables from the regional multiple listing service | Commission of 5 to 10 percent of price on land sales, allocated by written agreement since the 2024 National Association of Realtors settlement rather than by an automatic seller payment |
| Accredited Land Consultant directory (rliland.com) | Brokers trained in water rights, timber, soils and resource zoning through 104 hours of coursework plus a transaction record | An ordinary negotiated commission, with no separate fee |
| County assessor and GIS portals | Absentee and inherited-land owners, by name and mailing address | Public record in Oregon, and the requester's purpose is no grounds for denial. Bulk-export format and fee are set by each county |
| Direct mail to absentee owners | Parcels with no listing | Response rates of about 1 to 3 percent, on the order of 300 to 500 letters per closed purchase, measured on house-focused campaigns |
| USDA Farm Service Agency resales portal (properties.sc.egov.usda.gov/resales) | Farm-inventory parcels from loan foreclosures, offered first to qualified beginning farmers and prevailing Pigford claimants, then to the public | Intermittent. Background monitoring only |
| BLM Oregon/Washington disposal notices | Scattered or isolated federal tracts found suitable for sale under the Federal Land Policy and Management Act | Announced through the state office's news releases and the Federal Register. Intermittent |
| Regional forums and groups | Owner-to-owner sales and early word of listings, in the permies.com real-estate forum's recurring Oregon threads and in county homesteading groups | Groups form and dissolve, so the list is rebuilt at search time |

1. Interview two or three land brokers for the region before signing anything.
2. Read the representation agreement for who pays the commission, and how much.
3. Compare the asking price with the assessor's real-market value for the tax lot.
4. Compare the per-acre price with the state farmland values for that county and land class.
5. On a forested parcel, order a timber cruise before pricing the offer: $8 to $15 per acre, with a $300 to $500 minimum.
6. Price the timber into the offer as a credit when you will not harvest, or as an asset when you will.
7. Log each channel checked, the date and the parcels it produced.

**Professional**

Oregon State University Extension publishes county-level farmland values from the USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service surveys. In the 2025 statewide figures, irrigated cropland ran about $8,000 per acre, non-irrigated cropland $3,000 and pastureland $1,080. Wasco, Morrow, Lake and Crook counties showed gains above 60 percent over the period reported. Every county assessor publishes real-market value by tax lot, a maintained, zoning-aware baseline for checking an asking price rather than a listing price. Closed-sale comparables sit in the regional multiple listing service, reached through a licensed broker or an appraiser.

Two Oregon brokerages publish a regional focus. Whitney Land Company in Pendleton has operated since 1970 and concentrates on Umatilla County and the northeast Oregon wheat, ranch and recreation land. Oregon Farm and Home Brokers works rural and farm property with stated water-rights, soils, zoning and timber expertise. Mossy Oak Properties is a national franchise, so its strength in a given county is the local franchisee's. Earning the Accredited Land Consultant designation takes two years in land brokerage or three in a related field. It also takes five closed land transactions totaling $15 million, or 25 separate transactions, on top of the coursework.

Industrial timberland in Oregon changes hands in bulk portfolio transactions between companies and investment managers. In 2020 Weyerhaeuser acquired about 85,000 acres in mid-coastal Oregon and sold about 149,000 acres in southern Oregon to Hancock-managed funds. Company sections sit in a checkerboard among public and private land, a pattern left by the nineteenth-century railroad grants. When a company sells an isolated inholding, the parcel reaches the market through an ordinary land brokerage, and no company runs a standing list a buyer can join.

Absentee and inherited ownership is more common for undeveloped land than for houses. For that reason land-investing guides rank mail to absentee owners above driving the roads for raw parcels. An unused parcel looks the same whether its owner is engaged or gone. Assessor data arrives from the county by records request or by a bulk-export subscription at the county's cost-recovery fee. Third-party aggregators sell the same data after licensing it.

Confirmed, transferable water rights raise a parcel's market value. Current listings of Oregon water-right properties average about $3,993 per acre across 21 dissimilar parcels. That figure is an asking-price average, so the premium for one parcel comes from an appraiser experienced in water-right valuation. Enrollment in farm or forest tax deferral lowers the seller's annual tax bill, and sellers price on market potential. So the deferral is a rollback exposure for the buyer to quantify with the assessor, by the method in the special-assessment section.

Cruise results convert to dollars through a stumpage price, the price paid for standing timber by species and grade. From 1977 the Oregon Department of Forestry published timber price reports. It now relies on a subscription log-price service for its own appraisals, so the current figure comes from the consulting forester. Cruise cost rises with steep ground and dense stands, and a small tract pays the minimum charge. On resource-zoned land the county's written dwelling-path answer is the slowest step of the search, and the search itself runs months to years.

## 3.3 Climate and design loads

**Simple**

Where your lot sits sets the numbers the house is designed around. Cold, heat, snow and wind each become a value on the drawings. So do the earthquake shaking the site can see and the depth the ground freezes to. Those values are looked up for the parcel itself. The same plan needs thicker insulation in the mountains and deeper footings where the ground freezes. Above the valley floor the roof is built for drifted snow. Most of western Oregon sits in the high earthquake categories, so the concrete walls get an engineer. Two more answers come from the county's maps and ordinances: whether it has adopted the wildfire-building rules, and whether the parcel lies in a floodplain.

**Applied**

| Value | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| Winter design temperature (the outdoor low the heating design uses) and summer design temperature (the high the cooling design uses) | The Building Codes Division's Design Criteria Hub where the jurisdiction participates, otherwise the building department |
| Ground snow load, and the roof snow-load method (flat or sloped, with shape coefficients) | The OSU/SEAO lookup at snowload.seao.org, by parcel coordinates |
| Basic wind speed and exposure category | The Design Criteria Hub and the building department |
| Seismic design category | The Design Criteria Hub, the Building Codes Division's how-to guide, and the geotechnical report's soil class |
| Frost depth or frost-protection requirement | The building department's adopted design table |
| Annual rainfall pattern, elevation and exposure | NOAA's U.S. Climate Normals (1991 to 2020, ncei.noaa.gov) for the nearest long-term station, and PRISM (prism.oregonstate.edu) for a parcel far from any station |
| Multi-year drought history for the basin | The U.S. Drought Monitor's historical series (droughtmonitor.unl.edu) |
| Hazard designations: wildfire-code adoption and floodplain status | The building department in writing, and the FEMA flood map |

1. Pull each value above for the candidate parcel before the structural design starts.
2. Save each answer with its source and date in the project binder.
3. Ask the building department in writing whether the wildfire-hardening section applies to the parcel.
4. Pull the nearest station's monthly normals and the basin's drought history while the other values are open.
5. Pass those two datasets to the water-storage and brick-weathering calculations.

Planning ranges below serve first sizing and first conversations, and a permit uses the parcel's own values.

| Condition | Early planning range across Oregon |
|---|---|
| Winter design temperature | -10 °F (high elevations east of the Cascades) to 25 °F (Willamette Valley) |
| Summer design temperature | 85 °F (coast) to 105 °F (high desert) |
| Ground snow load | 15 psf (valley lowlands), 25 to 50 psf (rural hills), 100 to 300 psf and more (mountain elevations above about 3,000 to 4,000 feet) |
| Basic wind speed | 95 to 115 mph in most of Oregon, higher near the coast and in the Columbia River Gorge |
| Seismic design category | Most of western Oregon is D0, D1 or D2 (Cascadia Subduction Zone), and eastern Oregon is largely C |
| Frost depth | 12 to 18 inches in most valleys, 24 to 36 inches in cold mountain zones |
| Annual rainfall | 15 to 20 inches east of the Cascades, 60 to 100 inches and more on the coast and the west slopes |

**Professional**

Structural design takes seven values from the parcel: ground snow load, roof snow load, basic wind speed, exposure category, seismic design category, flood hazard and frost protection. Envelope and mechanical design add the outdoor and indoor design temperatures, humidity and solar assumptions, rain exposure, and a wildfire-smoke assumption where the region has one. For a concrete house the load effects close into one permitted design: gravity, lateral load, uplift, sliding, overturning and foundation demand.

Added wall mass raises the seismic and foundation terms. Flat concrete roofs add three questions wood framing never raises: snow drifting against parapets, ponding instability, and parapet detailing. Oregon's structural code changed its snow maps with the 2025 edition, which adopts ASCE 7-22. Design snow loads rose in parts of the state and fell in others, so the structural drawings state which edition's map they use.

Oregon's climate datasets serve three later calculations. At the nearest station, the driest consecutive months size the rainwater storage. In the Drought Monitor's record, the frequency of consecutive below-normal wet seasons, as in 2020 through 2022, sets the margin above a single dry season. For brick, the ASTM C216 weathering index, the product of freezing-cycle days and winter rainfall, classifies the exposure as negligible, moderate or severe. Its category boundaries sit in the purchased standard. Storage sizing is in the water independence chapter and the weathering index in the durability chapter.

Senate Bill 83 of 2025 repealed the statewide wildfire hazard map, and the wildfire section of the residential code is now a local option. Municipalities adopt the section whole by ordinance and identify the areas it covers. The ordinance also provides a transition plan for subdivisions under development and a local appeal process, and the map goes to the Building Codes Division. Inside an adopted area the concrete walls meet the wall requirement outright as noncombustible material, and the roof, vents, eaves, decks and glazing carry the remaining requirements.

Nonhabitable accessory structures of 400 square feet or less are exempt from the section when they stand 50 feet or more from any building with habitable space. Agricultural buildings exempt by statute are exempt here too.

**Code**

## 3.4 Zoning and allowed use

**Simple**

Zoning is the local rule for what a piece of land may be used for. It decides whether a house can go there at all. Some land allows a home with an ordinary permit. Some allows one only after a special approval with its own tests. Some allows none. No building permit overrides zoning, and no design cures it. For this house the question is wider than a dwelling. The shop, the block-yard, the outbuildings and any future business each need their own yes. You get the answer in writing from the planning department before money is committed, with the tax lot number on it.

**Applied**

Put these questions to the planning department in writing before the offer, naming the parcel by tax lot or map number and never by description.

1. Whether a primary single-family residence is allowed outright, by conditional approval, or not at all.
2. Whether the parcel meets the zone's minimum lot size.
3. The front, side, rear, stream, road and resource setbacks.
4. Whether those setbacks leave room for the house, driveway, septic, well, drainage and staging.
5. The maximum building height and the maximum lot coverage.
6. Whether outbuildings are allowed, at what size and count.
7. Whether an accessory dwelling unit (a second, smaller home) is allowed, and under which rule.
8. Whether home occupations and home-based businesses are allowed, and on what terms.
9. Whether the jurisdiction has adopted the wildfire-hardening section for the area.
10. Any limits on outdoor storage, equipment, animals, farming and exterior work areas.
11. File the planner's name, the date, the parcel identifier and the written answer. Verbal answers in passing are no basis for a purchase.

**Professional**

| Zone family | Fit for this project | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Rural Residential (RR-2, RR-5, RR-10, RR-20) | The cleanest target | The number is the minimum lot size in acres. A primary residence is a permitted use, and accessory dwellings, home occupations and outbuildings come with county limits. |
| Mixed Use Agricultural (MUA-10, MUA-20) | Workable | County-created. Allows a primary residence alongside farm and timber uses in the counties that use it, Douglas County among them. |
| Farm-Forest (F1, F2) | Conditionally workable | More review than the RR zones, on county-specific terms. |
| Non-resource rural lands | Workable where a county has adopted it | Rare. Smaller parcels than farm or forest zones, with habitat, carrying-capacity and hazard findings still required. |
| Exclusive Farm Use (EFU) | Restrictive, and the highest-risk family for this project | A residence requires qualifying under one of the dwelling paths: farm income, nonfarm soils and dates, lot of record, or the template. A second dwelling takes its own approval under the same statutes. |
| Urban residential (R1, R2) | Constrained | Small-lot rules, and little room for the block-yard, the outbuildings or the privacy the project wants. |

Land-use review comes before building-code review. Zoning, overlays, resource-land rules, setbacks, access rules, floodplain rules and home-occupation limits can each reshape the project before the first footing is drawn. Allowing a residence does not mean the zone allows the rest of the project, whose six parts each need the zone's yes:

- long driveways and equipment delivery routes
- staging areas for materials, formwork and equipment
- outbuildings: a workshop, a garage, storage, an equipment shed
- testing space for compaction tests, concrete-mix trials and a block press
- material storage for rebar, block, lumber, sealants and insulation
- any future business use under the home-occupation rules

Accessory dwelling law splits at the urban growth boundary. Inside it, the state mandate applies and the county has no discretion. Outside it, on rural residential land, Senate Bill 391 of 2021 gave counties an option. A county may allow one rural accessory dwelling under fixed statutory limits on floor area, parcel size and distance from the existing house. Counties may restrict further and may not expand those limits, and a county that has not adopted the option can adopt it later.

Water is also in that statute. Where the state has restricted exempt groundwater use, the option is closed, which ties this question to the groundwater screen. On farm or forest land a second dwelling is a farm-dwelling or nonfarm-dwelling approval under the dwelling-path tests, and the accessory-dwelling statutes do not reach it.

MUA and RR zones are county-created districts, so their permitted uses, outbuilding counts and home-occupation standards come from the county's own ordinance. Statutory home-occupation caps apply by their own terms only in farm, forest and mixed farm-forest zones. A county may allow a home occupation in any zone on terms it sets. Whether a county's MUA or RR ordinance treats a block press as a home workshop or as small manufacturing is answered only by that ordinance's text.

Non-resource designations exist where a county has shown that land meets neither the farm nor the forest definition. DLCD states that several counties have adopted such zoning and publishes no list of them. In the Southern Oregon Regional Pilot Project, Douglas, Jackson and Josephine counties tried from 2012 to 2016 to write a joint non-resource framework, and the effort ended without consensus.

Non-resource zones can carry review layers a plain RR zone lacks. Goal 5 (habitat), Goal 6 (carrying capacity) and Goal 7 (hazards) findings still apply, with the burden set by the county's ordinance. Under the Goal 14 rule, a newly created exception area for rural housing carries its own minimum parcel size. County and city GIS portals list parcel identity, the zoning layer, the tax lot, overlays, mapped constraints and contours without account creation. DLCD publishes explanations of the statewide planning goals at oregon.gov/lcd.

**Code**

```

  Source: ORS chapter 215.

  Incorporated areas plan and zone under city authority.
  Source: ORS chapter 227.
```

## 3.5 Dwelling paths on farm and forest land

**Simple**

On farm and forest land the right to build a house is not automatic. Oregon law lists a few paths to a dwelling, and each has exact tests. Those tests are the farm's sales, the soil and the parcel's age, the tract's acreage, or how many neighbors already have houses. Land that fits no path cannot legally hold a home, whatever the purchase price. Each test is a fact about the land's history, checkable from public records before an offer. What turns a farm parcel into a building site is the county's written answer on the path.

**Applied**

1. Match the parcel to one of the five dwelling paths before anything else.
2. Pull the parcel-creation date and the deed chain from a title company or the county records.
3. For the nonfarm path, confirm the parcel was created before January 1, 1993.
4. For the lot-of-record path, confirm acquisition by the present owner before January 1, 1985, or by inheritance from such an owner.
5. For the template path, read the soil productivity class from the NRCS Web Soil Survey first.
6. Where the county wants more, hire a consulting forester: $8 to $15 per acre, $300 to $500 minimum.
7. Draw the 160-acre square on county GIS around the tract centroid.
8. Count the other lots, and the dwellings on them, as they stood on January 1, 1993.
9. Count only lawfully created lots, and treat the count as a screen.
10. Search the land-use file for a recorded condition from an earlier partition.
11. Get the county's path determination in writing before the contingency period ends.
12. Treat "probably" as a dealbreaker until it becomes a written yes.
13. On EFU land, ask in the same letter whether a block operation fits the home-occupation terms.

**Professional**

### The five paths

[FIGURE: template-dwelling-square, the 160-acre square on a tract's centroid and the road-aligned rectangle alternative, with the counted lots and dwellings marked]

| Path | What it tests | The parcel it fits | What to check first |
|---|---|---|---|
| Farm dwelling | Gross sales of farm products over several recent years, at a threshold that doubles on high-value farmland, or tract size alone for a large tract | A working farm operation, or a tract large enough to skip the dollar test | The operation's sales records, or the tract's acreage and rangeland status |
| Nonfarm dwelling | Poor soils (a class cutoff in the Willamette Valley, a functional unsuitability test elsewhere), the parcel's creation date, and the stability of the area's land-use pattern | An old parcel on ground that cannot farm | The creation date in the deed chain, and the soil class from the NRCS survey |
| Lot-of-record dwelling | How long the present owner has held a lawfully created lot, or inheritance from such an owner, with no dwelling on the tract | Land held in one family for decades | The acquisition date, and the high-value farmland status |
| Large-tract forest dwelling | Contiguous forest acreage, with a higher bar east of the Cascades, and a combination option for separate tracts that costs recorded no-dwelling restrictions on every tract counted | A large timber tract | Acreage, county, and whether any tract already carries a restriction |
| Template dwelling | The development pattern around the tract inside a fixed square, tiered by soil productivity, with a minimum number of existing dwellings | A small forest parcel in an already-settled area | The GIS count, and the lawful creation of every lot counted |

Litigation concentrates on the template path. Only lawfully created lots count toward the template (Reeves v. Yamhill County, 53 Or LUBA 4, 2006). Counties must show how they located the tract's centroid (Eng v. Wallowa County, 79 Or LUBA 421, 2019). Property-line adjustments made after the statute's 2019 cutoff cannot be what qualifies the parcel. In its 2025 session the legislature amended that provision again, so the wording is read fresh at application time. Where the tract fronts an old road, a long rectangle aligned with the road replaces the square. Larger tracts on a road or perennial stream have a further variant.

No state statute caps the size of the primary farm or forest dwelling. Statutory square-footage caps attach to other categories instead: a family-forestry accessory dwelling at 900 square feet (ORS 215.757) and guest-ranch lodging under its own limits (ORS 215.461). County ordinances can add a local size or siting cap.

### Assembly, size and denial patterns

Common ownership of adjacent parcels does not make their combined acreage count toward a dwelling threshold. Counties require consolidation into a single lot or parcel through the property-line-adjustment process. Only the consolidated parcel is treated as one qualifying unit, a condition Marion County writes into its approvals under ORS chapter 215. Denial patterns worth screening against come from the Land Use Board of Appeals:

- an overlay zone raising the effective minimum far above the base zone, where an 80-acre deer-winter-range minimum defeated a split of a 41-acre parcel (ODFW v. Klamath County, 66 Or LUBA 92, 2012)
- a recorded condition from a decades-old partition barring further division (Woods v. Wallowa County, LUBA No 2021-064, 2021)
- a road-improvement exaction across the full length of the access road, upheld under rough proportionality (Dudek v. Umatilla County, 42 Or LUBA 427, 2002)
- a failed suitability showing on a nonresource partition (Newsome v. Clackamas County, 27 Or LUBA 578, 1994)

DLCD's biennial Farm and Forest Land Use Report tabulates approved dwellings by county. Most denials are never appealed, so the county planning department's own files hold the local record of what was refused and why.

### A block operation is not farm use

Manufacturing concrete masonry units is not an agricultural activity at any scale, so on EFU land the operation fits only the home-occupation category. That category confines the work to a resident operator, a small on-site staff, and the dwelling or buildings that already go with permitted uses.

Confinement to existing buildings is what defeats a purpose-built production building. Statute bars the county from authorizing a structure the zone would not otherwise allow. On that ground LUBA rejected a free-standing building sized for a paying-events home occupation (1000 Friends of Oregon v. Clackamas County, 81 Or LUBA 664, 2020). Event and operation staff count toward the employee cap whoever hires them (Green v. Douglas County, 63 Or LUBA 200, 2011). In the same case LUBA read "operated substantially in" the dwelling or associated buildings as "to a large degree" or "in the main". The measure is the share of the work done outside them.

"Clearly secondary to the agricultural character of the property" appears in no statute. "Clearly subordinate" tests and square-footage caps are local-code creations that differ by jurisdiction. Clackamas County caps a home occupation at 1,000 square feet of an accessory building and Island City at 600, in the published cases. Production-scale planning is in the owner-made blocks chapter and the permit process in the legal chapter. Review track, fee and timeline for a home-occupation or conditional-use application come from the county's own ordinance.

## 3.6 Measure 49 authorizations and rezoning notice

**Simple**

Some rural parcels carry a Measure 49 authorization, a state order that allows a home site the current zoning would otherwise forbid. That order transfers with the land. Whoever won it has no deadline to build. Later owners do, and the clock starts at the first sale after the claimant, not at each sale after that. What the order allows is written in the order itself: how many dwellings, how many lots, and on what conditions. You read the order itself, because a listing's summary of it binds no one.

**Applied**

1. Open the Measure 49 Analyzer at hub.oregonexplorer.info.
2. Search by election number, claim name, or by clicking the parcel on the map.
3. Where the election number is unknown, search DLCD's registries of claims, election notices and returns.
4. Download the Final Order and Home Site Authorization for the claim.
5. Read the authorized counts of dwellings, parcels and home sites, and every condition.
6. Read the Analyzer's high-value farmland, forestland and groundwater-restricted overlays for the parcel.
7. Find the first transfer after the original claimant in the chain of title. That transfer starts the completion clock.
8. Treat a transfer into a revocable trust, or to the claimant's spouse, as no transfer.
9. Treat the trust turning irrevocable at the claimant's death, or a death after the final order, as the start.
10. Where the claimant died before December 6, 2007, treat the claim as having passed no right.
11. Have the title company confirm the order's recording.
12. Have an attorney review any claim that is old or unusual.

**Professional**

Measure 49, passed in 2007, replaced Measure 37 relief with transferable home-site authorizations. Its drafters put the completion deadline on buyers rather than on the claimant, which makes the chain of title, and not the order's date, the thing to read. For that deadline an owner is the holder of fee title, the purchaser under a recorded land sale contract, or the settlor of a revocable trust. Trustees become the owner only when the trust turns irrevocable.

Analyzer overlays matter because the measure defines high-value farmland, high-value forestland and groundwater-restricted areas and limited what a claim inside them could be approved for. Most Measure 49 determinations are not land-use decisions under the measure's own terms. That keeps them outside the Land Use Board of Appeals' ordinary jurisdiction and narrows the routes for a later challenge. So the title company's confirmation of the recorded order carries the weight it does.

Measure 56, approved by voters in November 1998, created the rezoning-notice duty now codified for counties at ORS 215.503. Counties mail individual notice when a proposed ordinance would rezone a property or limit a use it previously allowed. The statute prescribes the form down to its boldfaced warning. Cities carry the same duty under ORS 227.186. Under ORS 197.047 DLCD tells local governments when a new state law or rule limits a use, so that the local notice can follow.

**Code**

```
A subsequent owner's Measure 49 authorization expires.
  Development complete within 10 years, measured from the first ownership transfer after the original claimant; later transfers do not restart the clock; the original claimant has no deadline.
  Source: ORS 195.300 to 195.336; OAR chapter 660, divisions 029 and 041; DLCD Measure 49 FAQ (2023).

Counties mail individual notice before a rezoning hearing.
  Written notice of land use change to each affected owner at least 20 and not more than 40 days before the first hearing, describing in detail how the ordinance would affect the property, with the prescribed boldfaced warning.
  Source: ORS 215.503; ORS 227.186 (cities); ORS 197.047 (state notice to local governments).
```

## 3.7 Property rights, title and recorded restrictions

**Simple**

Buying land does not buy everything on it, under it, or leading to it. The timber, the minerals and the water can each belong to someone else. So can the right to drive across a neighbor's road to your own gate. Restrictions recorded decades ago still bind whoever owns the parcel, and some forbid any new building. All of it is listed in the preliminary title report, a title company's summary of every recorded document that affects the parcel. You read it in full, with the documents it points to, before the title contingency expires.

**Applied**

| Right or restriction | What to verify |
|---|---|
| Access easements | Legal and physical access from a public road, and who maintains the access |
| Utility easements | Routes, widths, and limits on planting or building over them |
| Road maintenance agreements | Cost-sharing rules, responsibility, dispute mechanisms |
| Mineral rights | Whether oil, gas and hard-rock rights come with the surface |
| Timber and forestry rights | Whether standing timber and future cut rights transfer |
| Water rights | Surface, spring and some groundwater rights are separate legal rights in Oregon |
| Riparian rights | Rights and obligations along creeks, rivers, ponds and lakes |
| Hunting and fishing rights | Sometimes deeded separately, especially near timber-company holdings |
| Air rights and height limits | Restrictions on building height, antennas and wind equipment |
| Grazing rights | Rights that attach to parcels adjacent to federal land |
| Conservation easements | Recorded permanent use restrictions that bind every future owner |
| Covenants, conditions and restrictions (CC&Rs) | Private subdivision or association rules that can control style, materials or use |
| Liens, judgments, recorded claims | Anything affecting transfer, financing or use |

1. Resolve access first when the parcel does not touch a public road.
2. Confirm the recorded easement exists, and drive the route in bad weather.
3. Confirm that the road carries a loaded concrete truck in winter, and who maintains it.
4. Confirm emergency access and delivery access at the same time.
5. Read every recorded document the title report lists, in full, before the contingency expires.
6. Order a current boundary survey where fences, old descriptions or an easement line leave any doubt.
7. Have an attorney read any mineral severance deed, timber deed or conservation easement before the offer is priced.

**Professional**

Title review and site feasibility are one exercise. Physically buildable parcels are still blocked by easements, severed rights, covenants, conservation restrictions, road-maintenance obligations, water-rights limits and survey gaps. Working documents are the preliminary title report, the assessor's records, the Water Resources Department's records, the seller's disclosures, any survey, any road agreement and the county's recorded documents. Of these the preliminary title report is the index to the rest.

Oregon water law allocates by prior appropriation, first in time and first in right. Under it a spring on the land can legally belong to a downstream right holder, and water rights are researched at the Water Resources Department rather than the county. The water-rights section states the exempt uses and the checks. On a shared private road, snow removal and drainage are the two maintenance categories that produce the disputes. The access section states the four terms a maintenance agreement needs.

### Severed minerals and timber

Older parcels in former timber and mining country can have a mineral estate owned by someone else. Under the common-law pattern that estate is dominant. Its holder may use as much of the surface as is reasonably necessary to explore and extract, without the surface owner's consent. Some severance deeds confine the mineral owner to deep extraction that never touches the surface, and others reserve broad surface rights, so the recorded severance deed sets the exposure. Where the estate is live, the house, driveway, well and septic system go on a compact, central envelope away from the parcel's edges.

Two statutes frame the edges. An old reservation that has gone unused and unclaimed for the statutory period is the case the extinguishment procedure exists for. Severed mineral interests stay off the separate tax rolls under ORS 308.115 unless they are mined. Parcels adjacent to federal land, or once federally owned, can also carry a federal mineral reservation, researched in BLM records.

Severed timber rights are created the same way and found the same way: a recorded timber deed, timber sale contract or reservation clause in an earlier deed. The right runs independently of the land until any stated term expires. Oregon keeps no timber-rights registry. Full title-company searches of the chain of title surface the document the same way they surface a mineral reservation. Sellers' assurances and tax-lot lookups miss one recorded decades ago.

### Conservation easements

Conservation easements are statutory nonpossessory interests, held by land trusts or public bodies and individually drafted. Their purposes run from habitat and open space to farmland, water quality and historic preservation. Past owners sign them for a tax benefit or as a condition of an earlier transaction. They bind every later owner whether or not anyone has ever litigated them. Some prohibit any new construction. Recorded text controls, restriction by restriction, and the Oregon Watershed Enhancement Board's published easement guidance describes the usual terms.

### Fence lines and adverse possession

Long-standing misplaced fences are a narrower risk in Oregon than in states that ask only for open and notorious possession. In 1989 the legislature added an honest-belief element, so the question is what the neighbor believed when the fence went up, and not only how long it has stood. Neighbors who always knew the fence was wrong cannot meet that element, and seasonal grazing across the line proves nothing on its own. What remains is a fence placed in good faith on a bad survey and relied on for a generation, which a current boundary survey resolves before closing.

**Code**

```
Recorded interests control, in recorded order.
  Recording gives deeds, easements, covenants and liens their legal effect and their priority against later interests.
  Source: ORS chapter 93; the county clerk's recorded-document index.

Timber harvest and riparian work follow the forest practice rules.
  Harvest restrictions and riparian setbacks under the Oregon Department of Forestry's rules apply to a private owner's timber.
  Source: OAR chapter 629.

Recreational use of the land has its own liability statute.
  An owner who permits recreational use, gardening, woodcutting or special-forest-product harvest on the land is not liable in contract or tort for injury arising from that use, subject to the statute's exceptions; an owner who posts the land closed may recover liquidated damages up to $1,000 from a trespasser.
  Source: ORS 105.672 to 105.696; ORS 105.700.

Adverse possession has a ten-year, good-faith standard.
  10 years of actual, open, notorious, exclusive, hostile and continuous possession; an honest, objectively based belief of ownership throughout; proof by clear and convincing evidence; grazing alone insufficient.
  Source: ORS 105.620.

Conservation easements are statutory and bind successors.
  A recorded conservation easement is a nonpossessory interest imposing limitations or affirmative obligations for natural, scenic, open-space, agricultural, forest, recreational, resource, water-quality or historic purposes; it binds successors.
  Source: ORS 271.715 to 271.795.

A dormant mineral interest can be extinguished by procedure.
  A surface owner may terminate an unused, unclaimed mineral interest through the statute's publication, mailing and recording steps.
  Source: ORS 517.180.
```

## 3.8 Access rights and road standards

**Simple**

Maps show roads that were platted decades ago and never built. Nor does a road on the map prove your right to use it. That right comes from a recorded dedication or a recorded easement. Fire code sets minimum driveway width, overhead clearance, grade and turnarounds, and a driveway that fails those numbers is rebuilt at your expense. On a shared private road the cost of plowing and grading is split only by a written agreement. Regrading, widening and easement negotiation are priced before the offer.

**Applied**

1. Check the county's accepted-public-road inventory, the "All Public Roads" layer on Oregon GEOHub or the county's own.
2. Check the recorded plat and the county surveyor's records for what was legally dedicated.
3. Drive the route. A dedicated road can be absent on the ground.
4. Where access crosses private land, confirm a recorded easement exists.
5. On a private shared road, require a recorded maintenance agreement before closing.
6. Check that the agreement states a numeric cost-sharing formula, snow and drainage responsibility by name, a dispute mechanism, and a right to compel a non-payer.
7. Ask the fire district in writing which access standard applies and which code edition it enforces.
8. Ask the county road department whether its own road standard supersedes the fire code's for the parcel.
9. Walk any culvert crossing before and after a storm while under contract.
10. After purchase, check the inlet for debris and the headwall each fall.

**Professional**

Step 2 catches what Jefferson County calls a Local Access Road, dedicated to the public through platting, built to no standard and maintained by no one. Most counties feed the Association of Oregon Counties' Integrated Road Information System, which is where the accepted-road layers come from. An unrecorded easement gives no notice to a later owner of the burdened parcel and is close to unenforceable in practice. Where residents of a private road have organized a special road district, the district levies its own property tax for maintenance, which converts informal cost-sharing into an enforceable assessment. Without an enforcement term, compelling a non-paying neighbor takes a lawsuit with no fee-shifting.

Establishing a new easement across a neighbor's land is a private negotiation with no fixed price and no clock. Mechanical costs are known ranges. Attorney drafting runs a few hundred to a few thousand dollars. Where the easement line needs monuments, a boundary survey applies, and the surveyor files the record of survey with the county surveyor within 45 days of setting them. Recording costs tens of dollars.

Negotiation is the unbounded cost, since the neighbor owes nothing, can demand compensation, or can attach use limits. One such limit, a residential-only clause, would matter later when block-yard delivery traffic uses the easement.

Where negotiation fails and the parcel is landlocked, the statutory way of necessity applies. Petitioners file with the county governing body, with a drawing, a narrative of the route and the evidence of necessity. They post a bond for the county's investigation, $500 under Multnomah County's local rule. Cases can transfer to circuit court, where an answer is due within 30 days of service and a reply within 10. Any party may appeal a county order within 30 days.

Petitioners bear the burden of proving that no other legal access exists (Tyska v. Prest, Oregon Court of Appeals, 1999). Those windows put even an uncontested case at several months, and the court may award the burdened owner costs and attorney fees. Oregon's separate common-law easement by necessity requires unity of ownership before the parcels were split and a necessity that existed at the split.

Dimensions in the Code block are the ones Clackamas Fire District #1 quoted from the adopted fire code in its 2021 guidance for applicants. That guidance was written against the edition built on the 2018 model code. Since then Oregon has moved to the 2025 Oregon Fire Code, built on the 2024 model code and in force from October 1, 2025. Each jurisdiction decides whether to adopt the appendix that holds these dimensions.

Under ORS 368.039 a county or city road standard adopted by charter, plan or ordinance supersedes the road provisions of the fire code. So the controlling document for a driveway is settled by the county road department and the fire district together. Whatever standard controls, the remediation cost is the gap between it and the driveway's as-built width, grade, turnaround and surface strength. A civil engineer or road contractor prices that gap against the site.

Base rock depth for a loaded ready-mix truck, at roughly 66,000 to 80,000 pounds gross, has no lookup value. It is a geotechnical design against the subgrade soil, the axle loads and the drainage. Culvert sizing at a watercourse crossing follows the county road standard, commonly a 25-year or 50-year design storm. Crossings that disturb a regulated streambed add Department of State Lands removal-fill permitting.

The build-sequence chapter states why the durable driveway comes first, the culvert sizing is in the soil chapter, and the second access is planned in the resilience chapter. Every later delivery uses the driveway, and a driveway that ruts under the first loaded truck delays the rest of the build. Second access routes, a graded secondary track or a formalized neighbor easement, cost least when arranged at design time. Then the negotiation is unpressured and the equipment is already mobilized.

**Code**

```
Fire apparatus access reaches every dwelling served by a driveway.
  Driving surface 20 feet unobstructed, 26 feet beside a hydrant; 12 feet permitted for three or fewer dwelling units inside a 20-foot cleared corridor, with turning radii of 44 feet inside and 56 feet outside; vertical clearance 13 feet 6 inches.
  Source: Oregon Fire Code 503.2.1 and Appendix D, D103.1 (2025 edition, 2024 IFC base, effective October 1, 2025; dimensions as quoted from the 2018-based edition in Clackamas Fire District #1's applications guide; the district's adopted amendments control).

Driveway grade and surface strength are fixed by the fire code.
  Grade 12 percent maximum, 15 percent where the dwelling is sprinklered; an all-weather surface for a 13,500-pound wheel load and 75,000 pounds gross vehicle weight.
  Source: Oregon Fire Code 503.2 and Appendix D (2025 edition, as above).

Dead ends and long driveways take turnarounds and turnouts.
  Dead-end access over 150 feet requires an approved turnaround (a 96-foot cul-de-sac or a 120-foot hammerhead among the approved shapes); over 500 feet, a 26-foot driving surface; any access road over 400 feet requires 10-foot by 30-foot turnouts at intervals of 400 feet or less.
  Source: Oregon Fire Code Appendix D (2025 edition, as above).

Locally adopted road standards supersede the fire code's.
  Where a county or city adopts road specifications and standards by charter, plan or ordinance, those standards prevail over the fire code's road provisions.
  Source: ORS 368.039.

A statutory way of necessity is capped and conditioned.
  Maximum 30-foot width without county authorization; twelve statutory conditions, including least-damage routing, no traffic-hazard connection, and no other available legal access.
  Source: ORS 376.180; ORS 376.150 to 376.200.
```

## 3.9 Utilities and solar access

**Simple**

Every utility the parcel lacks becomes a cost of the house. Those numbers are large. Bringing power from the road can cost more than the land. One new transformer alone runs $5,000 to $25,000. Internet, cell signal and gas each have a provider, and only the provider's written answer counts. Sunlight counts as a service too, because the solar panels need a clear view of the low winter sun. On the site, a sun-path instrument shows which trees and ridges block it, month by month.

**Applied**

| System | Confirm in writing |
|---|---|
| Electrical service | Distance to the nearest transformer or service point, the extension estimate, which utility serves the parcel (PGE, Pacific Power, a cooperative), service-entrance and metering requirements, available voltage and ampacity |
| Water | Municipal water available, or a well required. For an existing well, yield, recovery and water-quality results, nearby well depths and yields, and the legal water-use limits |
| Wastewater | Sewer available, or whether the parcel has passed or can pass a septic site evaluation |
| Internet | Which providers serve the exact address, the deliverable speed rather than the advertised speed, and the installation cost |
| Road access | Public or private, who maintains it, year-round truck access, bridge and culvert weight limits, overhead clearance |
| Natural gas | Available and at what cost. This design may not need it, and the answer affects future options and resale |
| Cellular service | Coverage at the building site rather than the property line, for more than one carrier |

1. Get a written quote from each provider. Coverage maps and listing descriptions are not quotes.
2. Run PGE's or Pacific Power's online residential line-extension estimator for the parcel's distance to the nearest line.
3. Where a cooperative serves the parcel, get its board-adopted extension policy in writing.
4. Budget electrical extensions at $15 to $30 per foot overhead and $20 to $50 per foot underground.
5. Add $5,000 to $25,000 where a new transformer is needed.
6. Budget the internet drop from free to $1,000 to $10,000 and more, by provider.
7. Run NREL's PVWatts calculator (pvwatts.nrel.gov) for the location's baseline production.
8. Bring a Solar Pathfinder or an equivalent phone application to the site visit and record the shading reading.
9. Save names, dates and the written answers in the project binder.

**Professional**

Utility feasibility sets early geometry. Service routes, transformer location and trenching paths shape the site layout. The water source drives pump, pressure, storage, treatment and backup-power planning, and wastewater feasibility can move the house before the floor plan exists.

PGE and Pacific Power each apply a tariffed residential allowance credit against the extension cost, and both exclude trenching, conduit and vault costs for underground service from the allowance. At least one investor-owned utility's terms exclude designated high-risk wildfire areas from certain new-home extension provisions, which matters on the forested parcels this search may consider. Pioneer Electric Cooperative extends up to a quarter mile at no cost and charges $6.50 per foot beyond it. A first-length-free pattern of 200 to 500 feet is common across cooperatives.

Grid extension and an off-grid solar-plus-battery system compare on lifecycle cost. The comparison needs two inputs that exist only after a parcel and an appliance-and-heating design are chosen. They are the utility's written extension quote and the household's itemized load calculation. On the grid side, the method sums the net extension cost and the discounted stream of monthly service and energy charges over the comparison period. On the solar side it sums the system capital cost, sized in PVWatts against the site's measured solar resource and the real load. Battery and inverter replacements at their service intervals are added.

Western Oregon's winter solar resource caps what any siting decision can deliver. From November through February the sun shines about 33 percent of the time. Pacific Northwest passive-solar guidance puts the realistic ceiling at a 20 to 30 percent heating-load reduction, against 40 to 60 percent in sunnier cold climates. For photovoltaics the working standard is the measured shading-loss percentage from the site reading rather than an hours-of-sun count. Output falls in proportion to how much of the sun's path the trees and terrain block.

The Solar Pathfinder is a reflective dome over a printed sun-path diagram, readable in any weather and any season, and PVWatts supplies the climate baseline it cannot. The sizing method is in the solar chapter, the design response to the winter sun in the passive design chapter, and the staging detail in the build-sequence chapter.

At the feasibility pass the site is also checked as a construction site:

- a concrete truck can enter, position and turn around without backing into traffic
- a pump truck can set up where its boom reaches the work
- a lowboy trailer can deliver an excavator and compaction equipment
- staged material (block, rebar, roofing, tanks, windows) has a safe spot that does not block the road
- emergency vehicles can reach the house

Parcels that look excellent from the air can still be poor construction sites. Permit filing is online in much of the state, where participating jurisdictions process land-use, building, electrical, plumbing and mechanical permits through Oregon ePermitting (oregon.gov/bcd/epermitting).

**Code**

```
Utility extensions follow each utility's filed policy.
  Investor-owned utilities extend under tariffs filed with the Public Utility Commission (PGE Schedule 300, Pacific Power's line-extension policy); cooperatives extend under board-adopted policies.
  Source: the serving utility's filed line-extension schedule or adopted policy.

Driveway connections to a public road need an approach permit.
  Sight distance, grade and drainage requirements apply at the connection; county road departments permit county roads and ODOT permits state highways.
  Source: ORS chapter 374 (state highways); the county road department's approach permit under its road ordinance.
```

## 3.10 Septic and water feasibility

**Simple**

Can a septic system legally go on this land? That question comes before any rural purchase, because a failed evaluation means no dwelling can be built there. Some soils drain too slowly, some too fast, and some parcels cannot hold any approved system. The test that answers it costs about $500 to $1,200. At the extremes the result swings the budget by $42,000, from an $8,000 standard system to a $50,000 engineered one. Soil, system type and installer pricing set the spread. Water gets the same treatment before closing, with a well log, a yield test and a lab report.

**Applied**

1. Open the NRCS Web Soil Survey (websoilsurvey.nrcs.usda.gov) and draw the parcel boundary.
2. Read each mapped soil unit's septic-absorption limitation rating.
3. Treat "severe" as a warning before paying for an evaluation, and "slight" or "moderate" as encouraging only.
4. Check the same data on GEOHub's SoilWeb layer and any county soils layer, as in Polk County.
5. Walk the site for standing water, water-loving plants, rock outcrops, shallow bedrock and steep slope.
6. Request existing septic records from DEQ's online tool (Curry, Jackson, Josephine) or from the county office elsewhere.
7. When sewer is unavailable, make the offer contingent on a satisfactory site evaluation by the local onsite-program authority.
8. Read the evaluation for the drainage rate, the primary system's area, and the reserve area.
9. Read it for the required system type (standard gravity, pressure distribution, sand filter, or alternative treatment technology).
10. Read it for the setbacks from wells, surface water, slopes, property lines and structures.
11. Check that the system fits alongside the planned house, driveway, drainage and future outbuildings.
12. When municipal water is unavailable, work the well table below before closing.

| Item | Source |
|---|---|
| Existing well log | OWRD records (oregon.gov/owrd) |
| Well yield and recovery | The log, or a new drawdown test |
| Static water level and drawdown | The log, or a new test |
| Water quality | A recent test from an accredited Oregon laboratory |
| Nearby well depths and yields | The OWRD database for adjacent parcels |
| Legal water-use limits | Oregon water-rights records and OWRD guidance |
| Setback conflicts | Local septic, well and building setback rules |

13. Draw house, driveway, septic primary and reserve, well, utilities and drainage on one site sketch.
14. Place water and wastewater first and fit the house into what remains.

**Professional**

Septic feasibility is a layout problem. Soil facts (texture, percolation, the limiting layer of clay, water table or rock, and seasonal high groundwater) have to fit with the slope, the drainfield size, the reserve area and the setbacks on one plan. Water feasibility joins the same plan with well yield, pump curve, static level, drawdown, storage, treatment, pressure, freeze protection and backup power. The DEQ records tool also holds pre-June-2025 records for Baker, Union and Wallowa counties. Because the soil survey's mapping scale spans acres, a clean map result is a screen and not a pass.

### The septic ladder

Site evaluations produce numbers. The evaluator's soil group and effective soil depth read directly into the trench-length tables. Shallower soil and finer texture mean more linear feet of absorption trench per unit of daily flow, three and a half times more between the best and worst table positions. A permanent water table close beneath the trench bottom rules out the standard trench designs entirely. The rule fixes that depth and halves it where DEQ has designated an area on the strength of a groundwater study.

From there the system escalates in a fixed order. Gravity trenches come first, then pressurized distribution (dosed, even distribution across the trench). Next come capping fill or a mound (imported fill that adds vertical separation), then a sand filter (an engineered pretreatment bed) or an aerobic treatment unit. Construction cost rises through that order. DEQ's permit fee schedule tiers its fees the same way, standard trenches and their variants lowest, alternative treatment technologies, capping fill and pressurized distribution next, sand filters highest. The fee increase of July 1, 2026 applies across the schedule.

Graywater diversion does not shrink the drainfield. Oregon permits graywater reuse as its own DEQ program in three tiers, and the program keeps a permitted full-size disposal system connected. So the diversion is parallel plumbing rather than a design-flow credit. County evaluators answer in writing, parcel by parcel, whether a graywater system or a composting toilet in the design earns a reduced design flow at evaluation time. Oregon's rules do contemplate toilet-type differences, since self-contained nonwater-carried systems have their own smaller separation table.

| System | Cost range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Septic site evaluation | $500 to $1,200 | The contingency test. The range extremes below differ by $42,000 |
| Standard gravity septic | $8,000 to $15,000 | Suitable soils, gravity drainfield |
| Pressure-distribution septic | $12,000 to $25,000 | Marginal soils needing even distribution |
| Sand filter or alternative treatment technology | $20,000 to $50,000 | Difficult soils or shallow groundwater |
| Standard well (Willamette Valley) | $8,000 to $20,000 | Shallow water table, moderate depth |
| Deeper well (Cascade foothills) | $15,000 to $45,000 | Greater depth, harder rock |
| Storage and pump system | $3,000 to $10,000 | Storage tank, pressure tank, pump, plumbing |
| Water treatment | $1,500 to $8,000 and more | Iron, manganese, hardness, bacteria |

Two ongoing obligations affect the purchase decision, and both attach to the alternative systems rather than to gravity trenches: the certified-provider service contract, and the manufacturer service an aerobic unit needs on top of it. So a difficult-soil parcel obligates the household to a permanent service contract on top of the construction cost. Owners may install their own system under their own DEQ construction permit and may drill their own well under a Landowner Well Permit. State drinking-water rules reach a well only when it serves more than the owner's household.

### The well and the low-yield fix

Low-yield wells have a standard fix. A storage cistern, commonly 300 to 5,000 gallons, with about 1,100 gallons circulating in industry sources as a lending-standard minimum, fills slowly from the well. A booster pump then pressurizes the house from the cistern, which decouples the recharge rate from peak demand, with run-dry protection on both pumps.

Precast concrete tanks add a delivery constraint. Tanks of roughly 1,500 to 5,000 gallons ride a standard flatbed and a truck crane that needs a firm, level pad. Single tanks past roughly 5,000 to 10,000 gallons need a larger crane and a wider, taller route. A narrow rural driveway rules those out in favor of cast-in-place or several smaller manifolded tanks. A regional precast supplier quotes the crossover against the measured driveway.

Component sizing is in the water independence chapter, and the maintenance-contract mechanics are in the plumbing and documentation chapters. Septic primary and reserve areas stay clear of outbuildings, parking, stored material and landscaping for the life of the system.

## 3.11 Water rights and the exempt well

**Simple**

Oregon's water belongs to the public, and the right to use it comes from the state. Ordinary house wells fit an exemption: no permit and no water right, up to 15,000 gallons a day for a household. That exemption has categories, and in a few basins the state has closed or limited it. Rain off your roof is exempt entirely. Springs and ponds follow their own rules. So the four sources are four separate legal questions, and the answer to each is checked before you count on the water.

**Applied**

1. Check the parcel against OWRD's Groundwater Administrative Areas map and table, fresh at search time.
2. Pull nearby well logs from OWRD's Well Report Query at apps.wrd.state.or.us. Logs go back to 1955.
3. Compare each log's depth, flow and static level, and drilling dates against depths.
4. Where newer wells run deeper than older neighbors, consult a hydrogeologist before waiving contingencies.
5. Write the exempt-use filing into the drilling contract: the log and tax-lot map to OWRD within 30 days, $300 fee.
6. Keep copies of the filed log and map in the project binder.
7. For a spring, establish whether its water ever leaves the property under natural conditions.
8. For a pond, check the thresholds before design: 9.2 acre-feet of storage and a 10-foot dam.
9. Treat a planned rural accessory dwelling as closed where the state has restricted exempt wells.

**Professional**

ORS 537.545 is the exemption statute, and the household's claim rests on its domestic category, which covers a single dwelling or a group of them on one well. Down-hole heat exchange, stockwatering, a small lawn or garden, school grounds in critical areas and a narrow reused-water category round out the list.

The categories are legally separate. A household with a future accessory dwelling draws on the domestic allowance as group domestic use, and a personal workshop counts as domestic-incidental use. A block operation that becomes a commercial manufacturing activity falls into the separate industrial-commercial category, which is capped lower and is legally distinct. Where domestic use ends and industrial use begins for a home workshop is a classification OWRD makes case by case. If block production goes commercial, the classification is confirmed in writing before the well is designed.

Commercial garden excludes cannabis-family plants unless the grower is licensed under ORS 571.281. A temporary carve-out bars the commercial-garden exemption in the Lower Umatilla Basin Groundwater Management Area until January 2, 2028.

Inside a designated Critical Groundwater Area the exempt-well assumption can fail, because each area's own administrative rule can restrict, condition or close new exempt uses. The designated inventory includes four Umatilla Basin areas (Ordnance Gravel, Ordnance Basalt, Butter Creek and Stage Gulch), Cow Valley, The Dalles and Cooper-Bull Mountain. In December 2025 the Water Resources Commission added portions of the Harney Basin after a contested rulemaking. Mitigation programs run separately in the Deschutes, Grande Ronde and Klamath basins. In the Deschutes program under OAR 690-505, a new groundwater right proceeds by acquiring mitigation credits sold through the Deschutes River Conservancy. The Conservancy and OWRD quote the price directly.

Decline records justify the screening. Harney Basin groundwater has fallen more than 100 feet in places and drops up to 8 feet a year in the worst areas. About 70 residential and stock wells there are already dry, and irrigation takes about 97 percent of the basin's groundwater. An OWRD memorandum counts about 1,220 Oregon wells that went dry or fell below pump capacity between June 2021 and December 2023. Oregon has no mandatory dry-well reporting, so the absence of reported failures near a parcel proves nothing. WaterWatch of Oregon, an advocacy group, names the Deschutes, Umatilla, Klamath and Harney basins as the over-allocation pattern.

Rainwater has the simplest legal status of the four sources. Because the exemption turns only on the surface being artificial and impervious, with no cap and no limit on what else the structure is for, a purpose-built collection roof sits inside its plain text. OWRD's reading of such a roof is confirmed in writing before one is built. Combining sources adds no water-rights filing. It does add a plumbing requirement. An auxiliary source beside a well takes a reduced-pressure-principle backflow assembly on the service connection, under the plumbing code and the Oregon Health Authority's cross-connection rules. The assembly keeps the sources from contaminating each other.

Springs are classified by their outflow. Secondary legal sources report that a spring whose water, under natural conditions, forms no channel and never leaves the property can be developed without a standard permit. OWRD confirms the specific spring's status before it is priced into the offer. Where the water leaves the property, it is public surface water that takes a standard permit, with first-in-time priority. Springs above the house deliver by gravity through any power outage. Whether that outweighs other site factors is the owner's decision, and battery planning narrows the margin. Spring development (cutoff wall, collection bed, spring box, overflow, service line) is quoted by a spring-development contractor against a driller's quote for the same parcel.

The pre-1995 registered-pond exemption is closed to new construction, so a new pond takes a reservoir permit under one of two processes, with OWRD's guidebook of June 2024 as the application manual for the simpler one. A pond remains a workable supplemental source for irrigation, stock and fire reserve.

| Source | Legal treatment | The check before purchase |
|---|---|---|
| Drilled well | Exempt categories under statute, a permit beyond them | Administrative-area map, neighboring well logs |
| Developed spring | Exempt where no water flows off the property under natural conditions (OWRD confirms), a surface-water permit where it does | The outflow question, answered on the ground and by OWRD |
| Roof or impervious-surface rainwater | Fully exempt, no cap | None on the rights side. Plumbing rules apply at the house |
| Pond or reservoir | Reservoir permit required | The storage and dam-height thresholds |

The production-water estimate is in the owner-made blocks chapter, the treatment train and storage sizing in the water independence chapter, the battery margin in the solar chapter, and the fire reserve and stored-water plan in the resilience chapter. Neighbor-to-neighbor water sharing in a dry-well emergency depends on proximity and existing relationships. A remote parcel has fewer potential helpers within hauling distance, so stored water is the dependable answer.

## 3.12 Hazard and environmental screening

**Simple**

Free public maps screen a parcel for six hazards, from landslides and floods to wetlands and old contamination. Every screen costs nothing and takes minutes. These maps under-report, and their publishers say so. On its first release the landslide map found a quarter of the slides at most. The wetland map misses the small, seasonal and wooded ones. So a clean map result is only a screen. Whatever a map flags goes to a paid specialist before closing. Some checks happen whatever the map shows, a wetland delineation on wooded ground among them.

**Applied**

| Hazard | Free screen | Paid follow-up when flagged |
|---|---|---|
| Landslide | DOGAMI's SLIDO map, through Oregon HazVu or Oregon GEOHub. Read the inventory layer (mapped past slides) and the susceptibility layer (Low, Moderate, High, Very High) separately | An inventory hit, or High or Very High susceptibility, sends the parcel to a licensed geotechnical engineer or engineering geologist before closing. Match the quoted scope (desktop review, borings, slope-stability analysis) to the flag |
| Flood | FEMA's Map Service Center (msc.fema.gov), by address, immediate | An elevation certificate from a licensed surveyor on FEMA's form, roughly $600 to $2,700 in 5 to 10 business days. A site above the base flood elevation supports a Letter of Map Amendment removing the parcel from the mapped zone |
| Wildfire | The Oregon Wildfire Risk Explorer on Oregon GEOHub (OSU-maintained), an awareness tool rather than a regulatory assignment, plus the U.S. Forest Service's Wildfire Risk to Communities tool | The county's or fire district's defensible-space adoption status at defensiblespace.oregon.gov, and an insurance quote for the parcel |
| Tsunami, for a coastal or estuarine candidate | DOGAMI's Tsunami Inundation Map plates for the segment, local Cascadia and distant-source scenarios, with the companion evacuation walk-time map | A field check of the mapped walking route against the real terrain. In a local Cascadia event the first wave can arrive in minutes, before any warning |
| Wetlands | The National Wetlands Inventory and Oregon's Statewide Wetlands Inventory | A professional wetland delineation, routine on any parcel with forest cover, seasonal wet areas or drainage features, whatever the map shows |
| Contamination | DEQ's Your DEQ Online records portal, for the parcel and its neighbors, since plumes and tank leaks cross lot lines | A Phase 1 Environmental Site Assessment (a consultant's records-and-site review) on any hit or ambiguous old record |
| Orchard legacy, in fruit country | The parcel's history in the Rogue Valley around Medford, Central Point, Phoenix, Talent and Ashland, where former orchards hold lead, arsenic, DDT and its breakdown products, and dieldrin, and in the Hood River Valley at smaller scale | Consultant soil sampling before purchase |

1. Run every screen in the table on every serious candidate, in the first days of the inspection period.
2. Where the county offers an integrated GIS, as Deschutes County's DIAL does, start there.
3. Walk the ground for what no database holds.
4. Look for old dump sites in gullies and fence corners.
5. Look for a tank filler pipe or vent stub near an old barn, and for stained or dead vegetation around it.
6. Look for unnaturally regular pits or tailings from old prospecting, and for third-party dumping near the road frontage.
7. Send any of those finds to the Phase 1 assessment as well.

**Professional**

Each tool's own error statement limits what a clean result proves. DOGAMI states that SLIDO is not for site-specific planning and that its first release located between 0 and 25 percent of actual landslides at any location. Later releases improved coverage unevenly. DSL states that the wetlands inventory under-represents smaller, seasonal and forested wetlands and excludes certain farmed wetlands by federal-definition policy. Outside the coastal zone, which was reflown between 2001 and 2011, it rests mostly on mid-1980s imagery. County GIS parcel data is maintained for tax administration and is screening-grade rather than survey-grade. DEQ's online records portal replaced the ECSI cleanup database on April 16, 2024, and DEQ describes it as working information with unconfirmed and incomplete records.

Wetland buffers are local law, so the width on a given parcel is in the city or county ordinance and nowhere else. Adopted widths in documented examples run from 50 feet in Talent to 200 feet on wetlands and fish-bearing streams in the Columbia River Gorge National Scenic Area. Eugene writes its own overlay standards into its city code. Buyers get the jurisdiction's adopted buffer from its planning department, or file a Wetland Land Use Notification with DSL, which answers within 30 days. Deschutes County's DIAL overlays parcels, zoning, wildfire and wetland layers in one map with downloadable data. Where the target county offers nothing comparable, the state and federal tools above are assembled one by one.

Wildfire insurability is a purchase factor separate from construction cost. The Oregon FAIR Plan is the insurer of last resort and pays actual cash value rather than replacement cost. Qualifying for it means the standard market declined the property. Oregon insurers must disclose whether they consider mitigation in underwriting, and no current rule requires a discount. In 2026 a legislative proposal (LC 182) would, if enacted, mandate mitigation credits.

Two figures are documented today. IBHS Wildfire Prepared Home certification supports carrier discounts of roughly 10 to 20 percent, with the certification inspection at $150 to $300 and 2 to 3 hours. Independent-agent reporting describes specialty carriers pricing documented defensible-space and hardening work 15 to 30 percent below unmitigated baselines. This house's planned construction (noncombustible concrete walls, a Class A roof assembly, ember-resistant detailing) maps onto the scored hardening categories. Real quotes against a real parcel and design settle the credit.

Oregon's tsunami construction statute does not reach a detached house. It restricts major structures and buildings in Tsunami Risk Category III or IV. A single-family dwelling is Risk Category II under the structural code's risk table, whatever home occupation or accessory structure it carries. DOGAMI maps the inundation zone under ORS 455.446, and the inundation plates and walk-time maps are the siting tools.

Former-orchard cleanup for redevelopment runs through DEQ's published pathways (January 2025 guidance). The Independent Cleanup Pathway with technical consultation takes a $1,500 deposit and a 45-day review of a complete application. The Voluntary Cleanup Program takes a $5,000 deposit and up to 180 days for project-manager assignment where contaminated soil is managed on site. A Solid Waste Letter Authorization, for off-site soil disposal outside a permitted landfill, costs a $500 fee and about 45 days. Per-sample laboratory pricing depends on the consultant and the sampling plan, so it is quoted. Phase 1 assessments distinguish a recognized environmental condition from cosmetic old debris and recommend Phase 2 sampling where warranted.

## 3.13 Topography, drainage and buildability

**Simple**

Flat, dry land costs the least to build on. Slope and wetness add cost at every step: a bigger excavation, retaining walls, drains, and a driveway that has to climb. Water aimed at the house from uphill has to be caught and led around it. On a still winter night a valley floor can run 5 to 15 degrees colder than the hill above it. The heating design has to know that. None of this ends a purchase on its own. It changes the design and the budget. You price the change before the offer, from a walk on the ground after rain.

**Applied**

1. Use the topographic map and the county GIS contours before the visit. For early terrain work, use the free USGS 1/3 arc-second elevation model (usgs.gov).
2. Walk the site after rain.
3. Mark where surface water flows and where it pools.
4. Mark soft ground, springs, and erosion along driveway routes, swales and slopes.
5. Mark low spots near the planned building area and any uphill water aimed at the future house.
6. Look for signs of old grading or fill (different soil colors, buried debris, abandoned terraces, rip-rap).
7. Look for root systems and vegetation that suggest seasonal saturation, and drought-stressed plants on dry slopes.
8. Check for room for excavator setup, concrete delivery, pump-truck position and staging.
9. Trust the walk over the map. A contour line does not show standing water.
10. Ask the neighbors for the local flood history. Land outside the mapped zone can still flood in an extreme event.

**Professional**

Topography sets the cut-and-fill volume, the drainage strategy, the retaining-wall demand and the driveway grade. It also sets the erosion-control plan, the staging areas, the waterproofing demand and the foundation complexity. On a contour map a simple rectangular footprint can become stepped footings, tall retaining walls, imported fill, difficult compaction, and a driveway that fails every winter. Finished grade has to fall away from the foundation under the residential code, so a flat, wet lot takes imported fill to make the fall.

### Patterns that change the design

South-facing slopes receive winter solar gain, and north-facing slopes are colder, darker and slower to dry. Valley and basin sites pool cold air on still winter nights and run 5 to 15 °F colder than the regional design value. That changes heating sizing and freeze protection. Ridgelines take more wind, which raises lateral loads and infiltration, and valleys take less wind but pool cold air and smoke. Terraces, rip-rap and odd contours mean prior earthwork, and the geotechnical investigation resolves what sits at footing depth before footings bear on it.

### Clearing, grading and retaining walls

Clearing and grading the building pad has planning-range pricing from national contractor guides, none of it a bid. Lightly forested clearing runs about $733 to $2,333 per acre and heavily forested $3,395 to $6,155, with a Portland-area report spanning $610 to $5,000. Light grading runs $1,000 to $3,000 per acre, moderate grading $3,000 to $7,000, and steep ground above $7,000.

Building-pad preparation itself runs about $15,800 to $44,535 per acre. For a moderately forested, moderately sloped pad holding house, shop and block-yard on one to two disturbed acres, the total points to $10,000 to $40,000 or more. Tree density, slope, rock and haul distance drive it. An excavation contractor's bid against the real pad layout replaces every figure above.

Retaining-wall permits turn on where the height is measured, at the footing's underside rather than at grade, so a wall that looks short from the yard side can be a permit wall. Surcharge means any load beyond the retained earth itself, such as a driveway's vehicle and pavement load, a building pad, stacked block-yard material or parking. The code does not quantify the surcharge trigger, so the local building official applies it case by case under the jurisdiction's ordinance. The geotechnical work and the wall's separate engineering triggers are in the soil chapter.

Staging is part of feasibility for a concrete house, because trucks, pump setup, excavation, reinforcement and waterproofing all need physical room that a steep or constrained site may lack.

## 3.14 The site plan, in order of placement

**Simple**

Your buildable area shrinks fast once the fixed things are placed. Where the septic system can go is a soil fact, and the well keeps its distance from the drainfield. Hazard zones subtract ground, and the driveway enters where the road and the grade allow. So the plan locks elements from least movable to most movable, and the house takes what remains. When two fixed elements need the same patch of ground, the parcel fails this screen. One more patch stays open on purpose: the sun's path south of the future solar panels.

**Applied**

1. Lock the septic primary and reserve areas first. The reserve is an equal second footprint, mandatory, and the evaluator's qualifying soil is wherever it is.
2. Lock the well second, where water is likely and 100 feet from the absorption and reserve areas and 50 feet from the tank.
3. Work well and septic as one pair. Drilling first can waste good soil, and siting septic first can waste good water.
4. Subtract the floodplain, the wetland buffer and landslide-susceptible ground from the buildable envelope before anything else is drawn.
5. Fix the driveway entry, any culvert crossing, the achievable grade and the fire turnaround. Construction traffic reaches nothing until the driveway exists.
6. Fit the house inside what remains, 10 feet from the absorption and reserve areas and 5 feet from the tank. Keep the well clearances, and face the south wall to the sun.
7. Reserve the sun-path envelope south of the array location now. A tree or outbuilding placed there later shades the array for its whole life.
8. Fill the remaining ground last with the shop, the block-yard and the outbuildings.
9. On rural residential land where an accessory dwelling may come later, keep the ground within about 100 feet of the house clear of outbuildings.
10. Run a spare power, water and septic stub toward that zone while the trenches are open.
11. On a farm- or forest-deferred parcel, draw the homesite envelope (house, driveway, septic, disturbed yard) as compactly as the county's minimum-homesite rules allow.
12. Where a severed mineral estate exists, use the same compact, central envelope.

**Professional**

Block-yards are open pads rather than buildings, so the foundation setback from the drainfield does not bind one numerically. What binds it is keeping its traffic and material loading off the absorption and reserve soil. Accessory-structure setbacks from property lines are zoning, county by county. Of the well's clearances, the legal floor from a structure is a fraction of the room a drilling rig and a pump-service truck need. The rule's own power-pole recommendation signals the workable clearance.

No agency publishes a mandatory site-plan sequence. The order above follows from which constraints are fixed by soil, water, maps and code and which are the owner's to arrange. On farm or forest land the accessory-dwelling radius does not apply, because a second dwelling there is its own approval, resolved before any layout depends on it. Retrofitting utilities to a spot chosen years later costs more than a stub run while the trenches are open. On a deferred parcel, the smaller the homesite carve-out, the less acreage leaves special assessment.

Acreage arithmetic is an estimate with stated assumptions. A typical primary-plus-reserve septic footprint runs a fifth to a half acre before its setback rings, per general septic guidance. General homesteading guidance puts 0.75 to 1.5 acres as the floor for septic plus well alone, with the reserve requirement pushing toward the high end. Below roughly 2 acres, fitting house, septic primary and reserve, well separations and a block-yard without overlap gets tight on rolling terrain. Five acres and up gives working room for the project's full footprint. Soil quality, wetland buffers and slope move the number parcel by parcel. The rural accessory dwelling option also needs 2 acres, which makes 2 acres a floor twice over.

Block-yards need contiguous gentle ground. A working convention for a hand-and-wheelbarrow production pad is a grade under roughly 5 to 8 percent, a convention rather than a tested threshold. At the screening level the test is what level ground remains after the higher-priority subtractions. A parcel with none left fails the block-yard plan before any exact area target exists.

One permit exemption shapes early outbuilding planning. Under the 2026 residential code, a one-story detached accessory structure not intended for sleeping or permanent cooking needs no building permit up to 200 square feet of building or floor area and 15 feet of height. On a parcel of 2 acres or more the area limit rises to 400 square feet. In the 2023 edition the larger limit also required a 20-foot setback from property lines and regulated structures, a condition the 2026 text no longer states.

Nonhabitable membrane-covered frame structures up to 500 square feet, one story and 3 feet or more from any lot line, are exempt as well. A fabric-covered hoop shelter over a curing yard is one. The pad-grade decision and the full yard layout are in the site-development chapter, and the exemption entry is in the legal chapter. The exemption waives the building permit only, and zoning setbacks and any electrical, plumbing or mechanical permits still apply.

## 3.15 Distance and logistics

**Simple**

Distance adds a daily time cost that continues long after the purchase. Every mile between the parcel and a hardware store adds time to material runs, deliveries, inspections and emergency response. Past about 30 minutes from town, a missing fitting costs half a day. Past an hour, the ambulance can take more than 30 minutes to arrive. Land gets cheaper and more private with each band, with fewer zoning limits. You check the route itself in winter, because summer condition does not predict winter condition on a rural road.

**Applied**

1. Drive the route to the parcel before committing, and again in poor weather.
2. Time the hardware run, the grocery run and the drive to medical care.
3. Ask the fire district and the ambulance service for their response time to the parcel.
4. Note steep grades, sharp curves, and bridge or culvert weight limits on the route.
5. Check overhead clearance, width and weight limits for a concrete truck and a pump truck.
6. Check internet and cellular coverage along the route, for more than one carrier.
7. Find the serving building department in the Building Codes Division's Local Building Department Directory (oregon.gov/bcd/lbdd).
8. Call it before the offer. Ask for permit turnaround, staffing, and how inspections are scheduled around a pour.

**Professional**

Distance affects labor productivity, subcontractor willingness, delivery cost, inspection scheduling, emergency response, utility options, the resale pool and owner fatigue. The more remote the site, the more staging discipline matters: spare fittings, backup tools, weather protection, temporary power and water, fuel storage, and tightly scheduled deliveries.

| Distance to hardware and services | Effect on the build |
|---|---|
| Under 15 minutes | Easy runs, fast emergency response, higher land cost, less privacy. Zoning is tighter near towns, a correlation rather than an effect of the distance itself |
| 15 to 30 minutes | Reasonable runs, medium land cost, reasonable privacy, buildable zoning in most cases |
| 30 to 60 minutes | Every missing fitting costs a half-day, cheaper land, real privacy, more flexible zoning |
| Over 60 minutes | Plan around scarce trips and keep a deep inventory of fasteners and consumables. Emergency response can pass 30 minutes. Cheapest land, most flexibility |

Building-department service varies with the serving office. Oregon jurisdictions administer the building program locally, through the Building Codes Division directly, or through a contracted private firm. The Directory lists each department's contacts, building official and permit services without naming which arrangement applies, so the arrangement is asked for on the call. A concrete pour needs an inspector on a specific day, which makes the department's scheduling flexibility a site-selection fact. The three administration structures are described in the compliance-map chapter. For an owner-builder on a fixed schedule, distance changes staging and inventory planning rather than blocking a purchase.

## 3.16 Farm and forest special assessment and the rollback tax

**Simple**

Rural parcels in farm or forest use are taxed under special assessment, a reduced property-tax value tied to that use. That reduction ends when the qualifying use ends. Back taxes then come due for the past five or ten years, depending on the program and the zone. Whoever owns the land at the disqualifying act gets the bill. Building a house is such an act for the ground under and around it. Sellers who collected the benefit owe nothing. Your county assessor keeps a running figure for the exposure, and you ask for it before the offer.

**Applied**

1. Ask the county assessor for the parcel's current potential-additional-tax figure before the offer. It is a maintained roll notation.
2. Price the parcel on its market value, with no assumed discount for the deferral.
3. Plan the homesite carve-out in the site plan. The dwelling disqualifies the homesite acreage at minimum, and a compact envelope keeps the remainder qualified.
4. Where the seller should bear the rollback, write it into the contract as an indemnity or price adjustment.
5. Disclose the assessment on the standard Special Tax Assessment Addendum, OREF Form 2.22 in practice.
6. For a Small Tract Forestland parcel, confirm the program's two-track rule with the assessor before modeling the exposure.

**Professional**

Four acts disqualify, per the Department of Revenue's farm-use guidance and county assessor practice. They are a sale or transfer where qualifying use ends, the land ceasing qualifying use (including failing an applicable income test), recording a subdivision or partition plat for the platted portion, and the owner's own request. A transfer alone does not disqualify while qualifying use continues. The assessment and its potential-additional-tax notation run with the land. So a contract clause can shift the economic burden to the seller and cannot change the taxpayer the county collects from. Building a dwelling disqualifies at minimum the homesite.

Homesite treatment is statutory rather than assessor grace, which is why the carve-out can be planned: the house pulls its own ground out of the program and leaves the rest where it was. The rollback attaches where a nonfarm dwelling is established under ORS 215.236 in an EFU zone. A partition that carves the building site off a larger deferred tract works the same way, with the remainder keeping its qualifying use. The process is in the partition section.

Program economics differ. Designated forestland is assessed at 100 percent of forestland special value. Small Tract Forestland is assessed at 20 percent of that value, with an 80 percent severance tax due at harvest instead. The lookback doubles for EFU farmland that is still outside a growth boundary once disqualified. Small Tract Forestland splits by destination, with the longer window where the land moves into another qualifying program and the shorter on a full exit. Because the tax counts only the years the land qualified, a parcel enrolled for three years carries three years of exposure. One enrolled for decades carries the full window.

**Code**

```
The farm-use rollback lookback is ten or five years.
  10 years for EFU-zone farmland remaining outside an urban growth boundary after disqualification; 5 years for non-EFU farmland, for designated forestland in either region, and for EFU farmland inside a UGB.
  Source: ORS 308A.703(3).

The additional tax is the back difference between two valuations.
  For each year in the lookback window, the difference between the tax assessed under special assessment and the tax that would have been assessed without it, for the lesser of the statutory maximum or the consecutive years the land qualified.
  Source: ORS 308A.703.

Small Tract Forestland has its own two-track lookback.
  10 years where the disqualified land remains in another qualifying special-assessment program; 5 years where it exits special assessment entirely.
  Source: ORS 308A.707(2)(b), (3)(a)(B).

A homesite disqualification does not take down the tract.
  When a homesite is separated or disqualified, the remaining qualifying portion of the parcel continues to be valued under its special assessment; in most homesite cases no additional tax is imposed at disqualification.
  Source: ORS 308A.259; ORS 308A.253.
```

## 3.17 The purchase contract and contingencies

**Simple**

Contingency clauses are your walk-away rights. Each lets you exit before a stated deadline if a named report comes back wrong. Oregon courts enforce one on your own genuine dissatisfaction, and you do not have to prove that dissatisfaction reasonable. Each right disappears if its deadline passes without written action from you. Price renegotiation after a bad report exists only if the contract says so. Otherwise the choice is the agreed price or the exit. Earnest money, the deposit you forfeit or recover under those terms, runs 1 to 3 percent of the price.

**Applied**

1. Tie each contingency to a named report or event, such as approval of the septic site evaluation report.
2. Calendar every contingency deadline the day the contract signs. Missing one waives the contingency and accepts the condition.
3. Write the price-renegotiation option in expressly. Give it a stated window to request a credit or seller-funded remediation on a named report, with the walk-away right kept if the seller declines.
4. Set earnest money at the market pattern: 1 to 3 percent of price, or a flat $500 to $2,500 on lower-priced rural land.
5. In competition, raise the earnest money and offer closing-date flexibility before shortening the inspection period.
6. Where financing is involved, bring a fully underwritten pre-approval rather than a pre-qualification letter.
7. Keep the inspection period near the form's 10 business days. Competitive offers cut it to 5 to 7, and a shorter period is less protection by definition.
8. Have an Oregon real estate attorney draft the contingencies and any tiered structure. A tiered structure is a short initial walk-away window, then staged deadlines for financing, title and named reports.
9. On a resource-zoned parcel, make the contract contingent on the county's written dwelling-path confirmation and on verification of any Measure 49 order.
10. Set those deadlines to the county's real turnaround times.

**Professional**

Zygar v. Johnson (169 Or App 638, 10 P3d 326, 2000) is the tested case. A handwritten condition, "subject to purchaser's approval of a pest and dryrot report", let the buyer terminate on genuine dissatisfaction with the report. He also had unrelated reasons to exit, and that did not matter. The court applied a subjective good-faith standard, under which one contractual source of dissatisfaction makes other motives immaterial. Secondary summaries that describe an objective reasonable-basis standard are contradicted by the case text, and the subjective standard is the more buyer-protective rule. The handwritten condition also prevailed over the printed form in that case, which converts into drafting practice, since the specifically negotiated, named-report condition outperformed the form language.

Oregon contract law supplies no implied right to demand a price reduction on a marginal result. Without the clause, a marginal septic or well result leaves the two options the contingency states, proceed at the agreed price or terminate. Any renegotiation is then an out-of-contract request the seller is free to refuse.

"Sole and absolute discretion" clauses circulate in current templates, a Deschutes County purchase-and-sale template among them, and remain untested in published Oregon decisions. On a $30,000 parcel the earnest-money pattern is roughly $300 to $900, and on a $150,000 parcel $1,500 to $4,500. What price cut or credit an adverse septic, well or wetland finding wins is whatever the contingency text and the negotiation produce.

**Code**

```
Handwritten contract terms control printed terms.
  Where a contract mixes handwritten and printed provisions and they conflict, the handwritten language governs.
  Source: ORS 42.270.
```

## 3.18 The due-diligence window

**Simple**

Typical Oregon raw-land inspection periods run 30 to 60 days. Free screens run in the first days, so a bad parcel is eliminated before a dollar is spent. Slow, paid items start on day one in parallel, because the septic evaluation and the county's zoning letter take weeks each. Thirty days works only when both are ordered at once. By the last week every report is back and read against the severity bands. Each contingency is exercised or waived before its deadline.

**Applied**

| Days | Actions |
|---|---|
| 1 to 3 | Order the preliminary title report. In parallel run every free screen: county GIS (zoning, tax lot, overlays), the Measure 49 Analyzer where relevant, DOGAMI SLIDO, the FEMA flood lookup, the wetlands inventory, the OWRD well-log and administrative-area search, the NRCS soil survey, and Your DEQ Online for the parcel and its neighbors |
| 3 to 10 | Request the county zoning confirmation letter, a paid, multi-week item. Schedule the septic site evaluation with the onsite-program authority, the longest-lead paid item |
| 7 to 20 | Order the boundary survey where the legal description or the access is at all ambiguous. Commission a wetland delineation where the parcel holds forest cover, seasonal wet ground or drainage features, whatever the map showed |
| 15 to 30 | Get the well drawdown and yield test on an existing well, or a driller's opinion for a new one. Send every hazard flag to its professional: geotechnical for a SLIDO hit, an elevation certificate for a flood-zone hit, a Phase 1 assessment for an environmental hit |
| 30 to 45 and beyond | Read every returned report against the severity bands, renegotiate where the contract allows, and exercise or waive each contingency before its deadline |

For a tax-foreclosure or auction parcel with only the four-week notice window:

1. Pull the recorded-document chain yourself at the county clerk's index.
2. Run the OWRD well query, immediate and statewide.
3. Request county septic records, online for Curry, Jackson and Josephine and by direct request elsewhere.
4. Get a planner's phone confirmation of the zoning.
5. Treat all of it as screening short of title insurance and a real site evaluation.

**Professional**

Fixed-and-free comes first, and paid-and-slow starts in parallel. Every screening tool in the table is free and same-day, and any one of them can end the deal before a dollar is spent. Each carries the reliability limits stated in the hazard-screening section, so a clean free pass clears the parcel for paid follow-up and not for closing. The order shifts toward whichever risk is likeliest to end the specific deal. Paid items (septic evaluation, zoning letter, survey, delineation) turn around in one to a few weeks, which is what makes a 30-to-60-day window workable.

## 3.19 Financing and alternative purchase structures

**Simple**

Loans on raw land cost more than home loans. Down payments run higher, rates run higher, and fewer lenders offer them. Some ways of buying add a second problem years later. A land sale contract, where the seller keeps the deed until you have paid in full, leaves you without the title a construction lender needs. Buying with a sibling or a friend without a written agreement lets either of you force a sale through the courts. So the structure chosen at purchase decides how the house gets financed. You ask the lender and the title company before the offer.

**Applied**

1. Get land-loan quotes before the offer from lenders that work rural Oregon. AgWest Farm Credit, local credit unions and community banks handle most of it. farmcredit.com/states/oregon finds the county's association.
2. Expect a passed septic evaluation to serve as a lending gate.
3. For seller financing, read the preliminary title report for any underlying mortgage.
4. Require written disclosure of that mortgage's lender, balance and due-on-sale status.
5. Route payments through a licensed escrow or collection agent that verifies the underlying loan stays current.
6. Record the contract, or a memorandum of it, immediately.
7. In any land sale contract, include a written prepayment right without penalty and a stated tax-and-insurance payment procedure.
8. Include a default-cure procedure that references, and does not shorten, the statutory notice-and-cure timeline.
9. Where construction financing is a near-term goal, use a note and trust deed instead of a land sale contract.
10. Before bidding at a tax-foreclosure or sheriff sale, ask the title company whether it will insure the quitclaim chain.
11. Ask the construction lender whether it will lend on that chain, and whether a quiet-title action comes first. Budget the answer.
12. For a lease with purchase option, write every pre-option site activity into the landowner's consent: test pits, septic evaluation, any ground disturbance.
13. For co-ownership, sign a written agreement before the deed records.
14. Give it a buyout formula (appraisal plus first refusal), pro-rata expense sharing, a default remedy and an exit process.
15. Budget $10,000 to $15,000 for attorney drafting of that agreement, a general figure.
16. For a family transfer, bring the donor's purchase price and date, prior gift-tax filings and a current appraisal to a CPA.
17. Ask about the Form 709 threshold, carryover basis on a future sale, and whether an appraisal documents the transfer.

**Professional**

### Land loans

Institutional land-loan terms, from secondary-source snapshots rather than quotes, run to down payments of 20 to 50 percent, with raw unimproved land at 35 to 50 percent. Rates sit 1 to 3 percentage points above conventional mortgages, roughly 7.0 to 8.5 percent against a 5.875 percent conventional benchmark early in 2026. Development readiness, meaning confirmed septic, utilities and access, drives terms more than the zone label. Resource-zone dwelling restrictions reach underwriting through buildability.

AgWest Farm Credit markets land and construction products for rural parcels, including agricultural-zoned and bare land that banks decline, and Farm Credit is organized by regional association. Seller terms are not reliably cheaper than institutional terms. Balloon payments (a large final payment due when the term ends) and vague terms are the documented hazards.

### Seller financing and the land sale contract

Land sale contracts (contracts for deed) leave legal title with the seller until payoff. The buyer holds equitable title and receives a fulfillment deed at the end. Construction lenders secure against recorded legal title, so an outstanding land sale contract blocks or complicates construction lending until it is paid off or restructured. A seller-carried trust deed conveys title at closing and avoids the problem. The cure period after the statutory notice scales with how much of the price the buyer has paid.

Sellers' own mortgages are the second risk. A land sale contract puts the seller's mortgage into default under its due-on-sale clause. A seller default then leaves the buyer's later-recorded equitable interest junior to the lender's lien, with the land and the payments both losable. Title verification, escrowed payments and immediate recording are the working protections.

### Tax-foreclosure and sheriff-sale land

Tax-foreclosed county land sells as is, where is, by quitclaim deed, a deed that conveys only the county's interest with no title warranty. Because the foreclosure wipes private liens and spares assessments, the buyer's own records search targets irrigation and special-district assessments and the recorded easements and covenants that run with the land. Notice runs weekly for four consecutive weeks before the auction, and unsold parcels move to a standing surplus list. At least one county sends buildable property above $15,000 of real market value to public auction and sells lower-value parcels by sealed bid.

Title companies can insure a quitclaim chain after their own search, sometimes behind a quiet-title action with its own time and legal cost. Redemption turns on the sale type rather than on vacancy. A nonjudicial trustee's sale, the common mortgage foreclosure, has no post-sale redemption. A sheriff's execution sale after judicial foreclosure gives the judgment debtor and lien claimants separate windows, and the buyer's title is not final until they close. A construction lender's title policy also addresses Oregon's construction-lien recording window once work begins.

### Lease with option, co-ownership and family transfers

Lease-with-option contracts require a writing signed by the seller. An option without its own stated duration expires with the lease. Rent credits toward the price are forfeited when the option lapses. Tax and insurance responsibility during the lease is a negotiated term with no default. All four core terms (price-setting method, rent-credit percentage, option period, tax and insurance) are open negotiation, and an ordinary lease does not imply the right to dig.

Tenancy in common is the default the agreement has to override. Absent a signed agreement, any co-tenant can sue for partition, where court-appointed referees divide or sell the property. A buyout applies where division would cause great prejudice, and the court allocates costs. A default remedy in the agreement commonly lets the paying owners advance a missed share and add it, with interest, to what the defaulter owes at buyout.

Family transfers follow federal gift-tax mechanics, in 2026 figures that the legislature can change. Gifts above the $19,000 annual exclusion per recipient per donor require a Form 709 filing. A married couple can give $38,000 to one recipient by splitting the gift. Amounts above the exclusion count against the $15,000,000 lifetime exemption before any tax is owed. Gifted land takes the donor's carryover basis, which preserves the built-in gain, where inherited land steps up at death.

A below-market sale is a part-gift for the discount and a cost basis at the price paid. Life-estate-and-remainder arrangements leave present possession with the life tenant, so the remainderman builds only with the life tenant's written consent. Lien law, the partition statutes and the owner-builder exemption are in the legal chapter, and construction-loan sequencing is in the project-finance chapter. Whether a remainder interest satisfies "owns" in the owner-builder exemption is undefined in the statute.

**Code**

```
Land-sale-contract forfeiture requires statutory notice, always.
  A seller may enforce forfeiture only after the statutory notice of default, notwithstanding any contract provision to the contrary.
  Source: ORS 93.910.

The default notice and cure period are fixed by statute.
  Written notice served on the buyer, property occupants and recorded notice-requesters; the cure period runs 60 to 120 days, keyed to the share of the purchase price already paid; no receiver over an occupied principal residence.
  Source: ORS 93.915.

Sheriff's execution sales carry redemption windows, and trustee's sales do not.
  A judgment debtor may redeem within 180 days of an execution sale and a lien claimant within 60 days, vacant or occupied alike; a completed nonjudicial trustee's sale has no statutory redemption.
  Source: ORS 18.964 (ORS 18.960 to 18.983); ORS 86.705 to 86.815.

Tax foreclosure vests title in the county nearly clean.
  The county takes title free from liens and encumbrances except assessments; subsequent sale runs under the county-property statutes, with published notice before auction.
  Source: ORS chapter 312; ORS chapter 275.

Co-ownership defaults to tenancy in common.
  A conveyance to two or more unmarried persons creates a tenancy in common unless the deed clearly states survivorship intent.
  Source: ORS 93.180.
```

## 3.20 Partitions, property-line adjustments and parcel assembly

**Simple**

Partitions create new legal parcels. Property-line adjustments move a shared boundary without creating one. Adjustments are faster and cheaper, with staff review instead of a full land-use hearing. Combining two parcels to qualify for a dwelling takes a formal consolidation. Common ownership alone does not count. Counties have a legal deadline to decide a complete application. In practice the surveyor's calendar, not the county's, sets the pace, and an uncontested partition runs about four to six months start to finish.

**Applied**

1. Search the land-use file and title chain for conditions from any earlier partition.
2. Treat a recorded no-further-division condition as binding until it is proven released.
3. Budget the survey as the dominant cost and schedule driver: about six weeks to fieldwork, eight more to a recordable plat.
4. Plan an uncontested minor partition at about four to six months start to finish.
5. Expect county fees of about $100 per new parcel plus a base application fee.
6. Confirm fees, any hearing requirement and the current timeline with the planning department and the surveyor's office.
7. Use a property-line adjustment where the change only moves a boundary the two parcels share.
8. Expect administrative processing for an adjustment, six to eight weeks in Portland's published example, with a surveyor's legal description. Fees run county-specific, from a few hundred to a few thousand dollars.
9. When assembling adjacent resource parcels toward a dwelling threshold, acquire common ownership first, then file the consolidation.
10. Count the combined acreage only after the consolidated parcel records.

**Professional**

Minor partitions divide land into three or fewer parcels, and four or more in a calendar year is a subdivision. Only a licensed land surveyor can prepare the plat. Counties decide a complete application within a statutory ceiling, longer for land outside an urban growth boundary than inside it. Staffing sets the real pace within that ceiling.

Because an adjustment by definition creates no new unit of land, LUBA enforces the line between it and a partition strictly. Adjusting only shared boundaries qualifies (McKay Creek Valley Assoc. v. Washington County, 24 Or LUBA 187, 1992). Moving whole parcels or non-common lines is a partition or replat even when the parcel count never changes (Goddard v. Jackson County, 34 Or LUBA 402, 1998).

Consolidation for dwelling eligibility runs through the same adjustment process. Where lots are relied on together for a minimum-acreage dwelling standard, the county requires their consolidation into a single parcel as a condition of approval. Washington County publishes a specific application for adjustments outside the urban growth boundary, and Yamhill County a recordable property-line adjustment deed form. A homesite partition off a farm- or forest-deferred tract pairs with the homesite assessment mechanics in the rollback-tax section, and the remainder keeps its special assessment while it keeps its qualifying use.

**Code**

```
Partition plats are surveyed documents.
  The partition plat is made by a registered professional land surveyor and recorded with its legal descriptions.
  Source: ORS 92.050.

A property-line adjustment creates no new parcel.
  An adjustment relocates or eliminates a common boundary between abutting properties without creating an additional unit of land; parcel sizes after adjustment answer to the zoning ordinance.
  Source: ORS 92.010; ORS 92.192.

Land-use applications have statutory decision deadlines.
  Counties: final action within 150 days of a complete application, or 120 days for land inside an urban growth boundary, with written notice of missing items within 30 days of receipt. Cities: 120 days.
  Source: ORS 215.427(1) and (2); ORS 227.178.
```

## Decisions this chapter records

- (decided) Five conditions end a purchase: no legal dwelling path, a failed septic evaluation with no workable alternative, no legal access, no usable water, and a recorded restriction against residential use.
- (decided) Every finding is sorted into one of six bands, and a risk that cannot be priced, permitted, engineered, insured, accessed or inspected is a dealbreaker until a written answer lifts it.
- (decided) Zoning, the dwelling path, the home-occupation terms, and the outbuilding and accessory-dwelling rules are confirmed in writing against the tax lot before an offer.
- (decided) On farm or forest land the offer is contingent on the county's written dwelling-path determination and on verification of any Measure 49 order.
- (Jon to confirm) A block operation on resource land is planned as a home occupation, with no purpose-built production building assumed.
- (decided) Access is resolved first on any parcel that does not touch a public road: a recorded easement, a winter drive, concrete-truck capacity, and a recorded maintenance agreement with four terms.
- (decided) Every utility, and the winter sun, is confirmed by written quote or site reading before the offer.
- (decided) The septic site evaluation is the earliest paid gate, ordered on day one of the inspection period, with the offer contingent on it.
- (decided) The site plan locks septic primary and reserve first, the well second as one pair with the septic, hazard exclusions third, the driveway fourth, the house fifth, the solar envelope sixth, and the shop, block-yard and outbuildings last.
- (decided) Free screens run first and paid items start in parallel, and a clean free pass clears the parcel for paid follow-up rather than for closing.
- (decided) A wetland delineation is routine on any parcel with forest cover, seasonal wet ground or drainage features.
- (decided) Contingencies are tied to named reports, calendared on signing, and drafted by an Oregon real estate attorney with an express renegotiation clause.
- (decided) Where construction financing is a near-term goal, the land is bought by note and trust deed rather than by land sale contract.
