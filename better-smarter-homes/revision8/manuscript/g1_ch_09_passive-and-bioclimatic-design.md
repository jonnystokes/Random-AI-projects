# Chapter 9: Passive And Bioclimatic Design

Passive and bioclimatic design shapes the house to its climate before any equipment is sized: orientation, glazing placement and area, shading, natural ventilation, and the structure's own mass. Each is set so the heating, cooling, and lighting equipment starts from a smaller load. This chapter also states the regional winter-sky data that governs every solar-performance claim in this book. The Land, Site, and Feasibility chapter contains the parcel criteria the site work here depends on. The Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, and Radon chapter contains the control layers and insulation values. The Structural System and Concrete Design chapter contains the engineering of the mass. The What Concrete Uniquely Enables chapter contains the mass-enabled design options. The HVAC, Ventilation, Humidity, and Air Quality chapter and the Solar, Battery, and Energy Independence chapter contain the mechanical and power systems whose loads this chapter reduces.

## 9.1 The Bioclimatic Approach

**Simple**

Passive design uses sunlight, wind, and the building's mass to reduce equipment loads. Each strategy works only where the climate supplies its input. Western Oregon supplies cloudy winters, dry sunny summers, and cool summer nights. That mix decides which strategies work here. Winter solar heating is weak because the winter sky is mostly overcast. Daylighting, summer shading, and night cooling work well. This chapter sizes every strategy to measured Oregon data, not to national rules of thumb.

**The winter sky, measured.** The controlling climate facts, stated here once and cited by the rest of this book:

- From November through February, the Willamette Valley runs roughly 75-80 percent average cloud cover.
- Eugene's cloudiest month, December, averages roughly 76 percent cloud cover.
- Eugene's February averages about 4 hours of sunshine per day, roughly 39 percent of the possible daylight hours.
- A typical valley winter day therefore delivers well under half of its possible sunshine.

These figures come from secondary climate summaries (retrieved 2026), not from a station-level normals table. Before the design values are final, the owner pulls the climate normals for the station nearest the chosen parcel from the NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information normals portal, and those station values replace the figures above.

**The regional field study.** One monitored field study measured passive solar performance in this exact climate: Rempel et al., "Interpretation of passive solar field data with EnergyPlus models: Un-conventional wisdom from four sunspaces in Eugene, Oregon," Building and Environment 60 (2013), pages 158-172. The study instrumented four Eugene sunspaces at 10-minute intervals from January through June 2011 (air, mass, globe, and soil temperatures, relative humidity, and incident solar radiation) and validated EnergyPlus building models against the measurements. Its finding, confirmed at the abstract level: conventional thermal-mass sizing rules, developed in sunnier climates, require substantial revision in this climate, toward smaller, more responsive mass with reduced ground contact. Under a mostly overcast winter sky, a large mass often receives too little sun to charge, and the undercharged mass then absorbs heat from the room instead of supplying it. The paper's full text sits behind the publisher's access wall; the owner obtains it through a library route before any of its specific numbers are quoted in this book. The What Concrete Uniquely Enables chapter states the mass-sizing correction drawn from this study: less than half the generic mass quantity, with positive moisture isolation from soil.

**The unretrieved primary guide.** DOE and NREL published a design volume for this region: Passive Solar Design Strategies: Guidelines for Home Building, Eugene, Oregon (NREL document 17290), with worksheets scaled by heating degree days for use across the Willamette Valley. The project research confirmed the document exists but could not retrieve its text. The owner retrieves it before the final glazing and mass numbers are set. Until then, no figure from it appears in this book.

**What passive design delivers in this climate.** The strategies divide into three groups:

- Full value: daylighting (an overcast sky delivers diffuse, usable light all day); summer shading and orientation; night-flush cooling; radiant comfort from the mass; slow indoor temperature drift during a power outage. Night-flush cooling (running cool night air through the house to discharge the day's heat from the structure) is supported by the valley's summer day-night swing of roughly 25-29°F.
- Partial value: winter solar heat. Direct gain contributes on sunny cold snaps and through the spring and fall shoulder seasons, and it lowers seasonal heating energy. It does not shrink the heating equipment, because equipment is sized for a cloudy winter night; the passive solar heating section states the mechanism.
- Mismatched: strategies whose working input is strong winter or year-round sun. The Trombe wall, the dedicated solar chimney, and passive annual heat storage each fall in this group; the thermal mass and natural ventilation sections state the evidence for each.

The design order follows that ranking. The insulated envelope is set first (the Envelope chapter). Orientation, glazing placement, shading geometry, and ventilation openings are set next; these are drawing-stage decisions rather than purchased systems. Purchased passive hardware is sized last and conservatively, against the measured regional data.

## 9.2 Orientation, Site, And Microclimate

**Simple**

The sun's path at this latitude is fixed and computable. At the winter solstice the noon sun stands about 21 to 22 degrees above the horizon. At the summer solstice it stands about 68 to 69 degrees. Valley wind runs in two seasons: south in winter, northwest in summer. These numbers set the orientation, the overhangs, the windbreak, and the window placement. A parcel's own slope, trees, and terrain shift the local picture. The survey below verifies each number for the actual parcel.

**Solar geometry.** Solar noon altitude equals 90 degrees minus the difference between the site latitude and the sun's declination (the sun's seasonal angle north or south of the equator, ranging 23.45 degrees each way). Computed across the valley's latitude band: Eugene (44.05°N) reaches roughly 22.5 degrees at winter solstice noon and 69.4 degrees at summer solstice noon. Salem (44.94°N) computes to roughly 21.6 and 68.5 degrees. Portland (45.5°N) computes to roughly 21.0 and 68.0 degrees. Bend (44.06°N) computes almost identically to Eugene. The altitude shifts about half a degree per half-degree of latitude, and overhang and window-head-height sizing respond at the inch level, so the final numbers come from the parcel's own GPS latitude, not from a city figure. Source: the standard solar elevation-angle formula (PVEducation reference, retrieved 2026), recomputed independently in the project research.

**Applied: the site solar survey**

1. Generate a sun-path chart for the parcel coordinates during parcel shopping. Free tools: the University of Oregon Solar Radiation Monitoring Laboratory's online sun chart program, or the NOAA Solar Calculator.
2. Recompute the solstice noon altitudes from the parcel's GPS latitude, using the formula above or the same NOAA calculator.
3. Once a parcel is under contract, run its coordinates through NREL's PVWatts calculator. Record the point-specific monthly sun-hour and irradiance table. Replace every regional solar figure in the design calculations with the parcel's own numbers. The Solar, Battery, and Energy Independence chapter uses the same run for PV sizing.
4. Once a building footprint is staked, survey the real horizon obstructions (trees, structures, terrain) with a Solar Pathfinder. The instrument is a non-electronic reflective dome that maps obstructions onto a latitude-specific chart, and it works under overcast. Historic retail runs roughly $300-500 (retrieved 2026); confirm current pricing with the vendor.
5. Photograph the dome, trace the tree line, and file the shading results in the project binder. The glazing and overhang calculations reference them.

**Alternative instruments.** The Solmetric SunEye (an electronic fisheye shade-measurement camera) reads faster but costs more, needs a clear sky, and adds electronics to a multi-year build. The University of Oregon's Baker Lighting Lab stocks 44-degree-north sun-peg charts and loanable survey instruments. The lab's public-access policy is unpublished; the owner asks the University of Oregon Department of Architecture about access before budgeting for a purchased instrument.

**Seasonal wind.** The Western Regional Climate Center's Oregon climate narrative aligns the valley's prevailing winds north-south with the valley axis. Hourly station data for Eugene's Mahlon Sweet Field airport (1992-2002) resolves the seasonal split: south winds prevail from roughly November through March, northwest winds from roughly April or May through September, and April and September run mixed. Two siting consequences follow. The winter windbreak faces south, not the commonly assumed west. The summer cross-ventilation openings face northwest, paired with openings on the southeast. This is a valley-floor pattern; a foothill or high-desert parcel confirms its own pattern against the nearest representative station before either element is sited.

**Slope, cold-air pooling, and the day-night swing.** Eugene and Salem July days run mean highs in the low-to-mid 80s°F and mean lows in the mid-to-upper 50s°F: a day-night swing of roughly 25-29°F (secondary climate summaries, retrieved 2026). That swing is the working input for night-flush cooling. Terrain moves it. A valley-floor site pools cold night air (cold air drains downhill and collects in low ground) and swings wider. A ridge site, or a site under dense tree canopy with poor night-sky exposure, swings less. Eastern Oregon high-desert sites commonly swing 30°F or more. The National Weather Service climate book for Salem tabulates the diurnal normals; the project research retrieved it only as an unparsed file. The owner opens that document directly before a design number is stated, and checks the chosen parcel against the nearest comparable-terrain station.

**Windbreaks.** The windbreak is planted against the south winter wind. Siting and species guidance located in the project research:

- Distance: a windbreak of 35-foot-class trees sits within roughly 100 feet of the house. That figure lands inside the common 2-to-5-times-mature-height siting rule rather than replacing it.
- Species: western redcedar (Thuja plicata) is a native evergreen documented as suited to hedging and screen use. A mixed planting (cedar, spruce, pine, juniper) survives a single-species pest or disease event; a single-species row does not. Leyland cypress is discouraged in the Pacific Northwest for canker susceptibility.
- Spacing: columnar forms at 6-10 feet on center; spreading conifers at 10-20 feet; a two-row staggered planting with rows 10-20 feet apart is the standard high-effectiveness design.

An Oregon State University Extension publication, "Plant Selection for Windbreaks in Central Oregon," addresses this subject directly; the project research could not open it. The owner retrieves it before the planting plan is final and cites it in place of the general guidance above.

The windbreak-versus-summer-breeze conflict does not arise on a valley-floor site. The winter wind arrives from the south and the summer breeze from the northwest, so a correctly sited windbreak and the ventilation openings occupy different sides of the house. A site outside the valley-floor wind regime re-runs this check with its own station data.

**Site elements with two functions.** A detached shop or outbuilding sited south of the house works as a solid windbreak at the same distance guidance, and it covers the establishment years while a planted windbreak grows. Summer shade on the south wall from the same structure is a geometry check, not an assumption. Against a 68-69 degree summer noon sun, a structure shades an upper story only if it stands tall and close. The check runs once the outbuilding's height and position are drawn. A garden wall or berm shelters a courtyard or outdoor living area from ground-level wind but cannot shade an upper story. The Developing the Site chapter contains the outbuilding program and phasing.

**The high-desert alternative site.** For an eastern Oregon parcel (climate zone 5B), evaporative cooling is climate-matched: evaporation cools best in dry, hot air, and courtyard studies document temperature reductions of up to 5-7°C where water, vegetation, and shade combine. A small, wind-sheltered, recirculating courtyard water feature is a real comfort element on such a site. The same element in the humid valley returns less cooling for the same water. True water consumption scales with surface area and wind exposure (a recirculating feature loses only its evaporated water), and no gallons-per-day figure for a residential-scale feature was found in the project research. The Water Independence chapter's water budget prices the specific feature before it is adopted.

### Solar access law

**Simple**

Oregon law contains tools for protecting a house's sunlight, but none of them operate automatically. A neighbor's future building or trees can lawfully shade this house unless a recorded easement or a local ordinance says otherwise. Distance from the property line is the only protection the owner controls alone.

**Professional**

Three statute groups govern solar access:

- ORS 105.880 voids any deed or contract provision prohibiting use of a solar energy system. The definition covers any device, structure, or mechanism using solar radiation for heating, cooling, or electrical energy; that breadth reaches passive designs as well as hardware.
- ORS 105.885 through 105.895 define the recorded solar easement. It is appurtenant (it runs with the land, not with the current owner). The creating instrument contains legal descriptions of both the benefited and burdened parcels, plus a description of the protected airspace. The airspace is stated as a sun chart (a plotted representation of the sun's path by hour and month at the property's latitude) or as a solar envelope (a three-dimensional height and vegetation restriction space). The instrument is recorded in both parcels' chains of title; a city-issued instrument adds a city official's attestation and signature. The statute creates the mechanism only; a neighbor is never compelled to grant the easement. The correct solar citation range is ORS 105.880 to 105.895; ORS 105.900 to 105.915 are the parallel wind-energy sections.
- ORS 227.190 (cities) and ORS 215.044 (counties) are enabling statutes only. A jurisdiction may adopt a solar access ordinance protecting the south face of buildings during solar heating hours, defined as the three hours before and after the sun's highest point on December 21. Where no ordinance is adopted, these statutes provide no protection at all.
- ORS 94.778 voids planned-community prohibitions on solar panels, subject to the association's reasonable size, placement, and aesthetic requirements. By its plain terms it protects panel hardware only. Passive design elements (south glazing area, orientation, roof form, planting) receive no protection from it and pass through ordinary CC&R design review like any other design choice.

The ORS 105, 215, and 227 texts were read directly from the project code library. ORS 94.778 was read from a secondary source; the owner adds the primary ORS chapter 94 text to the code library and re-verifies the wording before it is quoted verbatim. No attorney-fee figure for drafting and recording a solar easement was found in the project research; that cost comes from a real-estate attorney's quote for the specific parcels.

**Applied**

1. Before making an offer, ask the county planning department whether a solar access ordinance under ORS 215.044 applies to the parcel.
2. At title review, read the preliminary title report for CC&Rs constraining glazing area, roof form, orientation, or planting.
3. Where the winter sun path crosses a neighboring parcel, site the solar-dependent glazing back from that line. Distance is the only self-executing protection.
4. If one specific neighboring owner controls the sun path, price a recorded solar easement with a real-estate attorney before closing.

**Code**

```
A deed or contract provision prohibiting solar energy system use is void.
  Applies to any device, structure, or mechanism using solar radiation for heating, cooling, or electrical energy.
  Source: ORS 105.880.

A solar easement exists only as a recorded instrument.
  Legal descriptions of both parcels plus the protected airspace (sun chart or solar envelope); recorded in both chains of title; appurtenant; terminates by stated condition, court judgment, or mutual agreement of all owners.
  Source: ORS 105.885-105.895.

Cities and counties may adopt solar access ordinances; none is required to.
  An adopted ordinance protects the south face during solar heating hours: three hours either side of the sun's highest point on December 21.
  Source: ORS 227.190; ORS 215.044.

A planned community cannot prohibit solar panels; passive design elements are not covered.
  Prohibitions are void; reasonable size, placement, and aesthetic requirements remain enforceable; the protection extends to panel hardware only.
  Source: ORS 94.778.
```

## 9.3 Passive Solar Heating

**Simple**

South windows admit winter sun, and the house's mass stores part of the heat. In this climate that mechanism is real but small. The winter sky blocks most direct sun for four months. South glazing is therefore sized conservatively, and winter sun is treated as a bonus. The savings appear on the heating bill, not in a smaller heat pump. On sunny cold snaps and in spring and fall, the contribution is real.

**The glazing ratio.** The widely used DOE-tradition rule of thumb (Whole Building Design Guide and related references, retrieved 2026):

- Sun-tempered: south glazing at 7 percent of conditioned floor area, with no added-mass requirement.
- Direct gain: south glazing up to 12 percent of floor area, with 3-6 square feet of 4-inch-thick masonry surface added per square foot of glazing above the 7 percent baseline.

Demonstration arithmetic at a placeholder 2,000 square feet (this project's program is not yet fixed): 7 percent gives 140 square feet of south glazing with no added mass. 12 percent gives 240 square feet, and the 100 square feet above the baseline requires 300-600 square feet of exposed 4-inch masonry surface.

The rule was built from sunnier climates and passive, conduction-only mass charging. A climate delivering well under half of possible winter sunshine cannot produce the same gain per square foot of glass, and no source in the project research derived a corrected percentage for a climate this cloudy. This book therefore treats 7-12 percent as a ceiling, not a target, and runs the arithmetic at 7-9 percent once a real floor area exists. Exposed-mass credit comes first from the radiant floor and structural mass the house already contains (the thermal mass section states the basis), not from mass added to satisfy the ratio.

**The contrarian position, attributed.** Peter Powell, writing in GreenBuildingAdvisor, argues that south glazing can run 15-20 percent of floor area and higher, up to 30-plus percent of the south-side rooms' floor area. His condition: thermostat-controlled fans actively moving captured heat to storage mass elsewhere in the house, instead of conduction into the sunlit room's own mass. Powell writes from central Pennsylvania. No monitored building tests his ratio in a climate comparable to the Willamette Valley's winter, and the claim rests on his own projects and reasoning, not a peer-reviewed study. The Rempel study's finding cuts across both camps: in this climate the binding constraint is how fast the mass returns heat, not glazing area alone. This book keeps the conservative DOE range as the default. An owner drawn to Powell's approach takes it to a passive-solar-experienced designer as a named, unverified alternative, not a rule.

**The closest local precedent.** The Rue-Evans house in Salem (Bilyeu Homes, ground broken August 2009) is among the first PHIUS-certified houses on the West Coast and the closest built local data point located. Its figures were triangulated from secondary sources; the certifier's own monitoring article could not be opened, and the owner re-verifies with PHIUS before treating them as measured results:

- final blower-door test of 0.20 air changes per hour at 50 Pascals
- measured whole-house electricity just under 14 kWh per day, against roughly 30 kWh per day for a typical American home
- glazing reduced during design to 15 percent of floor area
- certification model showing zero cooling energy and no modeled overheating
- solar water heating covering 69 percent of water-heating energy

The 15 percent glazing choice sits far below the contrarian range's upper reaches and reflects the same overheating caution this chapter applies. The house's post-occupancy measured overheating hours were not found; the modeled zero is a prediction, not a measurement.

**What winter sun does not buy.** Heating equipment is sized by a Manual J load calculation (the standard residential heating and cooling load method) at the 99 percent design temperature, a condition that typically occurs on a winter night with no sun available. Solar gain is absent at the sizing hour, and multi-day sunless stretches are normal here, so the heat pump keeps its full capacity regardless of the passive design. GreenBuildingAdvisor's technical contributors state the point directly: passive solar reduces how much the system runs across the season, not the size of the system. The defensible claim for this climate is seasonal energy savings; the What Concrete Uniquely Enables chapter states the reported regional range (roughly 20-30 percent heating-load reduction, against 40-60 percent reported in sunnier cold climates). A dollar figure for this house requires an hourly energy model of the finished design. Manual J does not credit time-varying passive contributions, so a passive-solar design is modeled in an hourly tool (PHPP or WUFI-Passive) alongside the code calculation. The HVAC, Ventilation, Humidity, and Air Quality chapter contains the equipment sizing.

**Glass specification.** Published solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC, the fraction of solar heat a window admits) guidance splits by design tradition: gain-chasing sources suggest 0.60-plus on south glass, and cooling-cautious climate-zone guidance suggests 0.30-0.40. Given the winter-sky data, this book weights the cautious end:

- South glazing: SHGC roughly 0.30-0.40.
- East and west glazing: SHGC roughly 0.25-0.30 or lower. No source in the project research supports a higher value there, because low-angle sun defeats overhangs on those orientations.

The cost difference between glass packages at the same U-factor comes from the window manufacturer's quote at order time; no generic figure was found. The Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, and Radon chapter's window specification is read together with these values, and daylighting adds a visible transmittance requirement (the daylighting section states it).

**The cost premium question.** No source in the project research isolates the dollar-per-square-foot premium of a direct-gain design (added glazing plus added exposed mass) over this project's default wall and window specification. A published zero-extra-cost claim produced no numbers when its own readers asked for them, and the widely quoted 3-8 percent Passive House premium prices the whole envelope standard, a different quantity. Once a window schedule exists, the owner prices the delta directly: the marginal cost of the added glazing area at the chosen window product, plus the marginal cost of the added exposed masonry area at the project's own material costs.

**Sunspaces.** An attached sunspace is the one passive-solar element measured in this exact climate, and the Rempel study is a direct warning against building one to textbook mass rules; a sunspace built to out-of-region sizing here is the documented failure mode. Costs (2026 guides): a four-season, insulated, conditioned sunroom runs roughly $150-350 per square foot, with prefab kits from roughly $100 per square foot before site work; a simple unheated attached greenhouse runs roughly $5-35 per square foot. A plant-growing sunspace that must also stay comfortable and frost-protected sits toward the sunroom end of that range. The justification for one on this project is the growing function itself (season extension, crop protection), which stands on its own; heating payback in this climate does not support the purchase for either option. If one is built, its mass follows the Rempel direction (smaller, responsive, isolated from ground moisture), not generic tables. The Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, and Livability chapter contains the growing-space program; the Water Independence chapter contains the food-production goals.

**The exposed face of an earth-sheltered form.** An elevational earth-sheltered design (one exposed, heavily glazed face; the What Concrete Uniquely Enables chapter states the form) concentrates the whole glazing decision on one wall. The earth-coupled mass raises overheating tolerance relative to a light-frame house, and the same single-face concentration amplifies the shoulder-season risk documented in the superinsulation section's modeled precedent. The rules for that face:

- glazing at the conservative end of the range above
- the overhang sized by the latitude-specific factor in the shading section
- adjustable exterior shading treated as required rather than optional
- the whole-design overheating check run in an hourly model before construction

**Storage systems beyond direct gain.** The Trombe wall decision, with its documented failure record, is stated in the thermal mass section of this chapter. Passive annual heat storage and annualized geo-solar are treated as unproven for this region in the What Concrete Uniquely Enables chapter. The Rempel evidence deepens that judgment: a system betting a full winter on months of stored solar charge leans harder on the same weakened input than the small, fast-cycling sunspaces the study measured. That extension is reasoned from the study's confirmed finding, not a tested result.

## 9.4 Daylighting

**Simple**

An overcast sky still delivers usable light, spread evenly from the whole sky. Daylighting keeps most of its value under Oregon's winter sky. How deep light reaches into a room depends mostly on the height of the window's top edge. Code counts glazing area only, so a code-compliant room can still be dark at its far end. Placement and head height fix that, not extra glass.

**The depth rule.** The practical daylit depth of a room runs 1.5 to 2 times the window head height (the height of the glazing's top edge above the floor). A window or clerestory with its head at 8 feet lights usefully to roughly 12-16 feet from the opening. The graduated version of the rule, under an overcast sky: roughly 400 lux (a comfortable task-light level) reaches about 1.5 times head height into the room, 200 lux about 2.5 times, and 100 lux about 3.5 times. The far zone of a deep room lit from one side is ambient light, not task light. Head height is the strongest lever. Raising a clerestory (a window band set high in a wall, including at the ridge of a vaulted space) extends its reach roughly in proportion to the added height; adding glazing area at the same height does not. For deep or interior rooms (an interior bathroom, a windowless hallway, a great-room center), placement is decided with these ratios once real room depths exist. Clerestory glazing is specified at high visible transmittance, with eye-level view glazing lower, so the clerestory does the lighting and the view windows control glare.

**The code minimum is not a daylighting design.** ORSC R303 requires habitable-room glazing at 8 percent of floor area for natural light, with 4 percent openable or with mechanical ventilation substituting; the Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, and Livability chapter states the full rule set. The requirement counts area only, without regard to placement or head height, so a room lit entirely by low windows on one wall can comply and stay dim at its far end. Compliance is verified with the Interiors chapter; light quality is designed with the ratios above.

**Code**

```
Habitable rooms have natural light from glazing, or the mechanical equivalent.
  Glazing at least 8 percent of the room's floor area; openable area at least 4 percent unless whole-house mechanical ventilation substitutes.
  Source: ORSC R303 (2023 edition).
```

**Window-to-wall ratio.** Generic daylighting guidance puts the optimum window-to-wall ratio at 25-40 percent. That figure assumes frame walls with a modest insulation gap between wall and window. This project's continuously insulated concrete wall out-insulates even a high-performing window by a wide margin, so each square foot of glass trades away more wall performance than in the assemblies the guidance assumed. No source in the project research reworks the optimum for this wall. The working default: the lower half of the range (roughly 25-30 percent) for rooms without a specific daylight, view, or solar-gain reason to go higher. The deciding arithmetic is a UA trade-off calculation (U-factor times area, summed across the envelope), run room by room through the ORSC Chapter 11 UA compliance path once the floor plan and window schedule exist. The Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, and Radon chapter contains that compliance work, and the project code library contains the trade-off calculator.

**Visible transmittance.** VT (visible transmittance, the fraction of visible light a window passes, rated 0 to 1) is specified alongside U-factor and SHGC. A heavily coated or tinted glass package can meet both energy numbers and still render rooms dim even on bright days. Targets: 0.50-0.65 for daylight-dependent rooms, 0.40-0.60 elsewhere. For east and west windows where the SHGC is pushed low, the owner asks the manufacturer for the VT-to-SHGC ratio of the specific coating. A spectrally selective coating holds VT high at low SHGC; a simple dark tint drops both.

**Skylights, roof monitors, and light shelves.** Three devices extend daylight past what wall glazing reaches. Two are conditional and one is rejected.

- Skylight: the default roof daylight device. A comparative study found skylights preferred over south-facing roof monitors on similar annual energy costs, lower peak loads, and lower construction cost.
- Roof monitor (a raised, vertically glazed structure built up from the roof plane): more framing than a skylight, and four walls of roof-to-wall flashing against a skylight's single curb. On this project's protected-membrane roof, every added penetration is a long-term leak-risk detail. A monitor is specified only where its operable version also serves as the stack-ventilation outlet; the natural ventilation section states that combination. It is conventionally buildable on the wood-truss roof and a higher-scrutiny detail on the membrane roof. No residential cost figure was found; a contractor prices the specific design. The Roof System and Roof-Wall Junctions chapter contains the penetration detailing.
- Light shelf (a horizontal reflective shelf at the window head that bounces high-angle sun onto the ceiling): not adopted for this project. Its exterior surface needs the same high-angle sun the already-specified deep overhang exists to block, and the sources describe it as an expensive architectural addition. A light ceiling finish captures most of the same depth benefit without a device.

**Interior finishes as daylight hardware.** Light reflectance value (LRV, the percentage of light a finish reflects, published by paint manufacturers for every color) is specified for daylight-dependent rooms:

- ceilings at LRV 80 or higher; the ceiling is the most important reflecting surface
- upper walls at LRV 50 or higher
- floors at LRV 20 or higher, in a matte finish

A polished or glossy floor under a bright window reflects the window's image toward the eye (specular reflection); a matte finish scatters the same light diffusely and adds no glare. Sheer interior treatments soften direct-sun glare while passing daylight. Blackout treatments belong to rooms needing full darkness on demand and defeat daylighting while closed. Which rooms get which finish and treatment is coordinated room by room with the Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, and Livability chapter. A dark ceiling in a media room is a legitimate choice; a dark ceiling in the daylit great room works against the clerestory that lights it.

**Glare control, ranked.** Assembled from the component figures in this chapter; no single source compares all four options at one window size:

1. The glass package itself (spectrally selective, low SHGC, adequate VT), chosen at order time. No separate installed cost beyond the package upcharge, and it works every hour with no operating step. This is the baseline for east and west windows.
2. Interior blinds or shades: commodity products, typically under roughly $200-300 per window installed (retrieved 2026). Occupant fine control; weak against heat gain (the shading section states the performance gap).
3. Exterior roller shades or shutters: the highest performance at the highest cost, roughly $900-3,000 manual or $1,000-5,000 motorized per window installed (retrieved 2026). Reserved for windows where the heat-gain gap justifies the price.
4. Light shelf: not adopted, per the device comparison above.

## 9.5 Natural Ventilation And Summer Comfort

**Simple**

Summer nights here are cool even after hot days. Night air run through the house carries the day's heat out of the concrete. The chilled mass then absorbs heat through the next afternoon. The day-night swing of 25 degrees or more is what makes this work. The openings need screens and security hardware that keep them usable in practice. In wildfire smoke weeks the strategy reverses: the house stays closed and the ventilation system filters.

**The air-change target.** Night-purge guidance recommends 5-15 air changes per hour (ACH) during the flushing period. Three to five air changes replace the room air itself. Drawing real heat out of concrete or CMU mass sits toward the upper part of the range. The flush runs sustained across the night at a moderate rate rather than as one short high-speed burst, because the mass releases heat slower than the air changes. This project's larger-than-typical exposed mass places the target in the middle-to-upper range, run as a long overnight flush at moderate speed. The house's CFM number converts from volume, not floor area: target ACH times house volume in cubic feet, divided by 60. The generic 2-3 CFM per square foot fan-sizing rule is a cruder proxy for the same target. The number is run once a floor plan and ceiling heights exist.

**Cross-ventilation layout.** True cross ventilation requires two openings on different walls (opposite, or at least non-adjacent) of the same connected space, and the smaller opening limits the flow; one large window on one wall moves little air regardless of its size. For this climate, openings sited to catch the northwest summer flow pair with openings on the southeast. Interior partitions block the path unless the plan provides transoms, louvered doors, or door undercuts along the route to the outlet. The room-by-room layout is drawn at floor-plan stage with the seasonal wind data from the orientation section.

**Stack outlets.** A tall interior volume the house already contains (a stairwell, a vaulted space, an operable clerestory or roof monitor) serves as the vertical exhaust path: warm air rises and exits high while cooler air enters low. The What Concrete Uniquely Enables chapter states the vented-shaft option built on structure the house needs anyway. Two dedicated devices are not adopted:

- A dedicated solar chimney (a sun-heated shaft that boosts stack flow) is documented in hot, sunny climates. No Pacific Northwest or comparably cloudy monitored data was found, the boost depends on solar heating of the shaft, which this climate supplies weakly, and the device adds a single-purpose roof penetration. An operable clerestory or roof monitor bought for daylighting captures a comparable benefit without the dedicated structure.
- A windcatcher (a traditional arid-climate tower pairing wind capture with evaporative cooling) does not transfer. Published research documents constrained performance in humid climates, with condensation and mold risk from introducing moist air. Its non-evaporative half duplicates the clerestory and monitor function already specified.

**The whole-house fan and the hybrid strategy.** Documented figures (retrieved 2026):

- installed cost roughly $900-2,400 for common sizes: $800-1,800 near 6,000 CFM for a 1,500-square-foot house, $1,100-2,400 near 12,000 CFM for 3,000 square feet
- electrical draw roughly 200-800 watts, against 3,000-5,000 watts for comparable air conditioning
- a dedicated 15-20 amp circuit or 240V service on some high-CFM and smart models, adding $150-350 of electrical work
- noise of 70-85 dB in older direct-drive units, 35-55 dB in modern ducted variable-speed units The strategy adopted here is a hybrid. Passive cross-ventilation and stack paths run as the default on ordinary nights, at zero electrical load (relevant to the battery budget in the Solar, Battery, and Energy Independence chapter). A modern, quiet, variable-speed fan provides the boost on the hottest nights and moves the night's air through smaller, securable openings, which the security ranking below uses. An old-style loud unit is a poor purchase for a fan meant to run all night near bedrooms.

**Security at the night openings.** Rural response times raise the value of passive security. Four documented options, ranked for this project:

1. Retractable security-mesh screens (stainless-steel mesh over full-size openings, sold as an alternative to bars and grilles): the only option giving full airflow and forced-entry resistance at the same time. This is the primary treatment for the main night-flush openings. No published price was located; the figure comes from a supplier quote for the specific openings.
2. High clerestory openings: secure by height rather than hardware, and already built for daylighting; operable hardware there adds a protected flush path. Their flow is stack-driven only, so they supplement the low openings rather than replace them.
3. Lockable window restrictors (a keyed cable or arm limiting a window to a roughly 4-inch gap): commodity hardware, typically under $50-100 per window (retrieved 2026). The low-cost fallback; the narrow gap caps airflow far below an open window's.
4. A sealed house with the fan drawing through secured low vents: the highest baseline security, at the highest electrical load and noise. This is the right mode for smoke events rather than for every night.

Where a bedroom window doubles as the required emergency escape opening, its hardware keeps the escape function; the owner verifies any restrictor or screen hardware on those specific windows with the building department at plan review.

**Insect screens and weather.** Retractable insect screens cover large openings: double-panel systems to roughly 26 feet wide and 10.5 feet tall, motorized single systems to roughly 16.5 feet wide (retrieved 2026). A marketed security-mesh radiant-heat claim (attenuation up to 59 percent, relevant to wildfire radiant exposure) is verified against the named manufacturer's test report before the project relies on it. No screen product filters wildfire smoke through an open opening; smoke is handled by closing the house, below. No residential product closes an opening automatically against sudden rain; where that matters, the opening or its screen is motorized and joined to the same local automation as the smoke response.

**The smoke override.** Wildfire smoke is a recurring Pacific Northwest summer condition, and the response cannot depend on a manual decision made correctly every night. The assembled system, from documented components (no packaged residential product does this end to end):

- a local outdoor PM2.5 sensor (fine-particulate concentration, the smoke metric) at the house itself, not a regional index that can lag the site
- a threshold rule in the local automation controller: above the set concentration, motorized night-flush openings close, non-motorized openings trigger an unmissable alert, and the HRV/ERV switches to its highest-filtration mode instead of open-air intake
- a human-confirmable override, consistent with the manual-override control the ventilation code already requires

The Smart Home, Network, Security, and Local Automation chapter contains the local-first controller this rule runs on. Building the rule is an integration task for whoever configures that system, not a purchase.

**Open windows and the HRV.** The code-required whole-house ventilation system runs balanced (supply and exhaust matched within 10 percent). An open window unbalances it. The consequence is lost ventilation efficiency and heat recovery while the window is open, not equipment damage and not a violation, because the code governs the system's design and controls, not the occupants' windows. The code also requires manual-override controls, so pausing the system for an open-window evening is a built-in function. A window-contact-sensor link that pauses the HRV automatically is a documented, local-first option with its highest value in shoulder seasons; it is an owner addition, not a requirement. The HVAC, Ventilation, Humidity, and Air Quality chapter contains the system design.

**Floor coverings over the flushing mass.** The radiant floor's manufacturer limit and the night-flush requirement point the same direction: heat must cross the floor surface freely in both seasons. Manufacturer limits on floor-covering insulation run from a strict R-0.5 to a looser R-4.0 total, depending on the system; the project's actual ceiling comes from the chosen system's manufacturer at equipment selection. Within either limit, the compatible finishes are exposed sealed concrete (matte-finished where the daylighting section's glare rule applies), thin-set tile or stone bonded to the slab, and radiant-rated engineered wood or thin resilient flooring. Thick carpet with cushion (carpet alone runs roughly R-2.6 per inch of thickness), cork, and floating floors over foam underlayment insulate the slab against both radiant output and night-time heat release. All three are excluded from the mass surfaces the flush depends on. The HVAC, Ventilation, Humidity, and Air Quality chapter states the radiant covering cap; the Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, and Livability chapter contains the finish schedule.

**Applied: running the flush**

1. Watch the outdoor-versus-indoor temperature difference on the house's local sensors.
2. Open the night-flush openings when outdoor air drops below indoor temperature.
3. Run the flush all night at a moderate rate. A short high-speed burst refreshes the air but leaves the mass warm.
4. Close the openings and drop the exterior shades at dawn, before outdoor temperature rises.
5. In smoke season, keep the automation's threshold rule armed and keep the openings closed when it trips.

**Code**

```
The whole-house mechanical ventilation system must be balanced, with manual override.
  Supply and exhaust airflow matched within 10 percent; continuous rate of (0.01 x conditioned floor area) + (7.5 x (bedrooms + 1)) cfm; controls provide manual override.
  Source: ORSC M1505.4 (2023 edition; reverify at the 2026 ORSC adoption, expected near October 1, 2026).

Sleeping rooms must have an emergency escape and rescue opening.
  Minimum 5.7 square feet net clear opening; night-ventilation hardware on that opening preserves its operation.
  Source: ORSC R310.
```

## 9.6 Shading

**Simple**

Sun control works outside the glass, or it mostly fails. An inside blind intercepts sunlight after it has entered and become heat. South windows are shaded by a correctly sized overhang, because the summer sun stands high. East and west windows receive low, sideways sun that no overhang can block. Those windows need exterior shades, vertical fins, or less glass.

**Sizing the overhang.** The overhang is sized by a latitude-specific factor, not by a universal inch rule. Sustainable By Design's published overhang-factor table lists 0.37 for 44 degrees north, this project's latitude band. The projection equals 0.37 times the vertical distance from the windowsill to the underside of the overhang. The result gives complete noontime shade at the summer solstice and full noontime sun at the winter solstice. The factor restates the same solstice geometry as the orientation section's altitude formula, which is why it changes with latitude: a fixed 12-inch or 18-inch rule sized for a lower-latitude sun systematically under-shades a house at 44-45 degrees. Inputs at design time: the parcel's own latitude and each window's actual head and sill heights. The as-built dimension is photographed and measured before finish materials close it in; the verification protocol in the problems section states the record.

**The shoulder-season limit.** A fixed overhang is tuned to the solstices, and the shading need differs between the matching spring and fall sun angles. The same low sun angle that is welcome in February returns in October, when a warm afternoon needs shade instead; a fixed projection admits both equally. A modeled Passive House precedent (the Gaddy house, mid-Atlantic climate zone 4/5) documents the failure mode: fixed shading tuned for summer and winter left shoulder-month sun uncontrolled. A 23 percent south-glazing design there produced peak shoulder-season cooling loads three times the mechanical system's capacity. Cutting the south glazing to 15 percent removed the spikes in that project's model. The modeling method transfers even though the climate differs. The consequences for this project: conservative glazing ratios (the passive solar heating section), and adjustable shading on the orientations and rooms the overheating model flags, not fixed geometry alone.

**Roof form and self-shading.** A hip roof's eave wraps all four walls at a consistent depth, and that wraparound partially shades east and west walls in summer. A gable end has no overhang on its own wall face. One facing east or west with meaningful glazing therefore loses the shading a hip roof provides at no added cost. The check runs once roof form and orientation are both fixed; the fix is smaller glazing or a dedicated shading device on that wall, not a forced roof-form change. Neither roof form blocks low-angle east or west sun; no horizontal projection does. The Wall System, Foundation to Roofline chapter and the Roof System and Roof-Wall Junctions chapter contain the roof-form decision.

**East and west: the device decision.** Fixed vertical fins (a brise-soleil: vertical blades spaced along the wall and angled to block low side-angle sun while keeping view slots between blades) are the standard fixed answer for east and west glass. Adjustable exterior roller shades and shutters are the alternative, with two documented advantages. They retract fully in winter, when low morning and afternoon sun is useful rather than a problem. Their cost is known ($900-3,000 per window manual, $1,000-5,000 motorized, installed, retrieved 2026), while no residential fixed-fin cost figure was located. The default for this project's east and west windows is therefore the exterior roller shade or shutter. Fins are reserved for a location where the owner wants a permanent architectural element and prices it with a local fabricator.

**Buying exterior roller shutters.** The product category is the standard European Passive House shading answer, and it is purchasable from US-based suppliers building on European (typically German) hardware; the supply chain is import-based even where the seller is domestic. Product cost runs roughly $30-35 per square foot, with the installed per-window figures above (retrieved 2026). Lead time was not documented anywhere in the project research, and it differs between a supplier holding US inventory and one ordering per project from Europe. The owner obtains the lead time in writing from the chosen supplier before the build schedule depends on it.

**The performance gap that drives the tiering.** DOE-cited guidance: interior treatments reduce solar heat gain by roughly 18-45 percent, depending on the product and its reflectivity; exterior systems reduce it by roughly 55 to more than 90 percent. The mechanism is position. An exterior device rejects heat before the glass; an interior device intercepts light that has already entered, and its absorbed heat stays in the room. The tiered specification:

- East and west windows: exterior shading (roller shade or shutter) as the default, because no passive geometry works on those orientations.
- South windows: the latitude-sized overhang does the summer work; exterior shading is added only where the overheating model flags a specific room.
- North windows and other low-load glass: interior blinds are adequate.

The tiering captures most of the exterior-shading performance where it matters, and avoids specifying every window at exterior-shutter cost, which at $900-5,000 per window multiplies into tens of thousands of dollars across a full window schedule.

**Automating the shading.** Motorized shades and shutters run on a local sun-and-temperature rule with no cloud service. Sun position for the house's fixed coordinates is deterministic (the same formula as the orientation section), and outdoor temperature comes from a local sensor. The two feed an open/close rule in the local controller. Marketing labels do not distinguish local-capable motors from cloud-dependent ones. The purchasing requirement is therefore explicit: the motor and controller accept a local trigger (a dry contact, or a local network protocol through the house's own hub) and never require the manufacturer's app or account to operate. The owner obtains that confirmation in writing before ordering. The Smart Home, Network, Security, and Local Automation chapter states the local-first architecture.

**Deciduous trees, tested against the claim.** The textbook claim: a deciduous tree shades in summer and admits winter sun after leaf drop. Peter Powell's documented critique: limbs tall enough to shade summer windows block significant low winter sun even when bare, and leaf drop in his climate arrives in November, after weeks of useful heating-season sun have already passed. Applied to Oregon species: Oregon white oak (Quercus garryana), a well-adapted native, is documented with dense, twisting limb structure and is a poor fit for this specific role despite its value elsewhere on the site. No source in the project research confirms leaf-drop timing and winter branch density for vine maple or bigleaf maple, the two other commonly recommended natives. The procedure: no species is planted for passive shading on reputation. The owner observes a mature specimen of the candidate species bare in winter, checks local leaf-drop timing against the start of the heating season, and consults an arborist or the OSU Extension office before a tree enters the shading design. A new tree needs years to reach working canopy, so trees complement built shading rather than substitute for it.

**The pergola and vine option.** A pergola carrying a deciduous vine shades a wall or an outdoor room in summer and opens in winter. Documented species guidance:

- Hardy kiwi: a vigorous twining vine reaching 15-20 feet, suited to the Pacific Northwest, needing roughly 25 feet of growing room and regular pruning. Wet soil brings crown-rot risk, so its root zone is mounded or planted on a slope for drainage.
- Grape: vigorous and productive, needing strong support and strict pruning to 4-6 canes of 8-12 buds each. English ivy is excluded twice over: its aerial roots secrete enzymes that degrade lignin and weaken structural timber, and it is evergreen, which defeats the seasonal function. Wood pergolas in this climate use rot-resistant species, with cedar the documented recommendation; a concrete or CMU pergola from this project's own materials removes the structure's rot risk entirely, leaving vine pruning and root drainage as the maintenance items. The Owner-Made Blocks and Alternative Concretes chapter contains the material. No multi-year Oregon case history documenting time to full canopy was found; the coverage timeline is treated as unverified.

## 9.7 Thermal Mass And Comfort

**Simple**

Thermal mass changes how warm a room feels, not only how warm the air is. Your body exchanges heat with the surrounding surfaces as well as the air. A warm floor lets the thermostat sit lower at the same comfort. A cold concrete wall does the opposite until it warms through. This section states what the mass does for comfort and what managing it requires.

**Radiant surfaces and the thermostat.** Comfort tracks operative temperature: a combination of air temperature and mean radiant temperature (MRT, the weighted average temperature of the surfaces surrounding an occupant). ASHRAE established the comfort-MRT relationship decades ago. A radiant floor raises MRT without raising air temperature by the same amount, so a lower thermostat setting produces the same felt comfort. The documented range: 2-4°F below an equivalent forced-air setting per conservative industry sources, and 6-8°F per sources citing ASHRAE studies. The popular claim that 68°F radiant feels like 72°F forced air is one point inside that documented range, not a constant. This book states the range, 2-8°F, with the actual value depending on floor surface temperature, ceiling height, and the room's other surfaces. The HVAC, Ventilation, Humidity, and Air Quality chapter contains the setpoint schedule.

**The adaptive comfort band.** ASHRAE Standard 55's adaptive comfort model applies to naturally ventilated spaces whose occupants control operable windows; it was built from roughly 21,000 field data sets across 160 buildings on four continents. Its center line: comfort temperature in °C equals 0.31 times the prevailing mean outdoor temperature plus 17.8, bounded between 20.9°C and 28.2°C (roughly 69.6-82.8°F). The band widens as outdoor temperature rises, matching how occupants of operable-window buildings report comfort in the field. For this house the model applies in the shoulder seasons, when ventilation and shading do the comfort work and the mechanical system backs them up; the thermostat schedule widens seasonally instead of holding one fixed number year-round. In deep winter and peak summer, when the mechanical system leads, the fixed comfort band applies. The HVAC, Ventilation, Humidity, and Air Quality chapter contains the seasonal schedule.

**The cold-mass start.** The MRT mechanism runs in both directions: an uncharged concrete surface in deep winter produces radiant discomfort even at normal air temperature. Documented response times: wall mass lags outdoor temperature cycles by roughly 3-8 hours, and an in-slab radiant system takes several hours to warm from a cold start. The management is a control strategy, documented in radiant-heating guidance: overnight setbacks limited to 2-3°F at most, a steady setpoint through the heating season rather than deep swings, and controls that anticipate the lag instead of reacting to it. A true cold start (season opening, or return from a long vacancy) is planned as a gradual multi-day warm-up, not an overnight event. The exact hours-to-comfort figure for this house depends on the slab and system design and is not yet calculable.

**Where the mass is justified, stated as a decision.** This project's thermal mass is justified by three benefits, none of which depends on winter sun:

- summer night-flush cooling: the valley's 25-29°F summer swing and the ventilation section's method
- radiant comfort buffering: the setpoint range above
- outage resilience: indoor temperature drift over days rather than hours during a heating or cooling outage; the HVAC, Ventilation, Humidity, and Air Quality chapter states the outage case

Winter solar charging is the secondary and most conditional use, sized modestly per the passive solar heating section, because winter sun in this climate is unreliable. The What Concrete Uniquely Enables chapter states the same justification for every mass option it surveys; the two chapters are read together.

**Living with exposed mass.** Exposed concrete and CMU are hard, non-absorptive surfaces, and rooms built from them run reverberant: sound builds and carries more than in a drywall-and-carpet room. Documented mitigations keep the mass surface working: acoustical CMU (a unit with a molded face opening and internal sound-absorbing fill, a real product category), and absorption added elsewhere in the room (rugs, upholstered furniture, acoustic panels on non-mass surfaces). Room by room, exposure goes where reverberation is tolerable, and quiet-critical rooms get acoustic treatment or covered surfaces; the Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, and Livability chapter contains the finish schedule. Two aspects are undocumented in the project research: long-term cleaning and the day-to-day livability of exposed interior mass. No owner-builder account weighing them was found, and this book flags both as open questions rather than asserting either way.

**The Trombe wall: the decision and its record.** A Trombe wall (a sun-facing glazed mass wall that absorbs solar radiation by day and re-radiates it indoors after dark) is the classic passive storage device. The What Concrete Uniquely Enables chapter states its construction parameters. This chapter states the decision: none is built on a Willamette Valley site. The grounds:

1. Climate fit. The type's reference literature places it in sunny, high-diurnal-swing climates and states directly that it performs poorly in cloudy ones. Its working season here is the overcast season.
2. Documented field failures, from practitioner reports: single-room overheating while the rest of the house stays cold (missing heat distribution); summer overheating where the wall is unshaded; reverse thermosiphon at night; and condensation, dust fouling, and mold risk inside the cavity. In a vented design without one-way dampers, the airflow runs backward after sunset and cools the room through the cold glazing.
3. An unresolved detailing gap. No source documents an Oregon-specific vent, screening, and drainage detail managing the cavity's moisture, and practitioner opinion is split on venting at all. If the element is ever pursued, a building-science-literate engineer details the cavity with the same rigor the Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, and Radon chapter applies to vented assemblies.
4. A durability mismatch. The documented long-term pattern is abandonment: owners removing the glazing during window replacement to save money, and whole walls torn out within years over blocked views and lost light. No documented case was found of a planned multi-decade glazing replacement with the wall continuing in service. On a structure designed for multi-century service and legibility to future owners (the Designing for Centuries: Durability Engineering chapter), an element that owners historically defeat rather than maintain is a poor fit.
5. No construction cost figure was located for an owner-built or contracted version, so the comparison against added ordinary glazing could not even be priced.

A high-desert eastern Oregon site changes the climate item on this list and only that item; the owner re-runs the site's own diurnal-swing and sun data before the decision is revisited there.

## 9.8 Superinsulation And Mass Together

**Simple**

A superinsulated envelope and heavy interior mass are separate tools that combine well. The insulation cuts heat flow through the shell. The mass steadies the temperature inside it. Passive House practice supplies the envelope targets and the overheating test. This section adapts both to a concrete house in this climate.

**Form factor.** Passive House practice tracks the surface-to-volume ratio (S/V, the envelope area wrapping each unit of interior volume), with detached-house guidance at or below roughly 0.8 per meter: a compact form needs less envelope per unit of interior. Worked comparisons from the same sources: a compact two-story 10x10-meter footprint computes to roughly 0.77, and stacking more floors on the same footprint drives the ratio further down (a four-story block computes to roughly 0.37). The classic passive-solar tradition instead elongates the footprint east-west, at roughly 1.3-1.5 length to width, to maximize south wall area. The two rules pull in opposite directions, and the elongation figure traces to received design literature rather than to a primary study. For this climate the choice weights compactness: the S/V advantage applies during every heating hour, while the elongated form's payoff depends on winter sun that arrives less than half the possible hours. South-wall gain is taken as an opportunistic benefit of whichever face ends up south.

**Vertical massing.** A second story or a daylight basement adds floor area and volume with little added roof and foundation area, so it lowers S/V while also adding south-wall area at the same footprint width. It is the better tool than elongation for getting compactness and south exposure together. The Foundation, Basement, Slabs, and Concrete Placement chapter contains the daylight-basement and multi-story options. The S/V number for this house is computed once a floor plan exists.

**Certification targets.** PHIUS generates its prescriptive targets per city (heating and cooling design targets computed from local climate data through its own calculator), not as one number per climate zone. The published zone table states ranges only, and the project research could not extract the marine zone 4C table values. Once a parcel and its nearest represented city are known, the owner runs that city through the PHIUS calculator and compares the output against the envelope chapter's blower-door and HRV targets. Until then, no PHIUS target number is stated in this book. Certification is optional; the city-specific targets are a useful cross-check either way. The Salem precedent in the passive solar heating section demonstrates the approach locally: compactness, airtightness, and moderated glazing rather than gain-chasing.

**The overheating check.** The Passive House overheating criterion: indoor operative temperature above 25°C (77°F) for no more than 10 percent of occupied annual hours, roughly 876 hours per year, with careful designers targeting 5 percent, roughly 438 hours. Thermal mass and effective shading are the standard tools for meeting it. The check runs in an hourly energy model (PHPP or an equivalent hourly tool) against real weather data, once the floor plan, window schedule, and shading design exist. It is the deciding instrument for every glazing-versus-shading trade this chapter has qualified: the glazing ratios, the shading tiers, and the earth-sheltered exposed face all pass or fail on this run, not on rules of thumb. The Gaddy precedent in the shading section documents the cost of skipping it. After occupancy the same criterion becomes a measured check against logged data; the problems section states the protocol.

## 9.10 Problems And Solutions

**Simple**

Passive designs fail in known, repeated ways. Most of the failures below come from real practitioner reports, not theory. Each entry names the problem and the next step. The last part of this section is the measurement protocol that catches the rest.

**One room overheats while the rest of the house stays cold.** Captured solar heat is not moving past the room that caught it; this is the documented number-one field failure of storage walls, and it applies to any heavily glazed south room. The fix is distribution: interior doors, transoms, or louvers on the airflow path, or thermostat-controlled transfer fans moving warm air to the rest of the plan. The airflow path is checked on the drawings at design time.

**The house overheats in April and October but not in July.** The fixed overhang admits low shoulder-season sun; an opening that admits February sun admits October sun at the same angle. The precedent in the shading section documents peak shoulder loads at three times system capacity in one modeled case. The fix: adjustable exterior shading deployed through warm shoulder weeks, and glazing ratios kept conservative in the first place. The logged hour-count below verifies the result.

**The concrete stays cold for days after a winter absence.** This is the documented cold-mass start. The fix is a control strategy, not more heat: setbacks no deeper than 2-3°F, a steady heating-season setpoint, and a planned multi-day warm-up after any long vacancy.

**Night flushing falls out of use.** Where opening the house at night reads as a security risk, the openings stay shut and the strategy stops. The fix is hardware that removes the trade-off: security mesh over the main openings, operable clerestories secure by height, and motorized openings running the flush on the local automation without a nightly decision.

**The HRV runs against open windows.** The consequence is lost efficiency, not damage. The code-required manual override pauses the system for an open-window evening; a window-contact-sensor link automates the pause in the shoulder seasons.

**Smoke arrives on a flush night.** The PM2.5 threshold rule in the ventilation section closes the motorized openings, alerts on the manual ones, and switches the HRV to filtration. The owner arms the rule at the start of each fire season and tests it once.

**The shade tree does not do its job.** Bare winter limbs still block low sun, and leaf drop can arrive after the heating season starts. Oregon white oak is a documented poor fit. The fix is verification before planting: the owner observes a mature bare specimen, checks local leaf-drop timing, and consults an arborist or the OSU Extension office.

**The vine damages its pergola.** English ivy's aerial roots degrade structural timber, and wet-soil kiwi plantings rot at the crown. The fix: deciduous, non-destructive species (hardy kiwi, grape), a mounded or sloped root zone, and a concrete or CMU structure that removes the rot question.

**Glare appears on the new floor.** A polished light floor under a bright window reflects the window into the eye. The fix is specified before installation: matte or low-sheen finishes on the floors of daylight-dependent rooms, at the same light color.

**The great room echoes.** Exposed mass reverberates. The fix: absorption on non-mass surfaces (rugs, furnishings, ceiling panels), acoustical CMU where a room needs both exposure and quiet, and finish-schedule coordination room by room with the Interiors chapter.

**A code-compliant room is dark at its center.** The 8 percent glazing minimum counts area, not placement. The fix is geometric: higher head heights or a clerestory per the daylighting depth rule, and ceiling and wall finishes at the specified reflectance values.

**CC&Rs surface at title review with power over the passive design.** Oregon's statute protects solar panels only; glazing area, orientation, roof form, and planting pass through ordinary design review. The fix happens before the offer: the owner reads the CC&Rs and prices the constraint into the parcel decision.

**The neighbor's trees grow into the winter sun path.** Without a recorded easement or a local ordinance, no legal remedy exists. The protections are chosen at siting time: distance from the line, and a recorded solar easement where a willing neighbor exists.

**The window order arrives with the wrong glass.** SHGC, U-factor, and VT are per-orientation values in this design, and a substitution erases them silently. The fix: the values are written per opening on the order, and the NFRC labels (the standard window rating labels) are checked against the schedule at delivery, before installation.

### Verifying the as-built design

The three records below verify the design assumptions against measured data.

**Applied: the logging protocol**

1. Buy two standalone temperature and humidity data loggers (HOBO-class; Bluetooth models download to a free phone app). Price them from the manufacturer's listing at order.
2. Mount one logger on the exposed mass surface most central to the design: the radiant slab, or a south interior mass wall.
3. Place the second at a representative room location, away from mass surfaces and windows.
4. Log hourly for a full year, covering both the heating and cooling seasons. Download the data seasonally.
5. File the records in the project binder next to the design assumptions they test: the night-flush air-change target, the overheating criterion, and the diurnal-swing figures.

**Pre-cover photographs.** The pre-cover photography discipline in the Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, and Radon chapter extends to four passive details, each photographed with a tape measure or fixed reference object in frame at the last stage it is accessible:

- overhang projection and the sill-to-underside vertical distance, before gutters and finish materials
- clerestory head height, at framing
- shading-fin projection and spacing, before trim closes around them
- any storage-wall vent or damper detail, if one is ever built despite this chapter's findings

**The one-year check.** After the first full year of occupancy, the owner counts the logged hours above 25°C at the room logger. The comparison: 876 hours at the 10 percent limit, 438 at the stricter 5 percent target. A failing count, correlated with the orientation the afternoon spikes track, starts the correction ladder. An exterior shading retrofit on the flagged orientation comes first as the fastest fix, planting second at years to mature, and glazing reduction at a future window-replacement cycle last. A winter pattern of the mass-surface logger running well below the room logger for multi-day stretches after cold starts points at the control strategy (setbacks too deep), not at the shading. The annual record is filed under the record-keeping system in the Documentation, Maintenance, and Future Upgrades chapter.
