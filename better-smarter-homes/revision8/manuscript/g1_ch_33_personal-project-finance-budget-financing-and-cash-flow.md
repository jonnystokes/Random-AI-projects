# Chapter 33: Personal Project Finance: Budget, Financing, and Cash Flow

A construction lender pays for this house after the fact. Each draw is released once the lender's inspector has seen a phase finished, so the owner carries the cost of every phase until then, and a cash build has no draws at all. Few lenders write owner-builder loans, and those that do want 25 to 35 percent down against 10 to 20 on a contractor-built house. Behind either route sits a budget built from the cost ranges in the system chapters, stage by stage, with 10 to 20 percent on top for the unknowns, because owner-builders overrun by up to 30 percent where professional builders overrun by 5 to 15. Interest, property tax and insurance then run for every month the build lasts, and a solo build runs two to four times as long as a crew's.

> **Jon's note.** [JON: drafted from the record, confirm or rewrite] I am building most of this house myself, over several years, and making my own block on the site. A solo build runs two to four times as long as a crew's, so the loan interest, the property tax and the insurance run two to four times as long too. That puts a price on each day of my own time, before any helper's wage is counted. The land is bought by note and trust deed rather than a land sale contract, so a construction lender can take a first lien on it if I want one. [JON: cash, a construction loan, or cash with a loan approved and held in reserve?] [JON: if an appraiser cannot value a block house with no nearby sales, is cash in phases your fallback?] [JON: how many months of carrying cost do you want in the bank before breaking ground?]

## 33.1 The whole-project number

**Simple**

Your budget is one number made of hundreds of smaller ones, each from a quote, a listing or a published cost with its year attached. Builders split a new house into eight stages, from the site work through the shell to the finishes and the driveway. The finishes take the largest share, a quarter of the cost. In a concrete house the framing stage disappears into the walls and the roof deck, so the shell takes a larger share and the finishes a smaller one. Your own hours cost nothing in cash. The pallet of block you buy costs more than the same pallet costs a contractor with an account. That difference takes back part of the markup you save by not hiring one.

**Applied**

1. Start from the cost ranges each system chapter states, and copy each into one list with its source and its year.
2. Sort the lines into the eight stages in the table, so each stage can be checked against the published share.
3. Add the soft costs builders count under site work, meaning permit and system development charges, the engineer and surveyor, and the utility connections.
4. Add the land, its closing costs and the carrying costs of the months before construction starts.
5. Price your own labor at zero and every hired day at the local wage from two quotes.
6. Price materials at the counter price for your volume, never at a contractor's account price.
7. Re-quote any material line over $1,000 and any subcontract over $5,000 within 30 days of ordering.
8. Add the three contingency buckets and the time reserve as lines of their own, never as one lump at the end.
9. Lay the stages against the months of the build schedule, so the budget is phased the way a draw schedule is inspected.
10. Keep the list as the budget of record, dated, and log every change against it.

**Professional**

Costs are 2026 figures unless a different year is given. NAHB's 2024 Construction Cost Survey, built from 41 builders' reports of their typical single-family house, puts construction at $430,000 for 2,650 square feet of finished area, $160 a square foot, and at 64.4 percent of a $670,000 sales price. Its eight stages and their shares are the check a phased budget is read against.

| Stage | Share of construction cost, 2024 | What the stage holds |
|---|---|---|
| Site work | 7.6 percent | Building permit fees, impact fees, water and sewer fees, architecture and engineering |
| Foundations | 10.5 percent | Excavation, foundation, concrete, retaining walls and backfill |
| Framing | 16.6 percent | Framing including the roof, trusses, sheathing, steel |
| Exterior finishes | 13.4 percent | Exterior wall finish, roofing, windows and doors |
| Major systems rough-ins | 19.2 percent | Plumbing, electrical and HVAC, each near 6.3 percent |
| Interior finishes | 24.1 percent | Insulation, drywall, trim, paint, lighting, cabinets, appliances, flooring, fixtures |
| Final steps | 6.5 percent | Landscaping, outdoor structures, the driveway at 2.3 percent, clean-up |
| Other | 2.1 percent | Unassigned costs |

A concrete house has no framing stage. The 16.6 percent of a wood-frame budget that buys studs, trusses and sheathing moves into the foundation and shell lines, where the walls, the floor decks and the roof deck are poured or laid, and the exterior-finish share shrinks where the wall is its own finish. The shares test the shape of the estimate, and each line still comes from its own quote.

Acting as the general contractor saves the contractor's markup. Angi's markup article puts it at 15 to 20 percent of project cost, with 20 to 30 percent the 2026 market average and over 30 percent of builders marking up 25 percent or more. The Benton Builders' blog, a contractor's page, nets the saving on a $200,000 project. Markup avoided comes to $20,000 to $40,000. Lost volume pricing takes back 5 to 15 percent of that saving, rework and unforeseen problems add 10 to 30 percent to total cost, and $20,000 to $30,000 is left. On a build where cement, aggregate, block and reinforcing steel are most of the cost, the lost volume pricing falls on the largest lines, so the surviving share of the saving is smaller than on a wood-frame house. Owner labor is free in cash and absent from the tax basis, and it is paid for in calendar days, each with a carrying cost.

## 33.2 Contingency: three buckets and a time reserve

**Simple**

A contingency is money set aside for costs you cannot name yet, and the right amount shrinks as the drawings improve. Early sketches need 15 to 25 percent on top of the estimate, finished drawings 5 to 10, and construction itself 3 to 5. Owner-builders overrun by up to 30 percent, where professional builders overrun by 5 to 15, so your cushion sits at the top of each range. A single lump sum gets spent on the first overrun and leaves nothing for a later phase. Split it into three labeled pots: changes you choose, problems the ground or the work turns up, and gaps in the design. A fourth pot pays the interest, tax and insurance of the months a solo build runs past its plan.

**Applied**

1. Set the design bucket at 15 to 25 percent of the estimate while the drawings are schematic.
2. Cut the design bucket to 5 to 10 percent at complete construction documents, and to 3 to 5 once construction starts.
3. Set the construction-unknowns bucket at 20 percent for the shell, the top of the range for complex work, and hold it until the roof is on.
4. Set the scope-change bucket from the household's written list of open choices, priced from the finish schedules.
5. Hold a time reserve equal to six months of the daily carrying cost from the cash-flow worksheet, in its own account.
6. Give each bucket its own budget line, and draw against it in writing with the date and the cause.
7. Move money between buckets only with a dated note, never by overspending one.
8. Review the four buckets at each draw, and refill the construction-unknowns bucket before the next pour.
9. Treat a bucket that empties before its phase ends as a stop point, and re-plan the phase before spending further.
10. After move-in, fund maintenance the same way, with a yearly set-aside of each component's replacement cost divided by its remaining life.

**Professional**

The percentages come from the contingency guides of Mastt and Procore, two construction-management software companies, and the owner-builder overrun figure from calculatorconstruction.com, a construction-calculator website, so they are planning ranges rather than measured results for a house like this one. The American Institute of Architects' article Managing the Contingency Allowance makes the case for labeled buckets: one undifferentiated allowance invites overruns and disputes over who may spend it, and an owner's scope changes, construction unknowns and design gaps are three risks drawn down by three different people. Buildern's blog adds the tracking rule, each bucket a budget line with logged drawdowns.

Each bucket is allocated by phase rather than held whole, so a foundation overrun draws the foundation's share and leaves the finish phase's share intact, and the phased budget shows a lender the same split. The fourth bucket exists because a solo build's risk is calendar risk: the phases run longer than a crew's, and interest, property tax, insurance and interim housing accrue with the calendar whether or not the work moves. The six-month figure is the plan's own choice, carried in Open items until the first year's cash-flow sheet replaces it. After occupancy the same discipline becomes a component sinking fund, an annual set-aside equal to each component's replacement cost divided by the years it has left, computed from the replacement horizons in the documentation chapter.

## 33.3 The resilience line: no cost-share, one budget line

**Simple**

The safe room and the stored water, fuel and supplies cost the least when the shell is poured, because the reinforcing, the tank pad and the shelving go in with everything else. Added after move-in, the same scope means cutting into finished walls and floors. Federal safe-room money flows to states and counties rather than to households, and the building standard it requires is written for tornado wind. A room built against a Cascadia earthquake earns no share of it. The resilience scope is therefore a line of your own money, labeled, beside the house.

**Applied**

| Item built in at construction | What the built-in version includes | What a retrofit adds | Where the figure lives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Safe room cast into the shell | The rated door and frame, a debris-rated vent, any added bars the engineer calls for | Demolition of finishes and a new structural box inside a finished house | Structural System and Concrete Design chapter |
| Water reserve (cistern, tank, hand pump) | The tank, its pad or vault, and the plumbing tie-in at rough-in | Excavation beside a finished house and new penetrations | Water Independence chapter |
| Power and fuel (battery, generator, propane store) | The equipment, its pad and the fuel tank set with the site work | Trenching and pads cut into finished grade | Solar, Battery, and Energy Independence chapter |
| Food and medical stores | Shelving and a cool room drawn into the plan | A room taken from another use | Resilience and Disaster Independence chapter |

1. Price each item in the table twice, built in now and added after occupancy with the demolition and finish work a retrofit takes.
2. Carry the built-in total as one labeled budget line, so the household weighs it against the finishes.
3. Budget no federal or state cost-share toward a seismic safe room.
4. Where a wind-rated room is wanted as well, ask the county emergency manager whether a current hazard-mitigation program could include the parcel, and expect no.
5. Put the safe room's door, vent and bars on the structural drawings, so their cost sits in the shell's draw.

**Professional**

FEMA's Hazard Mitigation Grant Program takes applications from states and tribes, with local governments and qualifying nonprofits as subapplicants, and Oregon's Office of Emergency Management states that individual homeowners may not apply directly, though a local government may apply on their behalf. The program's safe-room standard is ICC 500, with FEMA P-361 for federally funded rooms, and FEMA's own ICC 500 highlights document describes a tornado and hurricane standard with no seismic design in it. A Cascadia-designed room therefore sits outside the one federal funding path, and Oregon's hazard-mitigation grants run through that same program.

FEMA P-320's cost figures assume a wood-framed house around an independent reinforced box: $9,400 to $13,000 for an 8-by-8-foot room and $19,000 to $26,000 for 14 by 14. In this shell the box is already the house, and the marginal cost is the rated door and frame, the debris-rated vent and whatever added reinforcing the engineer of record finds necessary, which the structural chapter prices. A retrofit on a finished house carries demolition, access and finish-matching that first construction does not, and the roll-up's totals wait on the sources its last column names.

**Code**

```
Federal hazard-mitigation grants go to governments, not to households.
  Applicants are states and Indian tribal governments, and subapplicants are state agencies, local governments, qualifying private nonprofits and tribal governments.
  Source: 44 CFR 206.434(a).
```

## 33.4 Build it once: the driveway

**Simple**

Some parts of the site are cheapest when built right the first time, and the driveway is the plainest case. Concrete trucks and loaded delivery trucks weigh more than 14,000 pounds, and the base under them needs 14 to 18 inches of road rock on good ground, against 5 or 6 inches for a car. Road base costs a fifth to a third more per ton than ordinary gravel. A thin base under those trucks ruts and heaves, and a rutted driveway in the week of a pour stops the pour. Built to the heavy standard before the first delivery, the base lasts a century with a top-up of gravel now and then.

**Applied**

1. Build the driveway base to the heavy-vehicle depth, 14 to 18 inches of road base on a good subgrade, before the first concrete or block delivery.
2. Get one quote for the full base and one for a light base plus a later upgrade, for the actual length and soil.
3. Put the driveway in the site-work phase of the budget and in the first draw.
4. Budget $100 to $400 a year for grading and gravel after the build.

**Professional**

Bovees' driveway-base guide gives the depths: 14 to 18 inches of total base on a good subgrade for concrete and large delivery trucks, against 5 to 6 inches for passenger cars. HelloGravel's 2026 road-base cost guide prices road base at $20 to $35 a ton against $15 to $25 for ordinary gravel. Both are gravel sellers' web guides, so the project's own figure is a local contractor's quote on the real length and soil. An under-built base fails under load by rutting and frost heave, and the load arrives early in this build: the concrete trucks for the footings and slab and the block deliveries for the walls come in the first year, in the wet months when a subgrade is weakest. NAHB's 2024 survey puts the driveway at 2.3 percent of a conventional house's construction cost, $9,600 on the average house. The driveway's drainage, geotextile and surface are in the Developing the Site chapter.

## 33.5 Cash, a construction loan, or both

**Simple**

You can pay for the build from savings, phase by phase, or borrow against a house that does not exist yet. Borrowing takes an owner-builder construction loan, which few lenders write, because the lender is funding a first-time general contractor. The lenders that do write them want a quarter to a third of the cost down, plans, an itemized budget and a record of what you have built before. The loan pays out in draws after each inspection and turns into an ordinary mortgage at the end. Building from cash removes the lender, the appraisal and the draw inspector. It also means the whole budget sits in the bank before the first pour.

**Applied**

1. Choose the route before the land closes, whether cash in phases, a construction-to-permanent loan, or cash with an approved loan held in reserve.
2. For a loan, call portfolio lenders and credit unions first, and ask each whether it lends to an owner-builder at all.
3. Ask the three questions the legal chapter lists on the appraisal, the draw inspector and the block-production milestones.
4. Bring plans and specifications, the phased budget with its contingency lines, two years of tax returns, the land's deed and an experience letter.
5. Where a side business files on the same returns, ask how its income and expenses enter the debt-to-income figure and the draw conditions.
6. Expect the Loan Estimate within three business days of applying and the Closing Disclosure three business days before closing, and read both against the budget.
7. Ask whether the construction and permanent phases are disclosed as one transaction or two, and how the permanent rate is set at conversion.
8. Ask whether land equity counts toward the down payment, and at what value.
9. Check the parcel against the flood map before applying, because a lender must require flood insurance inside a mapped hazard area.
10. On farm or forest zoning, add a Farm Credit association and the agricultural lenders to the call list.
11. Record each lender's answer in the binder with the date and the person's name.

**Professional**

An owner-builder construction loan is in most cases a construction-to-permanent product: one closing, interest-only payments on the drawn balance during construction, draws released against inspections, and conversion to a mortgage at completion without a second closing. Nav's self-build loan guide describes it that way and adds that few lenders offer the product and that it takes stronger credit, a larger down payment and documented construction experience. Biglaw Investor's Oregon construction-lender page, a lender marketplace, details six lenders. Goldwater Bank requires a third-party general contractor, Wintrust Mortgage requires an approved builder, and US Bank states that it cannot work with owner-builders. Oregon State Credit Union's one-time-close loan starts at 20 percent down with interest-only payments during construction, WaFd Bank handles draws and inspections itself, and Umpqua Bank is listed without terms. Minimum credit scores on that page run from 620 to 720.

The federal banking agencies' real-estate lending guidelines set a supervisory loan-to-value ceiling for a house under construction, and a bank that lends past it counts the excess against its capital, so the down payment on a contractor-built house starts at that ceiling and an owner-builder lender asks for more. Regulation Z treats a construction loan to a consumer, secured by the land, as closed-end credit secured by real property, so the Loan Estimate and the Closing Disclosure apply to it, and a construction-to-permanent loan may be disclosed as one transaction or as two, one for each phase. Where a lender expects a new-construction loan to settle more than 60 days after the Loan Estimate, under 12 CFR 1026.19(e)(3)(iv)(F) it may revise the estimate only if the first one said in plain words that it might.

A land sale contract leaves legal title with the seller until the price is paid, so the lender has no first lien position to take until the contract is paid off or subordinated, which is why the land is bought by note and trust deed. A side business on the same tax returns is read with them: a lender qualifying income and debt from personal returns sees the business's income and expenses there, so its treatment is settled at the application. On exclusive farm use and forest land, Whitney Land Company's and Loankea's land-loan guides state that a parcel zoned for farm use may not qualify for a standard land loan at all, and that rural Oregon land lending runs through credit unions, community banks, agricultural lenders and land-loan brokers.

**Code**

```
A bank's construction loan on a house has a supervisory loan-to-value ceiling.
  85 percent of value for one- to four-family residential construction, 75 percent for land development and 65 percent for raw land, with loans above the ceilings limited in aggregate to 100 percent of the bank's capital.
  Source: 12 CFR part 34, subpart D, appendix A (Interagency Guidelines for Real Estate Lending Policies), supervisory loan-to-value limits table.

A consumer gets a Loan Estimate within three business days of applying for a construction loan.
  Delivered or placed in the mail not later than the third business day after the creditor receives the application, and not later than the seventh business day before consummation.
  Source: 12 CFR 1026.19(e)(1)(iii)(A) and (B).

The Closing Disclosure arrives three business days before the loan closes.
  Received by the consumer not later than three business days before consummation.
  Source: 12 CFR 1026.19(f)(1)(ii)(A).

A construction-to-permanent loan may be disclosed as one transaction or two.
  Where a multiple-advance loan to finance construction of a dwelling may be permanently financed by the same creditor, the construction phase and the permanent phase may be treated as one transaction or more than one.
  Source: 12 CFR 1026.17(c)(6)(ii).

A regulated lender must require flood insurance on a building in a mapped flood hazard area.
  No loan secured by improved real estate in a special flood hazard area where flood insurance is available unless the building is insured for the loan term, at the lesser of the loan balance or the maximum coverage available.
  Source: 42 U.S.C. 4012a(b)(1)(A).
```

## 33.6 Draws, draw inspections and lien waivers

**Simple**

A draw is a payment from the loan for work already finished. You send the lender the invoices, the photos and the signed waivers from everyone you paid. The lender sends an inspector to confirm the phase is done, and the money arrives afterward. That inspector works for the lender and checks dollars against progress, which is a different visit from the county's code inspection. Behind the waivers sits Oregon's lien law. Anyone who supplies labor or material to your house and is not paid can record a claim against it, even when you paid the person who ordered the work. The waivers are your proof that the whole chain was paid.

**Applied**

| Draw | Phase finished | What the lender's inspector sees | Documents in the packet |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Site work and foundation | Footings, stem walls and slab poured and backfilled, underground utilities in | Excavation and concrete invoices, pour tickets, the county's foundation approval, waivers |
| 2 | Shell to the bond beam | Walls grouted to the top course, lintels set | Block, grout and reinforcing invoices, the block-production log and test reports, wall inspection approvals, waivers |
| 3 | Roof and dry-in | Roof deck placed, membrane on, windows and doors in | Roofing and window invoices, roofing approval, waivers |
| 4 | Rough-ins | Plumbing, electrical and mechanical rough-ins approved | Trade invoices and permits, rough-in approvals, waivers |
| 5 | Insulation and interior finishes | Insulation approved, drywall and finishes done | Finish invoices, insulation approval, waivers |
| 6 | Final | Certificate of occupancy issued, site finished | Final approval, the certificate, final waivers from every payee |

1. Agree the draw schedule with the lender before closing, written as the table, with each draw tied to a phase the inspector can see from the ground.
2. Where owner-made block is the wall, brief the inspector in writing on the production log, the test reports and what a finished course looks like.
3. Before each draw, assemble the packet: invoices, dated photos, the county's approvals for the phase, and a waiver from every payee.
4. Pay each supplier and subcontractor by check or transfer, and collect a conditional waiver with the payment and an unconditional one when it clears.
5. File every Notice of Right to a Lien the day it arrives, with the sender, the date and the person who ordered the work.
6. Answer an unfamiliar notice with a written demand for the list of materials and charges, sent by certified mail.
7. Where a supplier's notice names a subcontractor you paid, pay the balance by joint check to both.
8. Hold the final payment to each subcontractor until its 75-day lien window has run or its waiver is in hand.
9. Before any pause in the work that could pass 74 days, ask the lender and the construction attorney whether to post and record a nonabandonment notice.
10. When the last subcontractor has finished, post and record a completion notice and keep the recording receipt.
11. If a claim of lien is recorded, get the attorney's read within the week, and keep the bond route in reserve.

**Professional**

Nav's guide puts the count at five to seven draws over a build, with a lender inspection before each and a fee of $100 to $150 a visit charged to the borrower. WaFd Bank's builder guide for its Built platform describes the cycle from the lender's side: an inspection releases funds in proportion to progress, a draw requests the released funds, and the lender's policy decides whether the borrower may request an inspection or the lender schedules them. The inspector's percent-complete figure caps the draw, so whether delivered materials count before installation is the lender's stored-materials rule, asked before the first order. The legal chapter covers the draw inspector's standard and the Information Notice to Owner a contractor delivers at signing.

Lien law is why the packet carries waivers. A perfected construction lien on the improvement outranks the construction mortgage, with the statute's own exceptions, so the lender wants proof at every draw that the people who could lien the house have been paid. The statute exempts anyone who furnishes labor or materials at the owner's own request from the notice of right to a lien, so the subcontractors and suppliers the owner hires directly send none, and their suppliers and their subcontractors must. A supplier keeps its priority over the lender only by notifying the lender in the same window it has for the owner, and the lender may demand the supplier's list, as the owner may. The lien law prescribes a form for the notice and for the Information Notice to Owner and none for a waiver, and the one waiver it compels is a paid supplier's, on demand. Waivers are therefore contract documents in the lender's or the title company's form, conditional while the check is uncleared and unconditional after, one per payee per payment.

For lien purposes construction is complete at substantial completion, at a recorded completion notice, or when the improvement is abandoned, and the statute treats a pause past the 74th day without work as abandonment unless a nonabandonment notice is posted and recorded first. A solo build's winter pause can cross that line, which starts every claimant's filing window, so the lender and the construction attorney decide before the pause whether the notice is posted. A recorded claim clouds the lender's title, and the next draw can wait until the claim is released, bonded or has lapsed.

## 33.7 Cash flow across the years: the float and the cost of a day

**Simple**

The lender pays after the inspection, so you pay first. Every phase's materials and hired labor leave your account weeks before the draw puts the money back, and that gap is the float you need in cash beyond the down payment. A phase that takes a solo builder two to four times as long as a crew stretches the gap by the same factor. Some costs run with the calendar rather than the work. Interest on what you have drawn, the property tax, the insurance and the rent where you live now all arrive by the month whether or not a block was laid. Added up and divided by the days, they give the price of one idle day.

**Applied**

| Line | How to figure it | Where the number comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Construction interest | The drawn balance times the rate, divided by 365 | The lender's rate and the draw schedule |
| Land loan payment | The monthly payment divided by 30, while a separate land loan runs | The land loan note |
| Property tax | The last statement divided by 365, stepped up each January for the completed share | The tax statement and the assessor's rate |
| Builder's risk premium | The annual premium divided by the days insured | The policy |
| Housing elsewhere | Rent or mortgage divided by 30 | The lease or statement |
| Site services | Temporary power, water hauling, the container rental and site security, divided by 30 | The utility and rental bills |
| Total per day | The sum of the lines | Re-run after every draw |

1. Build a month-by-month sheet from groundbreaking to the certificate of occupancy, with columns for planned spending, the draw expected and the cash balance.
2. Set the float at one full phase's cost plus the lender's stated days from inspection to funding.
3. Add the draw-inspection fee and any title update fee to each draw month.
4. Enter the carrying-cost lines from the table, and total them by month and by day.
5. Multiply the per-day figure by the days each phase runs on the build schedule, and carry the product in the phased budget as a line of its own.
6. Re-run the sheet after every draw with actual figures, and compare the float to the next phase's cost before ordering materials.
7. Where a month's balance goes negative, move a materials order later rather than paying a supplier late.
8. Keep the time reserve in a separate account from the float, and spend it on carrying costs only.

[FIGURE: cash-flow-curve, month-by-month spending, draws and the float balance for a six-draw build, with the carrying cost per day along the bottom]

**Professional**

The timing problem is reimbursement. A draw releases after a completed phase is inspected, so the owner funds the phase's materials and any hired labor first, and the float has to cover a whole phase plus the lender's lag rather than the budget spread evenly over the months. Lenders differ on whether interest during construction is paid monthly from the owner's account or drawn from an interest reserve built into the loan, and the answer changes the float by the interest.

An illustration with round inputs shows the scale. A $300,000 construction loan at 8 percent, drawn in six equal draws across a 24-month build, carries an average balance near $125,000 and costs $20,000 in interest before conversion. The same loan stretched across 36 months costs $30,000, so each extra year costs $10,000 at that average balance, $27 a day, and once the loan is fully drawn the interest-only payment is $2,000 a month. The table carries the illustration through the other lines.

| Line in the illustration | Input | Per day |
|---|---|---|
| Construction interest | $125,000 average balance at 8 percent | $27 |
| Property tax | $3,000 a year on the land and the partial house | $8 |
| Builder's risk premium | $105 a month, Insureon's average | $4 |
| Housing elsewhere | $1,500 a month in rent | $50 |
| Site services | $200 a month | $7 |
| Total | | $96 |

Every input is replaced by the project's own figure in the worksheet. The result's shape holds regardless: interim housing and loan interest are the two largest lines, and both run by the calendar, so a month saved anywhere in the schedule returns near $3,000 in the illustration before any wage is counted.

## 33.8 Property tax during the build

**Simple**

The county taxes what stands on the land each January 1. A house that is half built on that morning is taxed as half a house for the following year, and work started after January 1 is taxed as bare land until the next one. Each new piece of value joins the tax roll at a discount set by the county's ratio, and from then on the taxable value can climb three percent a year. The statement arrives in October. It is due in thirds in November, February and May, with a discount for paying all of it in November. A wrong value can be appealed to the county's appeals board until the end of December.

**Applied**

1. Mark January 1 on the build calendar, and photograph the site's state in that week for the binder.
2. Read the assessor's percent-complete figure on the value notice against those photographs.
3. Petition the property value appeals board on a wrong figure between the statement's mailing and December 31.
4. Budget each build year's tax from the prior statement plus the completed share at the county's rate per $1,000.
5. Pay the whole year by November 15 for the discount when the float allows, and in thirds otherwise.
6. On a farm- or forest-deferred parcel, get the assessor's potential-additional-tax figure before the dwelling approval.
7. Ask the assessor when the homesite comes out of deferral and when the disqualification tax falls due.
8. Keep every tax statement and assessor notice with the January photographs.

**Professional**

Oregon's assessment year is the calendar year and its tax year begins the following July 1, so what the assessor records on January 1 is billed in October of the same year. The real market value standard and the assessor's statistical model for an unusual house are in the legal chapter. Clackamas County's assessor FAQ states the practice for a house in progress: new construction is valued at the percent completed on January 1, a house 50 percent complete is valued at half its market value, and a house started after January 1 pays tax only on the land until the following year.

Two constitutional measures bound the bill from different sides. Measure 50 limits how fast a property's taxable value grows and admits new improvements at their market value times the county's changed property ratio, so a half-built house enters the roll at a fraction of its market value and compounds from there at the capped rate. Measure 5 caps the rate charged on real market value for schools and for other government, with voter-approved bonds outside both caps. A multi-year build therefore reaches the roll in pieces, each January's completed share entering as new improvements at that year's ratio. Outbuildings added later enter the same way unless they fall under the minor-construction threshold, which leaves the maximum assessed value alone. The potential additional tax on a farm- or forest-deferred parcel falls due when the homesite is disqualified, and the assessor gives the figure before the dwelling approval.

**Code**

```
Property is assessed as it stands on January 1.
  The assessor records the value of all taxable property as of January 1 at 1:00 a.m. of the assessment year, and that calendar year's assessment corresponds to the tax year beginning July 1.
  Source: ORS 308.210(1); ORS 308.007(1) and (2).

Taxable value grows no more than three percent a year once set.
  Maximum assessed value is the greater of 103 percent of the prior year's assessed value or 100 percent of the prior maximum, and assessed value is the lesser of that maximum and real market value.
  Source: ORS 308.146(1) and (2).

  The maximum assessed value adds the new improvements' real market value multiplied by the ratio, not above 1.00, of average maximum assessed value to average real market value for the property class.
  Source: ORS 308.153(1) and (2)(a).

Small additions do not reset the maximum assessed value.
  Minor construction, meaning real property improvements worth no more than $18,200 in an assessment year or $45,000 over five years, indexed from 2025, is not new property.
  Source: ORS 308.149(5) and (6)(b).

  One-third on or before November 15, February 15 and May 15, with interest at one and one-third percent a month on a late installment.
  Source: ORS 311.250(1); ORS 311.505(1) and (2).

  Three percent off for paying the whole year by November 15, and two percent off two-thirds paid by that date.
```

## 33.9 Insurance during the build

**Simple**

A homeowner's policy does not exist for a house that is not built yet. The policy for a building under construction is builder's risk, bought by you as the owner-builder and priced as a share of the finished value. It pays for fire, theft, vandalism and storm damage to the structure and to materials on the site or on their way to it. It does not pay for an earthquake or a flood unless you buy that on top, and in the Cascadia zone the shell is at its weakest while that exclusion applies. Your tools, your own injuries and a paid helper's injuries each need a different policy.

**Applied**

| Coverage | What it covers | Where it is settled |
|---|---|---|
| Builder's risk | The structure under construction, and materials on site, in transit or in temporary storage | The insurance agent, before the first delivery |
| Earthquake and flood endorsements | The two excluded perils, by endorsement or a separate policy | The agent, with the builder's-risk quote |
| Inland marine (tools and equipment) | Owned tools and machines at the site and in transit | Tools, Equipment, and the Owner-Builder Shop chapter |
| Disability insurance | The owner's income after an injury | The Owner-Builder's Body and Mind chapter |
| Liability and workers' compensation | Injuries to people on the site, and paid helpers | Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules chapter |

1. Get the builder's-risk quote on the total insured construction value before the first material delivery, with the policy starting at that delivery.
2. Ask for the earthquake endorsement and its price, and the flood endorsement where the map requires it.
3. Ask whether materials stored in the site container are covered under the on-site or temporarily-stored wording, and get the answer in writing.
4. Ask whether a soft-costs endorsement is offered on an owner-builder policy, and what delay costs it would pay.
5. Set the policy term to the solo schedule rather than a crew's, and diary the renewal.
6. Convert to a homeowner's policy at the certificate of occupancy, and tell the construction lender the date.
7. Keep the declarations page, the endorsements and the agent's written answers in the binder.

**Professional**

Insureon's figures, drawn from the policies its 100,000 small-business customers bought, put builder's risk at an average of $105 a month, with 49 percent of buyers paying under $100 and the premium at 1 to 4 percent of the completed structure's value. Wexford Insurance's 2026 pricing guide gives 1 to 5 percent of total construction value, and Hotaling Insurance Services' 2026 guide states that on residential new construction the owner buys the policy and that single-family rates were softening as carriers entered the segment. Coverage runs to the structure under construction and to materials on site, in transit or in temporary storage, the wording a site container is covered under, confirmed in writing by the agent for the policy bought.

Earthquake and flood are sold as endorsements or separate policies, and US Assure lists both among the optional endorsements on its residential and commercial builder's-risk policies. Faulty workmanship and design are excluded, with the damage that results often covered, wear, rust and mechanical breakdown are excluded, injuries belong to workers' compensation, and contractor tools are excluded unless added, per Wexford's guide. On a Cascadia site the earthquake exclusion is the one that matters, because the policy runs through the months when the shell is least complete. US Assure defines the soft-costs endorsement as the delay expenses a covered loss causes, naming construction-loan interest, property tax assessments, architects' and engineers' fees and permit fees, and notes that the examples vary among providers. Its loss-of-rents endorsement is the one written for a commercial owner. Whether a residential owner-builder policy offers soft costs is a question for the agent, and the time reserve stands in for it meanwhile.

## 33.10 What a day of help is worth

**Simple**

Hiring a tender for a day costs a wage and saves the carrying cost of every day the wall finishes sooner. On a solo build the wall rises at the speed of one person mixing, carrying and laying by turns. A tender keeps the mortar and the block coming, so the mason lays all day. The saving is largest on the busiest laying days and once the wall is above five feet, where carrying takes the most time. A paid day pays for itself when the carrying cost of the days it saves is larger than the wage, and on a low course it saves less than it costs.

**Applied**

1. Take the daily carrying cost from the cash-flow sheet.
2. Get two local day rates for a tender or laborer, with the prevailing-wage figure in the Body and Mind chapter as the ceiling.
3. From the logged solo output and the tended rate, estimate the wall-days a tender saves per hired day.
4. Multiply the days saved by the daily carrying cost, and compare the product with the wage times the hired days.
5. Hire for the high-volume days and the courses above five feet first, where the days saved per wage dollar are greatest.
6. Add the employer costs before the first paid day: withholding, unemployment insurance and workers' compensation.
7. Record each hired day with the output achieved, and re-run the comparison monthly.

**Professional**

The comparison has two sourced parts and one measured one. The wage is a local quote: talent.com's 2026 salary data puts Oregon's open-market construction labor at an average of $21.58 an hour, $170 for an eight-hour day, below the prevailing-wage ceiling set for a public-works tender. The productivity side comes from the masonry trade's own numbers. Masonry Magazine and a Contractor Talk production thread put a tended mason's day at 400 to 600 bricks, with one tender supplying three or four masons at ground level and one mason once the wall reaches five feet, because hoisting and longer carries multiply the tender's work. A mason who tends alone absorbs that work and more, since none of it runs in parallel with laying, and the loss grows with the wall height. The solo rate itself is measured in the first two weeks of laying, and the schedule is replanned from it.

At a carrying cost of $100 a day and a wage of $200, a hired day pays when it saves more than two days of wall. On a low course a tender frees a quarter to a third of the mason's day, so a hired day saves a fraction of a day and costs a full wage. Above five feet, where the trade's ratio reaches one tender per mason, a hired day can double the day's output and save a day or more, and on a high-volume day the gain is largest. The finance case for a tender is therefore thinnest on low courses and strongest at height, and the body's own hiring triggers add days the arithmetic does not. Oregon treats the first paid day as employment, which is the reason the three employer costs are priced before it.

## 33.11 Taxes: the basis ledger and construction interest

**Simple**

Every receipt from the build is also a tax record. When you sell, the taxable gain is the price minus what the house cost you, and that cost is the land plus every dollar you paid for materials, hired labor, permits and professional fees. Your own hours count for nothing in that sum. Interest on a construction loan can be deducted like mortgage interest for up to two years of building, as long as the house becomes your home when it is ready. Land that came as a gift keeps the giver's old purchase price as its cost, which raises the gain years later.

**Applied**

1. Keep a basis ledger from the first permit fee, with the date, the payee, the amount and what it bought.
2. Enter every paid invoice, permit, professional fee and utility connection charge tied to the build.
3. Leave your own labor and any unpaid help out of the ledger.
4. Keep the ledger and the receipts until three years after the due date of the return for the year the house sells.
5. Treat the house under construction as a qualified home for up to 24 months of construction-loan interest, and keep the lender's annual interest statements.
6. For a gifted parcel, bring the giver's purchase price and date and the appraisal at transfer to the CPA before the first return of the build.
7. Keep the property tax statements and the loan's Closing Disclosure with the ledger.

**Professional**

The basis rule is the Code's cost rule, and the 2025 edition of IRS Publication 523 applies it to a house built on land the owner holds. The basis is the land's cost plus what it cost to complete the house: the labor and materials paid for, contractor payments, architect fees, building permit charges, utility meter and connection charges and the legal fees tied to the building. It excludes the value of the owner's own labor and of any labor not paid for, and the publication keeps the basis records until three years after the due date of the return for the year of the sale. The 2025 edition of Publication 936 restates the construction rule, a home under construction treated as a qualified home for a limited period if it becomes the qualified home when ready for occupancy, and gives the acquisition-debt limit for debt incurred after December 15, 2017. The statute's text on October 29, 2026 carries that limit with no end date. A gifted parcel carries the giver's basis and a below-market purchase is part gift, both set out with the gift-tax filing in the land chapter.

**Code**

```
The tax basis of property is its cost.
  Basis equals the cost of the property, except as the Code's subchapters on corporate distributions, partnerships and capital gains provide otherwise.
  Source: 26 U.S.C. 1012(a).

A home under construction counts as a qualified home for up to 24 months.
  The taxpayer may treat the residence under construction as a qualified residence for up to 24 months, but only if it becomes a qualified residence when it is ready for occupancy.
  Source: 26 CFR 1.163-10T(p)(5)(i).

Deductible home-acquisition debt is capped.
  $750,000 of acquisition indebtedness, $375,000 for a married individual filing separately, for debt incurred after December 15, 2017.
  Source: 26 U.S.C. 163(h)(3)(F)(i)(II) and (III).
```

## 33.12 Insurance after the build: wildfire credits and the earthquake question

**Simple**

Once you live in the house, part of the premium depends on paper you can earn. A wildfire-preparedness designation from a third-party evaluator earns a discount with some insurers, and Oregon is inside the program's territory. The application costs $125, the designation lasts three years, and the evaluator checks twice more that the cleared zone and the vents are as they were. Oregon law now makes every home insurer say on its website how such work changes its rates. Earthquake cover is sold separately, and insurers have priced old brick houses above wood ones because unreinforced masonry shatters. Whether an underwriter rates a reinforced, engineered concrete house below that old-brick figure is settled only by a quote on the finished design.

**Applied**

1. Before the finish design is fixed, download the Wildfire Prepared Home checklist and build its required items into the drawings.
2. Apply for the designation at completion, pay the $125 fee, and photograph each item for the evaluator.
3. Diary the two annual reviews and the three-year renewal.
4. Read each candidate insurer's website statement on wildfire mitigation and rates before asking for a quote.
5. Ask the agent for quotes with and without the designation, including Mercury Insurance's Catastrophe Fire discount for Oregon homes.
6. Ask the earthquake-insurance broker for a quote on the engineered reinforced design, and whether the carrier rates it with wood frame or with masonry.
7. Give the agent the exterior finish's cost per square foot for the insured replacement cost.
8. Keep the designation certificate, the evaluator's report, each insurer's website statement and each quote in the binder.

**Professional**

The Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety's Wildfire Prepared Home program lists Oregon among the 14 states it operates in. Its application page sets the fee, the three-year term and the two annual reviews, and its base level covers ember protection while the plus level adds flame and radiant-heat protection. Oregon's 2023 insurance law turned the discount from a favor into a published policy: it names the designation and a Firewise USA site as mitigation actions, makes each insurer's treatment of them a website statement and a rate-plan term, and ties any wildfire-related premium increase to a notice of the discounts the owner could earn. Mercury Insurance partnered with the institute in May 2025 to discount the catastrophe-fire portion of premiums for designated homes in California and Oregon, as Insurance Journal reported on May 29, 2025. The program's partner list names State Farm, PEMCO, Mercury, CIG, CSAA, Farmers and USAA, and its incentives page states no discount size. Insure Pacific's blog puts Bend-area reductions at 10 to 15 percent for homes meeting Firewise standards with a recorded defensible space, with a payback of two to five years on the mitigation. SB 1540 of 2026, which would have required insurers to account for mitigation in pricing or offer a discount, died in the 2026 session after insurer opposition, as InsuranceNewsNet reported on March 12, 2026. FORTIFIED Home, the institute's wind and hail program, lists incentives in six southern and plains states and none in Oregon.

Oregon has no state earthquake authority, so earthquake cover is private. Oregon Insurance Advisor's and ABI's agency pages give a 2009 Portland figure of $200 to $300 a year for a wood-frame house insured for $300,000 with $150,000 of contents, and a higher figure for brick or masonry, and both describe older masonry. The earthquakeradar.io blog separates reinforced concrete, which it prices 20 to 40 percent below average, from unreinforced masonry at 50 to 200 percent above. Whether an Oregon carrier rates an engineered, fully grouted reinforced wall with concrete or with masonry is answered by the quote on the finished design. Builder's-risk and homeowner underwriting price a custom concrete or masonry finish by its replacement cost, and the lender's appraiser by its contribution to value against conventional finishes, and neither treats the finish as an underwriting class of its own. The approval letter's weight with an underwriter is a question the legal chapter puts to the broker.

**Code**

```
A home insurer publishes how wildfire mitigation affects its rates.
  Information on whether and how wildfire risk mitigation actions may impact underwriting and rates, on the insurer's website, and reflected in its underwriting guidelines and rate plans.
  Source: ORS 737.310(17).

The Wildfire Prepared Home designation is a mitigation action in Oregon law.
  Defensible space, hardening a building or certification by the Insurance Institute for Business and Home Safety for a Wildfire Prepared Home, and community actions such as Firewise USA recognition.
  Source: ORS 742.277(1)(b).

A wildfire-related premium increase comes with a notice of the discounts available.
  The notice names the mitigation actions that would earn a discount, incentive or premium adjustment and the amount of each.
  Source: ORS 742.277(2) and (3).

An insurer may not use the state wildfire map against a policyholder.
  No cancellation, nonrenewal or premium increase on a homeowner policy based on a state-published map of wildfire risk or exposure.
  Source: ORS 742.278.
```

## 33.13 Problems and solutions

**Simple**

Most money trouble on a self-built house comes from two calendars that do not match: the lender's, which pays after each inspection, and yours, which buys materials weeks before. The rest arrives as paper at an awkward time, a lien notice, a tax statement or an insurance renewal. Each problem in the table has a likely cause and a response.

**Applied**

| Problem | Likely cause | Response |
|---|---|---|
| No lender will take an owner-builder | The product is rare, and the house has no comparable sales | Portfolio lenders and credit unions by phone, then cash in phases with a loan held in reserve |
| The draw is short of the invoices | The inspector's percent complete is below the spend, or delivered materials do not count | Ask the stored-materials rule, keep the float at one phase, and time orders to the inspection |
| The float runs out mid-phase | The phase ran longer than the schedule and the lag is longer than planned | Move the next order later, pay every supplier on time, and spend the time reserve on carrying costs only |
| A Notice of Right to a Lien arrives from a company you never hired | A subcontractor's supplier protecting its lien right | File it, demand the list and charges by certified mail, and pay that supplier by joint check |
| A claim of lien is recorded | An unpaid balance down the chain, or a dispute | The attorney within the week, the 120-day clock watched, and a bond or deposit at 150 percent if the lender needs clear title |
| The winter pause will pass 74 days | Weather and the solo schedule | The lender and the attorney asked before the pause about a nonabandonment notice |
| The value notice puts the half-built house too high or too low | The mass-appraisal model has no comparable | Compare with the January photographs and petition the appeals board by December 31 |
| The builder's-risk quote excludes earthquake | The standard form | Price the endorsement, and if none is offered, hold the risk knowingly in the contingency |
| A contingency bucket is empty before the shell is done | Overrun in the unknowns, or scope changes charged to the wrong bucket | Stop at the phase end, re-plan, and refill only with a dated decision |
| The appraisal comes in below cost | No sales of similar houses | Expanded-radius comparables, a portfolio lender, or cash for the gap |
| The tax basis is questioned at sale | Missing receipts | The ledger and the receipt files, kept three years past the sale year's return |
| A hired tender costs more than it saves | Low-volume days or low courses | Hire only for the high-volume days and the courses above five feet |
