# Chapter 33: Personal Project Finance: Budget, Financing, and Cash Flow

A construction lender pays for this house after the fact. Each draw is released once the lender's inspector has seen a phase finished, so the owner carries every phase's cost until then. Few lenders write owner-builder loans at all, and those that do want 25 to 35 percent down against 10 to 20 on a contractor-built house. Either route starts from a budget built from the cost ranges in the system chapters, stage by stage. On top of it goes 10 to 20 percent for the unknowns, because owner-builders run over budget by up to 30 percent, against 5 to 15 for professionals. Interest, property tax and insurance then run for every month the build lasts.

> **Jon's note.** [JON: drafted from the record, confirm or rewrite] I am building most of this house myself, over several years, and making my own block on the site. A solo build runs two to four times as long as a crew's, so my loan interest, property tax and insurance run two to four times as long too. That puts a price on each day of my own time, before any helper's wage is counted. If I finance the build, the land is bought by note and trust deed rather than a land sale contract, so a construction lender can take a first lien on it. [JON: cash, a construction loan, or cash with a loan approved and held in reserve?] [JON: if an appraiser cannot value a block house with no nearby sales, is cash in phases your fallback?] [JON: how many months of carrying cost do you want in the bank before breaking ground?]

## 33.1 The whole-project number

**Simple**

Your budget is one number made of hundreds of smaller ones. Each comes from a quote, a listing or a published cost with its year. Builders split a new house into eight stages, from the site work through the shell to the finishes and the driveway. Finishes take the largest share, a quarter of the cost. In a concrete house the framing stage disappears into the walls and the roof deck. The shell then takes a larger share and the finishes a smaller one. Your own hours cost nothing in cash. A pallet of block costs you more than it costs a contractor with an account. That difference takes back part of the markup you save by not hiring one.

**Applied**

1. Start from the cost ranges each system chapter states.
2. Copy each range into one list with its source and its year.
3. Sort the lines into the eight stages in the table.
4. Check each stage's total against the published share.
5. Add the site-work soft costs, meaning permit and system development charges, design fees and utility connections.
6. Add the land, its closing costs and the carrying costs of the months before construction.
7. Price your own labor at zero.
8. Price hired days at the local wage, from two quotes.
9. Price materials at the counter price for your volume, never at a contractor's account price.
10. Re-quote any material line over $1,000 and any subcontract over $5,000 within 30 days of ordering.
11. Add the three contingency buckets and the time reserve as lines of their own.
12. Lay the stages against the months of the build schedule, so each phase has its own total.
13. Keep the list as the budget of record, dated, and log every change against it.

**Professional**

Costs are 2026 figures unless a different year is given. NAHB's 2024 Construction Cost Survey rests on 41 builders' reports of their typical single-family house. It puts construction at $430,000 for 2,650 square feet, $160 a square foot and 64.4 percent of a $670,000 sales price. Its eight stages and their shares are the check a phased budget is read against.

| Stage | Share of construction cost, 2024 | What the stage holds |
|---|---|---|
| Site work | 7.6 percent | Building permit fees, impact fees, water and sewer fees, architecture and engineering |
| Foundations | 10.5 percent | Excavation, foundation, concrete, retaining walls and backfill |
| Framing | 16.6 percent | Framing including the roof, trusses, sheathing, steel |
| Exterior finishes | 13.4 percent | Exterior wall finish, roofing, windows and doors |
| Major systems rough-ins | 19.2 percent | Plumbing, electrical and HVAC, each near 6.3 percent |
| Interior finishes | 24.1 percent | Insulation, drywall, trim, paint, lighting, cabinets, appliances, flooring, fixtures |
| Final steps | 6.5 percent | Landscaping, outdoor structures, the driveway at 2.3 percent, clean-up |
| Other | 2.1 percent | Unassigned costs |

Concrete houses have no framing stage to budget. In a wood-frame budget 16.6 percent buys studs, trusses and sheathing. Here that money moves into the foundation and shell lines, where the walls, the floor decks and the roof deck are poured or laid. Where the wall is its own finish, the exterior-finish share shrinks too. So the shares test the shape of the estimate, and each line still comes from its own quote.

Acting as the general contractor saves the contractor's markup. Angi's markup article puts it at 15 to 20 percent of project cost. It gives 20 to 30 percent as the 2026 market average, with over 30 percent of builders marking up 25 percent or more. The Benton Builders' blog, a contractor's page, nets the saving on a $200,000 project. Markup avoided comes to $20,000 to $40,000. Lost volume pricing takes back 5 to 15 percent of that saving. Rework and unforeseen problems add 10 to 30 percent to total cost, and $20,000 to $30,000 is left.

Cement, aggregate, block and reinforcing steel are most of this build's cost, and account pricing matters most on exactly those lines. Owner labor is free in cash and absent from the tax basis. It is paid for in calendar days, each with a carrying cost.

## 33.2 Contingency: three buckets and a time reserve

**Simple**

A contingency is money set aside for costs you cannot name yet. Better drawings need less of it. Early sketches need 15 to 25 percent on top, finished drawings 5 to 10, and construction itself 3 to 5. Owner-builders overrun by up to 30 percent, against 5 to 15 for professionals. Your cushion therefore sits at the top of each range. One lump sum goes on the first overrun and leaves nothing for a later phase. Three labeled pots work better: changes you choose, problems the ground or the work turns up, and gaps in the design. A fourth pot pays the interest, tax and insurance of the months a solo build runs past its plan.

**Applied**

1. Set the design bucket at 15 to 25 percent while the drawings are schematic.
2. Cut it to 5 to 10 percent at complete construction documents, and to 3 to 5 once construction starts.
3. Set the construction-unknowns bucket at 20 percent for the shell, and hold it until the roof is on.
4. Set the scope-change bucket from the household's written list of open choices, priced from the finish schedules.
5. Hold a time reserve of six months of the daily carrying cost, in its own account.
6. Give each bucket its own budget line.
7. Draw against a bucket only in writing, with the date and the cause.
8. Move money between buckets only with a dated note, never by overspending one.
9. Review the four buckets at each draw.
10. Refill the construction-unknowns bucket before the next pour.
11. Treat a bucket that empties before its phase ends as a stop point.
12. Re-plan the phase before spending further.
13. After move-in, fund maintenance with a yearly set-aside of each component's replacement cost divided by its remaining life.

**Professional**

Mastt's and Procore's contingency guides, from two construction-management software companies, supply the percentages. Calculatorconstruction.com, a construction-calculator website, supplies the owner-builder overrun figure. Both are planning ranges rather than measured results for a house like this one. Managing the Contingency Allowance, an American Institute of Architects article, makes the case for labeled buckets. One undifferentiated allowance invites overruns and disputes over who may spend it. An owner's scope changes, construction unknowns and design gaps are three risks drawn down by three different people. Buildern's blog adds the tracking rule, each bucket a budget line with logged drawdowns.

Each bucket is allocated by phase rather than held whole. A foundation overrun then draws the foundation's share and leaves the finish phase's share intact, and the phased budget shows a lender the same split. Six months of carrying cost is the plan's own choice for the fourth bucket, until the first year's cash-flow sheet replaces it. After occupancy the same discipline becomes a component sinking fund. Each year sets aside each component's replacement cost divided by the years it has left, computed from the replacement horizons in the documentation chapter.

## 33.3 The resilience line: no cost-share, one budget line

**Simple**

The safe room and the stored water, fuel and supplies cost the least when the shell is poured. The reinforcing, the tank pad and the shelving go in with everything else. Added after move-in, the same scope means cutting into finished walls and floors. Federal safe-room money flows to states and counties rather than to households. Its building standard is written for tornado wind. A room built against a Cascadia earthquake earns no share of it. Your own money therefore pays for the resilience scope, as one labeled line beside the house.

**Applied**

| Item built in at construction | What the built-in version includes | What a retrofit adds | Where the figure lives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Safe room cast into the shell | The rated door and frame, a debris-rated vent, any added bars the engineer calls for | Demolition of finishes and a new structural box inside a finished house | Structural System and Concrete Design chapter |
| Water reserve (cistern, tank, hand pump) | The tank, its pad or vault, and the plumbing tie-in at rough-in | Excavation beside a finished house and new penetrations | Water Independence chapter |
| Power and fuel (battery, generator, propane store) | The equipment, its pad and the fuel tank set with the site work | Trenching and pads cut into finished grade | Solar, Battery, and Energy Independence chapter |
| Food and medical stores | Shelving and a cool room drawn into the plan | A room taken from another use | Resilience and Disaster Independence chapter |

1. Price each item in the table twice, built in now and added after occupancy.
2. Include the demolition and finish work a retrofit takes in the second price.
3. Carry the built-in total as one labeled budget line.
4. Budget no federal or state cost-share toward a seismic safe room.
5. Where a wind-rated room is wanted as well, ask the county emergency manager whether a current hazard-mitigation program could include the parcel.
6. Put the safe room's door, vent and bars on the structural drawings, so their cost sits in the shell's draw.

**Professional**

FEMA's Hazard Mitigation Grant Program takes applications from states and tribes, with local governments and qualifying nonprofits as subapplicants. Oregon's Office of Emergency Management states that individual homeowners may not apply directly, though a local government may apply on their behalf. ICC 500 is the program's safe-room standard, with FEMA P-361 for federally funded rooms. FEMA's own ICC 500 highlights document describes a tornado and hurricane standard with no seismic design in it, and Oregon's hazard-mitigation grants run through that same federal program.

FEMA P-320's cost figures assume a wood-framed house around an independent reinforced box. They run $9,400 to $13,000 for an 8-by-8-foot room and $19,000 to $26,000 for 14 by 14. In this shell the box is already the house. What remains is the rated door and frame, the debris-rated vent and whatever added reinforcing the engineer of record finds necessary, which the structural chapter prices. Totals for the roll-up wait on the sources its last column names.

**Code**

```
Federal hazard-mitigation grants go to governments, not to households.
  Applicants are states and Indian tribal governments, and subapplicants are state agencies, local governments, qualifying private nonprofits and tribal governments.
  Source: 44 CFR 206.434(a).

## 33.4 Build it once: the driveway

**Simple**

Some parts of the site are cheapest when built right the first time, and the driveway is the plainest case. Concrete trucks and loaded delivery trucks weigh more than 14,000 pounds. The base under them needs 14 to 18 inches of road rock on good ground, against 5 or 6 inches for a car. Road base costs a fifth to a third more per ton than ordinary gravel. A thin base under those trucks ruts and heaves. A rutted driveway in the week of a pour stops the pour. Built to the heavy standard before the first delivery, the base lasts a century with a top-up of gravel now and then.

**Applied**

1. Build the driveway base to the heavy-vehicle depth before the first concrete or block delivery.
2. Use 14 to 18 inches of road base on a good subgrade.
3. Get one quote for the full base and one for a light base plus a later upgrade.
4. Put the driveway in the site-work phase of the budget and in the first draw.
5. Budget $100 to $400 a year for grading and gravel after the build.

**Professional**

Bovees' driveway-base guide gives the base depths by vehicle class, and HelloGravel's 2026 road-base cost guide prices road base at $20 to $35 a ton against $15 to $25 for ordinary gravel. Both are gravel sellers' web guides, so the project's own figure is a local contractor's quote on the real length and soil. Drainage, geotextile and the surface are in the Developing the Site chapter.

Heavy loads arrive early in this build. Concrete trucks for the footings and slab and block deliveries for the walls come in the first year, in the wet months when a subgrade is weakest. NAHB's 2024 survey puts the driveway at 2.3 percent of a conventional house's construction cost, $9,600 on the average house.

## 33.5 Cash, a construction loan, or both

**Simple**

You can pay from savings, phase by phase, or borrow against a house that does not exist yet. Borrowing takes an owner-builder construction loan. Few lenders write one, because they would be funding a first-time general contractor. The lenders that do write them want a quarter to a third of the cost down. They also want plans, an itemized budget and a record of what you have built before. The loan pays out in draws after each inspection and turns into an ordinary mortgage at the end. Building from cash removes the lender, the appraisal and the draw inspector. It also means the whole budget sits in the bank before the first pour.

**Applied**

1. Choose the route before the land closes, from cash in phases, a construction-to-permanent loan, or cash with a loan held in reserve.
2. For a loan, call portfolio lenders and credit unions first.
3. Ask each whether it lends to an owner-builder at all.
4. Ask the three questions the legal chapter lists on the appraisal, the draw inspector and the block-production milestones.
5. Bring plans and specifications, the phased budget with its contingency lines, two years of tax returns and the land's deed.
6. Bring a letter on your building experience, or the engagement of a construction manager.
7. Where a side business files on the same returns, ask how its income and expenses enter the debt-to-income figure.
8. Expect the Loan Estimate within three business days of applying, and read it against the budget.
9. Expect the Closing Disclosure three business days before closing.
10. Ask whether the two phases are disclosed as one transaction or two, and how the permanent rate is set.
11. Ask whether land equity counts toward the down payment, and at what value.
12. Check the parcel against the flood map before applying.
13. On farm or forest zoning, add a Farm Credit association and the agricultural lenders to the call list.
14. Record each lender's answer in the binder with the date and the person's name.

**Professional**

Nav's self-build loan guide describes the owner-builder construction loan as a construction-to-permanent product, with one closing and interest-only payments on the drawn balance until conversion. Its guide adds that the product takes stronger credit than a contractor-built loan.

Biglaw Investor's Oregon construction-lender page, a lender marketplace, details six lenders. Goldwater Bank requires a third-party general contractor, Wintrust Mortgage requires an approved builder, and US Bank states that it cannot work with owner-builders. Oregon State Credit Union's one-time-close loan starts at 20 percent down with interest-only payments during construction. WaFd Bank handles draws and inspections itself, and Umpqua Bank is listed without terms. Minimum credit scores on that page run from 620 to 720.

Federal banking regulators' real-estate lending guidelines cap the loan-to-value ratio on a loan for a house under construction. A bank that lends past the cap counts the excess against its capital. The down payment on a contractor-built house therefore starts at that cap, and an owner-builder lender asks for more.

Regulation Z treats a consumer's construction loan, secured by the land, as closed-end credit secured by real property. The Loan Estimate and the Closing Disclosure therefore apply to it, and the lender may disclose the two phases together or separately. Where a lender expects a new-construction loan to settle more than 60 days after the Loan Estimate, it may revise the estimate later. Under 12 CFR 1026.19(e)(3)(iv)(F) it may do so only if the first estimate said in plain words that it might.

Land sale contracts leave legal title with the seller until the price is paid. The lender then has no first lien position until the contract is paid off or subordinated, which is why the land purchase uses a note and trust deed.

A side business on the same tax returns is read with them. Lenders qualify income and debt from personal returns, so the business's income and expenses are seen there and settled at the application. Whitney Land Company's and Loankea's land-loan guides state that a parcel zoned for exclusive farm use may not qualify for a standard land loan at all. Both describe rural Oregon land lending as the work of credit unions, community banks, agricultural lenders and land-loan brokers.

**Code**

```
A bank's construction loan on a house has a supervisory loan-to-value ceiling.
  85 percent of value for one- to four-family residential construction, 75 percent for land development and 65 percent for raw land, and loans above the ceilings capped in aggregate at 100 percent of capital.
  Source: 12 CFR part 34, subpart D, appendix A (Interagency Guidelines for Real Estate Lending Policies), supervisory loan-to-value limits table.

A consumer gets a Loan Estimate within three business days of applying for a construction loan.
  Delivered or placed in the mail not later than the third business day after the creditor receives the application, and not later than the seventh business day before consummation.
  Source: 12 CFR 1026.19(e)(1)(iii)(A) and (B).

  Received by the consumer not later than three business days before consummation.
  Source: 12 CFR 1026.19(f)(1)(ii)(A).

  Where a multiple-advance loan to finance construction of a dwelling may be permanently financed by the same creditor, the construction phase and the permanent phase may be treated as one transaction or more than one.
  Source: 12 CFR 1026.17(c)(6)(ii).

A regulated lender must require flood insurance on a building in a mapped flood hazard area.

  Source: 42 U.S.C. 4012a(b)(1)(A).

## 33.6 Draws, draw inspections and lien waivers

**Simple**

Each draw pays you back for work already finished. You send the lender the invoices, the photos and the signed waivers from everyone you paid. Then the lender sends an inspector to confirm the phase is done, and the money arrives afterward. That inspector works for the lender and checks dollars against progress. Your county's code inspection is a different visit. Behind the waivers sits Oregon's lien law. Anyone who supplies labor or material to your house and is not paid can record a claim against it. That holds even when you paid the person who ordered the work. The waivers are your proof that the whole chain was paid.

**Applied**

| Draw | Phase finished | What the lender's inspector sees | Documents in the packet |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Site work and foundation | Footings, stem walls and slab poured and backfilled, underground utilities in | Excavation and concrete invoices, pour tickets, the county's foundation approval, waivers |
| 2 | Shell to the bond beam | Walls grouted to the top course, lintels set | Block, grout and reinforcing invoices, the block-production log and test reports, wall inspection approvals, waivers |
| 3 | Roof and dry-in | Roof deck placed, membrane on, windows and doors in | Roofing and window invoices, roofing approval, waivers |
| 4 | Rough-ins | Plumbing, electrical and mechanical rough-ins approved | Trade invoices and permits, rough-in approvals, waivers |
| 5 | Insulation and interior finishes | Insulation approved, drywall and finishes done | Finish invoices, insulation approval, waivers |
| 6 | Final | Certificate of occupancy issued, site finished | Final approval, the certificate, final waivers from every payee |

1. Agree the draw schedule with the lender before closing, written as the table.
2. Tie each draw to a phase the inspector can see from the ground.
3. Where owner-made block is the wall, brief the inspector in writing on the production log and the test reports.
4. Before each draw, assemble the packet of invoices, dated photos, the county's approvals and a waiver from every payee.
5. Pay each supplier and subcontractor by check or transfer.
6. Collect a conditional waiver with each payment and an unconditional one when it clears.
7. File every Notice of Right to a Lien the day it arrives, with the sender and the date.
8. Answer an unfamiliar notice with a written demand for the list of materials and charges, by certified mail.
9. Where a supplier's notice names a subcontractor you paid, pay the balance by joint check to both.
10. Hold the final payment to each subcontractor until its lien window has run or its waiver is in hand.
11. Before any pause that could pass 74 days, ask the lender and the attorney about a nonabandonment notice.
12. When the last subcontractor has finished, post and record a completion notice.
13. If a claim of lien is recorded, get the attorney's read within the week.

**Professional**

Nav's guide puts the count at five to seven draws over a build. A lender inspection comes before each, at a fee of $100 to $150 a visit charged to the borrower. WaFd Bank's builder guide for its Built platform describes the cycle from the lender's side. An inspection releases funds in proportion to progress, and a draw requests the released funds. Lender policy decides whether the borrower may request an inspection or the lender schedules them. Because the inspector's percent-complete figure caps the draw, whether delivered materials count before installation is the lender's stored-materials rule, asked before the first order. The legal chapter describes the draw inspector's standard and the Information Notice to Owner a contractor delivers at signing.

A perfected construction lien on the improvement outranks the construction mortgage, with the statute's own exceptions. That priority is the reason for the waiver requirement at every draw. Anyone who furnishes labor or materials at the owner's own request is exempt from the notice requirement. The subcontractors and suppliers the owner hires directly therefore send none, and their suppliers and their subcontractors must. A supplier keeps its priority over the lender only by notifying the lender in the same window it has for the owner. The lender may then demand the supplier's list, as the owner may.

Oregon's lien law prescribes a form for the notice and for the Information Notice to Owner, and none for a waiver. The one waiver it compels is a paid supplier's, on demand. Waivers are therefore contract documents in the lender's or the title company's form, conditional while the check is uncleared and unconditional after, one per payee per payment.

For lien purposes construction is complete at substantial completion, at a recorded completion notice, or when the improvement is abandoned. Work stopped for two and a half months counts as abandoned unless the owner or the lender has posted and recorded the statutory notice first. A solo build's winter pause can cross that line, which starts every claimant's filing window. So the lender and the construction attorney decide before the pause whether the notice is posted. A recorded claim clouds the lender's title, and the next draw can wait until the claim is released, bonded or lapsed.

## 33.7 Cash flow across the years: the float and the cost of a day

**Simple**

The lender pays after the inspection, so you pay first. Every phase's materials and hired labor leave your account weeks before the draw puts the money back. That gap is the float you need in cash beyond the down payment. A solo phase runs longer than a crew's, and the gap stretches with it. Some costs run with the calendar rather than the work. Interest on what you have drawn, the property tax, the insurance and the rent where you live now all arrive by the month. They arrive whether or not a block was laid. Added up and divided by the days, they give the price of one idle day.

**Applied**

| Line | How to figure it | Where the number comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Construction interest | The drawn balance times the rate, divided by 365 | The lender's rate and the draw schedule |
| Land loan payment | The monthly payment divided by 30, while a separate land loan runs | The land loan note |
| Property tax | The last statement divided by 365, stepped up each January for the completed share | The tax statement and the assessor's rate |
| Builder's risk premium | The annual premium divided by the days insured | The policy |
| Housing elsewhere | Rent or mortgage divided by 30 | The lease or statement |
| Site services | Temporary power, water hauling, the container rental and site security, divided by 30 | The utility and rental bills |
| Total per day | The sum of the lines | Re-run after every draw |

1. Build a month-by-month sheet from groundbreaking to the certificate of occupancy.
2. Give it columns for planned spending, the draw expected and the cash balance.
3. Set the float at one full phase's cost plus the lender's stated days from inspection to funding.
4. Add the draw-inspection fee and any title update fee to each draw month.
5. Enter the carrying-cost lines from the table, and total them by month and by day.
6. Multiply the per-day figure by the days each phase runs on the build schedule.
7. Carry that product in the phased budget as a line of its own.
8. Re-run the sheet after every draw with actual figures.
9. Compare the float to the next phase's cost before ordering materials.
10. Where a month's balance goes negative, move a materials order later rather than paying a supplier late.
11. Keep the time reserve in a separate account from the float, and spend it on carrying costs only.

**Professional**

Lenders differ on whether interest during construction is paid monthly from the owner's account or drawn from an interest reserve built into the loan. That answer changes the float by the interest, and the lender's days from inspection to funding set the rest of it.

An illustration with round inputs shows the scale. Take a $300,000 construction loan at 8 percent, drawn in six equal draws across a 24-month build. Its average balance is near $125,000, and it costs $20,000 in interest before conversion. Stretched across 36 months, the same loan costs $30,000. Each extra year therefore costs $10,000 at that average balance, $27 a day, and once the loan is fully drawn the interest-only payment is $2,000 a month. Its other lines are in the table.

## 33.8 Property tax during the build

**Simple**

The county taxes what stands on the land each January 1. A house that is half built on that morning is taxed as half a house for the following year. Work started after January 1 is taxed as bare land until the next one. Each new piece of value joins the tax roll at a discount set by the county's ratio. From then on the taxable value can climb three percent a year. Statements arrive in October. It is due in thirds in November, February and May, with a discount for paying all of it in November. A wrong value can be appealed to the county's appeals board until the end of December.

**Applied**

1. Mark January 1 on the build calendar.
2. Photograph the site's state in that week for the binder.
3. Read the assessor's percent-complete figure on the value notice against those photographs.
4. Petition the property value appeals board on a wrong figure, between the statement's mailing and December 31.
5. Budget each build year's tax from the prior statement plus the completed share at the county's rate per $1,000.
6. Pay the whole year by November 15 for the discount when the float allows, and in thirds otherwise.
7. On a farm- or forest-deferred parcel, get the assessor's potential-additional-tax figure before the dwelling approval.
8. Ask the assessor when the homesite comes out of deferral and when the disqualification tax falls due.
9. Keep every tax statement and assessor notice with the January photographs.

**Professional**

Oregon's assessment year is the calendar year, and its tax year begins the following July 1. What the assessor records on January 1 is therefore billed in October of the same year. Real market value and the assessor's statistical model for an unusual house are in the legal chapter. Clackamas County's assessor FAQ states that practice, valuing new construction at the percent completed on January 1.

Two constitutional measures bound the bill from different sides. Measure 50 limits how fast a property's taxable value grows and admits new improvements at their market value times the county's changed property ratio. A half-built house therefore enters the roll at a fraction of its market value and compounds from there at the capped rate. Measure 5 caps the tax rate on real market value, one cap for schools and one for other government, and voter bonds sit outside both.

Multi-year builds reach the roll in pieces, each January's completed share entering as new improvements at that year's ratio. Outbuildings added later enter the same way unless they fall under the minor-construction threshold, which leaves the maximum assessed value alone. On a farm- or forest-deferred parcel the potential additional tax falls due when the homesite is disqualified, and the assessor gives the figure before the dwelling approval.

## 33.9 Insurance during the build

**Simple**

Builder's risk is the policy for a house that is not built yet. You buy it as the owner-builder, and it is priced as a share of the finished value. It pays for fire, theft, vandalism and storm damage to the structure and to materials on the site or on their way to it. It does not pay for an earthquake or a flood unless you buy that on top. In the Cascadia zone the shell is at its weakest while that exclusion applies. Your tools, your own injuries and a paid helper's injuries each need a different policy.

**Applied**

| Coverage | What it covers | Where it is settled |
|---|---|---|
| Builder's risk | The structure under construction, and materials on site, in transit or in temporary storage | The insurance agent, before the first delivery |
| Earthquake and flood endorsements | The two excluded perils, by endorsement or a separate policy | The agent, with the builder's-risk quote |
| Inland marine (tools and equipment) | Owned tools and machines at the site and in transit | Tools, Equipment, and the Owner-Builder Shop chapter |
| Disability insurance | The owner's income after an injury | The Owner-Builder's Body and Mind chapter |
| Liability and workers' compensation | Injuries to people on the site, and paid helpers | Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules chapter |

1. Get the builder's-risk quote on the total insured construction value before the first material delivery.
2. Start the policy at that delivery.
3. Ask for the earthquake endorsement and its price, and the flood endorsement where the map requires it.
4. Ask whether materials stored in the site container are covered, and get the answer in writing.
5. Ask whether a soft-costs endorsement is offered on an owner-builder policy, and what delay costs it would pay.
6. Set the policy term to the solo schedule rather than a crew's, and diary the renewal.
7. Convert to a homeowner's policy at the certificate of occupancy, and tell the construction lender the date.
8. Keep the declarations page, the endorsements and the agent's written answers in the binder.

**Professional**

Insureon's figures come from the policies its 100,000 small-business customers bought. They put builder's risk at an average of $105 a month, with 49 percent of buyers paying under $100. Premiums run 1 to 4 percent of the completed structure's value. Wexford Insurance's 2026 pricing guide gives 1 to 5 percent of total construction value. Hotaling Insurance Services' 2026 guide states that single-family rates were softening as carriers entered the segment. A site container falls under the policy's on-site or temporarily-stored wording, confirmed in writing by the agent for the policy bought.

Earthquake and flood are sold as endorsements or separate policies, and US Assure lists both among the optional endorsements on its residential and commercial builder's-risk policies. Faulty workmanship and design are excluded, and the policy's own wording decides whether the damage that results is covered. Wear, rust and mechanical breakdown are excluded, injuries belong to workers' compensation, and contractor tools are excluded unless added, per Wexford's guide. On a Cascadia site the earthquake exclusion is the one that matters, because the policy runs through the months when the shell is least complete.

US Assure defines the soft-costs endorsement as the delay expenses a covered loss causes. It names construction-loan interest, property tax assessments, architects' and engineers' fees and permit fees, and notes that the examples vary among providers. Its loss-of-rents endorsement is the one written for a commercial owner. Whether a residential owner-builder policy offers soft costs is a question for the agent, and the time reserve stands in for it meanwhile.

## 33.10 What a day of help is worth

**Simple**

A hired tender costs a day's wage and saves the carrying cost of every day the wall finishes sooner. On a solo build the wall rises at the speed of one person mixing, carrying and laying by turns. A tender keeps the mortar and the block coming, so the mason lays all day. The saving is largest on the busiest laying days, and above five feet, where carrying takes the most time. A paid day pays for itself when the carrying cost of the days it saves is larger than the wage. On a low course it saves less than it costs.

**Applied**

1. Take the carrying cost per day from the cash-flow sheet.
2. Get two local day rates for a tender or laborer.
3. Use the prevailing-wage figure in the Body and Mind chapter as the ceiling.
4. From the logged solo output and the tended rate, estimate the wall-days a tender saves per hired day.
5. Multiply the days saved by the carrying cost, and compare the product with the wage times the hired days.
6. Hire first for the high-volume days and the courses above five feet.
7. Add the employer costs before the first paid day, meaning withholding, unemployment insurance and workers' compensation.
8. Record each hired day with the output achieved, and re-run the comparison monthly.

**Professional**

Two parts of the comparison are sourced and one is measured. Talent.com's 2026 salary figures put Oregon's open-market construction labor at an average of $21.58 an hour, $170 for an eight-hour day. That sits below the prevailing-wage ceiling set for a public-works tender.

Productivity comes from the masonry trade's own numbers. Masonry Magazine and a Contractor Talk production thread put a tended mason's day at 400 to 600 bricks. One tender supplies three or four masons at ground level and one mason once the wall reaches five feet, because hoisting and longer carries multiply the tender's work. A mason who tends alone absorbs that work and more, since none of it runs in parallel with laying, and the loss grows with the wall height. The solo rate itself is measured in the first two weeks of laying, and the schedule is replanned from it.

At a carrying cost of $100 a day and a wage of $200, a hired day pays when it saves more than two days of wall. On a low course a tender frees a third of the mason's day at most, so a hired day saves a fraction of a day and costs a full wage. Above five feet, where the trade's ratio reaches one tender per mason, a hired day can double the output and save a day or more. On a high-volume day the gain is largest, and the body's own hiring triggers add days the arithmetic does not. Oregon treats the first paid day as employment, so the day rate is not the whole cost.

## 33.11 Taxes: the basis ledger and construction interest

**Simple**

Every receipt from the build counts twice, once for the budget and once for tax. When you sell, the taxable gain is the price minus what the house cost you. That cost is the land plus every dollar you paid for materials, hired labor, permits and professional fees. Your own hours count for nothing in that sum. Interest on a construction loan can be deducted like mortgage interest for up to two years of building. Only one condition applies, that the house becomes your home when it is ready. Land that came as a gift keeps the giver's old purchase price as its cost, which raises the gain years later.

**Applied**

1. Keep a basis ledger from the first permit fee, with the date, the payee, the amount and what it bought.
2. Enter every paid invoice, permit, professional fee and utility connection charge tied to the build.
3. Leave your own labor and any unpaid help out of the ledger.
4. Keep the ledger and the receipts until three years after the due date of the return for the year the house sells.
5. Treat the house under construction as a qualified home for up to 24 months of construction-loan interest, and keep the lender's annual interest statements.
6. For a gifted parcel, bring the giver's purchase price and date and the transfer appraisal to the CPA before the build's first tax return.
7. Keep the property tax statements and the loan's Closing Disclosure with the ledger.

**Professional**

The basis rule is the Internal Revenue Code's cost rule, and the 2025 edition of IRS Publication 523 applies it to a house built on land the owner holds. The basis is the land's cost plus what it cost to complete the house, which the publication lists:

- labor and materials paid for, and contractor payments
- architect fees and building permit charges
- utility meter and connection charges
- legal fees tied to the building

Publication 936's 2025 edition restates the construction rule. A home under construction is treated as a qualified home for a limited period, provided it becomes the qualified home when ready for occupancy. It also gives the acquisition-debt limit for loans taken out after mid-December 2017, and the statute now carries that limit with no end date. A gifted parcel carries the giver's basis and a below-market purchase is part gift, both set out with the gift-tax filing in the land chapter.

**Code**

```
The tax basis of property is its cost.
  Basis equals the cost of the property, except as the subchapters on corporate distributions, partnerships and capital gains provide otherwise.
  Source: 26 U.S.C. 1012(a).

A home under construction counts as a qualified home for up to 24 months.
  The taxpayer may treat the residence under construction as a qualified residence for up to 24 months, but only if it becomes a qualified residence when it is ready for occupancy.
  Source: 26 CFR 1.163-10T(p)(5)(i).

Deductible home-acquisition debt is capped.
  $750,000 of acquisition indebtedness, $375,000 for a married individual filing separately, for debt incurred after December 15, 2017.
  Source: 26 U.S.C. 163(h)(3)(F)(i)(II) and (III).
```

## 33.12 Insurance after the build: wildfire credits and the earthquake question

**Simple**

Once you live in the house, part of the premium depends on paper you can earn. A wildfire-preparedness designation from a third-party evaluator earns a discount with some insurers, and Oregon is inside the program's territory. Applying costs $125, and the designation lasts three years. The evaluator checks twice more that the cleared zone and the vents are as they were. Oregon law now makes every home insurer say on its website how such work changes its rates. Earthquake cover is sold separately. Insurers have priced old brick houses above wood ones, because unreinforced masonry shatters. Whether an underwriter rates a reinforced, engineered concrete house below that old-brick figure is settled only by a quote on the finished design.

**Applied**

1. Before the finish design is fixed, download the Wildfire Prepared Home checklist and build its required items into the drawings.
2. Apply for the designation at completion, pay the $125 fee, and photograph each item for the evaluator.
3. Diary the two annual reviews and the three-year renewal.
4. Read each candidate insurer's website statement on wildfire mitigation and rates before asking for a quote.
5. Ask the agent for quotes with and without the designation, including Mercury Insurance's Catastrophe Fire discount for Oregon homes.
6. Ask the earthquake-insurance broker for a quote on the engineered reinforced design, and whether the carrier rates it with wood frame or with masonry.
7. Give the agent the exterior finish's cost per square foot for the insured replacement cost.
8. Keep the designation certificate, the evaluator's report, each insurer's website statement and each quote in the binder.

**Professional**

Wildfire Prepared Home, the Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety's program, lists Oregon among the 14 states it operates in. Its application page is the source for the fee and the review schedule, and its base level covers ember protection while the plus level adds flame and radiant-heat protection. Oregon's 2023 insurance law turned the discount from a favor into a published policy. It names the designation and a Firewise USA site as mitigation actions, and makes each insurer's treatment of them a website statement and a rate-plan term. It also ties any wildfire-related premium increase to a notice of the discounts the owner could earn.

In May 2025 Mercury Insurance partnered with the institute to discount the catastrophe-fire portion of premiums on designated homes in California and Oregon, as Insurance Journal reported. Seven insurers appear on the program's partner list, State Farm, Farmers and USAA among them, and its incentives page states no discount size.

Insure Pacific's blog puts Bend-area reductions at 10 to 15 percent for homes meeting Firewise standards with a recorded defensible space. It gives a payback of two to five years on the mitigation. SB 1540 of 2026 would have required insurers to account for mitigation in pricing or offer a discount. It died in the 2026 session after insurer opposition, as InsuranceNewsNet reported on March 12, 2026. FORTIFIED Home, the institute's wind and hail program, lists incentives in six southern and plains states and none in Oregon.

Oregon has no state earthquake authority, so earthquake cover is private. Oregon Insurance Advisor's and ABI's agency pages give a 2009 Portland figure of $200 to $300 a year for a wood-frame house insured for $300,000 with $150,000 of contents. Brick or masonry cost more, and both pages describe older masonry. Earthquakeradar.io's blog separates reinforced concrete, which it prices 20 to 40 percent below average, from unreinforced masonry at 50 to 200 percent above, and an engineered, fully grouted reinforced wall sits in neither of its classes by name.

Builder's-risk and homeowner underwriting price a custom concrete or masonry finish by its replacement cost, and the lender's appraiser by its contribution to value against conventional finishes. Neither treats the finish as an underwriting class of its own. Whether the approval letter carries weight with an underwriter is a question the legal chapter puts to the broker.

**Code**

```
A home insurer publishes how wildfire mitigation affects its rates.
  Information on whether and how wildfire risk mitigation actions may impact underwriting and rates, on the insurer's website, and reflected in its underwriting guidelines and rate plans.
  Source: ORS 737.310(17).

The Wildfire Prepared Home designation is a mitigation action in Oregon law.
  Defensible space, hardening a building or certification by the Insurance Institute for Business and Home Safety for a Wildfire Prepared Home, and community actions such as Firewise USA recognition.
  Source: ORS 742.277(1)(b).

A wildfire-related premium increase comes with a notice of the discounts available.
  The notice names the mitigation actions that would earn a discount, incentive or premium adjustment and the amount of each.
  Source: ORS 742.277(2) and (3).

An insurer may not use the state wildfire map against a policyholder.
  No cancellation, nonrenewal or premium increase on a homeowner policy based on a state-published map of wildfire risk or exposure.
  Source: ORS 742.278.
```
