# Chapter 2: Oregon Code and Compliance Map

Oregon's residential code changed edition on October 1, 2026, and until March 31, 2027 a permit application may be designed to either the 2023 or the 2026 Oregon Residential Specialty Code. Which edition the permit vests under is the first compliance decision for this house. Every rule that controls the house comes from one of seven layers: state statutes, agency rules, the specialty codes those rules adopt, the local department's process, the approved drawings, referenced standards, and product installation instructions. On most western Oregon sites the earthquake rules take the concrete walls out of the code's pre-approved tables and into engineered design. The project stays a residential-code project for permits, inspections and occupancy, and its engineering is in the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter.

## 2.1 The Oregon rule stack

**Simple**

Who makes the rules for your house? Rules for a house come in layers, and each kind of question is settled at a different layer. Legislators pass the laws. In Salem, the Building Codes Division writes the detailed rules and adopts the technical codes for building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical and energy work. Your county or city building department runs the permits and inspections under all of it. At the bottom sit your approved drawings and the installation sheet that comes in the box with every furnace, window and water heater. When a question comes up on site, the first step is finding which layer settles it.

**Applied**

Fix the edition while the drawings are still changeable.

1. Open the Building Codes Division's adopted-codes page at oregon.gov/bcd.
2. Write down the edition, effective date and mandatory date of each specialty code the permit will cite.
3. During a phase-in, choose the edition the drawings are designed to, and state it on the cover sheet.
4. Ask the building official in writing which edition the permit vests under and which local amendments apply.
5. File the answer, and record the edition and the application date on the first page of the binder.
6. Keep building to the permit's edition when a new one takes effect mid-build, unless you agree in writing to the change.

**Professional**

Top to bottom, the rule stack:

| Layer | What it does | What it controls for this house |
|---|---|---|
| Oregon Revised Statutes (ORS) | State laws passed by the legislature | Authority for building codes, contractor licensing, owner-builder rules, disclosures and enforcement |
| Oregon Administrative Rules (OAR) | Agency rules that implement state law | Adoption of specialty codes, permit procedures, certificate-of-occupancy procedures, agency program detail |
| Oregon specialty codes | Technical building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical and energy rules | The house systems and their inspections |
| Local jurisdiction process | Plan review, permit issuance, inspection scheduling, correction notices, approval records | The local submission and inspection workflow |
| Approved plans and engineering | Project-specific drawings, calculations, specifications and stamped sheets | The legal design for this house in particular |
| Referenced standards | Standards adopted by the codes (ASTM, ACI, ASCE, ASHRAE, NFPA, UL, ANSI, TMS and others) | Test methods, materials, equipment ratings, fire ratings, listed assemblies |
| Manufacturer instructions | Required installation rules for listed products | How a specific product must be installed, supported, sealed, vented, tested and maintained |

Between code cycles the structure of the stack does not change. Editions do, one specialty code at a time, on schedules the Building Codes Division sets by rule. Residential, structural, mechanical, plumbing and electrical codes are seldom on the same cycle. Each adoption rule names the edition, its base model code, its effective date and a phase-in period. During the phase-in, every department in the state must accept plans designed to either the outgoing or the incoming edition.

Requirements in effect on the day a plan review or permit application is filed control the construction under that permit. Later changes apply only where the applicant agrees to them. ORSC Chapter 1, the administrative chapter, is adopted without a phase-in. Permit, inspection and certificate-of-occupancy procedure therefore changes on the effective date, while the technical chapters are still in transition.

## 2.2 One code per system

**Simple**

Each part of the house has its own code. Its structure is built under the residential code. Your wiring is installed under the electrical code, your pipes under the plumbing code, and the furnace, fans and ducts under the mechanical code. Septic systems and wells are in none of those. Two other state agencies permit them, and the septic side goes to the county health office where the state has delegated it. Picking the matching code is the first step in any code question. From one code to the next, the answer, the inspector and even the permit form differ.

**Applied**

Build a one-page lane matrix as the first page of the binder.

1. List every permit the house needs. Include structural, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, septic, well, driveway approach, and grading or erosion control where the county requires one.
2. Write beside each permit the code and edition it is reviewed under.
3. Write beside each the office that issues it and the office that inspects it. Structural, mechanical, electrical and plumbing can be four different departments in one county.
4. Mark the work closed to the owner: LP gas (a State Fire Marshal licensee) and refrigerant connections (an EPA-certified technician).
5. Add the phone number, the scheduling channel and the lead time for each inspecting office.
6. Keep the matrix at the front of the binder and update it when a contract or a department changes.

**Professional**

| Work area | Primary lane | What that lane controls |
|---|---|---|
| Residential building | Oregon Residential Specialty Code (ORSC) | Building planning, structure, foundations, walls, floors, roofs, stairs, openings, fire separation, energy (ORSC Chapter 11), inspections, occupancy |
| Electrical | Oregon Electrical Specialty Code (OESC) | Service, panels, grounding, bonding, branch circuits, receptacles, lighting, protection devices, electrical inspections |
| Plumbing | Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code (OPSC) | Water supply, drain-waste-vent, fixtures, water heaters, backflow, tests, plumbing inspections |
| Mechanical | ORSC mechanical chapters first, then the Oregon Mechanical Specialty Code (OMSC) where they are silent | HVAC equipment, ventilation, exhaust, ducts, hydronics, appliance clearances, mechanical inspections |
| Fuel gas | ORSC Chapter 24 | Gas piping, appliance connections and the gas pressure test |
| Onsite wastewater | DEQ rules (OAR 340-071), administered by the county where DEQ has delegated the program | Septic feasibility, permits, design, installation, inspection, ongoing maintenance |
| Water wells | Oregon Water Resources Department (OWRD) | Well construction, well logs, water-source legality |
| Fire and fuel systems | State Fire Marshal rules and product listings | LP gas licensing, tanks, special fuel systems |
| Workplace safety | Oregon OSHA, once anyone is hired | Trenching, fall protection, equipment, jobsite safety duties |
| Engineering and standards | Approved engineering and adopted standards (ACI, ASCE, ASTM, ASHRAE, NFPA, UL, ANSI, TMS) | Concrete design, loads, reinforcing, materials, test methods, ratings, listed assemblies |

Wood-framing provisions apply to this house only where wood is used. Those places are roof framing if the roof is wood-framed, formwork, framing at openings, fire-separation details, and comparisons against the concrete or masonry detail replacing a wood one. One appliance, the heat pump, sits in two lanes at once. Its equipment, clearances and refrigerant lines fall under the mechanical code, and the circuit that feeds it falls under the electrical code. The installation takes a mechanical permit and an electrical permit.

Oregon's hazard-zone construction statute does not reach a detached dwelling. ORS 455.447 restricts two categories of building. One is major structures, defined by height and floor area. The other is buildings assigned Tsunami Risk Category III or IV, the categories for substantial-hazard and essential facilities. Under ASCE 7 and the structural code's risk table, a single-family house is Risk Category II. Neither a home occupation, an accessory structure nor a later change of use within residential occupancy moves it to Category III or IV.

Inspection by inspection, with each authority's scheduling channel, the full version of the lane matrix is in the Inspection and Approval Sequence chapter.

## 2.3 Where to look for each kind of answer

**Simple**

Most code questions on a job site are lookups, once you know which paper to open. Your approved drawings state what to build. The code states what any wall, pipe or wire must satisfy. How a product goes in is on the sheet in its box. Whether the work can be covered is recorded by the inspector's signature on the card. When two of them disagree, the adopted code text wins, then your stamped drawings, then the product's listing and instructions. Below all of them sit a brochure, a video, or a habit from another state.

**Applied**

Find the answer in this order, and write down where it came from.

| Question | First place to look |
|---|---|
| Am I allowed to act as the owner-builder? | ORS, OAR, the Building Codes Division's homeowner guidance, and the Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules chapter |
| Which permit path applies to this work? | The local building department or Oregon ePermitting |
| What must this wall, slab, wire, pipe, vent, drain, appliance or window satisfy? | The specialty code for that lane and the approved plans |
| Who must design this detail? | The code's prescriptive path, the engineer of record, or the building official |
| How is this product installed? | Its listing, its label and the manufacturer's instructions |
| How is this work tested? | The specialty code, the referenced standard, or the inspector's stated requirement |
| Can I cover this work? | A passed inspection, plus your own photographs and records |
| Can I occupy the home? | The certificate of occupancy or a temporary certificate of occupancy |

1. Read the approved drawings and specifications first. Each stamped sheet controls the detail it shows.
2. When the drawings are silent, open the lane's specialty code at the edition the permit vests under.
3. When the code names a listing or a standard, read the product's listing and installation instructions. Keep both on site for the inspection.
4. When two sources disagree, follow the higher one: adopted code text, then the approved drawings, then the listing.
5. Rank a brochure, a video, a habit from another state, or any secondhand restatement of a rule below all three.
6. When the answer depends on the jurisdiction, ask the building official in writing and file the dated reply.
7. Treat a field change to approved work as a design change. Get the engineer's letter or a plan revision before building it.

**Professional**

Adding concrete does not establish compliance on its own. Each thicker or heavier element adds dead load and seismic mass, changes the reinforcement the engineer must provide, and can interrupt the insulation layer or a connection detail. Compliance is judged on the whole assembly under the site's loads, soil and seismic category.

Only a material, design or method the code does not prescribe takes the alternative-materials path. Under the engineer's stamp, a design more conservative than the code's minimum, built with materials the referenced standards recognize, is ordinary engineered design. More concrete cover than the exposure class requires falls on that side of the line. So do stainless or glass-fiber reinforcement within its recognized applications and a crystalline admixture from a category ACI 318 recognizes. What goes through the approval path is the element the code has no row for, such as an owner-made unit or a wall system outside every prescriptive table.

Where an answer depends on the jurisdiction, the edition, the site or a product listing, the official's written reply is the record that outlasts staff turnover. Statements made in a meeting bind nothing, and the plan-review decision is made against the submitted application.

**Code**

```
Listed products are installed per the listing and the manufacturer's instructions.
  Where the code or a referenced standard requires a listing, installation follows the listing and the manufacturer's instructions, and both are made available to the building official where needed to verify compliance. Materials approved by the building official are installed in accordance with the approval.
  Source: ORSC R104.9 (2023 and 2026 editions); ORSC R104.2.1 (2026 edition).

Manufacturer's installation instructions must be on the job site at inspection.
  Available on the job site at the time of inspection, wherever the code requires them.
  Source: ORSC R106.1.2 (2023 and 2026 editions).
```

## 2.4 Prescriptive, engineered and alternative paths

**Simple**

You have three ways to prove a design acceptable. On the prescriptive path the code states exactly what to build, and you build to those values. On the engineered path a licensed engineer sizes the structure for your soil, your loads and your earthquake category, and stamps the drawings. That category is the code's rating of how hard the ground can shake at your address. On the alternative path the building official agrees in writing that an unlisted material or method still meets the code's intent. This house takes the engineered path for its walls: concrete walls in western Oregon's earthquake categories are outside the tables. Owner-made blocks would go the alternative way.

**Applied**

Give every structural and envelope element a path while the drawings are in progress.

1. Check whether a prescriptive section of the residential code applies to the element within all its limits: geometry, seismic design category, load and height.
2. If it does, have the engineer confirm that the prescriptive values fit the actual application, and cite the table on the drawings.
3. If it does not, have the element engineered under the governing standard. Use ACI 318, ACI 332 or PCA 100 for concrete, TMS 402 for masonry and ASCE 7 for loads.
4. For a material or method no section prescribes, request alternative-method approval from the building official in writing before plan review. Attach the tests and reports the code names.
5. State the chosen path in the permit submittal, as the prescriptive table cited, the stamped drawings and calculations, or the approval letter.
6. Resolve every open code question before concrete is placed.
7. Treat a field change to an approved element as a design change. Keep product listings and installation instructions with the permit record.

**Professional**

| Path | Best fit for this project |
|---|---|
| Prescriptive | Ordinary details that fall inside the code tables and their standard assumptions |
| Engineered | Reinforced concrete, unusual foundations, retaining conditions, seismic design, large openings, complex load paths, nonstandard assemblies |
| Alternative method | Innovative systems, unusual materials and tested assemblies that meet the code's intent by another route |

Engineered design is the default for a concrete or reinforced-masonry house in western Oregon. Seismic Design Category D0, D1 or D2 covers the Willamette Valley, the I-5 corridor and the coast. ORSC R608, the prescriptive route for above-grade concrete walls, is open only in Seismic Design Category C, which maps to most of eastern Oregon. Mapped ground motion and the site's soil class under ASCE 7 set the category, so two parcels in one county can fall in different categories. Specific triggers (seismic design category, two-wythe walls, irregular building conditions, nonstandard wall heights and expansive soil) are stated in the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter.

By occupancy, a concrete or masonry dwelling stays under the residential code. Engineering the above-grade walls does not move the project to the commercial structural code. Permitting, inspection and occupancy stay under the residential code, and only the structural design method becomes nonprescriptive engineering, which references the same standards the commercial code uses. Only an occupancy or use outside the residential code's scope moves a project to the commercial code. R608 and R606, the prescriptive sections for concrete and masonry walls, remain useful as vocabulary and as minimum-expectation references. Neither substitutes for the engineered design of a nonstandard concrete home.

That reference to the commercial code is itself layered. In the 2023 ORSC the defined term Building Code means the Oregon Structural Specialty Code. A nonprescriptive residential design therefore takes its loads and design provisions from the structural code and, through it, from ASCE 7.

Because the two codes adopt on staggered schedules, the structural edition the residential code points at changed after March 31, 2026, from the 2022 OSSC to the 2025 OSSC. Its base code adopts the ASCE 7-22 snow-load maps, which raise design snow loads in some parts of the state and lower them in others. Statewide Alternate Method 26-01 lets a design under the 2023 ORSC keep referencing the 2022 OSSC until the 2026 ORSC is mandatory. The structural drawings therefore state which structural edition they reference.

Every alternative-method request is an evidence package, and the 2026 edition's administrative provisions state what goes in it. Evaluation reports count when an approved agency issued them and the product and method fall within the scope of the official's recognition of that agency. Each report identifies its criteria.

Reports from any other source have to describe their criteria, testing or analysis and come from a specialist, laboratory or organization the official accepts. Tests offered as equivalency evidence have to be at a scale that predicts the end-use configuration, run by a party acceptable to the official. ICC-ES and IAPMO UES reports, and another state's compliance finding, can be submitted under these rules, and the official decides case by case whether they satisfy them.

Denied requests get a written response stating the reasons. Nothing in the code fixes the form of those reasons. On appeal, a denial is most useful when it names which equivalency criterion the official found unmet, because the appeal is heard against the same criteria. Appeals run through the municipality's appeals procedure or the state process the statute provides.

## 2.5 Statewide alternate methods

**Simple**

Statewide alternate methods are rulings from the state's Building Codes Division that approve one way of building for use anywhere in Oregon. Local approval under the alternative-materials rule applies to one project in one jurisdiction. Within the limits it sets, a statewide ruling binds every building department in the state, and using it is your choice. In January 2026 the state issued one that matters to this house. It lets an engineer working under the 2023 residential code keep the older structural code's snow loads until the 2026 code is mandatory. Each ruling is a short numbered PDF on the division's website.

**Applied**

Apply a statewide ruling as a code section, with the same record.

1. Open the division's statewide alternate methods list at oregon.gov/bcd and download the ruling as a PDF.
2. Read its scope and its numbered requirements against your design, line by line.
3. Cite the ruling number on the cover sheet and in the calculations where it applies.
4. File the PDF, with its issue and update dates, in the binder with the permit set.
5. For a method no ruling addresses, ask the engineer whether a local approval or a new statewide ruling fits the schedule. For a new ruling, the request process is in the Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules chapter.

**Professional**

On its website the Building Codes Division publishes the current rulings and the separate request process that produces new ones. Each ruling is a numbered PDF in a fixed format. It opens with a header giving the number and subject, then the statement of legal effect. A code-references list names the code section and the consensus standards involved, with the issue and update dates. A background section, a discussion of the engineering basis and a conclusion follow, and the operative alternate method path closes it as a numbered list at full code specificity.

Every ruling includes the same statement of legal effect. Building officials must approve a method the ruling addresses, and use of the method is at the applicant's discretion. Nothing in a ruling limits an official's authority to consider other requests on the same subject.

Statewide Alternate Method No. 26-01, issued January 20, 2026, is the ruling a residential engineer uses in this edition cycle. Its code references are the 2023 ORSC and the 2022 OSSC. Its background states that using the structural code's design provisions for nonprescriptive portions of a residential-code building is established practice. It also states that the 2025 OSSC changed snow-load design by adopting ASCE 7-22, raising design snow loads in some parts of the state and lowering them in others. Its conclusion lets a 2023 ORSC design keep referencing the 2022 OSSC until the 2026 ORSC is mandatory. Because the method it approves is the continued use of a code edition, it contains no numbered requirements.

Statewide Alternate Method No. 15-01, Cross-Laminated Timber Seismic Force-Resisting Systems, issued January 15, 2015 and updated October 1, 2025, shows the format at full depth. Cross-laminated timber is a structural panel of glued lumber layers. Its code references name the structural specialty-code section and five consensus standards: ASCE 7-22, ASCE 41-23, ANSI/APA PRG 320-2019, ANSI/AWC SDPWS-2021 and ANSI/AWC NDS-2024. Its alternate method path is seven numbered sections. They specify seismic design factors, ductile yield modes, overstrength load combinations, structural modeling, drift limits, deformation compatibility and limits on holes and notches. One section rewrites an ASCE 7 table row. It runs four pages. Its subject is the commercial structural code, so it serves here as the format example.

Within its stated scope, a granted ruling applies in every jurisdiction. What remains to the building official is whether a specific project falls inside that scope. Failure to apply a ruling can cost a building official, plans examiner or inspector their certification. Local interpretations and policies cannot waive a statewide alternate method or a statewide code interpretation.

## 2.6 Who runs your building department

**Simple**

Not every county runs its own building department. Oregon has three arrangements, and the code is the same under all of them. Either a county or a city can staff its own program. In some counties the state Building Codes Division runs the program with state employees. Or the county can hire a private firm to run plan review and inspections under contract. What changes between them is who signs the correction notice and which phone number you call. In the contract case there is one extra check: a county employee reviews the contract official's decision on any alternative material. In all three, the inspector holds the same Oregon certificate.

**Applied**

Find the department before you make an offer on a parcel.

1. Open the Local Building Department Directory at oregon.gov/bcd/lbdd. Select the city or town, or the county when the town is not listed.
2. Filter by permit service, because structural, mechanical, electrical and plumbing can each name a different department.
3. Record each department's building official, address, phone number and scheduling channel in the lane matrix.
4. Ask each department whether it is county-run, state-run or a contract firm, and when a contract firm's current contract ends.
5. Ask about permit turnaround, inspection route days for the parcel's area, and how a concrete pour inspection is scheduled.
6. For a contract department, ask who the county's qualified employee is and how ratification of discretionary decisions is handled.
7. File the answers, dated, in the binder.

**Professional**

Which arrangement applies is set per jurisdiction under state law. When a county does not take on its own building inspection program, the director of the Building Codes Division steps in. The director contracts with another municipality or a private firm, or staffs the program with state employees. That one statute is the basis for both the state-direct model and the privately contracted model.

For a given city or county, the Directory names the department that currently administers each permit service. It does not label the result as county-run, state-direct or contracted, so the arrangement is confirmed by asking the department, and it is current only at lookup time. Northwest Code Professionals, based in Eugene, is one private firm operating Oregon departments under contract.

Authority does not vary by arrangement. Inspectors and plan reviewers employed by a private firm hold the same Oregon certification as county employees, and the same authority to issue correction notices and to sign off work. Certification attaches to the role of building official, inspector or plans examiner, not to the employer. Under the statute a municipality treats a licensed firm's inspections and plan reviews as its own and bears no liability for the firm's work. That liability rule does not reduce the firm's authority.

One oversight step is specific to contract officials. State law defines a contract building official's discretionary decision as waiving a plan review, an inspection or a code provision, or allowing an alternative material, design or method. Each such decision is ratified or disapproved by a designated, trained county employee, and a local appeal board exists in addition to the state appeal path. Ratification deadline, notice duties and the audit rule are in the Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules chapter.

In a county where the Building Codes Division administers the program directly, a state-employed building official fills the role of building official for that jurisdiction. That official holds the same alternative-materials approval authority as a county employee. No alternative-materials request routes to Salem because the state administers the program. Whichever certified building official the division has assigned decides it, with the same written approval or written denial stating reasons. Where the state has assumed a program that a contract official serves, the director holds the ratification power a municipality would hold. For a second opinion on a novel proposal, that official can request a Statewide Code Interpretation, a Building Codes Division ruling on how a code provision applies.

Stop-work authority follows the same pattern. ORSC Chapter 1, in both the 2023 and the 2026 editions, lists the model code's stop-work-order section as not adopted by the State of Oregon. A note beside it states that a municipality may regulate the matter under its own ordinances. Stop-work authority therefore comes from the municipality's ordinance, which the state building code statute preserves. The certified building official exercises it whether the department is county-run, state-direct or a private contract.

**Code**

```
A county that does not assume its own program is served by the state or a contractor.
  Where a county does not notify the director that it will administer and enforce a building inspection program, the director contracts with a municipality or other person, or uses state employees or agencies, to run the program.
  Source: ORS 455.148(6).

A licensed firm's inspectors and plan reviewers hold the full authority of their certified role.
  Certification and duties attach to the Oregon-certified role of building official, inspector or plans examiner regardless of employer; a municipality recognizes and accepts a licensed firm's activities as its own and bears no liability for them.
  Source: OAR 918-098-1470; ORS 455.080; ORS 455.148(9) and (10).

A contract building official's discretionary decisions are ratified by the municipality.
  A discretionary decision is waiving a plan review, an inspection or a provision of the state building code, or allowing an alternative material, design or method of construction; a designated qualified employee, trained to the director's standard, ratifies or disapproves it. Where the state has assumed the program, the director holds the same ratification power.
  Source: ORS 455.202(1); ORS 455.148(13)(d).

Stop-work authority is local, not a residential-code provision.
  ORSC Chapter 1 lists Section R114, Stop Work Order, as not adopted by the State of Oregon in both the 2023 and 2026 editions; the state building code statute preserves municipal authority to enforce through penalties, stop-work orders or other means.
  Source: ORSC R114 (2023 and 2026 editions, not adopted); ORS 455.020(4).
```

## 2.7 The owner-builder's place in the map

**Simple**

Oregon lets you build your own house without a contractor's license. That role comes with duties. You become the permit holder, the one who signs the homeowner statement, schedules every inspection and answers for the jobsite. Names on the permit, the licenses, the inspection card and the certificate have to match who did the work. Two jobs are closed to you at any price: propane piping and a heat pump's refrigerant lines. Two more, septic and the well, run on their own permits from other agencies.

**Applied**

Assign the roles in writing, and keep the list with the permits.

| Role | Who holds it on this project | Where the name appears |
|---|---|---|
| Permit holder | The owner, as homeowner applicant | Each permit and the inspection card |
| Signer of the homeowner statement | The owner | The Property Owner Statement filed with the structural permit |
| Structural, plumbing and electrical work | The owner under the homeowner, plumbing and electrical exemptions, or a licensed contractor | The permit application and the contractor disclosure form |
| LP gas work | A State Fire Marshal licensed installer | The gas permit and the tank installation report |
| Refrigerant connections | An EPA Section 608 certified technician | The mechanical permit record |
| Septic installation | The owner under a DEQ construction permit, or a certified installer | The DEQ or county permit |
| Well drilling | The owner under a Landowner Well Permit, or a licensed constructor | The OWRD permit and well log |
| Inspection scheduling and the jobsite | The owner | The inspection record, the labor log and the insurance certificates |

1. Match every name on a permit, license, inspection record and certificate to the person who did the work.
2. Hire a licensed installer for LP gas and a certified technician for refrigerant, and record each license number in the binder.
3. Decide which of the five timing rules applies before any sale, lease or transfer plan.

**Professional**

Oregon has no statewide three-year resale clock for owner-built homes. Five separate rules govern the timing questions instead, and each controls a different thing.

| Rule | What it controls | What it does not control |
|---|---|---|
| Intent-to-sell test in contractor-licensing law | Whether the construction counts as owner-occupier work or as contracting for sale | No fixed time period |
| Structural-exemption cooldown | How often an owner can reuse the structural-code exemption on a new permit | Resale timing |
| Rural-county exemption cooldown | How often a participating county issues an exempt owner-built-dwelling permit to one person | Resale timing |
| Post-completion lien notice | A notice duty when a home is sold soon after substantial completion | Whether the sale may happen |
| Federal capital-gains exclusion | How long the owner lives in the home before a sale escapes capital-gains tax | Oregon construction law |

Each rule, with its citations and its resolved position, is in the Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules chapter. Homeowner, owner-builder, contractor, trade contractor, engineer, inspector, jurisdiction and brand entity are different legal persons with different duties. Each of the permit, the license, the inspection record and the certificate of occupancy names someone, and the names must match the work as performed.

Four trade-side rules are hard boundaries. LP gas has no homeowner exemption at all. Septic and water wells run on separate state permit paths, each with its own owner permit. Refrigerant handling is federal, and homeowner status is irrelevant to it.

**Code**

```
The licensing exemption turns on intent, with no fixed time period.
  The owner-occupier exemption does not apply to a person who builds or arranges construction intending to offer the structure for sale before, upon or after completion; non-occupancy after completion is prima facie evidence of intent to sell.
  Source: ORS 701.010(5).

The structural and rural-county exemptions each have a five-year reuse limit.
  An owner who used the structural-code exemption may not use it again for five years; a participating rural county issues an exempt owner-built-dwelling permit to a person at most once every five years. Both limit exemption frequency, and neither restricts resale.
  Source: ORS 455.320; ORS 455.335.

A sale soon after completion requires a lien-protection notice.
  The seller serves the Homebuyer Protection Act notice when a residential structure is sold within about 75 days of substantial completion.
  Source: ORS 87.007; ORS 87.093.

Federal tax sets its own occupancy clock.
  The personal-residence capital-gains exclusion requires ownership and use as a principal residence for two of the five years before sale.
  Source: IRC section 121.

An owner may install their own septic system under their own permit.
  The property-owner exception to installer licensing applies to installation on the owner's property under a properly issued construction permit; a DEQ-certified installer is otherwise required.
  Source: OAR 340-071-0600.

Refrigerant work requires federal certification regardless of ownership.
  EPA Section 608 technician certification applies whenever refrigerant is handled, including connecting line sets and charging.
  Source: 40 CFR part 82 subpart F.
```

## 2.8 Inspection checkpoints

**Simple**

Inspections set the order of the build. Concrete cannot be poured until the footing inspection passes, and walls cannot be closed until the rough plumbing, electrical and mechanical inspections pass. Nobody moves in until the finals pass. At each one a certified inspector checks the work before it disappears under concrete, backfill or drywall. Each ends with a signature on the card posted by the door. Missing one means uncovering or demolishing finished work so the inspector can see what is underneath. For a concrete house the footing and wall inspections matter most, because an hour after the pour nothing can be changed.

**Applied**

Schedule each pour around its inspection.

| Inspection | When | What gets verified |
|---|---|---|
| Permit application and plan review | Before any regulated work | Approved drawings, calculations, engineering |
| Erosion control or site inspection | Where the jurisdiction requires it | Erosion measures, site-disturbance limits |
| Footing | Before concrete placement | Footing geometry, reinforcement, anchor bolts, bearing surface, concrete-encased grounding electrode |
| Foundation drainage and dampproofing | Before backfill | Drain rock, perimeter drain, membrane, capillary break |
| Under-slab | Before slab placement | Subgrade, capillary break, vapor retarder, soil-gas retarder where required, insulation, sleeves |
| Concrete or masonry wall | Before grout or concrete placement | Reinforcement size, location, spacing and laps, embedded systems, grout cleanouts and grout space |
| Rough plumbing | Before walls or chases close | DWV test, water-supply rough, gas pressure test where applicable |
| Rough electrical | Before walls close | Boxes, conductors, panel rough, grounding, bonding, low voltage where included |
| Rough mechanical | Before walls or ceilings close | Equipment, ducts, gas piping, ventilation, exhaust, refrigerant lines |
| Insulation and air sealing | Before drywall | Insulation type, depth, continuity, sealed penetrations |
| Energy and blower door | Where the compliance path requires testing | Blower-door result, duct-leakage result if applicable |
| Final building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical | Each discipline separately | Each discipline's final record |
| Site-development final | Before the certificate of occupancy | Permanent erosion control, stormwater, address |
| Certificate of occupancy | All disciplines final, Form 4823 submitted | Certificate issued, binder closes |

1. Call for the footing or wall inspection before you order the concrete, and confirm the inspector's route day for the parcel.
2. Order the concrete for a day after the approval.
3. Before the inspector arrives, have the reinforcement, embeds, sleeves, anchor bolts and the grounding electrode in place, and the approved drawings on site.
4. When the drawings call for special inspection, have that inspector on site for the placement and keep the report.
5. Photograph every element before it is covered, with the date and the inspection card in the frame.
6. When an item fails, correct it, request reinspection, and keep the correction notice with the photograph of the fix.
7. Submit the contractor information form before the certificate of occupancy.

**Professional**

In the residential code itself the inspection list is short: foundation, concrete slab or underfloor, mechanical and gas rough, frame and masonry, insulation and vapor retarder, and final. The building official may require others. Oregon adds a concrete-specific inspection. In Seismic Design Categories D0, D1, D2 and E, reinforced masonry, insulating-concrete-form and conventionally formed concrete walls get an inspection of their own. It comes after the embedded plumbing, mechanical and electrical systems and the reinforcing steel are in place, and before grout or concrete is placed. That inspection verifies the size, location, spacing and lapping of the reinforcement and, for masonry, the grout cleanouts and the grout space.

Engineered concrete design brings its own inspection program. ACI 318, in its 2019 and 2022 editions, places construction documents and inspection in Section 26, and Section 26.13 sets the categories. Continuous inspection applies during concrete placement, during tensioning and grouting of prestressing steel, during installation of adhesive anchors and, in some conditions, during placement of reinforcement. Periodic inspection applies to reinforcement and embedment placement, curing, formwork and reshoring. In-place strength is verified before shores come out from under beams and structural slabs.

ACI 332-20, the residential-scoped code for footings, foundation walls and slabs on ground, addresses materials and testing mainly by reference to the ASTM standards. ACI 318 governs any element outside its scope. On the construction documents the engineer of record states the inspection and testing program, and the building official enforces it through plan review and field inspection.

In Oregon a correction notice must cite the exact specialty-code section, administrative rule or statute and state the facts behind it. Each gate's readiness criteria, the corrective steps when work fails, and the records that result are in the Inspection and Approval Sequence chapter.

**Code**

```
Permitted work stays accessible and exposed until approved.
  Construction or work for which a permit is required remains accessible and exposed for inspection until approved; the permit applicant has the duty to keep it so, and neither the building official nor the state is liable for the cost of removing material to allow inspection.
  Source: ORSC R109.1 (2023 and 2026 editions).

Work may not proceed past an inspection point without approval.
  Work is not done beyond the point indicated in each successive inspection without the building official's approval; a portion that fails is corrected and is not covered or concealed until authorized.
  Source: ORSC R109.4 (2026 edition).

Concrete and reinforced-masonry walls in high seismic categories get their own inspection.
  In SDC D0, D1, D2 and E, reinforced masonry, ICF and conventionally formed concrete walls are inspected after embedded systems and reinforcing steel are in place and before grout or concrete placement; the inspection verifies reinforcement size, location, spacing and lapping and, for masonry, grout cleanouts and grout space.
  Source: ORSC R109.1.5.3 (2023 and 2026 editions).

The permit holder requests inspections and provides access.
  The permit holder or agent notifies the building official when work is ready and provides access to and means for inspection.
  Source: ORSC R109.3 (2026 edition).

A correction notice must cite the exact provision.
  Certified building officials, inspectors and plans examiners include an exact reference to the specialty-code section, administrative rule or statute, and a plain statement of the facts, in every corrective notice at a construction site or in plan review.
  Source: OAR 918-098-1900.

The certificate of occupancy waits on the contractor disclosure.
  The holder of the structural permit provides the building official with contact and license information for the general, electrical, HVAC and plumbing contractors who worked on the dwelling, on BCD Form 4823, before the certificate issues.
  Source: OAR 918-480-0140(3); BCD Form 4823, oregon.gov/bcd.
```

## 2.9 The project binder

**Simple**

Your binder is the proof that the code path was followed, from the first permit to the certificate of occupancy. On this project it is a paper set in a fireproof box, plus digital copies in two places, one off site. During construction it is the compliance record and the working reference at each inspection. After move-in it serves repairs, a sale, and whoever later asks what is inside a wall. Every inspection adds its signed card and photographs to it.

**Applied**

Start the binder at permit application and add to it at every inspection.

- permit applications, approvals and revisions
- approved plans, including any sheets revised after issuance
- plan-review comments and the response to each
- inspection cards, approvals and correction notices for each discipline
- the signed Property Owner Statement and any local equivalent
- engineering letters, stamped sheets, calculations, and the inspection and materials-testing records the engineered design requires (concrete strength tests, reinforcement-placement verification)
- product data, listings and manufacturer installation instructions
- concrete batch tickets, soil reports, septic approvals and well records
- the OWRD landowner permit and well log, and the State Fire Marshal tank report where one applies
- photographs taken before each cover stage
- subcontractor CCB and BCD license records and insurance certificates
- BCD Form 4823, the contractor information notification
- the final approval and occupancy records (the certificate or temporary certificate and any extensions)

Organization, retention and digital backup are in the Documentation, Maintenance, and Future Upgrades chapter.
