# Chapter 4: Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules

In Oregon the owner-builder exemption belongs to a person who holds the title, signs the homeowner statement at the permit counter and intends to live in the finished house. Land held in a limited liability company, or a house built to sell, puts the builder outside that exemption and makes the builder a contractor by operation of law. Oregon sets no waiting period before an owner-built home may be sold, and an intent test and four separate timing rules govern the sale instead. For an owner-made block, the state's Building Codes Division can rule on the material before any land is bought, and the ruling binds every building department in Oregon within its scope. Costs are 2026 figures unless a different year is given.

> **Jon's note.** [JON: drafted from the record, confirm or rewrite] I will hold the land in my own name and pull every permit as the homeowner. Better Smarter Homes stays a separate business that owns the plans, the writing and the brand, and never the land or the build. [JON: is the title yours alone, or yours and your wife's?] I mean to live in this house, and nothing in the plan assumes a sale. Family and friends who help are unpaid. Anyone I pay is either a licensed contractor or an employee with withholding and workers' compensation in place before the first day. Propane and refrigerant work go to licensed people because the law closes them to me. The block wall is the one material in the house the code does not list. [JON: do you want the state's ruling on it before you buy land, or a local approval at plan review?]

## 4.1 What stops the work and what only pauses it

**Simple**

Only a few legal problems stop this project outright. Most of the rest pause it until someone answers in writing. Propane work without a state license is a hard stop. So are moving in before the occupancy certificate and building through a company that means to sell. A trust on the title, a paid helper, a farm-exemption shop or an unlisted wall material waits for an official's answer. Everything else is a gate you pass with a permit or a record. Each question goes into one of five groups. A question put in the wrong group comes back later as a correction notice on finished work.

**Applied**

| Band | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Hard stop | The work does not proceed until the condition is cured | LP gas work without a State Fire Marshal licensee, occupying any structure before its certificate of occupancy or a temporary certificate, constructing through an entity that intends to sell |
| Pause and verify | The path may be acceptable, and an authority confirms it in writing first | Trust ownership, LLC involvement, paid helpers, a future resale or lease plan, the rural-county exemption, the agricultural-building exemption for a shop, several permit-exempt structures, a deferred submittal for the wall system |
| Permit gate | Work proceeds inside the permit path | Structural, plumbing, electrical, mechanical, septic, well, grading, driveway access |
| Documentation gate | Work proceeds with a record kept | Inspection passes, batch tickets, product listings, manufacturer instructions, photographs, written pre-application answers, alternate-method ruling conditions |
| Continuing duty | An obligation that outlasts the approval | Permit non-transferability, certificate conditions, deferred-submittal closures, surety releases |

1. Sort each legal question into a band before the phase it belongs to starts.
2. On a hard stop, wait until the licensed person, the certificate or the change of ownership is in place.
3. On a pause-and-verify item, put the question to the authority in writing and file the dated answer.
4. Treat a permit-gate item as scheduled work, with the permit before the work and the inspection before the cover.
5. On a documentation-gate item, decide what the record is before the work and file it the day it exists.
6. Put every continuing duty on a calendar with its date.

## 4.2 Who holds the title

**Simple**

Whose name is on the deed decides whether you can be the owner-builder at all. A company cannot live in a house, so one built through a limited liability company counts as built for sale. The company then becomes a contractor under the licensing law. Spouses on one deed fit the exemption. So do a living trust the occupant controls and two friends buying together, each with its own paperwork. The permit does not transfer if the title changes later.

**Applied**

1. Decide before the offer whether the title will be held by an individual, by spouses, by a revocable trust or by co-owners.
2. For any form other than an individual or spouses, get the county's written answer on who signs as owner before relying on the homeowner path.
3. Keep every property bought to build on out of any company, including the brand company.
4. Where a trust will hold title, confirm in writing that the occupant is the trustee and that the department accepts the trustee's signature.
5. Check any planned transfer to a child, a trust or a buyer against the intent test and the five-year reuse rule first.
6. Where a lender or an insurer requires a particular form of title, settle that before closing.
7. Budget attorney time at the Oregon average of $325 an hour, in a $206 to $504 range by practice area.

**Professional**

| Structure | Effect on the owner-builder posture |
|---|---|
| Individual title | The direct fit: the statutes' owner of record, performing work on property that person owns. |
| Spouses on title, as tenants by the entirety | Accepted, and spouses are on the CCB's family-member list. Portland's electrical permitting page lists a narrower immediate family (mother, father, sister, brother, daughter, son) without spouses, so the local list is read before a spouse pulls the electrical permit. |
| Revocable living trust with the occupant as trustee | Accepted by jurisdictions where the occupant plainly controls the trust, and confirmed locally before application. |
| Irrevocable trust with the occupant as beneficiary only | Fact-specific, with no statewide rule. The building department makes the call. |
| LLC ownership of property bought to build on | Outside the exemption. An LLC cannot occupy, so the Tandem Properties pattern applies. |
| A married couple titling the project in a jointly held LLC | The same result, with the natural-person occupancy defense given away. |
| Tenancy in common between unmarried co-owners | Oregon's default for two or more owners without a survivorship declaration. The posture follows the co-owner who signs, occupies and keeps the records, and a written agreement sets the exits. |
| Life estate with the builder holding the remainder | Unresolved under statutes that presume an applicant with present possession. The creating instrument gets attorney review and the department answers in writing who signs as owner. |

The licensing, structural and contractor-definition statutes each define the owner differently. Under the licensing exemptions the owner is one who contracts with licensed contractors or does the work on property that person owns. Under the structural exemption the owner is the title holder or contract purchaser of record on the last assessment roll.

An LLC that holds the land and builds meets the contractor definition on its face. In Tandem Properties LLC v. Construction Contractors Board (184 Or App 28, 2002) the Court of Appeals held such an entity a contractor by operation of law, with no occupancy defense open to it.

### Co-owners who are not married

**Simple**

Buying land with a sibling, a friend or an unmarried partner creates a tenancy in common unless the deed says otherwise. Each owner holds a separate share with no survivorship, and each share passes separately at death. Any co-owner can go to court to force a division or a sale of the whole parcel. A written agreement signed before closing keeps those exits out of court. On raw land meant for a house, an attorney drafts or reviews it. Without one, the failure mode is a partition lawsuit over land one owner has already poured concrete on.

**Applied**

Sign a co-ownership agreement before the deed records, drafted or reviewed by an Oregon real-estate attorney, with at least these terms:

- a buyout formula: appraisal, or a shotgun clause where one owner names a price and the other buys or sells at it
- a right of first refusal before any sale of a share to an outsider
- expense-sharing terms for taxes, insurance and site work, with a deadline and a remedy for a co-owner who stops paying
- a decision rule for day-to-day use of the land
- an exit timeline

**Professional**

In a partition suit every tenant, lien creditor and unknown claimant is joined as a defendant, with service by publication for those who cannot be found (ORS 105.215 to 105.230). Because the statute sets no fee, no timeline and no administrative shortcut, cost and duration follow whether the suit is contested and how many parties hold interests.

Querin Law's practitioner guidance puts the minimum agreement at a right of first refusal plus a shotgun or appraisal buyout, with the exit and day-to-day decision rules written out. Its comparison is a few hours of drafting against the cost of the suit. Generic co-ownership templates are sold online, and the same guidance calls for custom drafting or attorney review on raw land.

### A life estate and a remainder

**Simple**

Under a life estate an older family member keeps a property for life. The builder holds the remainder, the ownership that begins when the life estate ends. Oregon law defines that arrangement. It does not say whether the remainder holder can pull a building permit and build before the life estate ends. Until then the life tenant holds the right to possess the land, and the permit process is written for an applicant who possesses it.

**Professional**

ORS 112.345 vests a life estate in the devisee and the remainder in the named heirs, and ORS 93.050 governs the same arrangement created by conveyance. Present possession, and the duty to maintain the property, sit with the life tenant. Both owner-builder statutes (ORS 455.320 and ORS 701.010) presume an applicant with present possession and say nothing about divided present and future interests.

Whether a remainder interest satisfies "owns" in the licensing exemption is a question the statutes leave open. So is whether the remainder holder's intent to occupy on taking possession satisfies the structural exemption's intent test. Before any application under this structure, an estate-planning attorney reads the instrument that created the arrangement, including whether it addresses the life tenant's consent to construction. The county's building official then states who signs as owner.

**Code**

```
A conveyance to two or more persons creates a tenancy in common.
  Tenancy in common unless the deed clearly and expressly declares a right of survivorship; a conveyance to spouses married to each other creates a tenancy by the entirety unless it says otherwise.
  Source: ORS 93.180(1).

Any tenant in common may sue to partition the land.
  A circuit court suit for division by three court-appointed referees, a sale where division would cause great prejudice, or a court-valued buyout where neither is possible without great prejudice.
  Source: ORS 105.205; ORS 105.210; ORS 105.245.

An owner who builds to sell is a contractor.
  A person that owns property and constructs one or more residential structures with the intent of selling them is a contractor; a developer is such a contractor in the business of arranging construction.
  Source: ORS 701.005(5)(b) and (6).
```

## 4.3 The owner-builder role and the homeowner statement

**Simple**

Oregon lets you build your own house without a contractor's license, under three conditions. You own the land, you intend to live in the house, and you sign the state's homeowner form at the permit counter. That form lists what you are taking on. It adds no test, no interview and no callback, so your signature is the whole process. Every code rule, permit and inspection that binds a licensed builder still binds you. The exemption removes only the license, and the inspector checks your wall the same way as a contractor's.

**Applied**

1. Apply for each permit as the property owner, with your name as it appears on the deed.
2. At the structural permit, read the Information Notice to Property Owners About Construction Responsibilities before signing the Property Owner Statement below it.
3. Check the boxes for work on property you own and will live in, and for being your own contractor hiring only licensed, endorsed contractors.
4. Check the electrical and plumbing boxes where you will do that work yourself.
5. Keep the permit and the approved plans on the site, and the signed statement in the binder.
6. Schedule each inspection before the work is covered, and clear each correction notice before the next phase.
7. When the plan changes to a licensed general contractor, notify the permit office of the name and license number at once.
8. Hire a CCB-licensed contractor for any work the law requires one for, and for any work beyond your own skill.

### What the statement says

**Professional**

Under ORS 701.325(3) the CCB adopts one statewide form, the Information Notice to Property Owners About Construction Responsibilities, revised January 2, 2025. It serves the residential building, electrical, mechanical and plumbing permits and is published at oregon.gov/bcd/epermitting. Its first page is the notice. There the homeowner reads that unlicensed paid labor can make them an employer, with state and federal withholding, unemployment insurance and workers' compensation to pay. It also states that the homeowner is responsible for code compliance and inspection scheduling, and that liability and property-damage insurance should cover accidents to visitors on the site.

Below the notice sits the statement, a set of check boxes and a signature. Checking the boxes, the applicant states that the work is on property they own and reside in or will reside in, and that the permit is non-transferable. Two optional boxes cover the applicant's own electrical work under ORS 479.540 and own plumbing work under ORS 693.020. At the end the signer certifies that they are the homeowner applying for the permit and that the statement is true. No minimum holding period before sale appears on the form, since the sale and rental restrictions come from the exemption statutes themselves.

Beyond the signature there is no verification step. Neither the statute nor the current form adds a callback, a competency check or an interview. States with owner-builder examinations do that under their own law.

Two subsections of the licensing statute exempt the owner-builder. ORS 701.010(5) exempts an owner who contracts for work to be performed by a licensed contractor. ORS 701.010(7) exempts a person doing the work on their own property. Both cover an owner who hires licensed subcontractors directly. One condition, that permit-required work proceed by or under a residential general contractor, attaches to a third exemption, ORS 701.010(6). That subsection covers work on an owner's existing residential structures, up to three a year, and it does not reach a new dwelling built to occupy.

In the CCB's rules, family members are parents, spouses, siblings, children and their spouses, and grandchildren (OAR 812-002-0275). Neither "friends and relatives" in the structural exemption nor "immediate family" in the electrical exemption is defined in the statutes.

**Code**

```
An exempt applicant signs the property-owner statement before the permit issues.
  The applicant states the basis for the exemption, acknowledges the information notice, and names either the licensed general contractor or declares that they will be their own contractor hiring only CCB-licensed, properly endorsed contractors.
  Source: ORS 701.325(1) and (2).

An owner-built dwelling is built by the occupying owner and unpaid friends and relatives.
  Structural components constructed entirely by the title owner or contract purchaser of record who intends to occupy, or by that owner with friends and relatives assisting on an unpaid basis.
  Source: ORS 455.320(1).
```

## 4.4 Intent to occupy and the resale clocks

**Simple**

Whether a later sale causes trouble turns on one question: did you build the house to live in, or to sell? Living in the finished house is the strongest proof of the first answer. Never moving in is the law's standing evidence of the second. Four other clocks run beside that test, each on its own subject. Two limit how often you may reuse an owner-builder exemption. The third covers construction liens when a brand-new house is sold. The fourth, a federal rule, sets how long you live in a home before a sale escapes capital-gains tax.

**Applied**

1. Do not list, market or solicit offers while owner-builder work is in progress.
2. Move in only after the certificate of occupancy or a temporary certificate issues.
3. Keep the ordinary records of occupancy, meaning utility bills, mail, voter registration and the tax and insurance addresses.
4. When circumstances change after move-in, write down the reason at the time, whether a job change, a family change or a health change.
5. Before any sale, lease, trust transfer or company involvement, get an attorney's and a CPA's advice.
6. When selling a new house, choose one of the five lien-protection methods before closing and deliver the state form by closing day.

**Professional**

| Regime | Authority | What it controls | What it does not control |
|---|---|---|---|
| The intent test in contractor-licensing law | ORS 701.010, subsections (5) and (7) | Whether the construction counts as owner-occupier work or as contracting for sale | Any time period. Not living in the finished house is the statute's presumptive proof of intent to sell |
| The structural-exemption reuse limit | ORS 455.320 | How often one owner can use the ceiling-height, room-size and temperature exemption on a new permit | Resale timing |
| The rural-county exemption limit | ORS 455.335 | How often a participating county issues an exempt owner-built permit to one person | Resale timing |
| The Homebuyer Protection Act | ORS 87.007 | The seller's duty to shield the buyer of a new residence from construction liens | Whether, or when, the sale may happen |
| The federal capital-gains exclusion | 26 U.S.C. 121 | How long the owner lives in the home before a sale escapes capital-gains tax | Oregon construction law |

Under Tandem Properties, the controlling case on intent, plans that change after move-in are a different fact from building to sell. Occupancy has already rebutted the presumption, and the record then has to show the reason for the later sale. Occupancy is a dated event under the CCB's rules, so the record of the move-in is what rebuts the presumption. A second presumption, in ORS 701.031, matters only if the build repeats, and the business chapter treats it.

Portland's residential permitting page allows an owner's electrical permit only to one who owns and occupies the home with no sale or rental planned within six months. That six-month figure is Portland's working reading of the statute's phrase "not intended for sale, exchange, lease or rent". Other jurisdictions use other thresholds or none.

Federal tax starts its clock at move-in, because its test is use as a principal residence, and the certificate of occupancy starts no clock of its own. A sale forced earlier by a change in place of employment, health or unforeseen circumstances keeps a reduced exclusion, prorated by the months of use (26 U.S.C. 121(c)). The federal window is therefore the practical floor on a resale.

Oregon's Homebuyer Protection Act also reaches a house sold within three months of $50,000 or more of improvements, because a lien on that work can still be perfected after closing. For an owner-built house with no unpaid contractors, the simplest of the five methods is to close after the lien-filing window has run.

At any resale a separate duty attaches, on property condition rather than timing. The disclosure statement (ORS 105.464) covers only the seller's actual knowledge, and no hazard study is required. Its line items include floodplain status and designated slide or geologic hazard zones, and the OREF-020 form in statewide use adds wildfire-risk status. Whether a tsunami-inundation designation falls under the geologic-hazard line is read on the current form at the sale. An IBHS FORTIFIED-class design changes no disclosure duty, and its documented market effect is insurance pricing, settled with the specific insurer.

A concrete house at ordinary proportions meets all three rules the structural exemption waives (ceiling heights, room sizes and temperature), so it triggers no county-clerk recording. A county's rural exemption can never waive fire egress, smoke alarms, the structural code's maximum bending stress, or insulation and energy conservation (ORS 455.340). No recording requirement attaches to the alternate-method approval letter at all, so the binder is where a later buyer's lender or title company finds it.

### Three postures

| Posture | Facts | Result under the rules |
|---|---|---|
| Clean owner-builder | Land in the owner's name, permits as the homeowner, two years of building while living elsewhere, the certificate of occupancy, then move-in with utilities, mail, voter registration, tax filings and insurance at the address, and a sale after three years of residence | Actual occupancy rebuts the non-occupancy presumption. The federal exclusion applies after two of the last five years as a principal residence. No CCB exposure |
| Borderline, fact-dependent | The same start, then nine months in the house, an out-of-state job offer and the house listed | A genuine change of circumstances after completion is a defense to the intent test, not a violation. The federal exclusion is reduced in proportion to the nine months. The lien-protection duty applies to the sale of a new house. CCB exposure turns on the original intent |
| Speculative | Land in an LLC, permits pulled as a developer, no one moving in, and the house listed before the certificate issues | The LLC is a contractor by operation of law, and the exemption is unavailable to an entity. CCB enforcement under ORS 701.098, civil penalties under ORS 701.992, no standing to bring claims before the CCB, and implied-warranty exposure as a speculative builder under OAR 812-002-0680 |

**Code**

```

  Neither owner exemption applies to a person building, in an independent business, with intent to offer the structure for sale; not occupying the structure after completion is prima facie evidence of that intent.
  Source: ORS 701.010(5) and (7); Tandem Properties LLC v. Construction Contractors Board, 184 Or App 28, 2002.

Occupancy can begin at the first of four events, among others.
  A majority of furniture and personal belongings moved in, utility service begun, the certificate of occupancy issued, or the resident preparing meals and staying overnight.
  Source: OAR 812-002-0520.

Offering two or more new houses for sale within 36 months is evidence of contracting.
  A person who builds for their own use and offers for sale two or more newly built structures within any 36-month period is prima facie doing business as a contractor.
  Source: ORS 701.031(1).

Speculative construction is construction intended for sale.
  "Speculative" means in anticipation of, or with the intent of, selling to another entity during or after construction.
  Source: OAR 812-002-0680.

The owner-built structural exemption may be used once in five years, and its recording is conditional.
  An owner who used the exemption in the prior five years cannot use it again; the county-clerk recording applies only where the dwelling does not meet the waived requirements.
  Source: ORS 455.320(1)(a), (2) and (3).

A missing recording does not cloud title.
  Noncompliance with the recording requirement does not affect, in any manner, any conveyance of an interest in the property.
  Source: ORS 455.320(4).

A rural-county exempt permit issues to one person at most once every five years.
  The county's ordinance bars any person from receiving an exempt owner-built permit more than once every five years, and a seller must tell each potential buyer about the recorded notice of noncomplying items.
  Source: ORS 455.335(2); ORS 455.345(3) and (4).

  An action for actual damages within two years of the sale contract; the omission does not affect the conveyance itself.
  Source: ORS 455.350.

The seller of a new residence protects the buyer from construction liens perfected after the sale.
  By title insurance without lien exceptions, 25 percent of the price held in escrow, a 25 percent bond, lien waivers above $5,000 in aggregate, or closing after the 75-day lien-perfection deadline.

A seller delivers the statutory property-disclosure statement to every buyer who makes a written offer.
  One to four dwelling units bought as a residence; the buyer may revoke within five business days of delivery; a never-occupied dwelling's first sale is excluded if the seller names its permit numbers and issuer.
  Source: ORS 105.465; ORS 105.470(1); ORS 105.475.

Federal tax sets its own occupancy clock.
  Ownership and use as a principal residence for two of the five years before the sale; $250,000 of gain excluded for an individual, $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly.
  Source: 26 U.S.C. 121(a) and (b).
```

## 4.5 Who may do which work

**Simple**

Most of the work on your own house is yours to do: the building, the plumbing, the wiring, the heating equipment. With your own permits you may even install the septic system and drill the well. Some jobs stay closed to you whatever your skill. Propane work takes a state-licensed installer, and anything that touches refrigerant, such as connecting a heat pump's lines, takes a federally certified technician. Every job, yours or hired, still gets its permit and its inspection.

**Applied**

| Work area | Owner-builder posture | Permit and inspection |
|---|---|---|
| General residential building | Allowed under the homeowner path | Yes |
| Plumbing | Owner work on the owner's own building, with no sale-or-lease condition in the statute and no family extension | Yes |
| Electrical | Owner or immediate family, on property not intended for sale, exchange, lease or rent | Yes |
| Mechanical (HVAC) | No separate trade license, since the homeowner-applicant framework covers it | Yes |
| Refrigerant | EPA Section 608 certification, whoever handles it | Yes (a mechanical permit, and a heat pump also takes an electrical permit) |
| Natural gas piping | A homeowner or a licensed contractor may install it, per BCD | Yes |
| LP gas, any work | Hard stop: a State Fire Marshal licensee only | Yes, by the licensee |
| Septic | Owner installation under the owner's own DEQ construction permit, otherwise a DEQ-licensed installer | Yes (DEQ or the county) |
| Water well | A Landowner Well Permit, otherwise a licensed Water Supply Well Constructor | Yes (OWRD) |
| Fire sprinklers | Not required by the residential code in a one- or two-family dwelling. Where installed, plumbing-licensing rules govern the installer | Yes where installed |
| Boilers and water heaters | Water heaters within the exemption limits are outside the boiler law. A hydronic boiler takes an installation permit, and installing boilers as a business takes a boiler license | Yes |

| Trade | Permit required | No permit (like-for-like replacement only) |
|---|---|---|
| Electrical | Any permanent wiring, new circuits, panel upgrades, fuse-to-breaker conversions, garage-door-opener receptacles, low-voltage systems (alarms, audio-video, network) | Replacing outlets, switches and fixtures in place with GFCI where installed, lamps, resetting breakers, replacing a disposal or dishwasher of 30 amps or less, low-voltage garage-door wiring |
| Plumbing | New supply piping, drain-waste-vent piping, fixtures needing concealed work, water heaters, sewer lines, water service, backflow devices | Replacing a faucet, toilet, sink, hose bibb, water filter or disposal without altering concealed piping |
| Mechanical | Installing, altering or repairing vented heating or cooling systems, woodstoves, inserts and pellet stoves, ductwork, gas piping, bath fans, dryer and range exhausts, heat pumps, mini-splits and condensers | None for equipment replacement: a like-for-like furnace swap still takes a mechanical permit and a final inspection |

**Professional**

Contracting-side and trade-side exemptions are separate statutes with separate tests, so a person inside one can be outside another. Under the electrical exemption's immediate-family clause a spouse may wire the house. Under the plumbing statute, which names only the owner of the building, the same spouse has no exemption. On a rental or a house for sale the electrical exemption narrows again, to maintenance of existing installations and not new ones. Property managers, landlords' employees and business entities cannot use the owner-occupier exemption for someone else's residence.

Oregon adopted NFPA 13D (2022 edition) as its sprinkler installation code for one- and two-family dwellings, and the Building Codes Division's interpretation applies it to voluntarily installed systems. NFPA 1142 (2022 edition), the rural water-supply sizing standard, lets the fire authority reduce the required water supply where the dwelling is sprinklered throughout. An NFPA article on rural water supply (2024) gives a 1,000-gallon-per-minute, one-hour supply for a dwelling under 5,000 square feet, falling to 500 gallons per minute with sprinklers. Because the standard permits the reduction and no Oregon rule mandates it, the fire district decides it. Cistern sizing that depends on that answer is in the water independence chapter.

An owner installing a boiler in their own house runs no boiler business, so the contractor license does not reach the owner and the installation permit does.

DEQ's fact sheet on installing a new septic system states that a system is constructed by either the property owner or a DEQ-licensed installer, with DEQ-approved materials and equipment. Under the landowner well permit the owner, an immediate family member or a full-time employee runs the drilling machine. Before work begins the start card goes to the Water Resources Department, electronically or by mail three days ahead. After the statutory notice, department personnel may attend the sealing.

**Code**

```
An owner may do the plumbing on a building they own.
  Exempt from plumber licensing if the work complies with the plumbing rules, the plumbing law and the building code, permits and inspections included; the plumbing law itself exempts work not done for hire.
  Source: ORS 693.020(1)(a); ORS 447.060(1)(a).

An owner or immediate family may do the wiring on property not meant for sale or rent.
  No license for an installation on residential or farm property owned by the person or immediate family and not intended for sale, exchange, lease or rent; code and permit requirements still apply.
  Source: ORS 479.540(1).

LP gas work takes a State Fire Marshal license, with no homeowner exemption.
  No person, as employer or individual, may install, extend, alter or repair LP gas appliances, piping, vents or flue connections without the State Fire Marshal's installation license (Master Fitter, IC Fitter or RV Fitter).
  Source: ORS 480.432(1); OAR chapter 837, division 30.

The permit holder may install their own septic system without installer certification.
  The person responsible for constructing or installing an onsite system must be a certified installer unless that person is the permittee or the permittee's regular employee.
  Source: OAR 340-071-0650(1)(a).

A landowner may drill their own well under a Landowner Well Permit.
  $550 fee, $10,000 bond or letter of credit, the licensed constructor's construction standards, 10 days' written notice before sealing, a six-month permit, and the well report within 30 days.
  Source: ORS 537.753(4); OAR 690-205-0175; OAR 690-205-0200.

Refrigerant work requires federal certification regardless of ownership.
  EPA Section 608 technician certification applies whenever refrigerant is handled, including connecting mini-split line sets and charging.
  Source: 40 CFR part 82, subpart F.

Domestic water heaters are outside the boiler law up to fixed limits.
  Potable-water heaters with an approved relief device up to 120 gallons, 210 degrees Fahrenheit, 150 psi gauge and 200,000 Btu per hour, and tankless heaters, are exempt.
  Source: ORS 480.525(1)(b) and (c).

A boiler in a residence takes an installation permit, and the installing business a boiler license.
  Boilers in private residences are exempt from the boiler law except its construction, installation, alteration and repair provisions and installation permits; a person in the business of installing boilers holds a boiler contractor license.
  Source: ORS 480.525(3)(c); ORS 480.630(1).

A tenant has no owner exemption.
  A tenant or applicant doing repairs for a rent reduction may not do electrical or plumbing installation, maintenance or repair unless licensed under the electrical or plumbing law.
  Source: ORS 90.145(2).
```

## 4.6 Paid help, family and subcontractors

**Simple**

Friends and family can help build the house for free with no legal complication. A paid helper with no contractor's license makes you an employer from the first day. Tax withholding, unemployment insurance and workers' compensation come with it. Unlicensed work also draws the licensing board's fine, and the helper, not you, risks the criminal charge. Unpaid help and licensed contractors are the two clean arrangements. A licensed contractor comes with two consumer rules: a written contract above $2,000, and one business day to cancel it.

**Applied**

Write the labor plan before anyone works on the site:

- who will work, and whether they are paid
- what each person will do, and which permit covers it
- what license each holds
- who supervises
- what insurance covers the jobsite

1. When a person will be paid and holds no contractor license, stop and settle the employer duties in the table below before they start.
2. Before a subcontractor starts, check the CCB license at ccb.state.or.us and the complaint and disciplinary history at search.ccb.state.or.us.
3. For plumbing or electrical work, check the BCD trade license as well.
4. Require a written contract on the CCB's standard terms for any agreement above $2,000, and put every subcontract on paper whatever its size.
5. At signing, collect the contractor's Information Notice to Owner about construction liens and file it with the contract.
6. Collect the certificate of insurance, with the homeowner named as additional insured where the carrier allows it.
7. Confirm workers' compensation coverage on the subcontractor's employees.
8. File the contract, the license record, the insurance certificate and the inspection result in the binder.
9. For unpaid helpers, keep a dated log of each work day naming each helper, the relationship and the work done.
10. Have each unpaid helper sign a short statement, at or near the time, that no payment or exchange was made or expected.
11. Keep the paid subcontractors' records in a separate file from the unpaid-helper records.
12. Photograph the crew on structural work days, with names and dates noted.

|---|---|---|
| Workers' compensation coverage | ORS 656.027 | Coverage in place before the first paid day of any helper who is not a licensed contractor |
| The private-home exception | ORS 656.027(2) | The statute's case annotation summarizes Caddy v. SAIF (110 Or App 353, 1991) as limiting it to an already existing home, which leaves new construction on raw land outside it |
| The casual-labor exception | ORS 656.027(3) | A 30-day labor-cost cap the statute indexes each July 1, and no shelter for a helper paid week after week |

Unpaid family or friends, with no wages and no in-kind compensation, helping on structural work fit the owner-built definition. Everyone else triggers employer duties under the workers' compensation, state tax and federal employment laws, and the one safe case for paid help is a licensed, endorsed contractor. Coordinating the project is covered by the owner's own exemption. In the CCB's rules, construction management means selecting contractors, obtaining permits, scheduling specialty contractors' work and purchasing materials (OAR 812-002-0160). An owner doing that for their own house is doing work on their own property, which the statute exempts.

Between a hired contractor and the owner, the written-contract rule governs. It does not reach a subcontractor's agreement with a general contractor, a contract between two businesses. Written subcontractor agreements therefore stay the project's practice on liability grounds alone. Standard contract terms come from the CCB by rule, and a contractor who skips the writing answers to the board's penalty schedule.

Under ORS 701.225 the CCB investigates activities, not categories of person. Because the property owner receives the notice of noncompliance too, an owner-builder learns of an investigation from the board directly. Joint investigations with city or county inspectors are allowed, at the board's own expense. That track is licensing. Code compliance is the building department's separate track under ORS chapter 455, and whether one track opens the other is local practice.

CCB field investigators also sweep active construction sites, and the 2024 statewide sweep produced 226 proposed violations (Newport News Times, 2024). Visible, active construction is therefore itself a trigger, independent of any complaint. Neighbor complaints, building-department referrals and workers'-compensation-carrier flags are the other known triggers.

Four exposures stack when a paid helper is unlicensed:

- the board's civil penalty on any person who violates the chapter
- the helper's own misdemeanor
- the workers'-compensation penalty, keyed to the unpaid premium
- back withholding and unemployment tax, with interest

The daily compensation penalty runs only after a final order, so a mistake corrected before an order avoids it. Whether voluntary disclosure of a mid-project employer mistake draws different treatment than discovery, and which agency to approach first, is an attorney question before any contact is made.

## 4.7 The brand company and the words it may not use

**Simple**

Better Smarter Homes, the company behind the plans and the writing, has to stay out of the house itself. Designs, words, photographs and the name belong to the company. You own the land, pull the permits and live in the house. Money moves the same way: company income stays in the company account, and the house is paid for from personal funds. A further rule applies to what the company says about itself. In Oregon, calling yourself an engineer on a card, a website or in conversation is practicing engineering, and practicing without a license is unlawful. Oregon reserves the word architect the same way.

**Applied**

Hold both separations for the life of the company.

Keep the activity separate:

- the company owns the intellectual property: plans, content, branding, trademarks, domain names, video, photographs, written material
- the company sells educational material, runs advertising, accepts sponsorships and gives advisory consulting
- the company buys, builds and sells no homes, and supervises or coordinates no construction on the owner's homes
- the natural-person owner holds title, pulls the permit, signs the homeowner statement and occupies
- an Oregon-licensed professional engineer is hired per project where stamped engineering is required

Keep the money separate:

- company revenue stays in the company account
- home-build expenses come from personal funds
- the company pays for no materials that go into the personal home
- company and personal accounting never commingle

Use the right-hand column in every public statement.

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| "Engineered by Better Smarter Homes" | "Designed by Better Smarter Homes" |
| "Better Smarter Homes-engineered design" | "Built using Better Smarter Homes design principles" |
| "Engineering services by Better Smarter Homes" | "Based on research by Better Smarter Homes" |
| "Stamped by Better Smarter Homes" | No visual that resembles a professional engineer's seal |
| "Better Smarter Homes-certified" | "Built to Better Smarter Homes' published methodology" |
| "Approved by Better Smarter Homes engineering" | Only where a licensed engineer is involved: "Structural engineering by [name and Oregon license number], in collaboration with Better Smarter Homes" |

**Professional**

Under the engineering statute's definition, the exposure starts with the words on the website, whatever services follow them. Oregon's State Board of Examiners for Engineering and Land Surveying has imposed civil penalties on individuals who held themselves out as engineers without registration. It publishes its disciplinary actions at oregon.gov/osbeels. Where a project needs stamped engineering, the registrant who stamps the sheets is the engineer of record for them. Beside the engineer's name the company's appears only in the form shown in the table.

Under the architecture statute's single-family exemption, a design-and-research company can produce home plans without an architect. "Home designer" is permitted wording, and "residential architect" is not.

**Code**

```

  A verbal claim, sign, advertisement, letterhead, card or any other implication that a person is a professional engineer, or any title implying it, is practicing or offering to practice engineering.
  Source: ORS 672.007(1).

  No practice or offer to practice without a valid certificate; falsely representing authority, using another's seal, and practicing when not qualified are separately prohibited; each final document carries the registrant's seal and signature.
```

## 4.8 The alternate-method path for the block wall

**Simple**

Blocks you make yourself are not on the residential code's list of approved materials. The code gives them two paths to approval. Locally, the building official can approve them for one project under the code's equivalence rule. You show they match what the code lists in strength, durability and fire safety. Or the state's Building Codes Division can issue an Alternate Method Ruling, a statewide decision that the material is acceptable. That ruling does not expire with one project or depend on one official's opinion. You can request it before you own any land. Any Oregon resident or property owner may file the request, and there is no fee.

**Applied**

1. Complete the Alternate Method Ruling Application, BCD Form 2936c.
2. State the material, design or method, the code section at issue, and the technical and scientific facts behind the request.
3. Attach the engineering and test data that exist: unit compression tests, the mix and curing records, and any prism or wall tests.
4. Submit by mail to P.O. Box 14470, Salem, OR 97309-0404, or by email to bcd.ptsptech@oregon.gov. No filing fee applies.
5. File before buying land where the material question should be settled first. No site address is required.
6. Expect division staff to work the proposal with you, local building officials and industry groups, and answer each round of questions in writing.
7. Track the board meeting where the request is heard, and sign up for verbal testimony or submit written testimony 24 hours ahead.
8. Save the ruling with its stated scope and conditions in the binder, and cite its number on the drawings.
9. For a later project inside the ruling's scope, submit the ruling with the permit application.

**Professional**

Because any Oregon property owner is a petitioner, an owner facing a reluctant local official can start a state-level review without that official's cooperation. By statute a request from anyone other than a building official is made, and the ruling issued, before the material is used (ORS 455.060(1)), which puts the ruling ahead of the permit. With a ruling in hand, plan review covers the project itself, the foundation, the connections and the fire separation, and not the material again.

Three stages make up the division's stated process. Program staff work with the petitioner, local building officials and industry advisory groups to develop the proposal. Then the appropriate advisory board recommends on its technical and scientific facts, and the division decides and distributes the ruling. No statutory deadline binds any stage. For a structural masonry or concrete request the reviewing board is the Building Codes Structures Board, which administers the structural, prefabricated-structures and energy programs. Its eleven statutory seats:

- an architect
- a general contractor specializing in buildings more than three stories above grade
- a contractor specializing in construction for heavy industry
- a representative of the building trade
- a representative of a utility or energy supplier
- a representative of a fire protection agency
- a building official
- an owner or manager of a commercial office building
- a representative recommended by the Oregon Disabilities Commission
- a fire protection engineer
- a structural engineer specializing in residential buildings more than three stories above grade

Under ORS 455.135 the Residential and Manufactured Structures Board administers the low-rise residential program, and it has a public-member seat. Which board takes a residential-scale structural novelty is put to the division at filing. Boards are separate per program (structures, residential and manufactured structures, electrical and elevator). A project with a structural novelty and an electrical one therefore files separate requests reviewed by separate boards, and the rules name no joint intake. The rules state coordination only at the appeal stage.

Reconsideration asks the division to look again, with no described second level of review, and whether it reaches a denied ruling is unresolved. For a complex issue the division administrator can suspend the chief's decision clock. No rule allocates the cost of technical review a board commissions, so that question goes to the division at filing.

Benton County permitted a rammed-earth house, reported in 2017 as the county's first, after requiring architectural drawings, engineered drawings and compression testing of the earth-cement mixture (Corvallis Advocate, 2017). Its Community Development office holds the permit record. Evidence rules for the request (test scale, approved agencies, evaluation reports) are stated in the compliance-map chapter, and the test program for the units in the owner-made blocks chapter.

### Three orders for the approval

Ruling first, before land purchase or permit filing, has rule text behind every step: no site address required, statewide effect, and no dependence on local staff. Its cost is the calendar, because no deadline binds the division, and site-specific questions (setbacks, local amendments) surface only once a site exists.

Filing the alternative-materials question inside the local permit application is one process instead of two. Whether the application is a complex structure off the plan-review clock is the reviewer's determination, since the statute names complex structures and nothing about alternative materials. Coupled schedules follow, and nothing on the permit is approved faster than the material question.

Main permit first, with the wall system deferred, depends most on local discretion, because a county may decline a phased permit for a dwelling. Wilsonville's published policy (BPP 111) is the named local example, with a plan-review fee on the deferred item's own valuation and a 5 to 10 business day review target. No rule protects the 180-day permit clock while a deferred item is pending. Before anyone relies on deferral, the official's written answer covers what a rejection triggers after other work has proceeded.

**Code**

```

  Where required, the request is in writing; approval needs equivalence to the prescribed requirements in quality, strength, effectiveness, durability, safety other than fire safety, and fire safety; a denial comes in writing with reasons.
  Source: ORSC R104.2.2 through R104.2.2.4 (2026 edition; R104.11 in the 2023 edition).
```

## 4.9 Contract building departments

**Simple**

Some Oregon cities and counties do not staff their own building department. They hire a private firm to run plan review and inspections under contract. Northwest Code Professionals is one, serving about 50 jurisdictions in Oregon, Washington and Idaho. Oregon law adds oversight to that arrangement. A public employee checks the contract official's decision on any alternative material. Appeals of that decision go to a local board, on top of the state's route, and the department's finances are audited. The contract itself has an end date, and a county can change firms in the middle of your project.

**Applied**

1. Ask the department whether a contract firm runs it, and under which arrangement.
2. Ask how long the current contract runs, and what the county's practice is for open permits and prior determinations when it changes.
3. Ask who the county's qualified employee is and how ratification of discretionary decisions is handled.
4. Where a firm sells pre-submission consulting in Oregon, treat a favorable consultation as information and not as approval.
5. File the answers, dated, in the binder.

**Professional**

Where a county runs no program at all, the division's director runs it and holds the ratification power a municipality would hold. The arrangements themselves, including licensed firms that do plan review and inspection for a municipality's own building official, are described in the compliance-map chapter.

The alternative-materials approval for this house is a discretionary decision in the statute's terms, so the oversight attaches to it. The qualified employee is the municipality's own staff member, trained to the director's standard before overseeing anyone.

On the local appeal board sits a building official from the county or an adjacent county, and never a contract official or anyone from an inspection company. On appeal the applicant carries the burden of proof, by a preponderance of the evidence. The showing is that overturning the decision creates no dangerous or unsafe condition and lowers no fire or life-safety minimum. Every permit fee collected and spent falls inside the audit.

Fee-schedule parity and inspector continuity are contract terms between the municipality and the firm, standardized nowhere in the statute. Contracts change or end mid-project, and the statute says nothing about an open permit or an in-process alternative-materials approval when one does. Northwest Code Professionals markets a paid code-consulting service to architects and developers, and its own website states that consulting services are not available in Oregon. Where a contracted firm does sell pre-submission consulting in Oregon, the paid consultation and the later official review are legally distinct even when the same staff perform both. Such a consultation binds nothing.

**Code**

```
A contract official's discretionary decision is ratified by the municipality.
  Waiving a review, an inspection or a code provision, or allowing an alternative material, is ratified by the qualified employee within 30 days of notice; the applicant gets each decision with its appeal options.
  Source: ORS 455.202(1)(c) and (3).

A local board hears appeals of those decisions, on top of every other appeal right.
  The applicant appeals within 30 days of the notice and the board decides within 30 days; the right is in addition to, and not in lieu of, the state appeal route.
  Source: ORS 455.202(4) and (5)(a).

A municipality using a contract official is audited.
  An independent audit of the building-inspection program's finances at least every two years for a city, and covering the contract period for a county, with the results posted on the municipality's website.
  Source: ORS 455.202(6).

A county's own contract official is a stopgap.
  After January 1, 2022 a county may hire a contract building official only for 180 days while recruiting a building official, plus 180 more days with the director's approval.
  Source: ORS 455.202(2); ORS 455.148(6).
```

## 4.10 Pre-application contact and the written record

**Simple**

Counties answer questions before a permit application, in a pre-application or early-assistance meeting where they offer one. Nothing said in that meeting binds the county. Your plan review is decided later, against the application you submit, by whoever holds the job that day. Over an owner-builder's two or three years, the code edition changes on its three-year cycle, and staff change. A written, dated answer from the office stays usable through those changes. A state-level ruling stays usable in every county at once.

**Applied**

Before purchase, send the target county's building official a written inquiry, addressed by title, asking for a written reply. Email is enough. Ask as separate numbered questions:

1. whether the jurisdiction has reviewed or approved any alternative-materials request for the material, and for a copy of any approval and its conditions
2. whether the department will treat a statewide Alternate Method Ruling as binding on a future permit application, or will conduct its own review
3. what engineering documentation (compression testing, a stamped design, named ASTM or ACI standards) the department requires to approve the material where no ruling exists
4. whether any local ordinance or amendment affects alternative-materials review beyond the statewide framework
5. who the current building official of record is

Then:

1. File the dated reply in the binder.
2. At any meeting, open with the code section the material must satisfy and present the engineering or test data in hand.
3. Schedule the meeting where the county offers one, at its fee and lead time.
4. Schedule the septic and well agencies separately around a shared date, septic first, because the other site-plan elements depend on its result.

| Jurisdiction | Pre-application offering | Fee |
|---|---|---|
| Benton County | Pre-development and pre-construction meetings | Free |
| Portland | Time Certain Appointment, and a paid Life Safety Preliminary Meeting program with a structural reviewer for an added fee | $77.50 for the appointment, and the meeting fees in Portland's current fee schedule |
| A rural county | Its own program, where it has one | Its own fee and lead time |

**Professional**

Each county sets what its pre-application meeting produces and what that output means, and no statewide rule makes a meeting statement binding. Minutes and checklists are written to list topics rather than to record a yes-or-no position on a material, which is why the written inquiry is the durable instrument. Nothing standardizes who attends. With the engineer of record at the first meeting the official has more technical framing to respond to than the owner's prepared talking points give.

Septic and well run on separate agency tracks, county environmental health or DEQ and the Water Resources Department. Those contractors attend a building-department meeting only where a cross-agency question is on the agenda. No combined multi-agency site-visit program exists, and one visit day is the owner's own scheduling.

Oregon adopts specialty-code editions on a three-year cycle tied to the model codes, and the 2026 residential code took effect on October 1, 2026. Its phase-in and the rule on which edition a permit vests under are in the compliance-map chapter. Two records outlast staff turnover, the dated written answer attributed to the office and a statewide ruling that no local change can revoke.

## 4.11 Equivalence paths at DEQ, OWRD and Oregon OSHA

**Simple**

Other rulebooks besides the building code have an equivalence path. Wastewater rules, well-construction rules and workplace-safety rules each have their own version. With a novel wall, an experimental wastewater component and a non-standard well, a project runs three separate applications at three agencies. Each runs on its own rules and its own timeline. No office joins them up, and the owner is the one party tracking all three.

**Applied**

1. For a wastewater component outside the approved list, apply to DEQ under the new-or-innovative-technology rule, with the review fee and the performance evidence.
2. For a well outside the construction standards, have the bonded well constructor request OWRD's approval before the well is completed.
3. Include the well's location, its distances to the nearest well and septic system, why the standard cannot be met, and a design with site map.
4. Once the project has a paid worker and shop-built equipment that matches no rule, apply to Oregon OSHA for a variance.
5. State the rule, the type of variance sought, the interim protection for employees, and the means that make the work as safe as compliance.
6. Run the three on one calendar with the building request, and expect no agency to cite another's decision.

**Professional**

| Track | Rule | Fee | Decision deadline in the rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Building: Alternate Method Ruling | OAR 918-008-0095 | None | None |
| Onsite wastewater: new or innovative technology | OAR 340-071-0135 | DEQ's innovative-technology review fee, in the onsite fee schedule's Table 9F, with the amount from DEQ | None |
| Water supply well: special standards | OAR 690-200-0021 | None | None, and the approval can be given orally |
| Workplace safety: variance | OAR 437-001-0400 to 437-001-0435 | None stated | None, and an interim order is available pending the decision |

DEQ applies the same equivalence test the building code does, decided by the agency instead of a local official. Its rule also provides for study installations of technologies not yet approved. Because the well rule names a bonded constructor as the requester, an owner drilling under a landowner permit asks the department before relying on a non-standard design.

Oregon OSHA's variance becomes relevant on the day the project has an employer, the first paid worker. Affected employees are told of the application and may comment.

None of the three rules references the others, and none references the building-code process. Wastewater and well sizing is in the water independence chapter, and production-equipment safety in the owner-made blocks chapter.

## 4.12 Permit exemptions for the shop and farm buildings

**Simple**

Small sheds and shops can be legal to build without a building permit. Oregon exempts a one-story detached accessory structure, a building serving the main use of the property, up to 200 square feet. On a parcel of two acres or more the limit is 400 square feet. The exemption skips only the building permit, so the wiring, plumbing and heating permits still apply. It cannot be built for sleeping or fitted for permanent cooking.

**Applied**

1. Confirm the area limit, 200 square feet of building area, or of floor area where there is no roof.
2. Confirm the height limit, 15 feet from grade plane to the average roof height. Grade plane is the average finished ground level against the building.
3. On a parcel of 2 acres or more, use the 400-square-foot limit where it fits.
4. Pull the trade permits anyway, since circuits for a press or a mixer, plumbing and mechanical work each take their own permit.
5. Build to the code. Exempt or not, the structure meets the residential code's structural, material and safety requirements, and the zoning setbacks apply.
6. For several exempt structures, get the building official's written position on whether the cluster counts as one structure.
7. Put the planned shop use to the building official as a written classification question before any side-business use begins.
8. Raise a permit-exempt test structure in the planned wall material at the pre-application meeting, as the demonstration it is.

**Professional**

Oregon added the larger-parcel exception by amendment, and the base IRC has no such expansion. In the 2023 edition it carried a setback condition from all property lines and regulated structures. In the 2026 edition's printed text that condition is gone, the undefined word "nonhabitable" is replaced by a two-part test, and the exemption extends to roofless structures measured by floor area. Zoning setbacks apply to the structure whatever the building code says. The code sets no limit on the number of exempt structures on a parcel, and nothing in it says when a cluster becomes one larger structure.

The residential code's scope covers detached houses of one or two units and townhouses, Group R-3, up to three stories, with their accessory structures. In the 2023 edition's definitions an accessory structure has a floor area up to 3,000 square feet and two stories, used incidentally to the dwelling on the same lot. The 2026 scope lists accessory structures up to three stories above grade plane.

A home business changes the classification by its use. The live/work provision the scope references sits in the structural specialty code, so a workshop converted to a business with public or employee occupancy raises a change-of-use question that can cross into that code. Renting a completed dwelling changes nothing in the residential code by itself, which lists post-occupancy lease and rental arrangements among the matters outside its statutory authority (R101.2.3.1, 2023 and 2026 editions).

Aerial and satellite imagery is already used to find unassessed improvements, and imagery vendors such as OnGeo market it for detecting unpermitted construction. A staged-construction plan therefore rests on written exemption confirmations and the exemption limits, with no reliance on going unnoticed.

Building a permit-exempt test structure in the planned wall material is a sequencing option with no disclosure duty attached. No alternate-method rule requires a list of unrelated exempt structures, and an exempt structure was never reviewed, so no official is bound by it in either direction. How a specific official weighs a weathered, standing example of the material is that official's judgment. Whether to build one first is the owner's sequencing decision, and the test-structure strategy is in the owner-made blocks chapter.

### The agricultural-building exemption

**Simple**

On farm or forest land, a true agricultural building needs no structural permit at all. A barn, an equipment shed or a crop-storage building can qualify. Qualification turns on the building's use, against a closed list in the statute. Dwellings never qualify, and neither do buildings the public uses. Manufacturing building materials fits none of the listed uses. Selling blocks from such a shop fails the personal-use test the statute sets for any extra use.

**Applied**

1. Check the use against the statute's list of equipment storage and repair, crops and forest products, livestock and dairying, and the farm's own produce.
2. Do not rely on the exemption for a block-production shop without the county's written answer. Block manufacturing appears nowhere on the list.
3. Ask the county which qualification form it uses.
4. Budget for the electrical, mechanical, plumbing and septic permits, which the exemption leaves in place.
5. Treat a later shop-to-house conversion as a full legal question. Conversion ends the qualification and exposes the never-reviewed structure to full code review.
6. Before any conversion or physical connection to the house, get the building official's written statement of what it triggers, and an attorney's review.
7. Compare the two paths on paper, the permit fee and plan review against the county's qualification fee, timeline and use risk.
8. For a production operation, describe the equipment and volume to DEQ's Air Quality program and ask whether a permit applies.

**Professional**

Qualification is a county planning function, and the statute names no state process. On the division's form the county verifies farm or forest zoning, checks the proposed use against the list, reviews, and mails a written decision. Fee and turnaround are the county's. Cities may regulate agricultural buildings within their limits. BCD's Form 2654 states that it serves the division's four field-office counties, Coos, Umatilla, Sherman and Wheeler. Five other counties post the same form: Clatsop, Polk, Malheur, Deschutes and Lake.

By the form's own words the exemption reaches the structural permit only, and the trade permits and any boiler installation permit still apply. Under the exemption the structure is never plan-reviewed. A conversion to a dwelling, or a physical connection to the house, therefore brings the whole structure under residential review at that moment rather than only the new work. An ordinary accessory-structure permit has a known fee schedule and carries neither the use-qualification risk nor the conversion risk, which makes it the lower-risk path for a block shop even where the exemption's fee is lower.

A block press and mixer raise a separate question at DEQ's Air Quality program. Cement and concrete dust is a regulated particulate category, and the permit threshold depends on production volume.

**Code**

```
A one-story detached accessory structure needs no building permit up to fixed limits.
  No sleeping or permanent cooking provisions; 200 square feet of building area and 15 feet to the average roof height, or 400 square feet on a parcel of 2 acres or more.
  Source: ORSC R105.2, Building, item 1 (2026 edition; the same item in the 2023 edition).

A fabric-covered frame structure needs no permit up to 500 square feet.
  Nonhabitable, one story, not more than 500 square feet of floor area, and not closer than 3 feet to a lot line.
  Source: ORSC R105.2, Building, item 16 (2026 edition; item 15 in the 2023 edition).

An agricultural building on a farm or forest operation is outside the structural code.
  Used for farm or forestry equipment storage and repair, crops or forest products, livestock, poultry, fur-bearing animals or honeybees, dairying, or other agricultural, forestry or horticultural use including preparing and storing the farm's produce.
  Source: ORS 455.315(1) and (2)(a)(A).

An agricultural building is never a dwelling or a public building.
  Not a dwelling, not a structure with 10 or more people present at once unless it grows plants, not a public-use or fire-marshal-regulated structure, and not a flood-insurance-act structure.
  Source: ORS 455.315(2)(a)(B).

  Incidental and accessory to a listed use, personal to the farm owner and the owner's immediate family or household, and no greater hazard to persons or property than the listed uses.

  Live/work units in detached one- and two-family dwellings comply with Section 508.5 of the Oregon Structural Specialty Code; an office under 10 percent of the dwelling's area is exempt from that section.
```

## 4.13 Insurance for an unconventional build

**Simple**

No code approval obliges an insurer to write a policy, and no Oregon rule conditions your owner-builder permit on insurance. Liability coverage is only recommended, on the state form. For a house with an unlisted wall material, the search for a carrier runs through a specialty broker. It starts before the design is final. Such brokers reach the surplus-lines market, where carriers write risks the standard companies decline. Underwriters there work from the broker's list of required documents.

**Applied**

1. Secure the alternate-method approval, or a clear path to it, before approaching any carrier.
2. Approach a broker with surplus-lines access, with the engineering documentation and any fire-test data in hand.
3. Ask for a non-binding indication, a carrier's preliminary read.
4. Expect a bindable quote only against finished construction documents.
5. Treat the broker's documentation list as the underwriting requirement.
6. Describe the house by its assembly performance, meaning noncombustible mass walls, the finish systems and their ratings, and any tested fire-resistance rating.
7. Ask the broker whether the carrier gives weight to the code-approval letter, and whether the mass wall's fire performance earns a premium credit.
8. Ask in the same letter what builder's-risk coverage requires for a self-made structural material, block production included.
9. Start with Insure Pacific, which advertises surplus-lines access for hard-to-place Oregon homes through more than 50 carriers, Lloyd's of London among them.

**Professional**

No Oregon statute conditions the owner-builder permit on insurance or a bond. License bonds attach to licensed contractors, and the CCB's information notice recommends liability coverage for injuries to people on the site who are not covered employees. Any local insurance or bond condition would come from a county ordinance, a question for the pre-application inquiry. Only a carrier's quote answers three questions:

- whether an approval letter carries underwriting weight
- whether fire performance offsets the unconventional classification
- what builder's-risk terms apply to owner-made materials

An assembly description gives the underwriter a tested property to price, such as a fire-resistance rating.

At resale, the buyer's insurer and appraiser check the finished, occupied house against its record. That record is the closeout binder, with the certificate of occupancy, the permits, the ruling and its conditions, and the inspection records.

## 4.14 Financing, appraisal and property tax

**Simple**

For a construction loan on an unconventional house, the hard step is the appraisal. Appraisers compare a house with recent sales of similar homes nearby. No owner-made-block house nearby has sold. The usual workaround is to search farther away and adjust the prices. Some lenders keep their loans rather than selling them. Those lenders can weigh the engineer's stamp and an in-person inspection over the appraisal. Building from cash removes the appraisal and the draw inspections, at the price of having the whole budget in hand.

**Applied**

1. Before applying, ask the lender how its appraisal panel handles a property with no local comparable sales.
2. Ask who performs the draw inspections, the lender's progress checks that release the money.
3. Ask whether a budget built on block-production milestones needs a briefing for that inspector.
4. Include portfolio lenders in the search, the banks and credit unions that keep loans on their own books and set their own underwriting.
5. Expect a down payment of 25 to 35 percent on an owner-builder loan, against 10 to 20 percent on a conventional construction loan.
6. Before bidding on land at a county tax foreclosure or a sheriff's sale, ask a title company whether it will insure the county's no-warranty deed.
7. Ask whether a curative step, a title certification or a quiet-title action, comes first.

**Professional**

Fannie Mae's selling guide requires the appraiser to address market resistance where improvements are incompatible with the competitive market, and to explain the effect on value and marketability (Selling Guide B4-1.3-05). Appraisers' standard workaround widens the comparable-sales search beyond the usual distance guidelines and documents dollar adjustments between locations. Whether that workaround is allowed, or the loan declined, depends on each construction-to-permanent lender's rules. Draw inspectors are third parties the lender hires to check percentage of completion against a line-item budget. Their standard is dollars against progress rather than the code, and a budget built on block-production milestones is unfamiliar to them.

County assessors face the same missing comparables when they value the finished house. They value by statistical models of similar-property groups rather than parcel by parcel. An unusual house fits such a model badly in either direction. It can be under-valued, or over-valued at the rate for a conventionally built house of the same size. Neither the approval letter nor the certificate of occupancy carries special weight in the valuation. A wrong assessed value goes to the county board of property tax appeals, as the Department of Revenue's real-property assessment guide describes. Foreclosure-land acquisition is in the land chapter, and the budget, draw schedule and cash flow in the project finance chapter.

**Code**

```
Assessed value rests on real market value.
  The amount in cash an informed buyer could reasonably be expected to pay an informed seller, each acting without compulsion, in an arm's-length transaction as of the assessment date.
  Source: ORS 308.205(1).
```

## 4.15 Permits, their clocks and the inspection gates

**Simple**

Every permit you hold has a deadline. Work has to start within 180 days of issuance. After a 180-day pause the permit ends, unless you asked for an extension in time. Plan review has a deadline too, ten working days for a simple house. An unlisted wall material can take a review off that schedule. Each inspection gates the next stage: work stays uncovered until its inspection passes, and the occupancy certificate waits on every trade's final. After a pour over unapproved steel, the only correction is removing the concrete.

**Applied**

For each phase of work, write down before it starts:

- which permit covers the work, and who pulls it
- who is legally allowed to do it
- what documents the application needs
- what the inspector must see before the work is covered
- which work cannot be covered until the inspection passes
- what the correction process is if it fails
- what record enters the binder
- when the permit expires if work pauses, and how to extend it

1. Calendar the 180-day clock from the day each permit issues, and again from the last inspection activity.
2. Request each extension by letter, with the reason, before the deadline, and file the official's grant.
3. During any dispute after issuance, ask the building official whether work on other parts of the permit counts as activity.
4. File extension requests in parallel with any dispute.
5. Ask at pre-application whether an alternative-materials request inside the application takes it off the plan-review clock.
6. Keep every element exposed until its inspection passes, and schedule each pour around its inspection.

**Professional**

Each city or county runs the state codes through its own permit, inspection and approval workflow, with its own fees and scheduling. Once concrete is poured, a trench backfilled, insulation installed or drywall hung, a permit problem can be fixed only by removing work. Expired permits have no reinstatement procedure in the code. What happens after expiration is therefore local policy, and the extension request before the deadline is the one remedy the code itself provides. No rule tolls the activity clock once the permit issues, so a dispute over a correction notice on an approved alternative material runs against the permit while it is argued.

At the legal level the inspection sequence runs:

- permit application and plan review
- pre-construction posting, site and erosion control
- footing, foundation, reinforcement and embeds, then foundation drainage
- under-slab
- the plumbing, electrical and mechanical roughs
- insulation, air sealing and energy testing
- the discipline finals and the site-development final

Each stage's legal trigger, the readiness criteria for every inspection and the record each produces are in the Inspection and Approval Sequence chapter.

**Code**

```
A permit expires after 180 days without work.
  Invalid when work does not start within 180 days of issuance or stops for 180 days; one or more written extensions of up to 180 days each, on request showing justifiable cause.
  Source: ORSC R105.5 (2023 and 2026 editions).

A pending application is abandoned after 180 days.
  An application not pursued in good faith is deemed abandoned 180 days after filing unless a permit has issued; extensions of up to 180 days each on a written request showing justifiable cause.
  Source: ORSC R105.3.2 (2023 and 2026 editions).

Plan review for a simple dwelling runs on a statutory clock.
  10 business days from a complete application in a jurisdiction under 300,000 people, 15 at 300,000 or more; a complex structure needing additional review, or a plan needing outside-agency approval, is exempt.
  Source: ORS 455.467(1) and (2).

An appeal extends the plan-review deadline.
  The inspection authority extends the plan review deadline by the number of days it takes for a final decision on the appeal.
  Source: ORS 455.475(3).
```

## 4.16 The certificate of occupancy

**Simple**

Nobody lives in the house until the building official issues the certificate of occupancy, the owner who built it included. When the house is safe to live in but small items remain, the official can issue a temporary certificate. It carries a deadline for finishing them. Moving in without either is illegal occupancy, and what that does to your homeowner coverage depends on the policy's wording. Your construction loan converts to a mortgage at the certificate, and mail, school enrollment and voter registration need an occupiable address. A shop or a garage needs no certificate, unless someone intends to live in it.

**Applied**

1. Pass the building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical and site-development finals.
2. For the site-development final, finish permanent erosion control, on-site stormwater, sanitary facilities and any planting requirement from the land-use review.
3. Submit the contractor information form, BCD Form 4823, naming the general, electrical, HVAC and plumbing contractors who worked on the dwelling, with their license information.
4. Close the deferred submittals with their engineering letters, and file the special-inspection reports, the geotechnical report and any fire-suppression sign-off.
5. Clear the local requirements, which can include address verification, the school-district excise tax certification and the system-development-charge confirmation.
6. Submit the septic as-built and certificate of satisfactory completion, the OWRD well log, and the State Fire Marshal tank report, where each applies.
7. Pay the outstanding permit, plan-review and surcharge fees.
8. For a temporary certificate, ask the official which life-safety items must be complete and what the validity period is.
9. Ask what surety the jurisdiction takes on unfinished work.
10. Check the certificate's permit number, address, covered portion and special conditions before filing it.

**Professional**

Each jurisdiction sets its own temporary-certificate conditions, and Portland's program guide lists these:

- the minimum fire and life-safety items complete and approved
- temporary erosion control in place
- all five inspection disciplines agreeing to the temporary certificate
- the issuance fee paid
- surety on the unfinished work where required, which Portland sets at 120 percent of its value
- a document listing the outstanding items, the completion date and the expiration date

A temporary certificate waives none of the remaining items, which still close within its period or its extensions before the full certificate issues. Portland's temporary certificates run 30 days from issuance, with extensions in 30-day steps at the official's discretion. Each extension takes a written rationale and a fee equal to the initial one, and no statewide cap on extensions exists. Bend, Eugene, Salem and the county departments use 30- or 60-day windows.

The state rule reaches new dwellings, duplexes and accessory dwelling units included, and not additions or alterations. Four other things wait on the certificate:

- lender disbursement, since construction-to-permanent loans convert at the certificate and some accept a temporary one with conditions
- insurance, since homeowner carriers bind occupancy coverage against the certificate or a temporary one
- the property-tax roll, where the assessor can treat substantial completion as the point to add the improvement's value
- the address services (mail, voter registration, school enrollment) that need an occupiable address

OAR 918-480-0140 is a state rule adopted under ORS 455.055, which lets the division set uniform permit, inspection and certificate requirements, so its disclosure and revocation terms apply in every jurisdiction.

### Living on the parcel before the house exists

**Simple**

Living in the shop while you build the house raises a zoning question and a code question at once. Any building used as a dwelling needs its own certificate of occupancy. A shop permitted for work was never built to dwelling standards. Living in it takes a permit revision and new inspections first. Whether anyone may live on the parcel before the house exists is a zoning question for the county. Some counties issue a temporary permit to live in an RV or a manufactured home during construction.

**Professional**

Residential use of any structure waits on its own certificate, whether or not a primary dwelling exists on the parcel. A structure permitted as a non-dwelling lacks the Group R-3 requirements for egress, light and ventilation, insulation and sanitation. Occupying it before a permit revision and residential inspections is a violation in its own right, whichever structure came first. Whether any dwelling use of an accessory structure is allowed before a permitted primary residence is set by the county's zoning code. So is any temporary occupancy permit for an RV or a manufactured home during construction, which is asked for by name at the county planning department rather than the building department.

**Code**

```
No one occupies a building until the certificate of occupancy issues.
  A building or structure shall not be used or occupied, in whole or in part, until the building official has issued a certificate of occupancy; permit-exempt work and accessory buildings need no certificate.
  Source: ORSC R110.1 (2023 and 2026 editions).

A passing final inspection entitles the owner to the certificate.
  Issued after an inspection finds no violations; it states the permit number, address, owner, portion covered, compliance statement, official's name, code edition, sprinkler status and any special conditions.
  Source: ORSC R110.3 (2023 and 2026 editions).

The temporary certificate is discretionary and time-limited.
  The building official may issue a temporary certificate before the permitted work is complete, provided the occupied portion can be occupied safely, and sets the period for which it is valid.
  Source: ORSC R110.4 (2023 and 2026 editions).

The certificate waits on the contractor disclosure.
  The owner or general contractor who holds the structural permit gives the building official contact and license information for the general, electrical, HVAC and plumbing contractors who worked on the dwelling, on BCD Form 4823.
  Source: OAR 918-480-0140(3); BCD Form 4823, oregon.gov/bcd.

A certificate can be revoked for a violation that threatens health and safety.
  Revocation of a certificate or temporary certificate is in writing and states its basis; the code also lets the official suspend or revoke a certificate issued in error or on incorrect information.
  Source: OAR 918-480-0140(4); ORSC R110.5 (2023 and 2026 editions).
```

## 4.17 The posture check before regulated work begins

**Simple**

Before the first regulated work, a short list of legal facts has to be true at once. Your name is on the title, on every permit and on the signed homeowner statement. On the site sit the posted permit and the current plans. Each subcontractor is licensed and insured, and each paid helper is either a licensed contractor or an employee on the books. Licensed people do the propane and refrigerant work. Approvals for the wall material are in hand or on a known path, and the county's written answers cover every exemption the plan uses. Each of those facts has its paper in the binder.

**Applied**

Confirm every item before regulated work begins, and resolve any that is false first.

- title held in a personal name, in tenancy by the entirety with a spouse, or in a revocable trust whose trustee is the occupant
- no company ownership of the property the house will stand on
- land-use approval and zoning verification on file
- building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical and any specialty permits planned and applied for as the homeowner
- the Property Owner Statement read and signed
- the posted permit and the current approved plans on site
- the subcontractor list: CCB-licensed where required, BCD-licensed for plumbing or electrical, with insurance certificates and contracts on file
- LP gas work, septic installation, well drilling and refrigerant work assigned to licensed people or to the owner-permit path
- workers' compensation, liability insurance and tax withholding in place for any paid help on the site
- the inspection schedule written into the construction schedule
- the project binder set up at permit application
- the brand company's marketing language checked against the engineering and architecture statutes
- the occupancy plan: move-in date, utility accounts, voter and tax registration
- the certificate requirements listed, Form 4823 included
- the alternate-method path chosen (state ruling, local approval, or both), with any ruling or approval letter and its conditions on file
- written county answers on file for every exemption relied on (accessory-structure limits and aggregation, agricultural-building use)
- an insurance indication from a broker with surplus-lines access where the wall system is unconventional
- the lender briefed on the appraisal approach and the draw milestones where the build is financed

Binder organization, retention and backup are in the documentation chapter.
