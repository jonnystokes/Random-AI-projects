# Chapter 11: The Wall System, Foundation to Roofline

Reinforced concrete block one unit thick, with continuous insulation outside it and cladding hung over a ventilated gap, is the default wall. For the alternative, a cavity wall puts brick outside the block, with insulation and a drained air space between brick and block and stainless steel ties across the gap. In both, rain that gets past the outer face drains down to flashing and back outside. Most western Oregon sites sit in Seismic Design Category D0 to D2, where the residential code requires all but the lowest one-story masonry walls to be designed under the masonry standards. With concrete floors and a concrete roof as well, an engineer designs either wall, and the structural engineering for both is in the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter.

## 11.1 What the wall has to do

**Simple**

Your exterior wall does three things at once, and each one has its own layer. It carries the roof and floors to the foundation, keeps rain and ground water out, and slows the flow of heat. Reinforced concrete block bears the load. Rain that soaks through the outer face runs down a drained gap behind it and back outside. Continuous insulation board slows the heat. West of the Cascades, walls stay wet for months at a time. East of them, walls dry between storms but freeze harder and more often.

**Professional**

Gravity load from the roof and floors, and out-of-plane wind and seismic load, all reach the foundation through the block wythe. That wythe is the whole structure of the default wall. In the cavity wall the brick adds its own weight and, read as a veneer, passes its wind and seismic load to the block through the ties.

Water that passes the face of a barrier wall reaches the structure. Behind the face of a drainage wall, that water runs down a gap to flashing and leaves through weeps. Both walls in this house are drainage walls. That gap is the air space between brick and block in the cavity wall. Behind the default wall's cladding it is a ventilated rainscreen gap, detailed with its attachment order and fastener penetrations in the Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, and Radon chapter (Above-Grade Wall Assembly).

Continuous insulation sits outboard of the structural block in both walls. Held at indoor temperature, the block's mass stores heat and evens out the indoor temperature between heating cycles.

In the Willamette Valley and on the coast, climate zone 4C in the residential code, walls see more rain and more days of saturation. Drainage details control durability there: open weeps, a clean air space and continuous flashing.

East of the Cascades, in the high desert of zone 5B, walls see fewer wet days and harder, more frequent freezes. There the brick's freeze-thaw resistance and the water the masonry absorbs control durability, through Grade SW brick, water-repellent admixtures and penetrating repellents. Both regions build the same wall. In the valley the likelier failure is moisture held in the wall through a long wet season, and east of the mountains it is frost spalling of saturated brick.

## 11.2 Two walls: the default and the cavity alternative

**Simple**

Brick laid a few inches outside the block, as a second wythe, turns the default wall into the cavity wall. Each wythe is one vertical layer of masonry, one unit thick. Steel ties cross the gap between the wythes and hold the brick to the block. That brick can carry only its own weight, as a facing called a veneer. It can also share the wind and earthquake load with the block. Which of the two it does is an engineering choice, and it changes the ties, the air space and the mortar. Nearly every brick-on-block wall built today is a veneer, and so is the one planned here.

**Professional**

On a western Oregon site with concrete floors and roof, the engineer of record designs either wall (Code below). Prescriptive masonry figures in the residential code also assume wood or cold-formed steel floor and roof diaphragms (ORSC R606.12.1.1), which this house does not use. The default wall leaves that design fewer decisions. It has one structural wythe and no tie schedule between masonry wythes. Its footing carries one wythe, and its cladding comes off without touching the structure.

Three readings of the brick wythe are open to the engineer, and the drawings name the one chosen:

| Reading | What the brick resists | Tie rules that apply | Where the rules are |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anchored veneer | Its own weight only. It passes all lateral load to the block, which resists the full load. | Veneer ties | ORSC R703.8; the TMS 402-22 veneer provisions |
| Noncomposite (cavity) wall | Its own weight and a share of the lateral load in proportion to its stiffness | Cavity-wall ties, closer in some configurations | ORSC R606.13; TMS 402-22 wall provisions (classification at section 5.1.4) |
| Composite wall | Vertical and lateral load, acting with the block as one section | Ties or masonry headers designed for shear across the joint between the wythes | TMS 402-22 wall provisions |

BIA Technical Note 28D is the Brick Industry Association's note on brick veneer over block backing. It states that nearly all multi-wythe masonry walls built today have an exterior wythe classified as anchored veneer. It also states that many designers and masons call any drained brick wall a cavity wall. On the structural drawings the classification is therefore written out by name. Masonry headers, the composite wall's traditional bond, are no longer recommended by BIA. They lack ductility, restrain the opposite movements of brick and block, and carry water across the wall along their joints. Anchored veneer is the plan for this house, and the engineer of record confirms or changes it.

Facing brick for the exterior wythe is ASTM C216 Grade SW, for severe weathering. ASTM's brick standards recommend Grade SW wherever the weathering index is 50 or more. BIA Technical Note 9B's weathering map puts all of Oregon above 50, in the moderate band west of the Cascades and the severe band in the northeast. Its grade table calls for SW in every exposure at that index. Most manufacturers make brick to Grade SW so they can ship it anywhere. Grade MW is limited to interior wythes even in BIA's reading, and this wall has none.

**Code**

```
Masonry walls in Seismic Design Categories D0 to D2 must be designed under the masonry standards.
  Design under TMS 402 chapters 1 to 7 and sections 8.1 and 8.3, or TMS 403, except one-story walls with 9 feet or less between lateral supports that meet the minimum reinforcement.
  Source: ORSC R606.12.3.1 and R606.12.4 (2023 edition).
```

## 11.3 Seismic design on a western Oregon site

**Simple**

Earthquakes shake the block structure sideways, and the brick tied to it has to follow without cracking loose. Most of western Oregon sits in the residential code's highest seismic categories for houses. Category D1 covers most of the Willamette Valley and the I-5 corridor, and D2 covers the coast. There every tie holds up less brick than it would elsewhere, which means about a third more ties. Along its top and sides, the brick is also kept free of the floors and roof, so the structure's sway does not crush it. On these sites the engineer designs the ties, the brick's mortar and its connections from the parcel's seismic category.

**Applied**

1. Look up the parcel's seismic design category at its coordinates and record it in the binder.
2. Bring the planned brick classification and the target air-space and insulation widths.
3. Bring the candidate tie product's data sheet, with its wire size, pintle legs and allowed vertical offset.
4. Ask whether the masonry standard's restriction on Type N mortar reaches an anchored veneer.
5. Have the answer written on the drawings.
6. Record the engineer's tie schedule, mortar types and isolation details before ordering ties or mortar.

**Professional**

The structural chapter's site-to-category table puts most western Oregon sites in D0 to D2. There the residential code's veneer-tie section cuts the wall area each tie may hold by a quarter (Code below). The Masonry Society's TMS 402/602-22 review course gives the same reduction, to 75 percent, for anchored veneer in Category D. Spacing limits stay the same, so the reduction is met with closer ties along each course or an extra course of ties.

TMS 402's veneer provisions include two more seismic requirements, as summarized in the same review course. From Seismic Design Category C up, the top and sides of anchored veneer are isolated from the structure. Drift of the structure then does not reach the brick as in-plane load. In Categories E and F, each story's veneer is supported independently instead of stacking story on story. Their exact TMS 402-22 section numbers come from the engineer's copy of the standard.

Mortar for the brick is the open seismic question. Anchored veneer built to R703.8 is outside the residential code's seismic masonry rules (ORSC R606.12). So the code's ban on Type N mortar and masonry cement in the lateral-force-resisting system (ORSC R606.12.3.4) does not reach the brick. TN 28D states that the TMS code prohibits Type N mortar in Seismic Performance Categories D and E, the older name for the seismic categories.

Whether that prohibition reaches a veneer that carries no seismic load of its own is a TMS 402 reading the engineer makes for this house. For the block wythe the answer is settled, because the block is the lateral-force-resisting system.

**Code**

```
Veneer ties hold less wall where seismic or wind demand is high.
  Each tie supports not more than 2 square feet of wall area in Seismic Design Categories D0, D1 and D2, and where wind pressure exceeds 30 pounds per square foot.
  Source: ORSC R703.8.4.1, exception (2023 edition).
```

## 11.4 Owner-made wall systems and the alternative-materials route

**Simple**

Earth block and ferrocement have no tables in the code, so each needs the building official's approval as an alternative. Earth block is soil and 4 to 10 percent cement pressed into units. Ferrocement is a thin shell of cement mortar over layers of fine wire mesh. The official approves an alternative only when tests show it is at least as strong, durable and safe as what the code lists. Approval is case by case and county by county. Owner-made concrete block that meets the block standard needs testing and nothing more. Reinforced concrete block stays in this house's structure. The other materials go into outbuildings and non-structural walls until they are proven.

**Applied**

Before designing around a material the code does not list:

1. Ask the building department in writing whether a dry-stacked ASTM C1314 prism test of the unit is acceptable evidence.
2. Ask whether a stamped engineer's evaluation report is enough, or the official wants a third-party evaluation report.
3. Record both answers, with the official's name and the date, before buying molds or mixing test batches.

**Professional**

### Interlocking compressed earth block

Mortarless interlocking walls have no bond in their joints, only friction and the interlock of the units, so a mortared prism test does not describe them. Researchers testing dry-stack interlocking block adapt ASTM C1314, the masonry prism compression test, by stacking the units dry. Examples are "Characterization of dry-stack interlocking compressed earth blocks" (Materials and Structures, 2014) and "Interlocking Compressed Earth Block Walls: Out-Of-Plane Structural Response" (15th World Conference on Earthquake Engineering, 2012). That research tests the dry bed joints separately for shear and friction. Unit strength is tested to ASTM D1633, the molded soil-cement cylinder test, which suits stabilized earth block. ASTM C90 governs concrete block.

New Mexico's Earthen Building Materials Code (14.7.4 NMAC) is the closest adopted precedent in the United States. It permits dry-stacked compressed earth block with demonstrated adhesion, a 10-inch minimum wall thickness, stucco outside and plaster inside. Its method wets the joints for cohesion, closer to adobe practice than to a mechanically interlocking block, and it binds New Mexico only. In Oregon an interlocking wall needs project-specific engineering and the adapted testing, submitted through the alternative-materials provision.

### Structural ferrocement

Planning values for structural ferrocement come from the ferrocement provisions of the Bangladesh National Building Code and two engineering reference sites, The Constructor and Civil Engineering Web:

| Parameter | Planning value |
|---|---|
| Cement to sand, by volume | 1:1.5 to 1:3, with 1:1.5 to 1:2 for loadbearing work |
| Water-cement ratio | 0.35 to 0.60, with 0.40 to 0.45 for structural work |
| Mesh | Wire 0.5 to 1.5 mm at 5 to 25 mm openings, and 0.5 to 1.0 mm wire at 5 to 10 mm for most structural work, in several layers held apart by small skeleton rods |
| Section thickness | 10 to 60 mm (3/8 to 2-3/8 inches) |
| Strength benchmark | 35 N/mm2 (about 5,000 psi) at 28 days on 75 by 150 mm moist-cured cylinders, from the Bangladesh code |

ACI publishes two ferrocement documents, ACI 549R-18 (a report) and ACI 549.1R-18 (a design guide). They design ferrocement from the volume fraction and the specific surface of its mesh. A loadbearing ferrocement element in Oregon takes the same route as earth block: engineered design and alternative-materials approval.

### Ferrocement as stay-in-place formwork

Ferrocement tanks and boat hulls show that the material works as a thin structural shell. Used as permanent formwork and filled or backed with owner-made concrete, it would make a hybrid wall. Proving that wall takes three tests: compression of witness panels built alongside the work (ASTM C1314 adapted to the composite section), shear at the interface between shell and fill, and water penetration where the shell is the finished face. Until those tests and a project-specific design exist, it stays out of the structure.

### What stays in the load path

Reinforced concrete block, owner-made or purchased, has a code path, decades of field performance, and no testing beyond its unit standard. The Owner-Made Blocks and Alternative Concretes chapter weighs the whole field of owner-made materials on durability, makeability and testing. Earth block and ferrocement have engineering literature and no Oregon path, so either one adds engineering cost, testing cost and schedule risk to the appeal of the material.

**Code**

```
An alternative material, design or method needs the building official's approval as equivalent.
  Not less than the code's equivalent in quality, strength, effectiveness, durability, safety and fire safety, shown by tests of a scale that predicts end-use performance, run by a party the official accepts.
  Source: ORSC R104.2.2.4 and R104.2.2.5 (2026 edition, mandatory October 1, 2026); ORSC R104.11 and R104.11.1 (2023 edition).
```

## 11.5 The wall section, layer by layer

**Simple**

From outside in, the cavity wall is brick, an open air gap, insulation board, and the block with its interior finish. Every layer outside the block can dry outward through the open gap. Wet brick in summer sun pushes moisture inward, toward the cooler rooms. A plastic sheet behind the drywall would trap that moisture in the wall, so this house uses none. With a few inches of insulation, the cavity wall runs 15 to 18 inches from brick face to drywall. Its windows therefore sit in deeper openings than the default wall's, which is thinner by about the brick and its air gap.

**Professional**

| Layer, outside to inside | Dimension | Function | Where it is specified |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brick veneer, ASTM C216 Grade SW | 4 in. nominal, 3-5/8 in. actual | Cladding and first water shed, and a store of absorbed rain | The two-walls section (grade) and the ties section |
| Clear air space | 1 in. minimum, 2 in. recommended | Drainage path and drying path | The cavity section |
| Cavity insulation rated for the wet zone | Set by the whole-wall energy target | Continuous thermal layer | The cavity insulation section and the Envelope chapter |
| Air and water-resistive membrane on the block's cavity face, where the drawings call for one | Membrane | Air control and backup water barrier | Envelope chapter (Vapor Control) |
| Reinforced concrete block | 8 in. nominal, 7-5/8 in. actual | Structure, Class III vapor retarder by its own permeance, and thermal mass inside the insulation | Structural System and Concrete Design chapter |
| Interior finish | 1/2 in. gypsum board, or parging | Finish, with Class III vapor control when the gypsum is painted | Interiors chapter |

Layer order follows the exterior-insulated wall framework of Building Science Corporation's Building Science Insights 001, The Perfect Wall (2010), which TN 28D lists among its references. From the outside, it runs cladding, drainage gap, water-resistive barrier, insulation and structure. Every layer outboard of the block dries outward through the ventilated air space, and the block sits inside the insulation line, where its mass holds indoor temperature.

Building codes do not require a water-resistive barrier over masonry or concrete backing, TN 28D states. Its use here follows the hygrothermal analysis (a combined heat-and-moisture model of the assembly) that the Envelope chapter calls for. The same note states that block filler or two coats of paint make a concrete block wall an approved air barrier assembly under the IECC, and that portland-cement parging 1/2 inch thick qualifies.

A low-permeance layer on both faces of one wythe leaves it no direction to dry. Interior polyethylene behind the drywall, with a low-permeance membrane or foam on the cavity face, would sandwich the block and keep any water that passed the outer defenses. No interior polyethylene is used anywhere in this house.

Brick absorbs rain and holds it, which makes it a reservoir cladding. Sun on wet brick drives water vapor inward, from the warm wet face toward the cooler, drier interior. Air conditioning steepens that drive, and the colder the house is held in summer, the more moisture moves inward out of the veneer. Building Science Corporation research report RR-0104, Solar Driven Moisture in Brick Veneer, documents the mechanism. Polyethylene behind an interior finish turns the seasonal drive into accumulation by blocking the inward drying. This wall's drained, vented cavity and the absence of an interior low-permeance sheet keep it out of the condition the report describes.

Wall thickness adds the actual sizes of brick (3-5/8 inches), block (7-5/8) and gypsum (1/2) to the cavity. With 2 inches of board and 1 inch clear, the cavity wall measures 14-3/4 inches. With 4 inches of board and 2 inches clear it measures 17-3/4, and that 6-inch cavity stays inside the largest air space the prescriptive veneer ties cover. Both are worked examples. The engineer and the energy target set the real cavity. With the same insulation, the default wall is thinner by the brick and its clear air space, less its own rainscreen gap and cladding.

## 11.6 The base: foundation seat, base flashing and slab edge

**Simple**

Two wythes need a wider seat than one. The engineer sizes it from the soil report and the weight of both wythes. The brick may step out past its seat, a step called a corbel, within limits set per course and in total. Below the first flashing, the gap between brick and block is filled solid with mortar or grout. That flashing sits above the finished ground, and from there up the cavity drains. Insulation at the slab edge continues up into the cavity, so the base of the wall has no cold strip. Some Oregon soils hold sulfates that attack ordinary cement, and the cement type below grade follows from a soil test.

**Applied**

1. Give the engineer both wythe thicknesses, the cavity width and the soil report's bearing value before the footing is designed.
2. Set the top of the foundation on the 8-inch coursing grid.
3. Lay a bond break of building paper, flashing or 4 to 6 mil polyethylene under the first brick course.
4. Fill the cavity solid with mortar or grout up to the base flashing.
5. Set the base flashing and weeps up to 10 inches above finished grade, clear of future planting and regrading.
6. Run the slab-edge insulation up into the cavity insulation without a gap.
7. Photograph that joint before brick covers it.
8. Add a soil and groundwater sulfate test to the geotechnical investigation.
9. Specify the below-grade cement type and maximum water-cement ratio from the sulfate result.
10. Parge and dampproof the below-grade masonry, or waterproof it where groundwater is known.

**Professional**

Footing width for two wythes is the engineer's calculation from the two wythe thicknesses, the eccentricity of their combined load, and the bearing capacity in the geotechnical report. A default-wall footing carries one structural wythe plus light cladding and needs no corbel or bond-break detail, so it runs narrower. Everything below the first course, footing to slab, is in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, and Concrete Placement chapter.

Corbelling lets the stem wall run narrower than the full two-wythe width, with the brick oversailing its seat. TN 28D and the residential code set matching limits per course and in total (Code below).

Brick on a bond break bears on the concrete without adhering to it. Its growth and the concrete's shrinkage then meet at the base as movement instead of cracks. BIA Technical Note 18A names building paper, flashing, or 4 to 6 mil polyethylene for bond breaks between brickwork and foundations or slabs. For design, a wythe on a bond break has a pinned base instead of a fixed one. That matters to a noncomposite wall's calculation and not to a veneer's, which carries no lateral load of its own.

With a basement, through-wall flashing at the bottom of the cavity keeps cavity water from tracking down into the basement wall. Below the lowest flashing the cavity is filled solid, so no open cavity runs below grade. Weeps buried by later grading or planting stop draining, which is why TN 28D sets them well above finished grade.

R-15 slab-edge insulation to 24 inches deep (ORSC Table N1101.1(1)) connects to the cavity insulation plane, not to the brick face. Between the wythes, the insulation plane is the cavity. A break at that connection turns the brick seat into an uninsulated thermal bridge at the edge the slab-edge rule targets.

Sulfates in soil or groundwater react with tricalcium aluminate (C3A) in ordinary portland cement and form ettringite. Ettringite takes up more volume than its reactants, and the growth cracks hardened mortar and grout from within. ASTM C150 caps C3A at 8 percent in Type II cement for moderate exposure and 5 percent in Type V for severe exposure.

Which cement the site needs follows from a soil or groundwater sulfate test, read against ACI 318's sulfate exposure classes S0 to S3. A low water-cement ratio matters as much as the cement type, because a denser paste slows the sulfate's way in. Parging and dampproofing (ORSC R406.1), or waterproofing where water is known (R406.2), keep bulk water off the below-grade wythe in the first place.

Soldier courses (bricks stood on end, narrow face out) and rowlock courses (bricks on edge, end out) are the standard vocabulary at a base or water table. A rowlock sheds water off a sloped projection. A flush band engages no structural rule, and a projecting one stays inside the corbel limits. No decorative unit interrupts the flashing plane.

## 11.7 One coursing grid for block and brick

**Simple**

Block and brick share one height module, so the two wythes can rise together. A block course is 8 inches tall with its mortar joint, and three courses of standard brick make the same 8 inches. Heights that must land on a full course in both materials go on that 8-inch grid. They include the top of the foundation, window heads and sills, the bond beam and the roofline. Ties depend on the grid too. Their two halves can sit only 1-1/4 inches apart in height, so courses that drift apart leave ties that cannot connect.

**Applied**

1. Fix the finished-floor datum on the drawings.
2. Set the foundation top, each window head and sill, and the bond-beam soffit at whole multiples of 8 inches from that datum.
3. Put the eave or parapet line on the same grid.
4. Mark the grid on a story pole for each wythe, both measured from the same datum.
5. Dry-lay the first block course to prove the module, with whole units at corners and openings.
6. Check the brick against the block at every 8-inch line as the walls rise.
7. Correct drift by adjusting bed joints, no more than 1/8 inch per joint.
8. Record the datum and the grid elevations on the as-built drawings.

**Professional**

TEK 05-12, Modular Layout of Concrete Masonry, from the Concrete Masonry and Hardscapes Association (CMHA), states the coordination. Nominal 8-inch block courses and modular brick courses of 2-2/3 inches share the 8-inch vertical module, three brick courses to one block course. Window heads and sills need no separate datum strategy, because on the grid they land on whole courses of both materials.

Courses that drift apart over the wall's height push the eye and pintle of each tie past its offset limit, and that voids the tie design. Masons hold alignment two ways: by the grid arithmetic, and by varying bed-joint thickness within tolerance at the courses where the wythes must meet at a tie. Story poles, leads and lines are Masonry Craft chapter subjects. The designer sets the actual elevations once the finish-floor elevation, the opening heights and the roof line are fixed.

## 11.8 Movement joints

**Simple**

Each wythe gets its own joints, because the brick grows for its whole life while the block shrinks. Fired brick takes up moisture from the air and swells, slowly and permanently. Concrete block loses its manufacturing water and reacts with carbon dioxide in the air, and both make it shrink. Over a 25-foot wall the brick can grow about a quarter inch while the block shrinks an eighth to a fifth of an inch. The brick takes expansion joints, which close over time, and the block takes control joints, which open. The two sets follow separate spacing rules and need not line up. Without them, the same movement shows up as cracks at corners and openings.

**Applied**

1. Lay out the block control joints first, at the spacing rule and at corners, openings and height changes.
2. Lay out the brick expansion-joint grid separately, starting at corners, offsets and height changes, then filling to the maximum spacing.
3. Mark both grids on the foundation before the first course of either wythe.
4. Record both grids on the as-built drawings.
5. Place a tie within 12 inches of each brick expansion joint and each free edge of the brick.
6. Build each expansion joint with compressible filler and a backer rod a quarter wider than the joint.
7. Fill the sealant to a depth of half the joint's width, and no less than 1/4 inch.
8. Use ASTM C920 Grade NS, Use M sealant of Class 50, and test its adhesion on the actual brick before construction.
9. Break the bond at each control joint with building paper, a plastic strip, or a rubber or PVC control-joint unit.
10. Finish the control joint with backer rod and sealant at the face.
11. Keep mortar and debris out of both kinds of joint, and never fill either with mortar.

**Professional**

### How the ties let the wythes move

Adjustable two-piece ties allow slip between eye and pintle, vertically and in a small horizontal play. That slip lets the brick grow relative to the block without loading either wythe through the connection. The ties still pass part of any restrained stress across the cavity, so a joint failure in one wythe reaches the other.

### Expansion joints in the brick

A vertical expansion joint is a gap through the full brick wythe, closed with compressible filler, a backer rod (a foam cord that sets the sealant depth) and elastomeric sealant at the face. Built at its widest, it closes over the wall's life as the brick grows. Packed tight or filled with mortar, a joint no longer absorbs movement, and the wall cracks elsewhere as though it had no joint.

BIA Technical Note 18A sizes the spacing from the joint's width and the compressibility of its least compressible component. Unrestrained brickwork grows about 0.0009 times its length, so the spacing equals the width times the percent compressibility divided by 0.09. With a 1/2-inch joint and 50 percent sealant it comes to 278 inches, 23 feet 2 inches.

Joints sized like mortar joints run 3/8 to 1/2 inch wide, and Class 50 sealant, which stretches and compresses half its width, allows wider spacing than Class 25. TN 18A and TN 28D place vertical joints:

- no more than 25 feet apart in walls without openings, and 20 feet in walls with several openings
- near outside corners, within 2 feet on either side where possible and within about 10 feet on at least one side, with the two distances to the corner adding to no more than the straight-wall spacing
- at inside corners of offsets and setbacks, at changes in wall height, and where the backing or the support of the brick changes
- at the corners of large openings, where rows of punched windows concentrate stress
- through every parapet, with extra parapet joints so none are more than 15 feet apart at its top

Horizontal expansion joints go only under shelf angles, so a wall without shelf angles has none.

### Control joints in the block

A control joint is a planned weak plane in the block wythe. Breaking the bond at one vertical joint makes the shrinkage crack there, sealed, instead of across a block face somewhere else. It opens over the wall's life, while the brick's expansion joint closes. Mortared solid, a control joint stops being a weak plane.

CMHA's crack-control note, CMU-TEC-009-26, is the current source for block joint spacing. Its empirical table spaces control joints in 8-inch block walls at 25 feet 4 inches or 1.5 times the wall height, whichever is less. That spacing assumes two-wire 9-gauge joint reinforcement every 16 inches, or 0.025 square inches of horizontal steel per foot of wall height.

The first joint sits within half that spacing of each corner, and more go at changes in wall height or thickness, at pilasters, beside openings and between intersecting walls. Block walls need no horizontal control joints. Below-grade walls go without them, because a joint that must hold back groundwater is hard to detail and buried walls move less.

Where the engineer's horizontal steel exceeds 0.2 percent of the wall's net vertical section, CMU-TEC-009-26 replaces control joints with reinforced relief joints. The note describes that approach as common in high-seismic areas, where the steel for in-plane shear is already in the wall. Long walls may still take relief joints. The joint reinforcement the structural chapter specifies, 9-gauge ladder every second course, meets the empirical table's steel condition.

### What happens without them

Restrained brick growth builds compression along the wall and releases at the weakest points: diagonal step cracks through the mortar, spalled corners, and in severe cases outward bowing of the veneer. Restrained block shrinkage cracks the wythe vertically through the cores at an opening, a height change or a corner.

### How the joints look

Every expansion joint is a visible vertical line on the finished wall. Sealant colored to the brick in running bond, or to the mortar in stack bond, makes it less prominent, and sand rubbed into fresh sealant takes off the sheen. Joints at inside corners, or beside quoins and reveals, read as part of the design. TN 18A advises against hiding joints behind downspouts, which blocks maintenance, and against toothing them along the bond pattern, which traps debris. Expressing the joints as deliberate lines or minimizing them is a facade decision, listed under Who decides what.

## 11.9 Mortar and grout

**Simple**

Mortar and grout look alike, and they are mixed for opposite purposes. Mortar is stiff, so a block or brick stays put the moment it is set. Grout is fluid, so it flows down a narrow block core and wraps the steel inside. Ordinary concrete is neither, and its gravel jams in a core. Packing cores with leftover stiff mortar leaves voids around the steel, where it rusts and loses its bond. Mortar can serve as grout only after enough water is added to make it pour. Freezing nights and hot, windy days each bring extra steps for fresh masonry.

**Applied**

1. Order Type S portland cement-lime or mortar cement mortar for the block wythe, never Type N or masonry cement.
2. Order the brick mortar after the engineer rules on Type N for the veneer.
3. Measure each grout space clear of bars and fins, then pick fine or coarse grout from the code table.
4. Mix site grout in a mechanical mixer for 5 minutes or more, to 8 to 11 inches of slump.
5. Place each batch within 1-1/2 hours of adding water, before it begins to set.
6. Rod or vibrate each lift as it goes in.
7. Reconsolidate once the block has drawn off the excess water and before the grout stiffens.
8. When work stops for an hour or more, stop every cell at the same level, 1 inch below the top of the unit.
9. Log each pour with its date, lift height, slump, reconsolidation time and weather.

**Professional**

### Mortar by location

| Wall location | Mortar | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Block wythe, loadbearing and lateral-force-resisting | Type S or M portland cement-lime, or mortar cement | ORSC R606.2.8.3 and R606.12.3.4 exclude Type N and masonry cement from the lateral-force-resisting system in SDC D0 to D2 |
| Brick veneer | Type N, BIA's preference for veneer, with Type S its preference for loadbearing brick | Outside the residential code's seismic masonry rules (ORSC R606.12), with the TMS 402 reading on Type N left to the engineer |
| Masonry foundation walls and other below-grade courses | Type M or S | ORSC R606.2.8.1 for foundation walls built to the code's foundation tables, and lower absorption against soil moisture |
| Bond-beam bed joints | As the block wythe | Same wythe, same seismic role |

BIA prefers Type N for veneer because the brick carries only its own weight, and the softer mortar takes up the small movements between the wythes better than Type S. TN 28D also recommends mortar air content under 12 percent for bond and water resistance. Under the TMS code, masonry cement and air-entrained cement-lime mortars get lower allowable flexural tension, which TN 28D gives as a reason to weigh them project by project.

### Grout

Grout flows because it holds more water than the cement needs to hydrate. The block around it absorbs the excess within minutes, which leaves the grout at a lower water-cement ratio in place and gives the high slump its purpose. Cutting the water to make placement tidier removes the flow that lets grout reach and encase the bars.

Codes and ASTM specifications recognize no strength difference between fine grout (cement, sand and water) and coarse grout (with pea-gravel aggregate), CMHA TEK 09-04A states. The choice turns on how much room the grout has to flow and how tall the pour is, and TEK 09-04A calls coarse grout more economical to produce where it fits. The residential code's table of minimum grout spaces:

| Grout | Pour height, feet | Minimum width between wythes, inches | Minimum cell, inches |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fine | 1 | 0.75 | 1.5 x 2 |
| Fine | 5 | 2 | 2 x 3 |
| Fine | 12 | 2.5 | 2.5 x 3 |
| Fine | 24 | 3 | 3 x 3 |
| Coarse | 1 | 1.5 | 1.5 x 3 |
| Coarse | 5 | 2 | 2.5 x 3 |
| Coarse | 12 | 2.5 | 3 x 3 |
| Coarse | 24 | 3 | 3 x 4 |

Each dimension is measured clear of mortar protrusions and widened by the diameters of any horizontal bars crossing the space. Vertical bars may fill no more than 6 percent of it.

Stiff mortar packed into cores cannot flow around a bar or into the corners of a cell. It bridges and leaves voids, the bar loses its bond, and the wall never develops its design tension capacity. Voids against unprotected steel become starting points for corrosion. Leftover stiff mortar fails the placement and consolidation rules below, and an inspector who finds it has grounds to reject the work.

Lift height is limited because fresh grout presses on the mortar joints with the full hydrostatic pressure of its column. Where that pressure exceeds the bond of a young joint, the joint opens and grout blows out through the face of the wall.

Consolidating each lift as it goes in closes placement voids. As the block draws water from the grout and entrapped air rises, new voids open and the grout pulls away from the cell walls and bars, so a second pass follows. A planning figure for that pass is 10 to 15 minutes after placement, judged in the end by the grout's stiffness. Grout never reconsolidated also leaves a shrinkage gap at the top of the cell, and that gap channels water straight down a grouted core wherever the top of a cell is left exposed.

Shrinkage-compensating admixtures expand the grout slowly to offset the shrinkage from its early water loss, and TEK 09-04A names high-lift grouting as their best use. Retarders keep grout workable in hot weather long enough to place and reconsolidate it. Admixtures need the engineer's acceptance, and antifreezes and chloride-bearing admixtures stay out of grout.

### Self-consolidating grout

Self-consolidating grout flows into the cell and consolidates around the bars under its own weight. Superplasticizer, with or without a viscosity modifier, gives the flow in place of extra water. It needs no rodding, vibration or reconsolidation, which suits a one-person crew. Slump flow, the diameter a sample spreads to on a flat plate from an inverted slump cone, replaces the slump test. TEK 09-04A gives 20 to 30 inches for the spread, with no bleeding or segregation at its edge. This house's target is 24 to 30 inches and a visual stability index of 1 or less, on the 0-to-3 scale that rates how well paste and aggregate stay together in the spread.

Its costs are the admixture dosing and a price premium per volume. A field-mixed batch without plant-grade proportioning can segregate and lose the flow it was bought for. Acceptance has come through the grout demonstration panel option in TMS 602, the masonry construction specification. The split planned for this house puts self-consolidating grout at tall lifts, congested cells and bond beams, where rodding access is worst, and conventional grout with reconsolidation everywhere else.

### Weather windows

TMS 602 sets temperature bands for masonry work, reproduced in CMHA TEK 03-01C, All-Weather Concrete Masonry Construction:

| Condition | What applies |
|---|---|
| 40 to 90°F | Normal construction, with no special measures |
| Ambient below 40°F while laying | Heat sand or water so mortar is 40 to 120°F, never heating water or aggregate above 140°F. Lay dry units down to 20°F, thaw wet or frozen ones, and never lay on snow or ice |
| Ambient 20°F and below while laying | Grout at 70 to 120°F, masonry heated to 40°F before grouting, windbreaks or enclosures when wind exceeds 15 mph, and heated enclosures holding the work above 32°F |
| Mean daily 25 to 40°F after laying | A weather-resistive membrane over the work for 24 hours |
| Mean daily 20 to 25°F after laying | Insulating blankets for 24 hours, 48 for grouted masonry unless its grout cement is Type III |
| Mean daily 20°F and below after laying | Masonry held above 32°F for 24 hours, 48 grouted unless Type III, by heated enclosure, electric blankets or infrared lamps |
| Ambient above 100°F, or above 90°F with wind over 8 mph | Damp, loose sand piles, mortar and grout below 120°F, mixer and boards flushed with cool water, retempering with cool water, and mortar used within 2 hours |
| Ambient above 115°F, or above 105°F with wind over 8 mph | Also shade materials and the mixer, and use cool mixing water, with any ice melted before it meets the other materials |
| Mean daily above 100°F, or above 90°F with wind over 8 mph | Fog spray new masonry until damp at least three times a day until it is three days old |

Mortar proportions, mixing and the weather procedures are in the Masonry Craft chapter, and the build calendar is in the Build Sequence and Site Coordination chapter. In Oregon the cold bands are an ordinary winter constraint on both sides of the Cascades, and the hot bands apply east of them in summer.

## 11.10 Ties and anchorage

**Simple**

A wall tie is a short piece of stainless steel crossing the gap between the wythes. It holds the brick to the block and passes wind and earthquake loads from one to the other. The connection also lets the wythes slide slightly against each other, because the brick grows and the block shrinks over the years. Each tie comes in two halves, an eye laid into the block and a hooked pintle laid into the brick. The brick can then go up after the block is finished. In a cavity that gets wet by design, stainless steel is the tie material that does not rust away.

**Applied**

1. Use ladder or tab joint reinforcement with adjustable eye-and-pintle ties, all stainless steel.
2. Use no Z ties on hollow block and no truss-type joint reinforcement in this wall.
3. Lay the eye halves in the block bed joints at the engineer's schedule as the block rises.
4. Keep separate ties out of any bed joint that already holds joint reinforcement.
5. Hook the pintles into the eyes as the brick reaches each tie course.
6. Press each pintle leg into a full mortar bed, 1-1/2 inches deep, with 5/8 inch of mortar to the face.
7. Never bend a tie once it is embedded.
8. Photograph each tie course before the next course covers it.
9. File the tie product's data sheet in the binder.

**Professional**

BIA Technical Note 28D prefers ladder or tab joint reinforcement with adjustable ties for brick veneer over block, because the combination avoids clashes between unit-tie wires and joint-reinforcement wires. Z ties suit solid units only. Truss-type joint reinforcement is recommended against: its diagonal cross wires collide with the vertical bars and grout of a reinforced inner wythe, and its horizontal stiffness restrains in-plane movement enough to bow the masonry. A bed joint has room to embed joint reinforcement or a separate tie, not both.

Tie requirements for the anchored-veneer reading, from TN 28D's Tables 1 and 2:

| Air space | Adjustable ties | Non-adjustable ties, wall area per tie |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 4-5/8 in. | Ladder, tab or eye of W1.7 wire, pintle of W2.8 wire with at least one leg, 1-1/4 in. maximum vertical offset | W1.7 wire 2.67 sq ft, W2.8 wire 3.50 sq ft |
| Over 4-5/8 to 6-5/8 in. | Two W2.8 eyes, a barrel of 1/4 in. outside diameter, or a plate 0.074 in. thick and 1-1/4 in. wide, with at least two pintle legs, 1-1/4 in. maximum offset, and 2 in. maximum from the brick's inside face to the end of the adjustable part | Sheet-metal unit ties 0.06 in. thick and 7/8 in. wide at 3.50 sq ft, joint reinforcement at 1.77 sq ft |
| Over 6-5/8 in. | Designed under the TMS provisions for alternative design of anchored veneer | Designed |

On a D0 to D2 site the residential code's seismic limit on wall area per tie governs every tie type, including those TMS allows at 3.50 square feet. The code prints a metric 635 mm beside its vertical spacing limit for veneer ties, and 635 mm is 25 inches, the TMS 402 figure in TN 28D. The inch value governs. Read as a noncomposite cavity wall instead of a veneer, the brick takes the code's multiple-wythe tie rules (second entry below), and TMS 402's noncomposite provisions add their own.

A tie leg left short of its embedment, or laid on bare brick with mortar on top only, sheds its load to the ties around it, and the overload moves from tie to tie. TN 28D's answer is completely filled bed joints at every tie.

The cavity is wet by design, so every tie spends its service life damp. Galvanizing is a sacrificial coating with a finite life. Once it is consumed or scratched, the carbon steel under it rusts, and rust takes up more volume than the steel it replaces. A rusting tie cracks the bed joint it sits in, and horizontal cracks repeating at the tie spacing are the sign.

Enough failed ties remove the veneer's out-of-plane support, and the brick bows and in the end detaches. This house uses stainless steel ties and joint reinforcement, ASTM A580 Type 304 or 316, against a code minimum of hot-dip galvanizing for most exterior ties (Code below). Stainless and carbon-steel parts are never mixed in one assembly, because the contact sets up galvanic corrosion.

The gap between eye and pintle breaks the metal path from the wet brick to the dry block. The structural chapter's tie table lists the noncomposite tie values and excludes drip bends in ties, because a bend lowers a tie's lateral capacity. Where the brick passes a cast-in-place concrete element, such as a slab edge, a beam or a column, dovetail slots cast into the concrete take the adjustable ties. Those slots belong on the concrete shop drawings before the pour.

**Code**

```
Veneer ties have a minimum size, a maximum spacing and a maximum wall area.
  Strand wire at least No. 9 gage with a hook in the joint, each tie supporting not more than 2.67 square feet, spaced not over 32 inches horizontally and 24 vertically.
  Source: ORSC R703.8.4.1 (2023 edition).

Adjustable ties between two masonry wythes have their own limits.
  One tie per 2.67 square feet, 24 inches maximum each way, bed joints offset 1.25 inches at most, 1/16-inch clearance, and two 3/16-inch pintle legs.
  Source: ORSC R606.13.2.2 (2023 edition).

  Ends in mortar joints with 5/8-inch cover from the exposed face, 1-1/2 inches into the bed for veneer and for solid or grouted units, never bent once embedded.
  Source: ORSC R606.3.3 (2023 edition).

Ties and joint reinforcement in exterior walls need corrosion protection.
  Hot-dip galvanized to ASTM A153 Class B-2 for joint reinforcement and for ties not fully embedded, and ASTM A167 Type 304 stainless steel for any exposure.
```

## 11.11 The cavity: air space, flashing and weeps

**Simple**

Rain that gets through the brick drains back out through the gap between the wythes. Wind-driven rain passes through brick, and the wall is built for that. Water that gets through runs down the back of the brick and lands on flashing, a waterproof sheet across the bottom of the gap. It leaves through weep holes, small openings left in the brick joints just above the flashing. The drainage works only while the gap stays open from top to bottom. Mortar dropped into the gap during laying can pile on the flashing or smear across to the block. Wet mortar then draws water across the gap like a wick.

**Applied**

1. Install the flashing first, fully supported, with its back leg up the block and ending in a bed joint or reglet.
2. Lap flashing joints 6 inches and seal them.
3. Turn the ends up 1 inch into a head joint wherever a flashing run stops.
4. Carry the outer edge to the brick face, or past it as a drip.
5. Set weeps in the brick course directly on the flashing, never a course or two higher.
6. Make them open head joints 2 inches high or more, 24 inches apart, with screens.
7. Bevel each cavity-side mortar bed away from the gap.
8. Draw a cavity board up the gap as the courses rise, and empty it before it overflows.
9. Where collection mesh is used, carry the flashing's back leg 6 to 8 inches above the top of the mesh.
10. Keep the weeps open through construction and every cleanup.
11. Photograph each flashing run, its end dams and its weeps before brick covers them.

**Professional**

The air space is 1 inch minimum, a nominal dimension in the residential code. TN 28D recommends 2 inches, because narrower spaces are hard to keep clean and to lay without dragging mortar across. TMS 402's prescriptive veneer provisions allow up to 6-5/8 inches between brick and block, and up to 4-1/2 inches for a noncomposite wall.

Where insulation fills part of the cavity, a nominal 1 inch stays clear between its face and the back of the brick, enough for the mason to lay brick and for the wall to drain. The residential code's veneer table lets a drainage air space hold some construction mortar, and TN 28D requires that any mortar present leave the cavity able to drain.

Through-wall flashing goes at five points in TN 28D's list:

- the base of the wall
- above and below every opening
- shelf angles
- the tops of walls and beneath copings
- any other break in the air space

Brick veneer on a concrete or block backing has no prescriptive height limit in TMS 402, and TN 28D recommends supporting it at the foundation for up to about 50 feet, so this wall needs no shelf angle. Its flashing lines are the base of the wall, every window and door head and sill, and the top of the wall.

The flashing's back leg stands high enough that water wetting the flashing cannot top it, and ending it in a bed joint or reglet keeps water from running down behind it. A reglet is a slot cut or cast into concrete to take the flashing edge, and a termination bar screwed to the face is the alternative TN 28D allows. End dams keep water on a short run, under a sill or head, from running off the ends into the wall. A drip past the face throws water clear instead of letting it run back under the flashing.

Weeps set a course or two above the flashing leave water standing on the flashing below them, which is why the code and TN 28D put them immediately above it. Of the weep types BIA lists, the open head joint drains best, and screens in it keep insects out and stop a later painter or repointer from filling it. Rope wicks and tube weeps are the alternatives where open joints are unwelcome.

Mortar droppings become a failure when they pile into a mound or smear that touches both wythes. Mortar is absorptive, and a continuous bridge carries water across by capillary action (water pulled through fine pores by surface tension) instead of letting it fall to the weeps. A single tie carrying enough squeezed-out mortar does the same.

Workmanship is the primary control in TN 28D, with mortar-collection devices and full-height drainage mats second. Those devices can move the bridge higher in the wall, which is why their makers want the flashing's back leg well above the device. The hand techniques for a clean cavity, the bevelled bed, the cavity board and the cleaning gaps above the base flashing, are in the Masonry Craft chapter.

TN 28D's own warnings name the details most often botched:

- weeps set above the flashing course instead of on it
- end dams left off where a flashing run stops
- a back leg too short, or not ended in a joint or reglet
- droppings left on the flashing
- sill flashing without end dams, which makes a flat sheet instead of a pan

**Code**

```
The outer wythe must drain through weepholes above the flashing.
  Not less than 3/16 inch in diameter, not more than 33 inches apart, and located immediately above the flashing.
  Source: ORSC R703.8.6 (2023 edition).

A cavity wall has minimum wythe thicknesses and a maximum cavity width.
  Each wythe at least 3 inches nominal, the backing at least as thick as the facing, and the cavity not over 4 inches nominal unless tie size and spacing are calculated.
  Source: ORSC R606.13 (2023 edition).
```

## 11.12 Cavity insulation

**Simple**

Insulation boards fasten to the block inside the gap, with an inch or more of open air between them and the brick. That gap is the wall's wet zone, where water runs down by design. So the insulation has to take years of wetting from the brick side without soaking up water or breaking down. Foam and mineral-wool boards made for that place qualify, and ordinary batts do not. Water runs down the inside face of the brick, and the insulation stays dry behind the open air.

**Applied**

1. Choose a board rated for the wet zone, with its ASTM standard on the label or data sheet.
2. Fasten the boards to the block's cavity face with insulation adhesive or mechanical fasteners.
3. Use the clips or plates that slip over the tie eyes where the product has them.
4. Lay two staggered layers with offset joints, and tape the seams of both.
5. Check for 1 inch of clear space to the brick line before each lift of brick goes up.
6. Photograph the insulation before the brick covers it.

**Professional**

Boards suited to the air space of a brick-on-block wall, each with its material standard, per TN 28D:

- expanded or extruded polystyrene (ASTM C578)
- polyisocyanurate (ASTM C1289)
- reflective insulation (ASTM C1224)
- mineral wool board (ASTM C612), which is semi-rigid, fire-resistant and does not absorb moisture
- spray polyurethane foam on the block's face (ASTM C1029), which can serve as the water-resistive and air barrier, with compatible membrane strips at edges and transitions to cover gaps the foam opens as it shrinks

For the block cores, not the air space, TN 28D names water-repellent vermiculite (ASTM C516) and silicone-treated perlite (ASTM C549), placed in lifts no taller than 20 feet and never rodded, and recommends against EPS bead fill. Foamed-in-place cellular cement and resin foams fill cores through ports in the wall face, and no ASTM material standard covers them.

TN 28D's selection rule is a product intended for the wet zone with an established history there. Among the boards, the Envelope chapter (Wall Insulation Strategy) gives mineral wool the most favorable durability profile, EPS the code-safe economical choice, and polyiso a place where the cavity drains and its foil facing is accounted for. The same section tabulates the thermal penalty of the ties beside the thermally broken alternatives.

Insulation placement sets where the dew point falls inside the wall section. TN 28D's goal for this wall type is condensation in the drainage cavity, the one place built to collect and discharge water. A wider air space and continuous cavity insulation both push the condensation plane into that wet zone, away from the block and the interior finish. An uninsulated cavity fails the energy target regardless of condensation, so it is not an option here.

Ties crossing the insulation are the unavoidable thermal bridge. A shelf angle would be a far larger one per foot, and standoff shelf angles exist to let cavity insulation run behind their vertical leg, but this wall has none. Two staggered layers with offset, taped joints leave no through-seam, and the 1-inch clear space to the brick holds everywhere, so the insulation face is never the drainage surface.

Drainage and continuous insulation fill the cavity's depth. Routing plumbing, electrical or low-voltage lines through it is a compromise. Anything in the cavity must tolerate a damp environment, and every penetration of the flashing plane or the brick is a new leak point on the one plane the design keeps continuous. A service cavity on the interior side of the block is the alternative. The block's own place, inside the insulation line, already gives the wall its thermal mass.

## 11.13 Openings

**Simple**

An opening interrupts every layer of the wall at once. The structure, the drainage gap, the insulation and the air barrier all stop at its edges. Each gets its own detail there. In this wall the window sits deep, at the face of the block, on the warm side of the insulation. That is the standard detail for brick on block, and it gives the house its deep window reveals. Depth costs some daylight compared with a window set near the brick face. Each window also needs an exterior return around its reveal.

**Applied**

1. Set the block wythe's lintel or lintel bond beam at each head as the block reaches it.
2. Wrap membrane transition strips from the block's air barrier into the rough opening.
3. Form the sill flashing as a pan, with end dams at both sides and the back.
4. Seal or solder every seam and corner of the pan.
5. Set the brick's lintel on 4 inches of bearing or more at each side.
6. Run the head flashing over the brick lintel and past its ends, and turn the ends up as dams.
7. Place weeps directly above the head flashing and at the sill.
8. Close the cavity around the opening with mineral wool, pre-compressed joint filler or sealant at the head, sill and jambs.
9. Join the jamb seals to the sill flashing so the window frame is cut off from the cavity.
10. Carry the cavity insulation behind the window buck with the same 1 inch clear to the brick.
11. Add ties within 12 inches of each opening larger than 16 inches in either dimension, 3 feet apart around it.
12. Close the window-to-masonry joint with backer rod and sealant, and seal the frame to the block inside.
13. Photograph the pan, the head flashing and the jamb seals before they are covered.

**Professional**

Anything that bridges the cavity at an opening conducts air and water. TN 28D states that the perimeter sealant joint between window and masonry is the primary defense against both. With the air barrier on the block's cavity face, membrane transition strips wrap from that plane into the rough opening, an interior air seal closes the gap between frame and block, and a second strip can close the cavity. The window ties into the air barrier on the structural wythe, never to the brick, the same plane rule the Envelope chapter sets for the default wall.

Mineral wool, pre-compressed joint filler or sealant in the cavity around the opening keeps cavity air off the window frame. The closure sits at the head between the back of the lintel and the block, below the sill, and at the jambs between the two wythes. Sealed to the sill flashing at the jambs, it completes a barrier between frame and cavity.

Window units weep only the water that passes their own frame seals, so the masonry opening needs its own flashing, TN 28D states. Head and sill flashing run past the ends of the lintel or sill and turn up as end dams. Weeps above the head flashing send water intercepted above the opening back out through the brick instead of toward the frame.

Where a one-piece masonry sill with lugs is used, the sill flashing extends into the brick at the jambs. Jamb flashing is optional, adhered to the block and lapped over the sill flashing. TN 28D's sill detail slopes the exposed sill at 15 degrees or more, overhangs the wall face by 1-1/2 inches or more, and puts a weep at its underside.

The cavity insulation runs behind the window buck with the same clear space to the brick as in the field of the wall. TN 28D's details set the frame at or near the block face, in line with the insulation plane. A frame there stays inside the continuous insulation, while a frame flush with the brick sits outside it and lets heat bypass the insulation around the opening. Depth costs daylight and adds an exterior reveal to detail. It adds nothing to the tie layout, because the perimeter ties and the cavity seal anchor to the wythes and not to the frame.

Two lintels span each opening, one for each wythe. The block wythe's lintel, a reinforced bond-beam course or a precast or steel member, is designed for the load it carries (ORSC R606.10). Corner windows with no pier at the corner, and ribbon windows that make one long span, push past the table and take lintels the engineer designs. Whether a long lintel shows as a band or hides behind brick-faced coursing is a facade decision. ## 11.14 Attachments, projections and the roofline

**Simple**

Everything that bolts to this wall bolts to the inner block wythe, and nothing crosses the gap. A connection planned ahead is cast or grouted in as the wall goes up. One added later is drilled into cured masonry to the engineer's design. No joist, ledger or bracket reaches into the open cavity. A part sticking into the gap becomes a shelf that water crosses. A concrete floor bonded to the block can crack the wall as the slab shrinks, so a slip layer between them lets the slab move. The top of the wall gets a cap that sheds water outward.

**Applied**

1. List every connection to the wall on the structural drawings before the block goes up.
2. Set cast-in inserts, embed plates and anchor bolts as the block and bond beam are grouted.
3. Grout solid every cell in each course that will receive a bolt.
4. Bolt ledgers only into solid-grouted cells.
5. Stop every joist, ledger and bracket at the block's cavity face.
6. Set wood roof plates on bolts grouted into the top cells, embedded 4 bolt diameters or 2 inches, minimum.
7. Hand-tighten the nuts over plate washers, then strap the roof framing to the plate.
8. Lay a bond break of building paper or a slip sheet where a cast-in-place slab bears on the block.
9. Seat precast hollow-core planks on 3 inches of bearing or more, over a bearing pad on the bond beam.
10. Take any porch or entry overhang to the engineer as its own design item.
11. Choose the roofline termination only after the roof structure is chosen.

**Professional**

### Connections into the block wythe

Wedge inserts and embed plates cast into concrete or grouted into the bond beam take bolted or welded attachments later, without drilling reinforced material. They suit every connection known before the pour, and the embed schedule belongs on the structural drawings and in the pour checklist. Post-installed anchors into cured concrete, grouted masonry or hollow masonry handle the rest. Their type, embedment and edge distance are engineered per connection, because edge distance protects the cover over the reinforcement. A bolt in a hollow cell bears only on the thin face shell, so the code fills those cells first (Code below).

Wood roof framing bears on a plate anchored by bolts grouted into the top of the wall. TN 28D cites the TMS code's minimum bolt embedment and adds that uplift can call for longer bolts or vertical bars, with nuts hand-tightened and plate washers under them. Roof anchorage values are the engineer's. Framing bears on the inner wythe only and stops at its cavity face, because a joist end projecting into the air space forms a ledge that water crosses. Wood joists in beam pockets take a 3-inch fire cut in TN 28D's detail.

### Concrete floors and roofs on the block

Cast-in-place slabs bearing on the block shrink, and they curl as the forms come off and the loads arrive. A slab bonded to the wall cracks it. TN 28D's remedy is a bond break between slab and block, which lets the slab move in the plane of the wall, and a slab thickened into a beam over the inner wythe to stiffen it against curl. Precast hollow-core planks seat on the inner wythe over a bearing pad on the bond beam, with the anchorage the engineer details.

### Porches and entry overhangs

Three structural options exist for a porch roof or deep entry overhang:

1. a cantilevered reinforced-concrete slab or beam out of the wall or bond beam, engineered for the cantilever moment
2. an independently posted roof with its inboard edge on the bond beam, which removes the cantilever from the wall
3. a bracket or knee-brace system on cast-in embeds, for lighter canopies

Each is its own engineered element. A cantilever calculation is not an extension of the roof diaphragm design, even where the two meet at the bond beam.

### The roofline

The roofline termination follows the roof decision, because each option presumes a roof:

- **Parapet with coping**, for a flat or low-slope roof behind it. The cavity continues up through the parapet to keep the wythes separate. A 12-inch parapet with brick on both faces leaves a nominal 4-inch center wythe, and cells that small do not take bars and grout, so on a concrete structure TN 28D's alternative is a concrete stem-wall parapet. The coping slopes toward a drip on at least one side, over through-wall flashing directly beneath it, with sealant compatible with the flashing at every anchor. Sheet-metal copings sit on cleats over a flashing membrane rated for the heat under the metal.
- **Exposed fascia**, a conventional eave for a sloped roof. It avoids the parapet's thickness problem and gives up the flush line of masonry against the sky.
- **Soldier-course cap**, a decorative top for an eave or low parapet edge. It does not replace a coping, and without flashing and a drip under it the top of the wall saturates, as an unflashed base would.

A parapet is wet on three sides and has no brick above to hold it down, so it moves more than the wall below. Its expansion joints run closer, as the movement-joint rules give, and it is not painted or coated except with a vapor-permeable coating. A roofing membrane run the full height of the parapet's inner face traps vapor in the masonry. Where the roof flashing ends on the parapet face, a layer of through-wall flashing above it keeps water that soaks the inner face from undermining the roofing. The Roof System and Roof-Wall Junctions chapter details the junction itself.

### Drips and rain streaking

Water running down a wall collects at every horizontal projection. Without a drip, it wraps back under the projection by surface tension and runs down the same strip of wall for years, carrying dirt, salts and biological growth into a visible streak. The sill and coping drips above control streaking and water entry in one detail, and detailing every projection to them keeps streaking to a minimum. Leaving a drip off to cultivate a weathering pattern is a facade choice with a durability cost, because the same wrapping water feeds efflorescence and coating failure.

**Code**

```
Bolts set in hollow masonry need solid-grouted cells.
  The cells in the courses receiving a bolt are grouted solid.
  Source: ORSC R606.11, note to Figures R606.11(1) to R606.11(3) (2023 edition).

Unreinforced masonry parapets have a minimum thickness and a maximum height.
  At least 8 inches thick, and no taller than four times the thickness for solid units or three times for hollow units.
  Source: ORSC R606.4.4 (2023 edition).
```

## 11.15 Proving the details on a mock-up

**Simple**

A test panel of the finished wall, called a mock-up, goes up before the house wall does. The same mason builds it with the same materials. It has both wythes with their ties and insulation, the base flashing with its weeps, and a window sill and jamb. Water sprayed on it shows whether the details drain. Its joints, color and tooling become the standard for the house wall. The test covers the mason's workmanship as well as the drawings.

**Applied**

1. Build the mock-up panel before the house wall, with the mason who will build the house.
2. Include the base flashing and weeps, a window sill and jamb, and any tall slit jamb the house will have.
3. Spray the face at a fixed rate for a timed period, following ASTM C1601, and watch the cavity side.
4. Pour water into the cavity above the flashing and time its exit at the weeps.
5. Treat water that disappears instead of reaching the weeps as a leak path to find before building.
6. Check the joint profile, mortar color and tooling against the intended finish.
7. Record the results with photographs.
8. Keep the panel standing as the reference until the house wall matches it.
9. Hire a certified C1601 test where a disputed or high-stakes detail needs third-party results.

**Professional**

ASTM C1601 is the field method. A spray rack wets the wall face at a fixed rate for a timed period while the back of the wall is watched. ASTM E514, the other masonry water test, is a laboratory test of water penetration through specimens built in a pressure chamber and does not run in the field. An owner-run spray to the C1601 pattern is a legitimate self-check without third-party standing. A certified C1601 test has the standing an inspector or engineer needs for a disputed detail.

The mock-up also settles appearance by inspection, and water introduced above the flashing should discharge at the weeps. TMS 602's grout demonstration panel is the parallel route for proving a grouting method, such as self-consolidating grout, before it goes into the house.

## 11.16 Laying order, foundation to bond beam

**Simple**

Both walls start the same way, with the block wythe built course by course from the foundation to the bond beam at the top. Steel bars stand in the block cores, and grout fills the cores around them in stages. An inspector checks the steel before each grout pour. On the default wall the insulation and cladding go onto the finished block. On the cavity wall the insulation goes onto the block first. The brick follows, tied to the block as it rises.

**Applied**

The hand skills these steps assume, from leads and lines to tie embedment and a clean cavity, are in the Masonry Craft chapter.

### The block wythe, both walls

1. Check the foundation top for its grid elevation, level and dowel positions before the first course.
2. Mark corners, openings and both joint grids on the foundation.
3. Dry-lay the first course, then lay it in a full mortar bed 1/4 to 3/4 inch thick over the foundation.
4. Build the corner leads, and fill between them course by course to a string line.
5. Lay ladder joint reinforcement in the bed joints at the drawings' spacing, with the tie eyes on a cavity wall.
6. Stand the vertical bars in their cells, held at top and bottom and every 200 bar diameters or less.
7. Remove mortar fins over 1/2 inch from every grout space before inspection.
8. Call for the reinforced-masonry inspection before each grout pour.
9. Grout in lifts of 8 feet or less.
10. Where a total pour exceeds 8 feet, place it in lifts of 64 inches or less, with special inspection during grouting.
11. Build cleanouts at the base of any pour over 64 inches where the building official requires them, 32 inches apart in solid-grouted walls.
12. Set each lintel or lintel bond beam as the wall reaches the opening's head.
13. Lay and grout the bond-beam course, with the roof embeds set in the wet grout.
14. On the default wall, attach the envelope layers in plane order: barrier, insulation, furring and cladding.

### The brick wythe, cavity wall

1. Confirm the bond break, the solid fill below the flashing, and the base flashing with its end dams.
2. Fasten and tape the cavity insulation, keeping 1 inch clear to the brick line.
3. Lay the brick from the base flashing up, with weeps in the first course on the flashing.
4. Hook the pintles into the eyes at each tie course.
5. Draw the cavity board up as the courses rise.
6. Flash and dam each opening as the brick reaches it, and add the opening ties.
7. Leave the brick expansion joints open at their marked lines.
8. Finish the top with the chosen roofline detail, flashed and dripped.

**Code**

```
The starting course's bed joint over the foundation has limits.
  Not less than 1/4 inch and not more than 3/4 inch thick.
  Source: ORSC R606.3.1 (2023 edition).
```

## 11.17 Problems and repairs

**Simple**

Masonry walls fail in a short list of known ways, and nearly all of them involve water. A white bloom on the face, cracks, peeling paint and brick bowing away from the block each trace to a cause that can be found. The repair order is always the same. The water source is found and stopped first, and the symptom is fixed after. Cleaning a stain or patching a crack without that first step brings the problem back.

**Applied**

1. When white deposits return after the first year, inspect the flashing, weeps and cavity before cleaning again.
2. Clean efflorescence only after the water source is stopped and the wall has dried.
3. Fix a crack monitor across any crack, and read it through a full year of seasons.
4. Repoint only dormant cracks confined to the mortar joints.
5. Route and seal dormant cracks too wide or irregular to repoint, with backer rod and elastomeric sealant sized to the measured movement.
6. Where a crack returns after repair or repeats along a wall, cut and build a movement joint there.
7. Apply a penetrating silane or siloxane repellent only to a dry wall free of salt deposits.
8. Use vapor-open treatments only, never a film-forming paint or sealer, on exposed masonry.
9. For failing ties or a leaking cavity, survey the cavity with a borescope through holes drilled in mortar joints, never through units.
10. Restore lost tie connections with remedial helical ties driven from outside through small pilot holes.
11. Cut new weeps in head joints directly above the flashing where weeps are missing or blocked.

**Professional**

### Efflorescence

Efflorescence is the white crystalline deposit on masonry faces. It needs soluble salts in or against the wall, water to dissolve them, and a path to a surface where the water evaporates and leaves the salt behind. BIA Technical Note 23A traces most efflorescing salts to the materials touching the brick, such as mortar, grout, concrete masonry and soil, and names rain as the primary water source. The principal salt source in mortar and grout is the alkali in portland cement, and ASTM C150 includes a low-alkali option.

New-building bloom appears in the first year in a fairly uniform pattern, as the construction water in fresh mortar and grout leaves the wall, and it ends as the wall dries. Efflorescence that returns after the first year marks a continuing water source, and in a drainage wall that points at the flashing, weeps or cavity. Four prevention levers act on the mechanism:

- low-alkali cement, which removes the main salt source
- an integral water-repellent admixture, which leaves less absorbed water to dissolve salts
- working flashing and weeps, the main lever against recurrence
- covered storage of the masonry units, because pallets left open through an Oregon wet season soak up the water that feeds the first-year bloom

### Integral water repellents

An integral water-repellent admixture is mixed through the mortar, so the repellency runs through the joint instead of sitting on its surface. GCP's DRY-BLOCK mortar admixture is dosed at 16 to 24 ounces per 3 cubic feet of mortar, and Euclid's HYDRAPEL is a powder dosed per its current data sheet. Both are made as two-part systems, with the brick or block plant-dosed with the matching admixture from the same maker. Mortar-side treatment against untreated, absorptive units delivers less than the rated result.

Admixture chemistries are not interchangeable between makers, so matching plant-dosed brick and block are confirmed with the suppliers before the system is chosen. Third-party data on absorption (ASTM C1403) and flexural bond (ASTM C1072) comes from the manufacturers' test reports.

### Coatings that peel, and the vapor-open alternative

A film-forming paint or sealer blocks the masonry's surface pores. Moisture already in the wall, or arriving through a defect, can no longer evaporate outward. In a freezing climate it freezes behind the film, and the expansion pushes the coating off or spalls the masonry face itself. The coating turns a moisture load the wall could have dried into freeze-thaw damage.

Penetrating silane and siloxane repellents react inside the pores and line them. They repel liquid water and leave the pore structure open to vapor. Two limits govern their use. The wall must be dry, because water in the pores blocks penetration and cure. It must also be free of active salts, because sealing over efflorescence traps the salts and the water cycle that feeds them. Silane needs an alkaline substrate to bond, so on aged, carbonated masonry siloxane is the broader choice. The moisture source is corrected and the wall dried before any repellent goes on.

### Cracks: reading them before repairing them

The first question about a crack is whether it is still moving. A crack monitor answers it: a two-piece gauge scribed to read width and offset, or two reference marks across the crack. It is read across a full seasonal cycle, because brick and block movement runs on annual moisture and temperature cycles. Measurable change across the year means active, and none means dormant.

The fix follows the diagnosis. Repointing, the removal and replacement of failed joint mortar, suits mortar deterioration and small dormant cracks confined to joints, and an active crack reopens a repointed joint within one movement cycle. Route-and-seal, which widens the crack and fills it over backer rod with elastomeric sealant, suits dormant cracks too wide or irregular to repoint and tolerates limited movement if the sealant is sized to what the monitor showed. A crack that recurs after repointing, or repeats at intervals along a wall, marks a missing movement joint, and building the joint ends it. Repointing and cleaning methods are in the Masonry Craft chapter.

### Failing ties and leaking cavities

A borescope, a camera on a flexible probe, surveys a cavity through small holes drilled in mortar joints at representative points. It shows droppings, standing water, tie corrosion and the state of the insulation without opening the wall.

Remedial helical ties restore the wythe connection from outside through small pilot holes, without rebuilding. Helifix makes three of them: DryFix dry mechanical pinning ties, TorkFix anchors for veneers detached from their backing, and CemTie grouted helical ties for solid masonry. Missing or blocked weeps are retrofit by cutting head-joint mortar out at the right spacing immediately above the flashing. Clearing droppings out of a finished cavity has no standard tool and works through small openings under the borescope, which is why the cavity is kept clean as the brick goes up.

### Where the durability money goes

Grade SW brick is compliance, not an upgrade, because the weathering index rules out Grade MW on the exterior face. Tighter weep spacing and complete flashing add a few linear feet of the same materials during construction. They act directly on a saturated wall, the condition behind most of these problems, which is disruptive to open and repair later.

Integral water repellents and penetrating sealers add a material and labor premium that the suppliers' quotes and third-party test reports put a number on, case by case. Mortar formulation counts too, because the TMS code gives masonry-cement and air-entrained cement-lime mortars lower allowable flexural tension, and a mortar with less flexural capacity cracks more readily under the movement between brick and block.

## Records to keep

- The parcel's seismic design category, and the engineer's tie schedule, mortar types and isolation details.
- The structural drawings naming the brick's classification.
- Tie and joint-reinforcement data sheets, with material, wire sizes, pintle legs and allowed offset.
- The brick supplier's Grade SW certification to ASTM C216.
- The cavity insulation data sheet, with its ASTM standard and wet-zone rating.
- The coursing datum and grid elevations, and both movement-joint grids, on the as-built drawings.
- Photographs of the base detail before brick covers it: the bond break, the solid fill, the base flashing and the slab-edge insulation joint.
- Photographs of each tie course, each flashing run with its end dams and weeps, each opening's pan, head flashing and jamb seals, and the insulation before cover.
- The grout pour log, with date, lift height, slump, reconsolidation time, weather and each inspection sign-off.
- Mortar and grout mix records, batch tickets and the engineer's acceptance of any admixture.
- The weather log for masonry work, with the protection used.
- The geotechnical sulfate result and the cement type chosen from it.
- Building department correspondence and test reports for any alternative material.
- Mock-up test results and photographs, with a description of the accepted panel.
- Crack monitor readings and repair records over the wall's life.
- Third-party test reports for any water-repellent admixture system used.
