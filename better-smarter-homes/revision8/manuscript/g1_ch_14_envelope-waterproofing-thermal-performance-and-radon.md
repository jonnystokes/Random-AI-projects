# Chapter 14: Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, And Radon

The envelope is the assembly that separates the conditioned interior from the exterior: the roof, the above-grade walls, the windows and doors, the slab edge, the below-grade walls, and every penetration through any of those surfaces. This chapter specifies how each envelope assembly controls bulk water, air, vapor, heat, and soil gas. It states the Oregon energy-compliance path with its testing thresholds, and it defines the records that prove the work before covering.

Adjacent material by topic: the foundation chapter contains the below-grade waterproofing and drainage construction detail. The structural chapter contains the structural side of wall ties. The HVAC chapter contains the mechanical ventilation that a tight envelope requires. The land chapter contains the parcel-level procedure for confirming whether wildfire-hardening rules apply; the construction requirements themselves are stated in this chapter.

## 14.1 The Five Control Layers

Every envelope assembly performs five control functions. Each function is assigned to a dedicated control layer, and each control layer must be continuous across the building.

**Simple**

A house keeps rain out and keeps its heated or cooled air in. It keeps moisture from condensing inside the walls, slows the flow of heat, and blocks gas rising from the soil. A different layer in the wall, roof, or floor performs each of those five functions. When every layer runs unbroken from one part of the house to the next, the envelope works. Where any layer has a gap, water, air, moisture, or soil gas enters through the gap. Adding thickness elsewhere does not close a gap in a control layer.

**Professional: the five control layers**

| Control function | Control layer | Typical material |
|---|---|---|
| Bulk water (rain, snow, groundwater, splashback) | Water-resistive barrier (WRB) on walls; roofing membrane on roofs; waterproofing membrane below grade | House wrap, fluid-applied WRB, peel-and-stick membrane; modified bitumen, EPDM, TPO, PVC; 60-mil peel-and-stick polymer-modified asphalt |
| Air | Air-control layer, continuous from foundation to roof | Sealed sheathing, sealed drywall, sheet membranes (peel-and-stick), fluid-applied air barriers, foam boards taped at seams, parged or grouted masonry |
| Vapor | Vapor retarder (placement and perm rating depend on climate and assembly) | Class I (0.1 perm or less), Class II (over 0.1 to 1.0 perm), Class III (over 1.0 to 10 perms); polyethylene, smart membranes, vapor-retarder paint |
| Heat | Thermal insulation, continuous and complete | Mineral wool, XPS, EPS, polyiso, spray foam, cellulose, fiberglass; placement varies by assembly |
| Soil gas | Soil-gas retarder plus passive subslab depressurization in the radon counties | 6-mil polyethylene retarder, or 15-mil ASTM E1745 Class A membrane serving both vapor and soil-gas functions; 3-inch passive vent pipe |

The five control layers are conceptually distinct. In practice, several layers can share one material. A peel-and-stick membrane serves as both the WRB and the air-control layer. A 15-mil ASTM E1745 Class A vapor retarder serves as both vapor retarder and soil-gas retarder. A foam insulation board with sealed seams serves as thermal insulation and as part of the air-control layer.

**The continuity check**

Each control layer must be traceable around the entire building cross-section. The pencil test states the requirement: each control layer, traced on the building section drawing, must form one unbroken line around slab, walls, and roof. A trace that breaks at a transition or penetration marks a gap in that layer at that location.

Common discontinuities:

- slab-to-wall transition, where the wall meets the foundation
- wall-to-roof transition, at the eave or parapet (a low wall extending above the roof surface)
- window and door rough openings: head, jambs, sill
- penetrations: pipes, conduits, vents, exhaust fans, hose bibs, condensate drains, structural ties
- below-grade-to-above-grade transition, where the foundation wall meets above-grade construction
- mechanical and electrical service entries: water service, electrical service, gas line, HVAC equipment

The project drawings must show the continuity detail for each of these locations. The window rough-opening sequence in the windows and doors section of this chapter illustrates the pattern.

## 14.2 Oregon Residential Energy Compliance

The thermal performance of the envelope is governed by ORSC Chapter 11, the residential energy provisions. The 2023 ORSC Chapter 11 became effective October 1, 2023 and mandatory April 1, 2024; the 2021 edition is no longer the minimum standard. Confirm the adopted edition with the local jurisdiction at permit application.

**Simple**

What does the energy code require of this house? Three things. The building shell must meet the insulation and window values in one state table. The project must add at least one extra efficiency measure from a second table. The builder chooses it from options such as extra-tight construction, higher-efficiency equipment, or solar panels. And the paperwork must declare the choice on a state form at permit application. Where testing is the chosen route, the results reach the building department before move-in. A concrete wall with continuous exterior insulation meets the wall value directly. A concrete roof needs a calculation instead of a table lookup, and the state publishes the calculator.

### 14.2.1 The Compliance Path

Every new dwelling must meet:

- the prescriptive envelope requirements in Table N1101.1(1), or demonstrate an equivalent whole-envelope UA through Table N1104.1(1), and
- at least one additional measure from Table N1101.1(2).

ORSC Section N1101.1 requires the additional measure for all new construction. It is not optional.

| Path | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Standard prescriptive | Table N1101.1(1) values plus one additional measure from Table N1101.1(2) |
| UA tradeoff | Table N1104.1(1) performance-based UA calculation; one additional measure still required from N1101.1(2) |
| Ducts outside the thermal envelope (Exception 3 of N1105.3) | Two additional measures required from N1101.1(2) instead of one |

The 2023 ORSC default rule locates all ducts within the conditioned space; the buried-duct exception costs the project a second additional measure.

**Permit submittal:** Form 440-4854j (Residential Energy Additional Measure Selection) must be submitted with the permit application. The form declares which additional measure the project uses. The jurisdiction holds the permit until the form is submitted.

### 14.2.2 Table N1101.1(1) Prescriptive Envelope Requirements

The values below are the 2023 ORSC Table N1101.1(1) prescriptive envelope requirements. The BCD slab-edge technical bulletin reprints the table verbatim and is the fastest public copy.

| Assembly | Required performance | Equivalent value |
|---|---|---|
| Wall, above grade | U-0.059 | R-21 intermediate framing |
| Wall, below grade | C-0.063 | R-15 continuous insulation or R-21 |
| Flat ceiling | U-0.021 | R-49 |
| Vaulted ceiling | U-0.033 | R-30 rafter or R-30A scissor truss |
| Underfloor | U-0.033 | R-30 |
| Slab-edge perimeter | F-0.520 | R-15, 24-inch minimum depth |
| Heated slab interior | not specified at table value | R-10 entire underside |
| Windows | U-0.27 | n/a |
| Skylights | U-0.50 | n/a |
| Exterior doors | U-0.20 | n/a |
| Doors with more than 2.5 sq ft glazing | U-0.40 | n/a |

Solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC): Table N1101.1(1) sets no mandatory SHGC value. Oregon's climate zones (4C marine west of the Cascades, 5B continental east) benefit from winter solar gain, and the state omitted the low-SHGC limits common in cooling-dominated states. SHGC remains a design choice affecting overheating, passive solar gain, glare, and cooling load. Do not treat any SHGC number as an Oregon residential code minimum.

The below-grade wall value, C-0.063 with R-15 continuous insulation, assumes insulation with no framing interruptions. A concrete or masonry below-grade wall with continuous exterior insulation matches that assumption exactly.

For cold-climate sites (mountain elevations, high desert), the table values are minimums, not targets. Where the winter design temperature runs below about 10 °F, run the comparison before finalizing the envelope: the cost of one additional layer of roof and wall insulation against the heating-equipment size reduction it allows, priced over the equipment's service life.

### 14.2.3 Table N1101.1(2) Additional Measures

ORSC Table N1101.1(2) lists the additional efficiency measures. At least one is required. The options include enhanced envelope values, enhanced air sealing with blower-door verification, ducts inside the conditioned envelope, heat-recovery ventilation, high-efficiency heating equipment, high-efficiency water heating, solar thermal, and photovoltaic generation. Form 440-4854j lists the current measures with full performance criteria.

The two selections that fit a concrete-and-reinforced-masonry house:

- **Measure 7, enhanced air sealing plus heat recovery:** blower-door result of 2.75 ACH50 or less, third-party tested, paired with heat-recovery ventilation at 66 percent minimum sensible recovery efficiency. A parged, grouted masonry shell reaches this leakage level with ordinary care at the penetrations, and the mechanical design already includes the HRV.
- **Ducts entirely inside the conditioned envelope:** the default condition for a concrete-house design that keeps mechanical equipment in conditioned space, so it costs nothing extra where the mechanical layout already complies.

### 14.2.4 Concrete Roof Energy Compliance

No prescriptive row in Table N1101.1(1) fits a concrete roof. A 6-inch concrete slab contributes roughly R-0.5; the assembly's thermal performance comes from the insulation above or below the deck, and the compliance demonstration runs through the UA tradeoff.

**The tool.** BCD publishes the Envelope Trade-Off Calculator, an Excel workbook (calculator-orsc-tradeoff.xlsx) linked on the BCD residential energy compliance page. Its function, from its own title block: demonstrate compliance with Table N1104.1(1) by showing the proposed envelope's total UA does not exceed the UA of the same building built to the prescriptive values. Do not confuse it with the separate Measure 6 calculator on the same page: the trade-off calculator demonstrates parity (0 percent), while Additional Measure 6 requires a UA 8 percent below the code baseline.

**Applied: the trade-off procedure**

1. Enter the project's actual areas in the Standard Base Case: flat ceiling, vaulted ceiling, gross wall including fenestration, underfloor square footage, and slab-edge length. The workbook multiplies them by the fixed prescriptive factors (ceiling U-0.021, wall U-0.059, windows U-0.27, slab edge F-0.52, and the rest of the table) to produce the base-case UA.
2. Calculate the concrete roof assembly's U-factor using ASHRAE Handbook of Fundamentals procedures. The code directs non-wood assemblies to this method; the workbook's ceiling drop-downs list only wood-frame and EPS-panel assemblies, so a concrete roof U-factor is a manual, documented entry with the ASHRAE calculation and product cut sheets attached.
3. Enter each proposed assembly. Walls that are not in the drop-down list go on the Mixed-Alt Walls tab with a calculated U-factor per wall type; the tab area-weights them. Windows, skylights, and doors are entered unit by unit from their NFRC ratings.
4. Buy back the roof's excess heat loss elsewhere until the Percent Improved cell reads 0.0 percent or better: walls below U-0.059 (the workbook includes an insulated-concrete-form row for exactly this case), windows below U-0.27, underfloor below U-0.033, slab edge below F-0.52.
5. Submit the printed workbook with the plans, cut sheets, and specifications to the building official.

Two geometry limits: vaulted-ceiling area entered in the workbook cannot exceed 50 percent of the conditioned floor area (beyond that, the workbook's own instructions require a different compliance method, and Table N1101.1(1) footnote g tightens the vaulted value to U-0.026), and only one solid door up to 28 square feet receives the U-0.54 allowance.

Sizing anchor for early design: with 4 inches of polyiso above the deck (R-25 nominal, derated for cold-weather service), a concrete roof assembly approaches the prescriptive flat-ceiling target. With 6 inches it meets or beats the target, leaving no deficit to buy back. Plan the additional measure independently: a project that only reaches UA parity through the trade-off cannot also claim Additional Measure 6, so select a different measure from Table N1101.1(2).

## 14.3 Air Sealing And Blower-Door Testing

Air sealing builds the air-control layer. Blower-door testing measures the result.

**Simple**

A blower door is a fan mounted in an exterior doorway that pulls air out of the house at a fixed test pressure. The airflow the fan must move to hold that pressure equals the air leaking in through every gap in the shell. The result is stated in air changes per hour. That number counts how many times each hour the house's full air volume leaks through the shell at the test pressure. Oregon's passing numbers are 3.25 where a test replaces the sealing checklist, and 2.75 where the tight-envelope extra measure is chosen. A hired test costs $300-$600 (2026); renting the equipment and running it yourself costs $150-$300 (2026).

**Applied: air sealing scope**

The air-control layer runs continuously around the conditioned space. A concrete and masonry shell has fewer leakage paths than a framed one: solid-grouted reinforced cells are airtight, and a parge coat (a thin cement plaster skim) seals the face of any ungrouted block. The leakage paths that remain are the joints, penetrations, and openings:

- top-of-wall transitions at eaves and parapets
- band or rim areas at any floor level with wood framing in a hybrid assembly
- top plate to ceiling drywall transition, where the prescriptive path uses framed ceilings
- penetrations: plumbing pipes, electrical conduit, HVAC ducts, exhaust fans, dryer vent, range hood, gas line, hose bibs
- window and door rough openings: head, jambs, sill
- attic hatch
- mechanical equipment penetrations (water heater, HRV ports, condensate lines)
- electrical boxes on exterior walls (airtight boxes, or sealed at the box-to-drywall interface)
- cavity insulation joints: tape foam-board seams on both faces, seal cut edges at openings with spray foam, and close the cavity at top and bottom with flashing or metal closure strips

Sealing materials:

- caulk: silicone, polyurethane, or acoustic sealant for gaps and cracks that stay put
- spray foam: expanding polyurethane for larger gaps and around penetrations
- sheet membrane: peel-and-stick at major transitions (window flashing, parapet, service entries)
- gaskets: at sill plates, electrical boxes, attic hatches

The blower-door result is computed as ACH50 = (CFM50 × 60) / conditioned volume in cubic feet, where CFM50 is the measured airflow at 50 pascals.

**Professional: Oregon thresholds**

| Threshold | Required by | Applies when |
|---|---|---|
| 3.25 ACH50 | ORSC N1104.8.2 | Testing is used as the compliance path instead of the prescriptive air-sealing checklist in Table N1104.8 |
| 2.75 ACH50, third-party tested | ORSC Table N1101.1(2), Additional Measure 7 | Measure 7 is the selected additional measure |

The prescriptive sealing path under Table N1104.8 requires no blower-door test; the builder completes the air-sealing checklist instead. Measure 7 pairs the 2.75 ACH50 result with heat-recovery ventilation: 66 percent minimum sensible heat-recovery efficiency and 1.6 CFM per watt total fan efficacy, combined supply and exhaust. The HVAC chapter contains the HRV selection detail.

**Test timing and documentation**

Where testing is part of the compliance path, the blower-door report goes to the building official after testing and before the certificate of occupancy is issued. The building must be substantially complete and testable. ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380 governs the test protocol. Measure 7 requires a third party: someone other than the builder runs the test, and the reporting form carries the testing company's CCB or EEAST number, the technician's name, and a signature.

The blower-door result gates the certificate of occupancy only; it does not gate permit issuance, and it is unrelated to the owner-builder resale rules in the legal chapter.

**Code**

## 14.4 Wall Insulation Strategy

The wall is the largest envelope surface. For a concrete or reinforced-masonry wall, insulation placement is a design choice with consequences for thermal performance, vapor drying, fire protection, and cost.

**Simple**

Where does the insulation go? Outside the concrete, wherever the assembly allows it. Placing insulation outside the structural wall keeps the concrete at conditioned temperature. Concrete held at conditioned temperature stores heat and steadies the indoor temperature between heating cycles. Placing insulation inside the wall exposes the concrete to outdoor temperature cycles, and the thermal-mass benefit is lost.

**Professional: placement options**

| Placement | Description | Strengths | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Continuous exterior insulation | Rigid foam or mineral wool board on the exterior of the structural wall, with rainscreen cladding outside the insulation | Best thermal performance; structural mass on the warm side; minimal thermal bridging at slab-to-wall and wall-to-roof transitions | Adds wall thickness; rainscreen cladding adds cost; foam plastics carry the R316 protection rules |
| Insulation in the cavity (double-wythe walls) | Rigid foam, mineral wool, or spray foam between two structural wythes | Insulation protected from weather and impact; slim wall section | Cavity width limited by the tie rules; thermal bridging at ties; no prescriptive path for double-wythe loadbearing walls (engineered design) |
| Interior insulation | Rigid foam or spray foam on the interior face of the structural wall | Lowest cost; fastest install | Structural wall sits on the cold side and cycles thermally; the mass no longer tempers the interior; vapor management becomes critical |

For a long-life concrete-and-reinforced-masonry house in Oregon, continuous exterior insulation with rainscreen cladding is the recommended strategy. The structural wall stays at conditioned temperature, the assembly dries in both directions (inward through the wall, outward through the rainscreen gap), and thermal bridging at transitions is minimized.

**Materials for continuous exterior insulation**

| Material | R-value per inch | Cost per sq ft per inch of thickness (2026) | Strengths | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rigid mineral wool board (Rockwool ComfortBoard, Owens Corning Thermafiber) | ~R-4.2 | $1.20-$1.60 | Vapor-open, non-combustible (no R316 rules), no off-gassing, no long-term R-value loss | Lowest R per inch; boards need lateral restraint and deform around ties |
| XPS rigid foam (DOW, Owens Corning Foamular, Atlas) | R-5 | $0.60-$0.80 | Closed-cell, water-resistant, standard below grade | R316 protection rules; R-value drifts down 10-15 percent over about 20 years as blowing agents escape; low permeance slows assembly drying |
| Polyiso rigid foam (Hunter, GAF, Atlas) | R-6 to R-6.5 nominal; plan on ~R-5 in cold service | $0.80-$1.20 | Highest R per inch; foil facing adds a radiant break | Loses 10-15 percent of rated R-value in sustained cold below about 30 °F; foil facing is a vapor barrier, so a foil-faced board in a sealed cavity traps moisture unless the cavity drains; R316 rules |
| EPS rigid foam | R-3.8 to R-4 | $0.40-$0.50 | Cheapest; vapor-open (2-5 perms); retains R-value long-term; accepted below grade | Lower compressive strength; R316 rules |
| Closed-cell spray foam | ~R-6 | applied cost is bid per job | Continuous air seal in one operation | Highest cost; R316 rules; cure off-gassing window |

Material verdicts by position, for this project:

- **At and below grade:** EPS or XPS. Both are moisture-tolerant and code-accepted below grade; EPS costs less and stays vapor-open, XPS resists water absorption better in saturated ground. Confirm the specific material with the building official; below-grade insulation acceptance is a local call.
- **Above grade, continuous exterior:** mineral wool board has the most favorable durability profile (no off-gassing, no R-value loss, no fire-protection rules, vapor-open for drying) at the cost of thicker walls for the same R-value. Rockwool ComfortBoard 80 at 4 inches provides roughly R-16.8, which with the masonry wall and interior finish meets the U-0.059 wall requirement after tie derating.
- **Inside a double-wythe cavity (engineered path; a wythe is one vertical layer of masonry):** EPS is the code-safe economical choice; high-density mineral wool works where the tie design restrains it. Polyiso fits where the cavity drains and the foil facing is accounted for in the vapor design. Install rigid boards in two staggered layers with joints offset (two 2-inch layers rather than one 4-inch layer): the stagger eliminates through-joints, the seams get taped on both faces, and 2-inch stock is a standard yard item that costs less per R than odd thicknesses.

**Wall ties and thermal bridging**

Continuous exterior insulation and cavity walls both require ties through the insulation. The structural chapter contains the tie structural requirements; the envelope consequence is the thermal bridge at each tie (a conductive path that lets heat bypass the insulation).

| Tie material | Thermal conductivity | Effect at typical spacing |
|---|---|---|
| Stainless steel (Type 304 or 316) | 14-16 W/m·K | Roughly 0.5-1 percent point bridging; reduces a 4-inch foam layer from R-26 nominal to about R-23 effective |
| Carbon steel | ~50 W/m·K | Worst bridge; requires coating or galvanizing against corrosion |
| Thermally-broken ties (Halfen FM, Bekaert Murfor THERM, Wagner ThermaTIE) | polymer or basalt break section | Reduces the penalty to roughly 0.1-0.3 percent |
| FRP ties | 0.3-1.0 W/m·K | Lowest bridging, one to two orders of magnitude below steel; structural acceptance requires an ICC ESR and per-project building-official approval |

For ordinary residential targets, stainless ties at the specified frequency are acceptable. For aggressive R-value targets, specify thermally-broken ties, or model the actual tie layout (THERM or equivalent software, typically by the mechanical or envelope engineer) before buying performance the ties will bleed off.

## 14.5 Foam Plastic Protection (R316)

ORSC R316 regulates foam plastic insulation because unprotected foam burns and produces dense smoke. The general rule: foam plastic must be separated from the interior of the building by a thermal barrier, normally 1/2-inch gypsum board or a material tested to NFPA 275.

**Simple**

Foam insulation burns, so the code requires covering it. The normal cover is half-inch drywall between the foam and the living space. Concrete satisfies the same rule. An inch of concrete or masonry on each face of the foam replaces the drywall entirely. That is how a concrete sandwich wall complies with no added layer.

**Applied: R316 in this project's assemblies**

- Mineral wool board is not a foam plastic. No R316 rule applies to it anywhere in the assembly.
- Foam in a masonry or concrete wall, floor, or roof covered on each face by at least 1 inch of masonry or concrete needs no separate thermal barrier. This is the sandwich-wall exception; using it changes the wall's structural classification, so the assembly is engineered.
- For a single-wythe CMU wall with exterior foam and rainscreen cladding, the masonry itself is the barrier toward the interior. The exterior side is not interior space, and the cladding provides the weather-side protection. Confirm the specific assembly with the building official; jurisdictions read the exterior side of R316 differently.
- Foam in an attic or crawl space entered only for service needs an ignition barrier instead of a thermal barrier: 1-1/2-inch mineral fiber, 1/4-inch wood structural panel, 3/8-inch particleboard, 1/4-inch hardboard, 3/8-inch gypsum board, corrosion-resistant steel, or the foam passes the R316.6 testing route. This applies to foam under a concrete roof deck over an unoccupied service space.
- Exterior foam above grade must be protected from sunlight and mechanical damage where it emerges from the ground: a parge coat, metal flashing, or other approved covering over the exposed strip, commonly with a 45-degree bevel cut at the top of the below-grade board. Buried foam needs no UV cover.
- On the prescriptive path with wood-framed floors or roof above the masonry, R316 applies conventionally: 1/2-inch gypsum board between any foam and the interior.

**Code**

## 14.6 Slab Edge And Below-Grade Envelope Interface

The slab edge is a thermal bridge and a water-management detail at the same time. Below-grade walls carry insulation and waterproofing as one integrated assembly.

### 14.6.1 Slab Edge

**Simple**

The edge of a heated concrete slab is a strip of concrete connecting the warm floor directly to the winter air and soil. Uninsulated, that strip conducts heat out of the floor all winter and shows up as a cold floor perimeter and a higher heating bill. A band of rigid foam around the slab edge, reaching 24 inches down, cuts the loss; the code requires it at R-15.

**Applied: slab-edge detail options**

The 2023 ORSC requires slab-edge insulation at F-0.520, R-15 minimum, 24-inch minimum depth. The insulation extends downward from the top of the slab for at least 24 inches, or down to the bottom of the slab and then horizontally until the total path reaches 24 inches. A monolithic slab may run the insulation to the bottom of the thickened edge.

| Configuration | Description |
|---|---|
| Vertical perimeter insulation | Rigid foam (XPS Type IV minimum) on the foundation exterior, 24 inches below grade or to the top of the footing |
| Vertical plus horizontal wing | Vertical board at the wall, then XPS Type VII (60 psi) extending horizontally outward under 6-12 inches of protective gravel or soil |
| Heated slab interior | R-10 rigid foam under the entire slab, in addition to the perimeter insulation |
| Insulated stem wall, combined | Insulation continuous from the above-grade wall, around the stem-wall corner, and down 24 inches below grade |

Where the wall uses continuous exterior insulation, run the slab-edge insulation as a continuation of the wall insulation: down past the foundation top and along the foundation exterior to the prescribed depth. The result is one unbroken thermal layer from roof to below grade, which is the pencil-test condition from the start of this chapter.

### 14.6.2 Below-Grade Wall Interface

Below-grade walls combine several control layers:

| Layer | Position | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation drainage | Outboard of the wall | Collects subsurface water before it loads the wall |
| Waterproofing or dampproofing membrane | On the soil side of the wall | Resists water that reaches the wall |
| Drainage board | Between membrane and backfill | Channels water down to the perimeter drain |
| Below-grade insulation | Outboard of the membrane, or inboard in some assemblies | Thermal control |
| Protection board | Between insulation and rocky backfill | Mechanical protection |

The foundation chapter contains the drainage and waterproofing construction detail, including the R406 dampproofing-versus-waterproofing triggers. The envelope-scope requirement stated here: the insulation, the membrane, and the air-control layer must run continuously from the above-grade wall down to the footing, and the below-grade insulation must be a material rated for wet ground contact (XPS Type IV minimum for typical residential; Type VI at 40 psi under rocky or pressure-loaded backfill; EPS acceptable with slower R-loss accepted in sustained saturation; mineral wool acceptable but lower in compressive strength). Local acceptance of the specific below-grade material varies; confirm it with the building official at plan review.

**Code**

## 14.7 Roof Assembly

The structural chapter states the roof structural options. The envelope side of the same roof is drainage, waterproofing, insulation, vapor control, and continuity at parapets and edges.

### 14.7.1 Concrete Deck With Protected Membrane Roof (PMR)

The recommended roof for a long-life concrete house. The structural deck (8-inch cast-in-place slab, or hollow-core plank with a 2-3-inch topping) slopes at 1/4 inch per foot minimum toward roof drains. The membrane is applied directly to the deck; the insulation sits above the membrane; ballast holds the insulation down. The industry name is the inverted roof.

**Simple**

An ordinary flat roof puts its waterproof membrane on top, where sun, hail, and foot traffic wear it out. A protected membrane roof reverses the order. The waterproofing goes directly on the concrete, the foam on top of the waterproofing, and river rock or pavers on top of the foam. The fragile layer is buried under the durable ones. When the membrane wears out after 30 to 50 years, the rock and foam lift off and a new membrane goes down. The concrete deck underneath is untouched.

**Layer order, top to bottom:**

1. ballast: river rock at 10-20 psf (sized for the wind-uplift check) or concrete pavers on pedestals
2. filter fabric under the ballast
3. rigid insulation: XPS Type VI (40 psi), 4-6 inches (R-20 to R-30 nominal), slip sheet between layers where two layers are used
4. roofing membrane, fully adhered: modified bitumen (SBS or APP, two-ply), EPDM, TPO, or PVC, 60-mil minimum
5. membrane-specific primer
6. structural concrete deck, sloped to drains

The membrane is protected from UV, foot traffic, and impact by everything above it. The deck stays at conditioned temperature because the insulation is outside it. The service-life mismatch between membrane (30-50 years) and deck (multi-century) is deliberate and managed: the wearing layer is the replaceable one.

**Drainage on a flat concrete deck:**

- 1/4 inch per foot minimum slope to drains
- two roof drains minimum per primary drainage area
- overflow scupper (a drainage opening through the parapet or roof edge) or secondary drain, sized for the 100-year storm with the primary drain blocked
- ASCE 7 ponding-instability check on the deck design
- snow load from the OSU/SEAO lookup, 25 psf minimum

**Solar integration:** cast anchor plates into the deck at design time, or use ballasted PV racks on the finished PMR surface. Ballasted racks add no roof penetrations, which preserves the membrane's service life; the added dead load enters the structural design.

### 14.7.2 Concrete Deck With Sloped Wood-Framed False Roof

The second concrete-deck option. The deck carries the structure and the waterproofing membrane; a sloped wood-framed false roof above it (3:12 to 6:12, treated sleepers) carries a metal or slate weather surface. The gap between deck and false roof drains and dries. The arrangement decouples the weather surface from the structure at a higher cost than PMR, and it produces a conventional sloped-roof appearance where that is wanted. Metal panel at 24 gauge runs 50-70 years; natural slate runs 75-150 years and its weight enters the structural design.

### 14.7.3 Wood-Framed Truss Roof On Bond Beam

The prescriptive-path option. Manufacturer-engineered trusses (the manufacturer's stamp carries the engineering) bear on the bond beam at the top of the masonry wall. Standing-seam metal roofing over synthetic underlayment on plywood sheathing; vented attic with R-49 ceiling insulation on the prescriptive energy path.

Roofing surface selection against the long-life goal:

| Surface | Service life | Position |
|---|---|---|
| Standing-seam metal panel, 24 gauge minimum | 50-70 years | The specified surface for this project |
| Natural slate | 75-150 years | Acceptable where the structure is designed for the load; highest cost |
| Concrete tile | 50+ years | Acceptable with structural design for the weight |
| Asphalt shingle | 15-30 years | Rejected; the service life does not fit the design horizon |

### 14.7.4 Roof Penetrations And Parapets

Every penetration (vent, plumbing stack, exhaust, skylight, equipment curb) and every parapet is a control-layer transition.

For PMR roofs, each penetration uses the membrane manufacturer's flashing detail, and the parapet receives continuous membrane up the inside face, over the top, and down the outside face under a metal coping or counter-flashing. Parapet failure modes to detail against: condensation inside the parapet cavity, freeze-thaw at the coping joints, and UV at any exposed membrane termination.

For wood-framed truss roofs, penetrations use sheet-metal flashing lapped with the underlayment. Parapets are uncommon on that assembly.

## 14.8 Windows And Doors

Windows and doors are the most-penetrated part of the envelope. Each opening interrupts all five control layers, carries a structural lintel above it in a masonry wall, and must meet its own thermal, air-leakage, and egress requirements.

**Professional: performance requirements**

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Windows | U-0.27 maximum |
| Skylights | U-0.50 maximum |
| Exterior doors, 2.5 sq ft of glazing or less | U-0.20 maximum |
| Exterior doors, more than 2.5 sq ft of glazing | U-0.40 maximum |
| Window and skylight air leakage | 0.3 cfm per square foot maximum |
| Swinging door air leakage | 0.5 cfm per square foot maximum |

The air-leakage values are tested to NFRC 400 or AAMA/WDMA/CSA 101/I.S.2/A440 by an accredited laboratory and shown on the manufacturer's label; site-built units are exempt from the leakage testing requirement. Water resistance follows the same AAMA/WDMA/CSA standard (the North American Fenestration Standard): select units whose performance grade meets the design pressure for the site's wind exposure. Condensation resistance (NFRC 500) is not code-regulated; use it as a selection criterion for cold-climate sites, where a higher rating indicates more resistance to interior condensation.

**Frame and glazing selection**

The U-0.27 ceiling eliminates most builder-grade single-seal packages. Selection data for the remaining field, with service life as the deciding column for a long-life house; low-e glazing (a thin metallic coating on the glass that reflects radiant heat) is assumed throughout:

| Frame and glazing | Typical U-factor | Typical frame service life | Position |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vinyl frame, double-pane low-e | U-0.25 to U-0.30 | 15-25 years | Lowest cost; units above U-0.27 cannot be installed; shortest life |
| Fiberglass frame, double-pane low-e | U-0.22 to U-0.27 | 40+ years | Strong service-life-per-dollar position |
| Aluminum-clad wood, double-pane low-e | U-0.25 to U-0.27 | 30-50 years | Traditional appearance; wood core needs a dry installation |

| Fiberglass or thermally broken aluminum, triple-pane | U-0.15 to U-0.22 | 40+ years | The specified package for this project: meets the requirement with margin, supplies UA trade-off headroom for the concrete roof, and matches the frame life to the cladding cycle |

**Lintels and sills at masonry openings**

Every opening in a masonry wall carries a lintel: a beam across the opening head that carries the wall above. The ORSC contains no prescriptive lintel table for masonry openings; each lintel is a designed element on the structural drawings.

- Lintel options: precast concrete lintel, a grouted and reinforced U-shaped bond-beam course, or a steel angle. The structural engineer sizes the member per TMS 402 (masonry) or ACI 318 (precast concrete) for the wall dead load plus any floor or roof load entering above the opening.
- Bearing: field practice runs 4-8 inches of bearing each side of the opening; the drawings state the required value.
- Sills: precast sill block or formed sill, sloped to drain outward.
- Flashing at masonry openings: corrosion-resistant flashing at the head and sill, extended to the exterior face with a drainage path; through-wall flashing at any veneer lintel.

**Emergency escape and egress openings**

Basements, habitable attics, and every sleeping room require an operable emergency escape and rescue opening; where a basement contains sleeping rooms, each sleeping room needs one. Minimum net clear opening 5.7 square feet (5.0 square feet at grade level), minimum clear height 24 inches, minimum clear width 20 inches, sill no more than 44 inches above the floor, operable from inside without keys or tools. In a masonry wall, the escape-opening size fixes the rough opening and its lintel early; design these openings before the wall reinforcement layout is final, because enlarging a grouted masonry opening later is demolition.

The dwelling also requires at least one egress door: side-hinged, not less than 32 inches of clear width and 78 inches of clear height, which a nominal 36-inch door leaf provides. Fit exterior doors with full-perimeter weatherstripping and a sill sweep; specify insulated fiberglass or foam-core steel slabs at U-0.20 or better.

**Applied: rough-opening sequence, continuous-exterior-insulation wall**

The window rough opening is the highest-failure-rate envelope detail. The sequence integrates all five control layers:

1. Complete the structural wall with the rough opening formed, lintel in place.
2. Prime the rough-opening surfaces for the flashing membrane.
3. Apply the sill pan flashing (peel-and-stick): pan across the sill, turned up 6 inches at the jambs, with a 4-inch leg down the wall face.
4. Apply jamb flashing, lapping over the sill flashing.
5. Install the WRB on the wall plane, lapped shingle-fashion at the head.
6. Set the window per the manufacturer's instructions: shimmed, plumb, square; backer rod and sealant at jambs and head; leave the sill unsealed so incidental water drains out.
7. Apply head flashing over the window flange, continuous with the WRB above, draining outboard.
8. Fit the exterior insulation to the frame.
9. Install the rainscreen cladding and trim.
10. Interior side: seal the frame-to-rough-opening gap with low-expansion foam or backer rod and sealant, maintaining air-control-layer continuity at the interior face.

Photograph each opening at steps 3, 5, and 7 before the next layer covers the work; the photos join the pre-cover record.

## 14.9 Above-Grade Wall Assembly: Drainage And Air Control

The above-grade wall manages bulk water, air, vapor, and heat in one assembly. The controlling design move is the rainscreen: an air gap between the cladding and the WRB that drains water and dries the assembly.

**Professional: drained and back-ventilated rainscreen**

| Layer | Position | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Cladding | Outermost | Primary weather surface; sheds most bulk water |
| Rainscreen gap | Between cladding and WRB | Drainage and drying ventilation; 3/4 inch typical minimum |
| WRB | On the insulation or sheathing face | Secondary water barrier for water that passes the cladding |
| Rigid exterior insulation | On the structural wall | Continuous thermal layer |
| Air-control layer | The WRB or a dedicated membrane on the structural wall | Continuous air barrier |
| Structural wall (CMU or concrete) | Inboard of the insulation | Load-bearing; thermal mass at conditioned temperature |
| Vapor control | Inherent in the assembly | Concrete and masonry are low-permeance; drying paths run inward and outward through the rainscreen gap |
| Interior finish | Innermost | Finish and protection of the wall face |

## 14.10 Vapor Control

Vapor control fails in two directions: an assembly with no vapor plan condenses, and an assembly wrapped in polyethylene on the wrong side traps water it can never dry out. The working rule for this project: design every assembly to dry to at least one side, and place a low-permeance layer only where the assembly analysis calls for it.

**Professional: vapor retarder classes**

| Class | Permeance | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Class I | 0.1 perm or less | Polyethylene sheet, sheet metal, aluminum foil facing |
| Class II | over 0.1 to 1.0 perm | Kraft-faced batts, vapor-retarder paint, some smart membranes |
| Class III | over 1.0 to 10 perms | Latex paint on drywall, many fibrous sheathings |

Permeance (the rate at which water vapor passes through a material) is measured by the ASTM E96 water-vapor transmission test; one perm is one grain of water vapor per hour, per square foot, per inch of mercury pressure difference.

In Oregon's climate zones (4C marine, 5B continental), framed walls take a Class I or II vapor retarder on the interior side, with Class III (painted drywall) permitted where the code's continuous-exterior-insulation conditions are met. A Class I polyethylene layer on the interior of a wall that also has low-permeance exterior foam produces a wall that cannot dry in either direction; no assembly in this design combines the two.

For the concrete and masonry walls of this project, vapor control is inherent in the assembly: the masonry is low-permeance, the exterior insulation and rainscreen dry outward, and painted drywall or a parged interior face provides Class III behavior inward. No interior polyethylene appears anywhere in this design.

For the wood-framed assemblies of the prescriptive path (roof, and any framed service walls against the envelope), conventional rules apply: Class I or II interior retarder, or Class III where the exterior continuous insulation meets the table values for the climate zone.

At the roof, the fully adhered PMR membrane is itself a Class I layer on the deck's exterior side, with the insulation outboard of it; the deck stays warm, the condensation plane stays outside the membrane, and no separate interior vapor retarder is added. For insulation placed under a concrete deck instead (the cold-deck configuration), the interior face of that insulation must be the vapor control layer: closed-cell spray foam serves directly, and open-cell or fibrous insulation requires a separate retarder. Any cold-deck assembly is analyzed against ASHRAE 160 moisture criteria before it enters the drawings.

Indoor humidity is the other half of every vapor calculation; the HVAC chapter contains the humidity-control design (40-60 percent relative humidity operating range).

## 14.11 Radon Control

Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas produced by uranium decay in soil and rock. It enters buildings through cracks, joints, and openings in the slab and below-grade walls, and it accumulates in enclosed lower levels. The EPA attributes more lung-cancer deaths to radon than to any cause except smoking, and it recommends action when indoor air exceeds 4 picocuries per liter (pCi/L).

**Simple**

Radon is an invisible radioactive gas that seeps out of some ground. Outdoors it disperses instantly; under a sealed house it collects. The protection is built into the foundation for $300-$800 (2026). A gravel layer under the slab collects the gas, and a sealed plastic sheet blocks it. A pipe running from the gravel up through the roof vents it to open air. After move-in, a $30 test shows whether the passive system is enough. If the number is high, a small fan added to the pipe pulls the gas out mechanically.

**Where the system is mandatory**

ORSC Appendix AF requires radon-resistant new construction in seven counties: Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, and Yamhill. The county list is set at the state level under ORS 455.365; the boards expand it in consultation with the Oregon Health Authority, and a county cannot opt itself in or out. Applicability is binary by county: inside the seven, Appendix AF applies to the parcel regardless of site geology; outside them, it is voluntary.

For a project outside the mandated counties, build the passive system anyway. The marginal cost at construction is $300-$800 (2026) for materials and labor already sequenced into the slab work; the same system retrofitted after occupancy costs $1,500-$5,000 or more (2026), and the sub-slab gravel layer doubles as the slab's capillary break in any case.

**Applied: the Appendix AF passive system**

The components integrate with the slab stack specified in the foundation chapter:

| Component | Specification |
|---|---|
| Gas-permeable layer | 4 inches minimum of clean #57 stone or equivalent under the slab (AF103.5) |
| Soil-gas retarder | 6-mil polyethylene over the gas-permeable layer, or one 15-mil ASTM E1745 Class A membrane serving as both vapor retarder and soil-gas retarder (AF103.3) |
| Vent pipe | 3-inch gas-tight ABS or PVC, embedded vertically in the aggregate before the slab is cast, with a T-fitting keeping the opening in the permeable layer (AF103.6.1) |
| Vent routing | Vertical through the slab and conditioned space (chase or interior wall), through the roof; termination 12 inches above the roof surface and 10 feet from any operable opening |
| Joint sealing | Every slab joint, control joint, and isolation joint sealed with polyurethane or elastomeric sealant (AF103.4.2); every slab penetration sealed (AF103.4.1) |
| Sumps | Gasketed, tight-fitting covers (AF103.4.4) |
| Hollow-block foundation walls | One continuous solid or grouted course at or above grade (AF103.4.5) |
| Future-fan provision | Vent pipe accessible in the attic (AF103.8) with an electrical circuit terminated there for a fan (AF103.12) |

The system is passive at completion: no fan, no moving parts, no operating cost. Stack effect (the buoyancy of warmer air rising in the pipe) pulls soil gas up and out. If post-occupancy testing shows elevated radon, an in-line fan is added at the prepared attic location, converting the system to active subslab depressurization. Activation on a prepared system costs $300-$1,500 (2026) for the fan, wiring, and a few hours of work; passive systems reduce indoor radon 50-90 percent, and activated systems 90-99 percent.

**Applied: testing after occupancy**

| Test | Cost (2026) | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Charcoal canister, 2-7 days | $15-$30 | Fast screening; mail to a laboratory |
| Alpha-track detector, 3-12 months | $30-$60 | The accuracy benchmark; captures seasonal variation |
| Continuous radon monitor | $150-$300 | Real-time and long-term data; Airthings Wave Plus and RadonEye are current examples |
| Professional measurement | $150-$400 | Certified measurement where a transaction or dispute needs one |

The testing protocol for this project: run a long-term test (90 days or more) in the lowest occupied level during the first heating season, keep a continuous monitor running in the same location permanently, re-test after any slab or foundation alteration, and activate the fan if results exceed the action level. The EPA action level is 4 pCi/L; the new-construction standard treats 2 pCi/L as the consider-activation threshold, and at an activation cost of $300-$1,500 (2026) this project activates at the lower number. Record every result in the project binder with date, device, and location.

**Professional: the standards behind the code**

ORSC Appendix AF is the binding construction requirement in the mandated counties; it specifies the components directly and references no outside standard. Two ANSI/AARST standards cover the same ground nationally and extend it: RRNC-2020 (Rev. 10/22) states minimum rough-in requirements for new dwellings, and CCAH-2020 (Rev. 5/23) adds the verification testing and fan-activation protocol. Cite RRNC for what gets built; cite CCAH for how the result is verified and activated. The EPA Radon Standards of Practice recognize both. For a project outside the mandated counties, "built to ANSI/AARST RRNC, verified per CCAH" is the strongest voluntary position and costs nothing beyond the components above.

## 14.12 Wildfire Hardening Where A Jurisdiction Adopts It

Oregon has no statewide wildfire-hardening mandate and no statewide hazard map; the 2025 legislature repealed both. What remains is ORSC R327, a complete hardening section available for local adoption: a municipality that runs a building-inspection program may adopt R327 by ordinance for designated areas, in whole and unmodified, applying to new construction only. Whether R327 applies to a parcel is a question only the local building department can answer, and the land chapter contains the written-verification procedure. As of July 2026 five jurisdictions have adopted it (Ashland, Bend, Deschutes County, Grants Pass, Sisters). The adopting-jurisdiction list changes over time; the applicant confirms the parcel's current status with the local building department at permit application.

**Simple**

In a wildfire, most houses ignite from embers, not from the flame front. Embers land in gutters, blow into attic vents, and collect against decks and siding. Some Oregon cities and counties now require new homes in designated areas to resist ember attack. A concrete house satisfies the wall requirement before the rule even applies, because concrete does not burn. What remains is the roof rating, the vent screens, the deck surface, and the glass.

**Professional: R327 construction requirements**

| Gutters and downspouts | Noncombustible, with an approved means of preventing leaf and debris accumulation (R327.3.1) |
| Vents | Attic, soffit, rafter, and underfloor vents covered with corrosion-resistant metal mesh, openings 1/16 to 1/8 inch, or listed ember-and-flame-resistant vent devices; no vents in the underside of eaves, soffits, or cornices except listed devices or openings at least 12 feet above grade (R327.3.2) |
| Exterior walls | Noncombustible material, ignition-resistant material, heavy timber, log wall, or an ASTM E2707-tested assembly; 5/8-inch Type X exterior gypsum behind the cladding is a deemed-to-comply alternative; the protection runs from foundation to roof (R327.3.3) |
| Overhangs and projections | Eave and soffit undersides below 12 feet, large porch ceilings, cantilevered floors, and exposed elevated underfloors protected to the same material options (R327.3.4) |
| Walking surfaces | Deck, porch, and balcony surfaces between 30 inches and 12 feet above grade of tested, ignition-resistant, fire-retardant-treated, or noncombustible materials; decks of 200 square feet or less built of nominal 2-inch lumber are excepted (R327.3.5) |
| Glazing | Exterior windows, door glazing, and skylights of tempered glass, multilayered glazed panels, glass block, or 20-minute fire-rated assemblies (R327.3.6) |

For this project, the concrete or masonry wall satisfies the exterior-wall requirement outright as a noncombustible material, and the specified 24-gauge standing-seam metal roof exceeds Class B. The cost-relevant items where R327 applies are the vent devices, the eave and projection protection, the deck surface, and tempered glazing.

Outside any adopted area, the same details are available as voluntary hardening, and most cost little when selected at design time: the metal roof and concrete walls are already specified, ember-resistant vents price within $100-$300 (2026) of ordinary vents for a whole house, and tempered glazing is the one meaningful premium, priced per opening. A parcel that is outside an adopted area today can be inside one after a single council vote; hardening chosen now does not depend on that vote.

**Code**

```

  Adoption is by local ordinance, in whole and unmodified, for designated areas, new construction only; the ordinance identifies the areas, includes a subdivision transition plan and an appeal process, and the municipality notifies BCD.
```

## 14.13 Envelope Testing

Envelope testing verifies the work while correction still means caulk and tape instead of demolition, then documents the final performance. The full project testing program, coordinated across all systems, is in the testing and commissioning chapter; the envelope-specific tests are stated here because their timing is bound to the envelope construction sequence.

**Professional: envelope test types and timing**

| Test | When | Standard | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blower door, initial | After insulation and air sealing, before drywall | ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380 | Find leaks while the air barrier is exposed and repairable |
| Blower door, final | After all finishes | ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380 | Compliance documentation: 3.25 ACH50 tested path, or 2.75 ACH50 third-party for Measure 7 |
| Duct leakage | Rough and final, where ducts sit outside the conditioned envelope | ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380 or ASTM E1554 | Duct-sealing compliance; unnecessary when all ducts are inside conditioned space |
| Infrared thermography | After insulation, with an indoor-outdoor temperature difference of 18 °F or more | RESNET thermography guidance | Locate missing insulation, thermal bridges, and air leaks across whole surfaces |
| Water-spray test at windows | After window installation, before insulation and cladding cover | AAMA 501.2; ASTM E1105 | Find water entry at the highest-failure-rate detail while the flashing is exposed |
| Smoke pencil | During blower-door depressurization | field practice | Trace individual leak locations |
| Slab moisture | After cure, before floor coverings | ASTM F2170 (in-situ RH probes) or ASTM F1869 (calcium chloride) | Confirm the slab is dry enough for the specified flooring |

Equipment and service costs (2026): hired blower-door test $300-$600; blower-door rental $150-$300 per day; smoke pencil $20-$40; thermal camera $400-$1,500 to own, with phone-attached units at the low end covering this work.

**Applied: the envelope testing program for this project**

1. **Pre-drywall blower door with smoke pencil.** The highest-value test on the project: with the air barrier exposed, every leak found is sealable by hand. Target 2.0 ACH50 or less at this stage, so the final test lands under the 2.75 Measure 7 threshold after finishes.
2. **Infrared scan after insulation.** Scan every wall and ceiling surface with the required temperature difference; photograph and correct any void, compression, or bridge.
3. **Water-spray test at each window.** 15 minutes or more of spray per AAMA 501.2; watch the interior of the frame, sill, and rough opening. Repair flashing before anything covers it.
4. **Final blower door.** Third-party for Measure 7; the report goes to the building official before the certificate of occupancy.
5. **Post-occupancy radon test.** Long-term test in the lowest occupied level, per the radon section of this chapter.

Every result enters the project binder: test, date, operator, instrument, reading, and the correction made where one was needed.

**Code**

```
Enclosure airtightness testing follows one standard.
  Test configuration, instrument calibration, and reporting for blower-door measurement.
  Source: ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380.

Window field water testing has its own methods.
  Field quality-assurance water check (AAMA 501.2) and water-penetration field test for installed fenestration (ASTM E1105).
  Source: AAMA 501.2; ASTM E1105.

  Source: ASTM E1554; ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380.
```

## 14.14 Ventilation Coordination

A tight envelope and a ventilation system are one design, split across two chapters. The envelope eliminates uncontrolled air exchange; the mechanical system supplies controlled fresh air at the designed rate. The HVAC chapter contains the ventilation selection, sizing, and commissioning; the coordination points where the two designs meet are stated here.

| Item | Envelope side | Mechanical side |
|---|---|---|
| Whole-house ventilation rate | Measured airtightness sets the uncontrolled baseline | Ventilation sized to ASHRAE 62.2 through ORSC M1507 |
| HRV intake and exhaust | Sealed penetration details; locations that keep intake away from exhaust and both away from operable windows | Equipment efficiency, flow balance, distribution |
| Combustion appliances | A tight envelope does not supply enough infiltration air for atmospheric venting | Sealed-combustion equipment throughout; no atmospherically vented appliance in this design |
| Bath, kitchen, and dryer exhaust | Sealed penetrations with dampers | Fan sizing, makeup air, controls |
| Humidity | Vapor-layer integrity at slab and walls | Whole-house humidity control to the 40-60 percent design range |

For this project: envelope specified to the Measure 7 threshold (2.75 ACH50 or less), HRV at 66 percent sensible recovery or better, all combustion equipment sealed-combustion, every mechanical penetration through the envelope detailed and photographed before cover.

**Code**

```
Whole-house mechanical ventilation is required and sized by standard.
  Dwelling ventilation per the mechanical ventilation section, with rates per the residential ventilation standard.
  Source: ORSC M1507; ASHRAE 62.2.

Combustion air rules govern any atmospherically vented appliance.
  Combustion-air supply per the fuel-gas provisions; sealed-combustion equipment avoids the requirement entirely.
  Source: ORSC Chapter 24 (fuel gas); ORSC G2407 provisions.

Comfort and IAQ reference standards.
  Thermal comfort conditions, and the voluntary indoor-air-quality construction specification.
  Source: ASHRAE 55; EPA Indoor airPLUS.
```

## 14.15 Pre-Cover Acceptance And Records

Envelope work is layered, and each layer hides the one before it. Once cladding is on, the WRB is invisible; once drywall is up, the air barrier is invisible; once ballast is down, the roof membrane is invisible. The record of each layer is created in the interval between its completion and its covering; after covering, the work cannot be photographed.

**Applied: pre-cover verification points**

| Stage | Verify and record | Covered by |
|---|---|---|
| Below-grade waterproofing | Membrane continuity, terminations, sealed penetrations; drainage board; perimeter drain | Backfill |
| Slab stack | Subgrade, capillary break, vapor retarder laps and seals, soil-gas retarder, under-slab insulation, radon piping, sub-slab utilities | Concrete slab |
| Air-control layer | Continuity around the full cross-section; sealed penetrations; transition details | Drywall and finishes |
| WRB | Laps, seams, terminations; integration with window flashing | Insulation and rainscreen |
| Window installations | Pan, jamb, and head flashing per the sequence in this chapter; sealant joints | Trim and cladding |
| Continuous exterior insulation | Thickness, staggered joints, attachment, fit at openings | Rainscreen and cladding |
| Roof membrane | Continuity, parapet and penetration terminations, drain detailing | Insulation and ballast, or covering |
| Cavity and ceiling insulation | Coverage, depth, no gaps or compression | Drywall, ceiling finish |

**Records**

For each stage, the project binder receives:

- date and weather at installation
- products installed: manufacturer, product name, batch numbers where available, cut sheets
- photographs at each stage with a reference scale
- the inspection record where a formal inspection applies
- test results from that stage
- any deviation from the approved drawings, with the reason and the as-built note

The documentation chapter contains the long-term archive structure. The envelope records stay with the building for its life; they are the evidence base for repairs, alterations, insurance, warranty claims, and resale.

```
  Source: ORSC R110; OAR 918-480-0140(3).
```
