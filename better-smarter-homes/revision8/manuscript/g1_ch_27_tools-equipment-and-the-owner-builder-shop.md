# Chapter 27: Tools, Equipment, and the Owner-Builder Shop

Buying a tool for this house pays when the days it would otherwise be rented, counted across the whole multi-year build, cost more than its price. For mid-size tools that line falls between 5 and 25 rental days. Excavators, loaders, concrete pumps and lifts work too few days to reach it and come by the day or with an operator. Hand tools, the mixer, the plate compactor and the scaffold pass it and are bought. Every tool that cuts, drills or grinds concrete or masonry comes with the dust control the silica rule lists for it.

## 27.1 Owning, renting and hiring

**Simple**

Dividing a tool's price by its rent gives the number of rental days that would pay for it. At an Oregon hardware store a plate compactor rents for $110 a day, and a new one costs $1,900 to $2,400. It pays for itself after 17 to 22 days of work. Mini excavators work a few weeks in a build that runs for years. Used name-brand machines cost $30,000 to $60,000, so the excavator comes by the day. Renting by the week costs three days' rent. Owning adds storage, upkeep and insurance for the months a tool waits for its next phase.

**Applied**

1. List every tool and machine the build needs, by phase, with the days each one works.
2. Get each yard's day, week and month rate, the deposit and the delivery charge to the parcel.
3. Divide the purchase price by the rate for the period the job uses. That number is the payback in days, weeks or months of rent.
4. Add fuel care, storage, insurance and repairs through the idle months to the price.
5. Buy when the tool's working days across the build pass its payback. Rent when they fall short.
6. Hire the concrete pump, crane lifts and large excavation with an operator.
7. Buy small gas engines, battery tools and bare steel tools shortly before the phase that uses them.
8. Buy tools that lose nothing while idle (scaffold frames, a rebar bender, protective gear) whenever a good price appears.
9. Log every rental with the tool, the dates, the days and the cost. Rental logs show when a tool has passed its payback.

**Professional**

Prices and rental rates are 2026 figures unless a different year is given. Oregon yards price by the day, the week and the month, and the longer periods cost less per day. Hometown Hardware in Myrtle Point charges 2.8 to 2.9 days' rent for a week on its skid steer, excavator and plate compactor. Its month rates equal 7 to 9 days' rent. Elite Equipment Rental in Lebanon lists a 4-ton mini excavator on the DOZR marketplace at three days' rent for a week and nine for four weeks. Myrtle Point's deposits run $15 to $1,000 and come back with the tool. Delivery to a rural address is quoted separately.

Payback is the purchase price divided by the rental rate for the period the work uses. Bought for $1,900 to $2,400, a forward plate compactor pays back after 17 to 22 single days. At Myrtle Point's $780 monthly rate it pays back in 2.4 to 3.1 months. This build's own phase schedule in the build-sequence plan supplies the day count, not a contractor's utilization rate.

Ownership carries costs the rental rate already covers. Ethanol fuel gums the carburetor of a small engine that sits for weeks. Lithium packs stored full or hot lose capacity, and bare steel rusts through an Oregon wet season. Buying those tools shortly before their phase shortens the idle months. Waiting has its own risk, a model discontinued or repriced before its phase arrives. New Holland opened orders for its electric E25X excavator in the second quarter of 2026 and left the price to dealer quotes. Every price in the kit is re-quoted at purchase.

Concrete pumping, crane lifts and large excavation are hired with an operator, whose employer carries the training, certification and workers' compensation for the machine. Manufacturer fleet programs (Milwaukee ONE-KEY, DeWalt Site Manager) track tools across many crews and trucks. One owner keeps one inventory in one shop, and the programs price only by fleet-sales quote.

## 27.2 Rental yards, used machines, tool libraries and one battery platform

**Simple**

Rural Oregon hardware stores and rental yards post day, week and month prices for the common machines. Bought machines come new, used from a dealer or a private seller, or from an auction. Auctions sell as-is, with no warranty and often no chance to start the engine first. Free tool libraries lend tools in Portland, Eugene and Hillsboro, a long drive from most rural land. Cordless tools run only on their own maker's batteries. One battery brand, chosen before the first purchase, lets every later tool share the same batteries and chargers.

**Applied**

1. Call the two or three nearest yards for day, week and month rates, deposits and delivery to the parcel.
2. Ask each yard whether it delivers and picks up machines, and the charge per trip.
3. Buy a small machine (plate compactor, mixer) from a local dealer or a private seller.
4. Before a private purchase, read the hour meter and the maintenance records.
5. Watch a cold start before money changes hands.
6. Pay an independent mechanic to inspect any used machine priced in the thousands.
7. Bid at an online auction only on a machine you or a hired inspector have seen, with transport arranged before the bid.
8. Pick one cordless battery platform before buying the first cordless tool.
9. Register each tool's warranty on the day of purchase. File the receipt, the serial number and a photo.

**Professional**

Hometown Hardware in Myrtle Point posts 63 rental lines with day, week, month and deposit columns. They run from a skid steer and an excavator to a bull float and a powder-actuated fastener. DOZR, a rental marketplace, lists mini excavators from Elite Equipment Rental in Lebanon, Interstate Rentals in Portland and Dura Built Construction in Salem, at prices that move with availability. Rent-All in Salem, Massachusetts posts rates for the machines those two sources leave out: the concrete-finishing machines, core drills, generators and lifts. Its figures stand as planning numbers until an Oregon yard quotes.

Used machines come through three channels, which differ in price, inspection, warranty and transport:

Auction savings outrun freight and inspection costs only on large machines with a wide price spread. Shanding's SD17E, a new entry-level import mini excavator, lists at $11,000. Used Bobcats list at $32,500 for a 2017 E50 and $59,900 for a 2023 E42R2. On a compactor, a mixer, a laser or a scaffold set, freight and a formal inspection consume most of what an auction saves.

Oregon's tool libraries serve city and county residents. Portland has seven independent libraries, among them the Rockwood Tool Library for East Multnomah County and the North Portland Tool Library. Multnomah County, Clackamas County (through LINCC) and Hillsboro run Library of Things collections in their public libraries, and Eugene's volunteer ToolBox Project lends to Lane County residents. In Bend, two residents proposed a lending library, as Central Oregon Daily reported. Neighboring owner-builders and homesteaders can run the same model as a tool swap among themselves.

DeWalt's FlexVolt battery switches between 20 and 60 volts by the tool it sits in, so one battery and charger serve both standard and high-draw tools. Milwaukee's MX FUEL line runs on a 72-volt battery and charger separate from its M18 platform. Owning both standard and equipment-class Milwaukee tools therefore means two battery systems (Pro Tool Reviews, ToolGuyd). Milwaukee backs its tools for five years, against DeWalt's three years plus a one-year service agreement. BuildToolHQ singles out Hilti's service guarantee among the rotary-hammer makers. For drills, drivers, saws and grinders either platform serves, and the choice turns on warranty and tool range.

## 27.3 Layout and leveling instruments

**Simple**

One rotary laser level with a receiver and a tripod handles the elevation work of the whole build. It spins a level beam around the site. Clipped to a measuring rod, a receiver beeps when it meets the beam. One person alone can then read footing depths, slab heights and the driveway grade. Roof slopes and the plumb of a masonry corner come from other tools: a framing square, a 4-foot level and a stretched line. Phone level apps give a quick go-or-no-go reading. Footings and block courses are set with the laser and a real level.

**Applied**

1. Buy a self-leveling rotary laser kit (laser, receiver, tripod, grade rod) before the first excavation.
2. Set a benchmark on a fixed object at a known elevation, and note it on the site plan.
3. Set the tripod on firm ground outside the dig, where machines will not bump it.
4. Check the laser against the benchmark at the start of each work day, and log the reading.
5. Write the target rod reading for each footing bottom, slab top and grade point before the machine starts.
6. Hire a registered professional land surveyor to locate property lines and corners.
7. Confirm any phone-app reading with the laser or a 4-foot level before it sets anything.

**Professional**

Self-leveling rotary lasers with a matched receiver cover a working radius of 1,000 to 2,000 feet or more. That reaches the driveway and the drainage swales of a large parcel without a second instrument. Bosch's GRL 800-20 HVK lists at $719. Topcon's RL-H5A kit (LS-80X receiver, tripod and grade rod) sits in the mid-range, and its dual-grade RL-HV2S packages run $2,100 to $2,600. VEVOR sells a consumer kit with tripod, receiver and remote for $240, a budget tier below construction grade.

Total stations measure and log horizontal and vertical coordinates for multi-point surveys of subdivisions, large commercial sites and topography. One house's layout takes only a level plane, a handful of corner and elevation points and a driveway grade, all within a rotary laser's reach. Locating or restoring a property line is land surveying, which ORS 672.025 limits to registered professional land surveyors whatever instrument the owner holds.

Roof slopes are laid out with a framing square and the pitch from the drawings. Masonry corners are held plumb with the mason's level, the line and the story pole from the masonry kit. Phone apps read the handset's accelerometer, and camera-based measuring reads its LiDAR sensor, and both serve as a field estimate away from the real instruments.

## 27.4 Earthwork and compaction machines

**Simple**

Two rented machines do the digging and moving for the few weeks of earthwork, a mini excavator and a compact loader. The excavator digs footings, trenches and the septic field. The loader, often called a skid steer, spreads gravel and carries pallets on forks. Plate compactors pack the ground in small jobs spread over years: footing and utility trenches, slab bases, the driveway and the block-yard pad. Together those jobs pass the compactor's price, so it is bought. Before any machine digs, the owners of buried power, gas and phone lines mark them on the ground.

**Applied**

1. Notify the Oregon Utility Notification Center by dialing 811 two to ten business days before digging.
2. Keep buckets and trencher teeth clear of the locate marks.
3. Count the earthwork days for footings, trenches, the septic field and pads from the site plan.
4. Rent the excavator and the loader for the same weeks where their tasks overlap.
5. Price a task at the week rate once it runs three days or more.
6. Rent a standalone trencher for a trench-only day.
7. Add a trencher attachment to the loader when the loader is already on site.
8. Buy a forward plate compactor when the compaction days across the build pass 17 to 22.
9. Rent a reversible plate for the driveway base and the main slab base.
10. Compact each lift to the thickness and density the soil specification sets, and log the area and the lift.

**Professional**

Excavators dig footings, trenches and drainfield lines with vertical faces. Loaders spread base rock, level pads and handle pallets and brush through interchangeable attachments: forks, an auger, a trencher and a grapple. If one earthmoving machine is ever owned, the loader covers more tasks per dollar, because its attachments multiply its uses. Attachments are worth owning only with an owned base machine. Forks come closest, since pallets move in nearly every phase.

Two battery-electric mini excavators are on the market in 2026. Volt's EV-X2 (2 tons, 28.8 kWh, 6 to 10 hours of runtime) starts at $29,500, and no yard lists it for rent. New Holland's E25X (2.5 tons, 5,445 pounds, 32.2 kWh at 102 volts) runs 4 to 8 hours and takes about 9 hours to charge fully, or 5 hours from 20 to 80 percent. Reversible plates deliver more centrifugal force and travel both directions without turning at the end of a pass. Vendor guidance from Contractors Direct and Diamond Tool Store rates them at 12 to 16 inches of granular fill per pass, against 6 to 8 inches for a forward plate of similar size. Footing subgrades and trench backfill are small areas a forward plate handles. Lift thickness and target density for each plate come from the soil specification.

A trencher attachment adds $135 a day to a loader already on site, and a standalone trencher rents at $250 a day. On a day the loader works anyway the attachment costs less, and on a trench-only day the standalone machine does.

## 27.5 Power, air and water before the house has service

**Simple**

Before the house has power, a portable generator runs the corded tools, the mixer and the work lights. One person using one tool at a time needs a generator of 3,600 watts. Crews running several tools, a compressor and lights need 6,000 to 8,000. This generator is a construction tool. Backup power for the home is a separate machine. It is sized to run the well pump, the heat pump and the freezer through a long outage. Water from wet saws and tool washing carries cement and stone dust and is strongly alkaline. It stays out of the septic field, the well area and the greywater system.

**Applied**

1. List the tools that will run at the same moment, with each one's running watts.
2. Add starting watts at twice the running watts for drills and circular saws, and three times for table saws, cutoff saws, compressors and pumps.
3. Add a 20 to 30 percent margin to the total.
4. Choose a generator whose continuous rating carries the total at no more than 70 to 80 percent.
5. Read the continuous rating on the label, and ignore the peak figure.
6. Rent a 6 kW or 12 kW generator for the days the mixer, saws and lights run together.
7. Plug every corded tool through a GFCI outlet or a GFCI cord set.
8. Run the generator outdoors, clear of doors, windows and vents.
9. Rent or buy a compressor that delivers at least 3.5 cfm at 90 psi.
10. Dig the lined washout pit before the first wet cut or mixer rinse.
11. Send all saw water, rinse water and tool-wash water to that pit.

**Professional**

Generator labels give a continuous (rated) wattage and a larger peak or surge figure, and sizing to the peak is the common error. Motor starting current sets the surge, and capacitor-start motors in saws, compressors and pumps draw the most. BigRentz and ToolGrit publish the sizing tiers and margins. Rent-All in Salem, Massachusetts lists 2,500-watt, 6 kW and 12 kW generators at $65, $90 and $150 a day.

Many block presses and pan mixers run on three-phase power, which single-phase portable generators in these sizes do not supply. On the shop's single-phase service, a 10-horsepower rotary phase converter at $1,500 to $2,000 runs that equipment. Utility three-phase drops cost $15,000 to $75,000 or more. Both belong to the shop's electrical service, planned with the site development.

Standby power for the home is sized to the permanent loads (the well pump, the heat pump, refrigeration and lighting) for days of continuous running, and it ties into the solar and battery system. Neither machine substitutes for the other. Tool-battery power stations such as Milwaukee's MX FUEL Carry-On run lights, phones and a fan from tool batteries during a short outage. Their capacity is too small to offset any part of the home's backup budget.

Hole cleaning for adhesive anchors and blowing down forms are low-flow tasks. ICC-ES report ESR-3814 for Hilti HIT-RE 500 V3 specifies air at 90 psi and 6 cubic meters an hour for holes up to 1-1/8 inches. Myrtle Point rents a compressor at $30 a day and $140 a month. On the Massachusetts list an 8 cfm compressor costs $60 a day and a 185 cfm unit $193, a size for pneumatic breakers this kit does not include.

Wet-cutting water and tool rinse water carry cement fines, rock dust and silt at high pH. Water controls under the silica rule suppress airborne dust and do nothing for the discharge. Those fines clog a drainfield and a greywater filter. The lined washout pit the Foundation chapter specifies for truck washout takes the mixer rinse, the saw water and the tool wash as well. It sits away from the drainfield, the well and any greywater line.

## 27.6 Concrete and reinforcing tools

**Simple**

Most concrete arrives by truck, and the owner's tools place, level and finish it. Hand floats and trowels finish small pours, and a rented power trowel finishes the big slab. Mortar, grout and small batches come from an owned mixer, which works in almost every phase. Paddle mixers push blades through the mix and handle mortar and the stiff block mix. Drum mixers tumble the mix and leave block mix in an unmixed lump. Reinforcing bars up to five-eighths of an inch are cut and bent by hand on a lever tool.

**Applied**

1. Order ready-mix by truck for the footings, walls and slabs.
2. Mix a test batch of the block mix in a paddle (mortar) mixer before buying one.
3. Buy the paddle mixer if the test batch mixes evenly. Buy a pan mixer if it rides in a lump.
4. Rent a drum mixer for small concrete batches, or buy a stationary one.
5. After every batch, run the drum with a scoop of coarse gravel and water while it is still wet.
6. Buy a dual-wheel poly-tray wheelbarrow for wet concrete and mortar.
7. Buy a second, steel-tray wheelbarrow for rock, block and scrap.
8. Buy a hand-finishing kit with a steel finishing trowel, a magnesium float, an edger and a groover.
9. Pay for quality in the steel trowel and the magnesium float. Buy the edger, groover and margin trowel at hardware-store grade.
10. Rent a power trowel for any slab too large to finish by hand before the concrete sets.
11. Rent a power screed with self-pickup for the main slab, and pull a straightedge and bull float on room-size pours.
12. Rent a walk-behind floor saw with its water feed for the control joints.
13. Buy a manual cutter-bender rated for #5 Grade 60 bar.
14. Order #6 and larger bar cut and bent by the supplier, or rent a hydraulic bender for those bars.
15. Check the field test kit's verification dates before each pour season.

**Professional**

| Tow-behind drum | 6 to 9 cu ft | $1,500 to $4,000 new, $800 to $2,500 used | Grout, small slabs, footings under 5 cubic yards a pour |

Paddle and pan mixers force blades through the mix, the only action that blends a zero-slump block mix. Drum mixers lift and drop the mix by gravity, and a stiff mix rides the drum unmixed. One paddle mixer therefore serves the mortar for the masonry campaign, the grout and the block line. Myrtle Point rents its larger cement mixer at $65 a day, $145 a week and $310 a month, so a stationary drum mixer at $300 to $1,500 pays back in 5 to 23 rental days. On the Massachusetts list a 6-cubic-foot gas mixer rents for $125 a day and an electric one for $75.

### Placing, finishing and saw-cutting

Dual-wheel poly barrows hold steady on a sloped, muddy site, and their trays neither rust nor corrode under wet cement. True Temper's 8-cubic-foot dual-wheel poly barrow costs $160 to $190, and its 6-cubic-foot steel single-wheel barrow $145. Heavier barrows from A.M. Leonard cost $311 with a steel tray and flat-free tire and $260 with a poly tray. Keeping a second barrow for rock, soil, block and scrap keeps the concrete barrow clean.

Marshalltown's CTK2 apprentice kit holds six finishing tools in a canvas bag for $220, among them a 14-by-4-inch steel trowel and a 16-by-3-1/8-inch magnesium float. Grade matters most in the steel trowel, where a thin, tempered blade works the surface and resists denting and rust. It matters next in the float, where genuine magnesium glides over fresh concrete. Edgers, groovers and margin trowels are simple shaped steel and perform at hardware-store grade.

On a large slab poured in one day, the concrete sets before hand finishing can cover it. Power troweling is therefore the default for the main slab. The power trowel, the power screed and the walk-behind floor saw rent for $90, $100 and $125 a day on the Massachusetts list, the screed with self-pickup. Chief Rental in Tennessee lists 36- and 48-inch trowels at $95 and $105. Where a yard delivers and collects, a power screed runs up to $550 a day. Myrtle Point rents a bull float at $12 a day.

EDCO sells its KL-14 14-inch walk-behind saw with a Honda engine for $2,673, so purchase pays back after 21 rental days, more than a single house's control joints and patch cuts add up to.

Grinding and polishing a floor takes 5 to 9 passes through finer diamond grits plus a densifier, and an uneven or blotchy result is the main risk of a first attempt. Rates outside Oregon run $70 to $130 a day for edge grinders and $250 to $550 for the 20- to 25-inch planetary grinders that produce a polished finish (Count Bricks, HN Machines). With a HEPA dust extractor, a full setup runs $500 to $750 a day. Visible polished floors go to a surface-prep contractor. Shop and garage floors with an exposed-aggregate or broom-and-seal finish suit a rented 10-inch walk-behind grinder at $165 to $220 a day.

Concrete pumps come with their operators, and grout lifts in reinforced masonry may call for a grout pump. Drum-cleaning acids and rinse disposal, the pump for each pour and the yearly verification of the field test kit are set in the Foundation chapter.

### Reinforcing bar

Manual cutter-benders from Marshalltown, OCM and BN Products (model MBC-16B1) are rated for bar up to #5 Grade 60, and the structural drawings show whether any bar is larger. Manual units run $200 to $600. Bar of #6 and larger takes a hydraulic or electric-hydraulic unit, or goes to the supplier's bending shop. Shop-built bending tables bend bar by hand at the cost of their materials. Each is a heavy plywood or steel top with steel pegs set to the bend diameters on the structural drawings, worked with a length of pipe as a lever.

## 27.7 Block-production equipment

**Simple**

Making block on site takes a press or a vibrating table and a mixer that forces its blades through a stiff mix. It also takes molds and pallets to cure the green units on. Hand-powered lever presses make 30 to 50 small blocks an hour, enough for the target of 100 a day. Imported machines make thousands a day and cost far more once freight, duty and a bigger mixer are added. Wet-season pallets are plastic or composite, because wood swells and rots in a year. Carts for the yard run on wheels 13 inches or larger, because warehouse wheels sink into soft ground.

**Applied**

1. Prove the mix and the method on the bootstrap rig before buying any machine.
2. Buy the paddle or pan mixer from the concrete section before the first production batch.
3. Buy a manual lever press or build one from plate steel for the small solid block.
4. For full-size hollow block, build or buy a vibrating table and a weighted follower plate.
5. Fit a positive mechanical stop under the platen of any hydraulic press before its first use.
6. Buy plastic or GMT composite pallets sized to the molds, enough for several days' production plus the cure hold.
7. Buy or build a yard cart with pneumatic or solid tires at least 13 inches in diameter.
8. Rent a pallet jack for one day to test it on the actual pad before buying anything of its kind.
9. Choose a semi-permanent release agent, and follow its maker's reapplication interval.
10. Log unit dimensions against the mold nominals weekly to catch release buildup and mold wear.

**Professional**

### Presses and vibrating tables

Compaction equipment divides by the force it can apply to the mold face. A manual lever press of the CINVA-Ram class multiplies about 80 pounds of hand force to a compaction pressure of 2 to 7 MPa on a small solid block. It weighs about 140 pounds and can be welded from plate steel. Its modern descendant, the Auram Press 3000, applies up to 15 tons and makes 30 to 40 blocks an hour, about 50 with a vibrator. Powered hydraulic machines (the AECT Impact 2001A at about 300 blocks an hour) and the open-source Liberator press at about $8,000 are built for output far above this house's target.

Compacting a full-size hollow block by static force alone takes 60 tonnes or more, beyond any lever press, so full-size units at owner scale come from vibration under a weighted head. Owner-built vibrating tables run on a motor with an eccentric mass on a spring-mounted top. Commercial 12-by-24-inch tables cost $800 to $1,000, and purpose-sold vibrator motors run 0.28 to 0.75 kW. Mix, moisture, vibration and curing rules for the equipment are in the Owner-Made Blocks chapter.

### Imported machines

Imported machines sell in three tiers by output. Mobile egg-layer machines of the QMY6-25 class cost $2,000 to $3,000 FOB and cast directly onto a 2-inch slab or compacted earth. Semi-automatic pallet-fed lines (QT4-24 class) run $6,000 to $12,000 FOB and keep about 1,000 pallets in circulation. Fully automatic hydraulic plants (QT10-15 class) run $50,000 to $100,000 FOB. The smallest egg-layer makes 3,840 to 4,000 units in an 8-hour shift, the day's target in about 40 minutes.

Landed cost adds freight, duty and import fees. A container from Shanghai to Los Angeles costs $3,100 to $4,200 in base rate and $4,000 to $6,000 all-in once surcharges of 30 to 50 percent are added. Duty runs by HS code from 0 to over 25 percent, with Section 301 tariffs on top, a merchandise processing fee of 0.3464 percent and a harbor maintenance fee of 0.125 percent. Default molds are 390 by 190 by 90 mm. Custom molds to the 7-5/8-inch US module come on a 4- to 6-week lead time with a tooling fee, priced only by quote. Most of these machines run on three-phase power, which the phase converter in the power section supplies.

With a mixer, pallets, freight and a generator, an imported line lands at $15,000 to $25,000 at the low end and $30,000 to $40,000 or more at the high end. The bootstrap rig reaches a working line for a few hundred dollars to the low thousands.

### Pallets, carts and release agents

Pallets support green block until the unit can bear its own weight. Wood absorbs water, swells and lasts 1 to 5 years in wet service, and plain steel rusts without refinishing. Plastic pallets resist moisture and chemicals. Glass-mat thermoplastic (GMT) pallets of polypropylene and chopped glass resist cracking and UV with a claimed service life of 20 years, at $3 to $8 each in bulk. Pallet prices depend on size, load rating and quantity and come by supplier quote once the mold size is fixed.

Yard carts run on wheels of 13 to 16 inches (325 to 400 mm) in solid rubber or pneumatic tires. ## 27.10 Drilling, coring and fastening into cured concrete

**Simple**

Holes in cured concrete come from a rotary hammer, a drill that pounds the bit as it turns. Its SDS-plus size covers holes up to about 1-1/8 inches, which includes nearly every anchor and dowel hole on a house. Pipe and conduit openings through a foundation wall come from a rented core drill, which cuts a round plug. Light fastening uses concrete screws driven into drilled holes. Heavier connections use expansion anchors, or steel bar set in epoxy. A powder-actuated tool fires a pin with a small cartridge, fast for sill plates and unnecessary once the rotary hammer is owned.

**Applied**

1. Buy an SDS-plus rotary hammer with a dust shroud and a HEPA vacuum when the largest hole on the drawings is under 1 inch.
2. Buy an SDS-max rotary hammer when the drawings call for holes of 1 inch or larger.
3. Write the penetration schedule, each pipe and conduit diameter with its wall location, before buying any core bit.
4. Rent a handheld core drill for conduit and small sleeves, and a rig-mounted drill for stacks and thick walls.
5. Rent the core drill for one block of days when the trades can cluster their penetrations inside it.
6. Rent by the day instead when the penetrations spread across weeks.
7. Stock 3/16- and 1/4-inch concrete screws in the lengths the fastening schedule calls for.
8. Buy expansion anchors in the count and size the structural connection details call for.
9. Drill epoxy-anchor holes to the bit size and depth in the product's evaluation report, and clean them as the report says.
10. Inject with the maker's dispenser, and record each anchor's location, bar size, embedment, hole cleaning and cure time in the binder, with a photo.
11. Skip the powder-actuated tool unless the sill-plate and furring volume proves high. If it is bought, test it each day before loading.

**Professional**

SDS-plus bits cover holes from 1/4 inch to roughly 1-1/8 inches in concrete and masonry, the range of concrete-screw, sleeve-anchor and standard epoxy-dowel holes on a house (BuildToolHQ, ToolUp). SDS-max is the heavier format for holes of 1 inch and larger, large anchors and sustained demolition. An SDS-plus tool pushed above its class wears bits faster and drills slower. Cordless SDS-plus hammers run $150 to $350 bare and $250 to $500 in kits with battery and charger. SDS-max units from Milwaukee and Bosch run $300 to $700 at Home Depot, and Hilti's $400 to $700 and up. Myrtle Point rents a rotary hammer at $40 a day, $75 a week and $120 a month.

Handheld core drills suit small-diameter sleeves and conduit. A rig-mounted drill supplies the stability and feed pressure for large stacks and thick walls and cuts a cleaner, better-located hole. On the Massachusetts list a handheld core drill rents at $100 a day and a wall-mount rig at $125, bits extra. A 2-inch diamond core bit costs $120 to $190 and a 4-inch bit $150 to $230, and each diameter on the penetration schedule takes its own bit. One multi-day rental spanning the rough-in window beats several single days only when the plumbing, electrical and mechanical penetrations cluster inside it.

Tapcon concrete screws, the ICC-ES-listed trade standard for light fastening, sell in 100-count boxes in 3/16- and 1/4-inch diameters and lengths from 1-1/4 inches up, at $10 to $40 a box by size. Furring, blocking, brackets and fixtures on one house take a few hundred screws, two to four boxes at $30 to $140 in all. Wedge and sleeve anchors cost $1 to $5 each by size.

Hilti HIT-RE 500 V3 is the reference epoxy for rebar dowels and threaded rod in cured concrete of 2,500 to 8,500 psi. ICC-ES report ESR-3814 covers it, reissued January 2025 and revised May 2025. Its installation instructions pair each bar with a carbide bit: 5/8 inch for #4 bar, 3/4 inch for #5, and 7/8 or 1 inch for #6. Embedment runs 2-3/4 to 30 inches for #4 and 3-1/8 to 37-1/2 inches for #5, and the engineer of record sets the depth.

## 27.11 Dust control and protective equipment

**Simple**

Cutting, drilling or grinding concrete and masonry makes silica dust fine enough to scar the lungs for life. Oregon's silica rule lists, tool by tool, the control that keeps the dust down and the breathing protection that goes with it. Water fed to the saw blade, or a vacuum shroud on the drill, is the control. With it in place, the rule lists no respirator for most outdoor jobs under four hours. Grinding out old mortar takes a respirator every time. A P100 half mask, safety glasses, earmuffs and gloves cost $75 to $200 for the set. Silica dust is never swept dry or blown off with compressed air.

**Applied**

1. Fit the water feed or the vacuum shroud before the first cut, hole or pass, and run it the whole time.
2. Keep the water flowing hard enough that no dust cloud shows at the blade.
3. Read the respirator column of the table for the tool and the place before each session.
4. Add up the minutes of every listed task in the day. Past four hours, every task takes its over-four-hour respirator.
5. Wear a P100 half-face respirator, with a seal check, at every mandatory task and at every indoor cut.
6. Wear the respirator at any duration when grinding out mortar.
7. Vacuum settled dust with a HEPA filter or wet it down. Never dry-sweep it or blow it off with compressed air.
8. Replace P100 cartridges when breathing resistance rises, and log the date.
9. Replace scratched glasses and worn gloves, and keep spares in the kit.

## 27.12 Fuel, batteries, rust and repair

**Simple**

Three things wear out tools that sit between phases: stale fuel, badly stored batteries and damp. A routine that costs almost nothing prevents each one. Oregon pump gasoline is 10 percent ethanol. Ethanol pulls water out of the air and leaves gum in a small engine's carburetor within weeks. A lithium battery left full loses up to a fifth of its capacity in a year of storage. One left half full loses under a twentieth. Bare steel on a shop floor rusts through the wet season, because a slab on the ground draws moisture up from the soil.

**Applied**

1. Buy ethanol-free gasoline where a station is within reach (pure-gas.org lists Oregon's), and otherwise buy no more than 30 days of E10 at a time.
2. Add stabilizer at the maker's dose to any fuel that will sit more than 30 days.
3. Run the tank and the carburetor dry before an idle period of months.
4. Charge lithium packs to 40 to 60 percent before storing them between phases.
5. Store packs in a cool, dry part of the shop, away from sun, heaters and vehicle cabs.
6. Wipe steel tools dry after wet work, before they go on the rack.
7. Oil bare steel or spray it with a rust preventive before a long idle period.
8. Store tools on shelves, pegboard or wall racks, never on the slab.
9. Run a dehumidifier, or keep desiccant in a closed tool cabinet, through the wet season.
10. Budget $500 to $1,500 for each active year's blades, bits, fuel and protective gear.
11. Buy the hardest-worked tools from makers who sell parts and honor a long warranty, and keep the receipts and serial numbers.

**Professional**

E10 gasoline absorbs atmospheric moisture at up to about 50 times the rate of ethanol-free fuel. After weeks in a vented tank the water and ethanol separate into a layer below the gasoline, the volatile fractions evaporate, and gum and varnish close the small jets of a carburetor. Ethanol also softens older rubber and plastic fuel lines, gaskets and diaphragms (Gold Eagle, Husqvarna). Carbureted equipment takes the damage: plate compactors, cutoff saws, gas mixers and non-inverter generators.

Oregon's ethanol-free stations are few, listed by pure-gas.org and buyrealgas.com, among them a station in Baker City, a Pacific Pride in Cottage Grove and Elk Lake Resort near Mount Bachelor. Stabilizer such as STA-BIL slows the breakdown for a season and does not stop it over many months, so a tank run dry is the protection for the gaps between phases.

A lithium pack ages fastest at full charge and at heat. Stored full for a year it loses up to 20 percent of its capacity, and stored at 40 to 60 percent under 5 percent. Each 10 C (18 F) rise in storage temperature roughly doubles the rate of chemical degradation (Fanttik, STIHL, Ingco, MakeUseOf).

A slab in contact with the ground draws soil moisture upward by capillary action unless a vapor retarder lies under it, and a shop slab poured without one raises the humidity at floor level. Residual mortar and concrete water on a tool is the faster rust driver, so the wipe-down comes before storage and the oil film before idle months.

Each active year of building uses diamond blades at $20 to $200 each and core bits at $120 to $230 per diameter. It also uses concrete screws, fuel and stabilizer, release agent and form oil, and the glasses, gloves and cartridges of the protective kit. Summed, they run $500 to $1,500 a year of active building and $1,500 to $5,000 or more across the build. That is a planning figure the purchase log replaces with real totals.

Repairability belongs in the purchase decision when a tool will outlast its warranty. Milwaukee warrants its tools for five years and DeWalt for three plus a one-year service agreement, and Hilti's service guarantee runs beyond both, at Hilti's prices. A multi-year build outlives a three-year warranty on the tools bought first.

## 27.13 The shop interior and tool storage

**Simple**

Inside the shop, tools are grouped by where they are used and how dirty they are. Earthmoving attachments and outdoor tools sit by the big door, where they roll straight outside. Fasteners and consumables fill labeled bins in the middle, since every phase draws on them. Battery chargers get their own circuit away from the saw dust, next to a bench with a vise and a grinder. 6. Label the bins by trade and the fasteners by size.
7. Build more shelving than today's inventory fills.
8. Cast a workbench base from return concrete or reject block only when a pour or a block run is already happening.
9. Load a wheeled cart with the day's tools each morning and park it at the active wall.

## 27.14 Security and insurance for the tool inventory

**Simple**

Tools that move around a job site are insured under an inland marine policy, also called a tools-and-equipment floater. It is bought separately and priced by the value of the inventory. Homeowner and builder's-risk policies are written for the building, and the chosen policy's own wording says whether any tool is covered. On a rural site most equipment theft happens at night, on weekends and on holidays, when nobody is there. A solar camera with a cellular connection watches the yard without grid power. A GPS tracker rides on the generator and on any owned machine. Portable tools left in an open yard are the easiest things on the site to carry off.

**Applied**

1. Total the inventory from the purchase log before calling the insurance agent.
2. Ask the agent whether the homeowner or builder's-risk policy covers tools and equipment at all, and what the deductible and exclusions are.
3. Price an inland marine policy for the inventory, scheduling the machines worth listing one by one.
4. Tell the agent which tools and materials sit outdoors in the block-yard overnight, and ask how the policy treats unenclosed property.
5. Ask for the GPS-tracker discount in writing.
6. Fit a GPS tracker to each owned machine and the generator.
7. Mount a solar, cellular camera to cover the shop door and the block-yard, not only the shop.
8. Carry portable, high-value tools back into the locked shop at the end of every day.
9. Photograph serial numbers, and keep the list off the site.

**Professional**

Inland marine coverage insures mobile tools and equipment against theft and damage wherever they are. Premiums start around $750 a year and scale with insured value. A contractor with $50,000 of tools pays $500 to $1,500 a year. Unscheduled tools are rated near 4 percent of value and scheduled equipment near 1 percent, and small-tool floaters run as high as $3 per $100 of coverage (F&O Agency, Construction Coverage, Grit Insurance). Property left in an open yard may carry a sublimit, a higher rate or an exclusion under the policy wording.

Contractors in the United States lose an estimated $300 million to $1 billion of equipment a year to theft, and fewer than 25 percent of stolen machines are recovered. Over 60 percent of the thefts fall on weekends, holidays and overnight (Spytec, Projul, FieldFix). GPS trackers cost $100 to $400 in hardware plus $15 to $50 a month per device. They are reported to cut theft and loss by up to 60 percent, and many insurers discount equipment-floater premiums 5 to 15 percent for tracked assets. Solar-powered cameras with 4G or LTE connections record at night, detect motion and store to the cloud without a wired connection, and that cloud dependence is a smart-home design question.

Fencing, gates and lighting for the whole site are in the Developing the Site chapter. The block-yard runs outdoors for weeks to months, so its cement, rebar, molds and tools cannot be locked away the way the shop's contents can. The yard gets its own coverage: a camera aimed at it, a tracker on any machine left in it, and the nightly carry of portable tools to the shop.
