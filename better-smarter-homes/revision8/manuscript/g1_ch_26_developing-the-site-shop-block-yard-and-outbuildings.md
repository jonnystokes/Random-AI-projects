# Chapter 26: Developing the Site: Shop, Block-Yard, and Outbuildings

Everything on this parcel besides the house goes up in a fixed order: the driveway first, because every truck for the next several years arrives over it, then temporary power and water, then the shop, then the block-yard, and the house shell once the block campaign is under way. At 1,500 to 2,400 square feet, the shop is placed for the decades of use after the build. Small accessory buildings need no building permit under the exemption the legal chapter states. That is how the first practice walls and the yard's fabric shelter go up before the house plans are final. Costs are 2026 figures unless a different year is given.

## 26.1 What gets built, and in what order

**Simple**

The driveway comes first, because nothing can be delivered until a loaded truck reaches the site. Temporary power and water come next, so tools and the mixer have something to run on. Then comes the shop, or at least a roof and a locked door, so tools and cement stay dry. After that the block-yard, and the months of block making. Once the campaign is well along, the house foundation starts. Making block and digging the foundation at once can save a season. But the two share one driveway, one turnaround and one pair of hands. Excavation mud ends up on the curing pad, and mixer dust settles on wet concrete.

**Applied**

1. Build the driveway to its full base, with the turnaround, before the first loaded truck.
2. Bring in temporary power and water, and set the generator and the washout pit where the shop will stand.
3. Raise the shop shell, or a fabric shelter and a locked job box, before buying the block equipment.
4. Grade and pave the block-yard pad, then run the production campaign.
5. Start the foundation once the stock of cured block reaches the first lifts of wall.
6. Overlap the campaign with excavation only where the pad and the house pad have separate truck routes.
7. On an overlap, give each weekday to one operation, and book the two sets of deliveries on different days.
8. Redraw the laydown plan at every phase, with each material's place, each truck route and what stays locked.
9. Stack aggregate, sand and cement beside the yard and the shop, never on the route to the house pad.
10. Keep rebar and lumber off the ground on dunnage, under a roof, through the wet season.
11. Lock pipe, wire, conduit and fixtures in the shop or a job box.
12. Stage cured block on a short, all-weather path to the wall, clear of the concrete-truck turnaround.

**Professional**

Each phase in that order supplies the next. Over the driveway come the excavator, the aggregate and the ready-mix. Temporary power runs the mixer, the saws and the lights, and temporary water feeds the mixer and the curing cover. Inside the shop sit the tools and the cement, and on the yard the units the foundation and the walls consume are made. Running the block campaign and the excavation at once compresses the calendar by up to the shorter of the two durations, since neither waits on the other's output.

Interference is what the overlap costs on a small site with one driveway. Ready-mix and pump trucks compete with cement and aggregate deliveries for one driveway and one turnaround. Excavation mud tracks onto the pad where green units are handled, and dust from dry batching settles on fresh concrete. Where the two work zones and their truck routes are separated, the overlap gains that time. On a single-access site the interference costs more days than the overlap saves.

Every phase gets its own laydown plan, with the laydown zone kept apart from the working footprint so deliveries never block the work. Heavy, weather-tolerant materials that feed the campaign for months sit beside the yard and the shop: aggregate, sand, and cement under cover.

Reinforcing bar rusts and lumber swells on bare ground in an Oregon winter, so both ride on dunnage under a roof. Pipe, conduit, wire and fixtures are small, valuable and portable, and they stay locked. One pile placed for convenience early, across what later becomes the only truck route to the foundation, costs a machine day to move again.

### Living on the parcel before the house exists

**Simple**

Whether you may live on the land before the house is finished is a county zoning question. Klamath County requires the house to be built before any accessory structure. Once the house's building permit is issued, it lets a family live in an RV on the parcel under a temporary use permit. Without that permit, camping there is limited to 21 days in any six months. Some owners build the shop first with a small apartment inside it, so the shop is the parcel's one dwelling until the house is done. A shop permitted for work was never inspected as a home. Living in it before it is permitted as one is a violation of its own.

**Applied**

1. Ask the county planning department in writing whether any accessory structure may be built before the dwelling, and under what condition.
2. Ask for the temporary-occupancy permit by name, with its fee, its renewal and its trigger.
3. Ask the insurer, before moving onto the parcel, how the policy treats occupancy of an unfinished or uncertified structure.
4. Keep the shop permitted as a shop unless the county's answer leaves the apartment route as the only one.

**Professional**

A dwelling-first rule is a zoning sequence, separate from the building code's permit exemption, which conditions nothing on a house being present. Klamath County's planning FAQ states the rule directly and names two routes around it. One is a temporary use permit for an RV, at $450 to apply and $150 a year to renew. The other is a shop permitted with a small apartment as the parcel's one allowed dwelling, converted back once the house is occupied. Whether the target county follows that pattern is confirmed with its planning department once a parcel is known.

Occupying any structure as a dwelling waits on its own certificate of occupancy, under the rules the legal chapter states. Insurance is a separate question, answered by the policy's own wording: whether a homeowner's or builder's-risk policy excludes occupancy of an unfinished structure or requires disclosure of it.

## 26.2 One plan for everything that is not the house

**Simple**

Onto the site plan, the shop, the yard and the outbuildings go last. The septic field, the well, the house, the driveway and the sun's path to the panels take their ground first. They go last because they can move and the others cannot. Within a few feet of a property line, a building needs fire-rated walls and no windows on that side. Placed to save steps during the build, a shop sits beside your living room for decades, with its noise. Anything tall south of the panels shades them, and anything close against a house wall keeps it damp and mossy. While the house trench is open, an empty conduit goes to every spot an outbuilding will ever stand.

**Applied**

1. Lock the septic and reserve areas, the well, the hazard zones, the driveway, the house and the solar envelope first.
2. Place the shop, the yard and the outbuildings in the ground that remains.
3. Draw the accessory-dwelling zone and keep every outbuilding out of it.
4. Draw each outbuilding at its full height and footprint, and check its shadow against the array's winter sun lines.
5. Keep the shop, the woodshed, the greenhouse and any tall fence off the house's south, east and west walls.
6. Hold every outbuilding wall 3 feet or more from the property line.
7. Where it must stand closer, design that wall as fire-rated with no openings.
8. Keep every outbuilding foundation outside the septic setbacks and the well's service clearance.
9. Place the shop where it serves the household after the build.
10. Place the yard between the shop and the driveway, on the far side of the shop from the house's truck turnaround.
11. Where a wanted outbuilding site conflicts with a fixed element, move the outbuilding.
12. Run a spare power conduit and a data conduit to every fixed outbuilding location while the house trench is open.
13. Run the same pair to the accessory-dwelling zone.
14. Oversize the power conduit, or lay a second empty one, and cap and stake both ends.
15. Photograph every sleeve with a tape to two fixed points before backfill.
16. Draw each sleeve on the as-built site plan.

**Professional**

Outbuildings are the most movable elements on the plan, so they absorb the conflicts the fixed elements create. Lock order, the septic and well rings and the accessory-dwelling zone are the land chapter's, and three constraints bind an accessory structure once its ground is chosen.

### Fire separation at the lot line

The residential code's exterior-wall table protects a neighbor's building from radiant heat across the lot line. Oregon's table draws its line closer to the lot line than the model code does. Pushed up against the line, an outbuilding loses its windows on that side and gains a rated wall, so the plan holds the distance instead. Structures small enough to be permit-exempt are excused from the wall protection, which is one more reason a first practice shed stays inside the exemption. Accessory structures take the county's own zoning setback, set apart from the dwelling's, and the figure is read from the ordinance once a parcel is chosen.

### Shade on the array and on the walls

OSU Extension's moss guidance identifies shade and sustained moisture as the conditions that grow moss on roofs, walls and paving. It recommends thinning or removing the vegetation that shades a surface. Close to a house wall, a shop, a woodshed or a tall fence shades it as a tree does, so outbuildings stand clear of the walls that dry slowest. Drawn at each tree's mature height and each planned structure's full height, the array's shadow lines close the ground south of the panels to anything tall.

### Placing for the decades after the build

Distance between the shop and the house trades haul time during the build against noise, fumes and vehicle traffic for the decades after it. Because the build ends and the proximity does not, the plan weights the permanent condition. For the yard the weighting runs the other way. Its hardstand has no use after the campaign unless its reuse is designed in. It therefore sits near the driveway and the shop, where deliveries are short, and away from the house's turnaround, where its mud and dust would reach the concrete work.

### Conduit while the trench is open

An open trench costs the width of a second conduit. Reopened after the driveway, the fence and the planting are in, the same trench costs excavation, restoration and a week of disruption. For a specific route, the figure comes from the excavation contractor's bid. Conduit sized only to the load known at the time leaves no room for a larger feeder. Years on, a shop or an accessory dwelling takes a data path as well. Each fixed location therefore gets an oversized power conduit or a second empty one, with a communications conduit beside it. At a separate building the feeder and its disconnect follow the electrical code's rules for a second structure, drawn with the house's service.

**Code**

```
An accessory building near the lot line carries a rated wall with no openings.
  At a fire separation distance under 3 feet: walls rated 1 hour from both sides, no openings, no projections under 2 feet and rated projections from 2 to 3 feet, penetrations per R302.4.
  Source: ORSC R302.1 and Table R302.1(1) (2023 edition).

Permit-exempt detached accessory structures need no wall protection for their location.
  Detached nonhabitable accessory structures exempted from permits are not required to provide wall protection based on location on the lot, and projections shall not extend over the lot line.
  Source: ORSC R302.1, exception 3 (2023 edition).
```

## 26.3 The driveway base, built once

**Simple**

Built for cars, a gravel driveway fails under the first loaded concrete truck. Repairing it means excavating and rebuilding the base, replacing the surface, and losing the delivery day. Under cars, 6 to 8 inches of compacted crushed rock is enough. At 66,000 to 80,000 pounds, a mixer truck takes 12 to 18 inches on good ground, and more on clay. Angular crushed rock locks together under the roller, and round river gravel rolls apart. Water has to leave the driveway too. It runs off a crown in the middle, into ditches at the sides, and through culverts cleaned before the autumn rains.

**Applied**

1. Get the subgrade classification before specifying the base.
2. Specify the full heavy-truck section wherever ready-mix and pump trucks will travel.
3. On clay or soft subgrade, lay a woven geotextile on the cut subgrade before the first lift.
4. Place 4 to 6 inches of 1-to-2-1/2-inch crushed stone as the subbase.
5. Place 3 to 4 inches of crusher-run base, then 2 to 3 inches of fine crushed surface rock.
6. Compact every lift at 4 inches or less with a reversible plate or a roller, and log each lift.
7. Build the full section before the excavator arrives.
8. Crown the surface so water runs to the ditches, and cut the ditches to a downhill outlet.
9. Each autumn before the rains, clear the culvert inlets and outlets and the ditch lines.
10. Grade out washboard and edge ruts when they appear.
11. Where the base itself has sunk, add base rock and recompact before grading the surface.
12. Mark any utility conduit crossing under the driveway on the site plan, with its depth.

**Professional**

Base-course depth scales with the wheel load that will cross it. Eger Excavating's and Hello Gravel's driveway guides put a passenger-car driveway at 6 to 8 inches of compacted gravel. For trucks of 14,000 pounds and up they put the total at 12 to 18 inches of compacted base on good subgrade. That section is built as a subbase of larger stone, a crusher-run base and a fine surface course, compacted in thin lifts. Both guides prefer angular crushed stone, which interlocks under compaction, to rounded gravel.

On heavy clay one of them specifies 12 to 16 inches of base and a geotextile separation layer. Without the fabric, the base rock works down into a soft subgrade under repeated loads, and the section thins from below. Subgrade classification, culvert sizing and the fish-passage review are the soil chapter's, and the geotechnical engineer sets the final section for the loaded ready-mix truck against the subgrade.

Built light for early access and then loaded by concrete trucks, a base fails by rutting and by base loss into the subgrade. Repair rebuilds the same base course, replaces the surface and stops deliveries while it is done, so the light base is paid for twice. Staging the base saves money only where cash is unavailable before construction starts and the light base survives the whole heavy-truck phase, which the load-to-depth relationship makes unlikely.

Crowned, the surface sheds rain to the ditches before it runs down the wheel paths and cuts them. Washboard and edge rutting come from traffic on a surface short of fines or a base short of compaction, and grading restores the crown and the profile. Where the base has failed rather than the surface, grading alone lasts one season, and fresh base rock recompacted under the surface is the repair. Half full of silt, a culvert backs water against the fill in the first storm, which is why the clean-out comes before the wet season.

## 26.4 The turnaround and the entrance: gate or cattle guard

**Simple**

One graded, based turnaround serves both the fire engine the code plans for and the concrete and pump trucks of the build. Its shape and size come from the fire code, and its base thickness from the concrete truck. At the road the plan puts a cattle guard, a steel grid over a shallow pit, in place of a gate. Cars cross it without slowing, and hoofed animals will not step onto it. Installing one costs more than a gate, and maintaining it costs almost nothing for thirty years. Where a gate is chosen instead, a solar opener runs it without grid power. The fire district has to be able to open it in an emergency.

**Applied**

1. Draw the fire turnaround to the shape the fire district accepts, and build it to the heavy-truck base section.
2. Make the turnaround large enough for a ready-mix truck and a pump truck to stand in it at once.
3. Put the turnaround and the yard's delivery stand on opposite sides of the drive where the ground allows.
4. Get the fire marshal's written answer on opening width and emergency operation before choosing a gate or a guard.
5. For a cattle guard, order a 20-foot unit with wing panels, and pour its footings to the maker's drawing.
6. Drain the pit under the guard to daylight, and clean it of silt every autumn.
7. For a gate, choose a slide gate where snow would block a swing gate's arc.
8. Buy a solar opener kit sized above the maker's minimum for the gate's weight.
9. Add a second panel and a larger battery for December.
10. Fit the fire department's emergency release the marshal names, and submit the lock method for approval.
11. Keep the approach-permit drawing, the marshal's letter and the guard or opener data in the binder.

**Professional**

Turnaround geometry, with the driveway's width, grade and surface strength, is in the land chapter. A turnaround is a shape requirement and a pump truck's stand is a load requirement, and the same graded area built to the heavy-truck base meets both. Built to a light residential base, a code-shaped turnaround still fails under the first pour.

Across the only access, a locked gate stops an engine at 3 AM unless the fire department can open it. That is why the appendix regulates a gate's width, its construction and its lock, and why an electric gate carries an emergency release. Under Oregon Fire Code 503.1.1, exception 1, the fire code official may modify the access rules where no more than two unsprinklered one- and two-family dwellings are served. The opening width at this driveway is therefore the district's written answer.

Residential solar openers pair a 10-to-30-watt panel with a 12-volt battery. Ghost Controls and ALEKO sell the kits. One of them, the JUJIANG C026, pairs a 20-watt panel with two 12-volt 8-amp-hour batteries for a single leaf up to 770 pounds or 16 feet. Makers size panel and battery to the gate's weight and daily cycles and publish no Oregon-winter figure. Sized to its minimum under a sunny-climate assumption, a kit runs flat in a western Oregon December. Swing or slide follows the site. A slide gate takes clear run-back along the fence, and a swing gate takes level ground through its arc, which drifted snow can block.

DRGAC Fleet's and Barnworld's price guides put economy steel cattle guards at $1,700 to $2,500 before shipping and footings. Mid-grade fabricated guards with skirts and end caps run $3,500 to $5,500. Installed totals come to $2,000 to $7,000, and custom or heavy-duty work runs above $10,000. Wing panels, which stop animals walking around the ends, sell separately.

Spanning a pit deep enough that livestock will not cross, the guard takes footings that carry the vehicle load across the span. It also takes drainage that keeps the pit from filling with water and silt, which a gate's post footings never need. Those makers put a steel guard's service life past 30 years with little maintenance, against a gate's hinges, latches and opener. With no livestock to hold and a stated priority of low daily friction and low upkeep, the guard serves the parcel at a higher one-time cost.

**Code**

```
A gate across a fire apparatus access road is wide, hand-operable and openable by the fire department.
  At least 20 feet wide for a single gate, swing, slide, lift or pivot type, operable by hand by one person, kept working, with an approved emergency opening on electric gates and an approved lock.
  Source: Oregon Fire Code Appendix D, D103.5, items 1 to 6 (2022 edition, 2021 IFC base).

Powered gates are listed and built to the automatic-gate standards.
  Electric gate operators listed to UL 325, and gates intended for automatic operation designed, constructed and installed to ASTM F2200.
  Source: Oregon Fire Code 503.6 and Appendix D, D103.5, items 7 and 8 (2022 edition).

A security gate across the access road is approved and openable in an emergency.
  Installation approved by the fire code official, with an approved means of emergency operation, both maintained operational at all times.
  Source: Oregon Fire Code 503.6 (2022 edition).
```

## 26.5 Fences: deer, elk, livestock and the line

**Simple**

To keep deer out of a garden a fence stands 6 feet 4 inches tall, and to keep elk out it stands 7 feet. Those are the state wildlife department's heights. Both sit at or under the 7 feet below which no city or county may require a building permit for a wood or wire fence. Around the parcel the fence does a different job. It marks the line and keeps a neighbor's cattle out, and one woven-wire fence with a barbed top strand does both. Inside a livestock district the animal's owner keeps it home. On open range, trespassing cattle count as strays only if your land is enclosed by a sound fence.

**Applied**

### The garden fence

1. Set the total height at 76 inches on level ground.
2. On sloping ground, add a few inches on the uphill side.
3. Set wood corner posts at least 9-1/2 feet long and 5 inches across at the top.
4. Sink them 2-1/2 feet and brace them.
5. Set 3-inch wood line posts or 8-foot #133 studded steel T posts 15 feet apart, with every seventh post wood.
6. Run woven wire from the ground to 58 inches, spliced with hog rings.
7. Use 14-1/2-gauge filler, 11-gauge top and bottom wires, and stays 6 inches or closer.
8. Add barbed strands above it, 5 inches apart or closer, to reach 76 inches.
9. Keep the bottom wire at ground level, and fill every dip with gravel, rock or logs.
10. Stretch the wire tight, and staple it with 1-1/2-inch staples, ten or more per post.
11. Treat wood posts for their bottom 3 feet with a preservative other than a copper type, or use untreated yew.
12. For elk, build to 84 inches with two 39-inch widths of woven wire.
13. Use 12 staples per post and a barbed strand on top.
14. For a cheaper garden fence, build the department's 60-inch electric design.
15. Run six 12-1/2-gauge high-tensile wires at 10-inch spacing, the bottom wire 10 inches up.
16. Tension those wires to 250 pounds.
17. Power them from a low-impedance energizer of 4,000 volts or more, on an 8-foot ground rod.

### The perimeter fence

1. Choose woven wire with a barbed top strand where one fence has to exclude livestock, mark the line and deter theft.
2. Choose five-strand high-tensile electric fence where cost rules.
3. Keep its charger working and its line clear of brush.
4. Buy no-climb horse mesh only where horses are planned.
5. Set gate posts in concrete footings sized to the gate's weight and swing.
6. Use hot-dip galvanized steel posts, or treated wood rated for ground contact.
7. Ask the county whether the parcel lies in a livestock district.
8. Decide how far the perimeter fence goes from that answer.
9. Where the line is in doubt, have it surveyed before a post goes in.
10. Agree the fence's cost-sharing with the neighbor in writing.

**Professional**

Deer in good condition clear a 76-inch fence when chased and otherwise do not. That behavior is why the Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife's fence guide sets that height for its woven-wire deer fence, with 84 inches for elk. Its electric alternative works on behavior too: deer try to slip between wires rather than jump, and after the first shock they stay away from the fence. Line posts for the electric design stand up to 50 feet apart, which is why it costs less over a long run. Its energizer has to be the low-impedance kind, since a conventional energizer's output does not deter deer.

Fixr's wire-fence guide, updated January 31, 2025, prices installed woven wire at $1.50 to $5.90 a foot and deer fence at $1.75 to $5.50. It puts high-tensile at $1.60 to $4.50, welded wire at $4 to $10 and electric fence at $6 to $9, with labor at $0.92 to $5 a foot.

Iowa State University Extension's FM 1855, in 2005 prices, built a quarter mile of woven wire for $1.51 a foot and barbed wire for $1.23. Its high-tensile fences came to $1.12 a foot non-electric and $0.70 electric, with annual ownership at $0.26, $0.21, $0.15 and $0.09 a foot for the four. In both lists the types rank the same way.

One woven-wire perimeter excludes livestock, marks the boundary and presents a visible barrier to a thief. High-tensile electric fence costs less per foot and holds livestock, and offers a thief a wire to step over. No-climb mesh exists for the hoof a horse can trap in a wider grid, and it adds nothing for cattle, sheep or deer.

Oregon's livestock statute divides land into livestock districts, where it is unlawful for livestock to run at large, and open range, where they may. Counties create or extend a district on a landowner's application under ORS 607.018. In a district the fence is the owner's choice and a neighbor's animals are the neighbor's liability.

On open range the fence is the owner's only remedy, since cattle or horses on the parcel are treated as estray only when an adequate fence encloses it. When cattle break through a fence on open range, the Department of Agriculture rules on the fence's adequacy (ORS 607.300). Under ORS 96.020 a justice of the peace can order a neighbor who neglects a partition fence to repair it. Under ORS 96.060 a fence built across the line by mistake can be removed by its builder within a year of discovering the error.

Posts fail at the ground line, where wet-season soil sits against them. Hot-dip galvanized steel resists that rot. A wood post lasts longer as a ground-contact-rated treated post or a naturally resistant species, cedar among them, which slows rot without stopping it. Gate posts carry a cantilevered load and a cyclic one, and a footing too shallow or too small lets one lean until the gate drags and will not latch. The department's corner-post sizes and embedment are a bracing standard for a fence stretched tight, and a gate post takes at least as much.

**Code**

```
A low fence of wood, wire or chain link needs no building permit anywhere in Oregon.
  A municipality's permit threshold may not include fences of wood, wire mesh or chain link that are 7 feet or less in height, and swimming pool barriers always need a permit.
  Source: ORSC R101.2.2, item 1 (2023 and 2026 editions).

Inside a livestock district the animal's owner keeps it off other land.
  No livestock owner or keeper may let an animal run at large or be pastured on another's land in a district closed to that class, and is liable to the landowner.
  Source: ORS 607.045(1) and 607.044.

On open range, trespassing cattle or horses are estray only behind an adequate fence.
  Cattle or equines found trespassing on land enclosed by an adequate fence, a continuous barrier of natural features, structures, masonry, rails, poles, planks or wire, may be taken up as estray.
  Source: ORS 607.007(1) and (2)(b); ORS 607.311(1).

A neighbor who encloses against an existing line fence pays half its value.
  One-half of the value of the fence that serves as the partition, agreed or recovered in a civil action with costs and attorney fees.
  Source: ORS 96.010.
```

## 26.6 Security through the build

**Simple**

Most equipment theft on a building site happens overnight, on weekends and on holidays, when nobody is there. A rural site adds a long drive for the sheriff. Plain defenses answer it. Signs on the fence line say the site is kept. Lights over the material stacks and the entrance take away the dark. Anything small and valuable, pipe, wire, fixtures and hand tools, spends the night in the locked shop. On a solar panel with a cell connection, a camera watches the yard and the gate without grid power. A determined thief with a truck still gets in. The measures lengthen the time a theft takes and raise the chance it is seen.

**Applied**

1. Fence the staging area and the yard inside the perimeter, with signs at the gate and the corners.
2. Light the material stacks, the shop door and the entrance, on a photocell or a motion sensor.
3. Lock pipe, wire, conduit, fixtures, molds and hand tools in the shop or a job box every night.
4. Cover bagged cement and rebar against weather, and leave them inside the fence, where their weight protects them.
5. Aim the cameras at the storage area, the shop door and the entrance.
6. Test the cellular signal at each camera position with a phone on the chosen carrier before buying.
7. Choose a camera that records on the device and sends alerts without a maker's cloud account, where one exists.
8. Walk the perimeter after every storm for fence damage and downed lights.

**Professional**

Rural sites carry more theft risk than urban ones for two reasons, low foot traffic and slow response. One measure set answers both: perimeter fencing with signage, lockable storage for portable and high-value material, lighting at storage and entry points, and cameras aimed at them. Spytec, Projul and FieldFix put over 60 percent of equipment theft overnight, on weekends and on holidays. Insurance, GPS trackers and the camera that watches the block-yard are in the tools chapter. Inside the fence the yard takes no second fence of its own, and a locked job box holds molds and small tools until the shop stands.

Where no wire and no network reach, a solar camera with a cellular link still works. Most such products relay alerts through the maker's cloud over a carrier's data plan. That dependence runs against the house's rule of local control over its own systems. The product is therefore chosen for local recording and a self-hosted alert path when the house network is designed. Signal strength at the gate and the yard is a measurement at the parcel, since terrain shadows a carrier's signal within a few hundred feet.

## 26.7 Clearing and grading the pads

**Simple**

House, shop and yard share one cleared, graded area, and clearing it is the first bill after the land itself. Trees come down, stumps come out, the topsoil is stripped and piled for reuse, and the pads are cut and filled to level. On a slope, the edge of every pad becomes a wall. All of the disturbed ground counts together: the house pad, the driveway, the septic field and the yard pad. Past one acre, the state's construction stormwater permit applies, and the silt fences go in before the first cut either way. Rain on bare ground carries soil into the ditch and the creek, and silt in the creek is what the permit exists to prevent.

**Applied**

1. Mark the house pad, the shop pad, the yard pad and the driveway on the ground.
2. Add their areas to the septic field's.
3. Where the total reaches one acre, apply for the state stormwater permit before the first cut, on its lead time.
4. Install the silt fence, the gravel construction entrance and the stockpile covers before the first tree comes down.
5. Clear only the pads, the drive and the utility corridors, plus any tree that would shade the array or a house wall.
6. Grind or pull every stump inside a pad footprint.
7. Strip the topsoil and stockpile it, covered, away from the pads, for the final grading.
8. Cut and fill to the pad grades, and place any fill under a footing to the fill specification.
9. Compact the yard pad's subgrade and base in lifts, and log them.
10. Grade every pad to shed water away from the structures and toward the site's drainage feature.
11. Get a clearing and grading bid against the real pad layout before closing the land budget.

**Professional**

Clearing prices, the grading permit and the one-acre stormwater threshold are in the land chapter. The combined pad for house, shop and yard is the one to two disturbed acres it prices. Byrd Land Co.'s and OWNR OPS's 2026 guides add stump grinding at $40 to $80 a stump and debris hauling at $100 to $600 a load.

Land-clearing permits run $175 to $500 where a jurisdiction charges one, and steep, rocky or remote ground doubles or triples the per-acre figures. A time budget for clearing and grading a combined pad comes from the clearing contractor against the pad layout.

Every disturbed surface counts toward the permit threshold. Compacted, a pad also counts in the drainage design as impervious area, since it sheds rain faster than the vegetated ground the original sizing assumed. Topsoil stripped and stockpiled separately returns as the growing layer at final grade.

Left under fill, a stump decays into a void that settles the pad above it. Fill under any footing follows the geotechnical fill specification, placed and tested in lifts. The yard pad's base takes the same lift and density record, because carts and a loaded loader run on it. On a slope, cut and fill meet the pad's edge as a retaining wall, and the wall's height rather than the pad's size decides whether the owner builds it.

## 26.8 Retaining walls and site walls from owner-made units

**Simple**

Most retaining walls fail from water trapped behind them. Rain soaks into the backfill, and water that cannot drain pushes on the wall with a force it was not built for. Every wall here is therefore drained. Clean gravel goes behind it, a fabric keeps soil out of the gravel, and a pipe at the bottom lets the water out to daylight. Under 4 feet, with nothing heavy above, a wall is yours to build. Your own block, stone-filled wire baskets or purchased interlocking units all serve. Taller than that, or under a driveway or a building, it is engineered. Several short walls stepped up a slope stay under that line and use more ground than one tall wall.

**Applied**

1. Measure each wall from the bottom of its footing to its top.
2. Keep owner-built walls at 4 feet or less, with no load above.
3. Dig the base trench 24 inches wide and 6 inches deep, plus 1 inch per foot of wall height.
4. For a wall under 4 feet, dig the trench 18 inches wide and 4 inches deep plus the buried course.
5. Compact the trench with two passes of the plate compactor.
6. Replace clay or wet subgrade with granular fill in 8-inch lifts.
7. Lay 1/4-to-1-1/2-inch wall rock in the cores and at least 12 inches thick behind the wall, to the cap.
8. Separate the wall rock from the native soil with filter fabric.
9. Lay a perforated toe drain at the back of the base trench on every wall over 4 feet and every reinforced wall.
10. Lay it too on poorly draining soil, beside paving, under a slope above the wall and in every terrace.
11. Vent each drain to daylight every 50 feet, away from the septic absorption and reserve areas.
12. Step terraces back by the setback the soil work sets between tiers, or have the pair engineered as one wall.
13. For a gabion, fill the baskets with angular broken rock in a nominal 6-inch mixture, hand-placed at the face.
14. Test site rock for size and angularity before planning gabions around it.
15. For owner-made units in a retaining wall, run the wet-dry and freeze-thaw durability tests on the mix first.
16. Build the first site wall from mortared owner-made units.

**Professional**

Water trapped behind a wall adds hydrostatic pressure to the earth pressure the section was sized for, and that added load is behind most wall failures. Allan Block's residential installation manual is the detail the steps follow. In it the wall rock drains the face, the fabric keeps fines from clogging the rock, and the toe drain carries incidental water out. The manual states that the rock and the drain are not the site's primary drainage, which is why the pad swales carry roof and surface water elsewhere. It also lists the conditions that call for a toe drain on even a low wall.

Its gravity-wall chart gives, for level ground above, 5 feet 4 inches in silty sand and 3 feet 6 inches in clay for its 12-degree-setback unit. For its 6-degree units the figures are 4 feet 7 inches and 3 feet 2 inches. Under a 100-pound-per-square-foot surcharge or a 3-to-1 slope above, those heights fall by a third to a half. The chart excludes seismic loading and calls for a local engineer in a seismic area, which is all of western Oregon. The chart therefore ranks soils and surcharges, and the engineering triggers, the wall ladder and the terrace setback are the soil chapter's.

Sustained contact with damp soil at the backfill face is a harsher exposure than a house wall's rain-wetted face. The house-wall test package of compression, absorption and shrinkage does not characterize it. ASTM D559 wetting-and-drying and D560 freezing-and-thawing cycling, about a month to run, are the durability tests for a stabilized mix. A retaining-wall mix passes them or carries a higher stabilizer content than the house wall's.

Whether the alternative-materials approval for the house extends to a retaining wall is a question only a wall over the permit floor raises. Below it no permit is pulled. Dry-stacked interlocking units lay faster, because the unit's lip or pin does the alignment a trowel otherwise does. A dry-stacked first wall teaches none of the mortar work the house will demand, which is why the first wall is mortared.

Under the Iowa Statewide Urban Design and Specifications' gabion section, baskets are filled with well-graded broken stone sized above the mesh opening. The outer layer is hand-placed at the face for a square appearance.

HomeGuide's and Shengsen Gabion's 2026 guides put an owner-built gabion wall at $4 to $20 a square foot and purchased stone at $45 to $130 a ton. Stone and baskets are most of the cost, so site rock that is angular and sized to the mesh removes the largest item. Rounded river rock does not qualify without crushing. Terracing trades horizontal ground for the engineering fee. Each tier's bench, base width and setback consume run that a single engineered wall leaves flat, and on a narrow buildable strip the single wall keeps more usable area.

## 26.9 The shop: size, doors and structure

**Simple**

The shop is 30 by 50 feet or 40 by 60 feet. It holds a parking bay, a workbench and a dry bay for cement and block equipment. A pole barn is the cheapest and fastest way to that much roof. Posts go in the ground, trusses on top and steel on the outside. Two people raise the shell in a long weekend. Its main door passes the loader with a pallet on its forks: 12 feet wide and at least 12 feet tall. A shipping container is a locked steel box from the day it arrives. But it is under 8 feet wide inside, and in an Oregon winter water condenses on its walls unless they are foamed.

**Applied**

1. Size the shop at 30 by 50 feet or more.
2. Draw a parking bay, a work zone and a dry materials bay on the plan.
3. Draw the mixer, the press or table and the loader's turning path on the floor plan before fixing the size.
4. Put the main door on the yard side, 12 feet wide and 12 to 14 feet high.
5. Put a person door beside it.
6. Add a second overhead door at 9 by 7 feet for a vehicle bay.
7. Price a pole-barn kit from a major supplier, delivered, with the slab, doors and electrical priced separately.
8. Get a second price for an engineered steel building erected, and a third for the same footprint in owner-made block.
9. Use a container only for locked storage beside the shop, insulated with closed-cell spray foam if anyone works inside it.
10. Keep a pole barn's posts out of ground contact on the kit's protected footing or concrete-collar detail.
11. Order manufactured trusses with the kit, with their design drawings.

**Professional**

Locke Buildings' sizing guide and Hansen Pole Buildings' pricing set a ladder. At 30 by 40 feet a shop holds one vehicle bay and a work zone. At 30 by 50 the parking bay separates from the workshop bay, and at 40 by 60 it takes farm-class equipment, several vehicles and a full fabrication area.

Block production adds a bay those guides do not count. It holds a dry store for bagged cement and covered aggregate, floor for the mixer and the press or table, and room for the loader and a pallet to pass. The 30-by-50 to 40-by-60 end of the ladder is therefore the starting point, and the final size follows the equipment list and the vehicle count. Inside, the zones, the bench, the charger circuit and the tool storage are in the tools chapter.

Dealer listings put a mid-size compact track loader such as the Bobcat T66 at 80 inches to the cab roof and 67 to 70 inches wide over its tracks. A pallet raised on its forks and a dump trailer behind a pickup both stand taller than the cab. Door height therefore follows the tallest load, and door width lets the loader and a pallet pass one after the other rather than side by side. HomeGuide prices a 9-by-7 residential door at $300 to $700 for the door alone. NT Industrial Door's and OfficeFinder's commercial guides put installed commercial doors at $1,500 to $7,000 by size, insulation and operator. A quote for the chosen size replaces the band.

Steel conducts heat, and a container has no vapor control, so interior humidity condenses on its cold walls and ceiling. Closed-cell spray foam applied to the steel insulates and retards vapor in one layer, per Jack Cooper's container insulation guide. Rigid board takes a sealed vapor retarder at every seam to do the same. Insulating a container therefore costs more per square foot than insulating a framed shop of the same floor area, on top of the box's narrow interior. For this parcel the pole-barn kit is the default shop, at the lowest cost per square foot and the shortest build. A container serves as locked storage beside it rather than as the shop.

## 26.10 The shop: floor, power and the permit path

**Simple**

A concrete shop floor drips in spring. Warm, damp air meets a slab still cold from winter, and its water condenses on the concrete, the way it does on a cold bottle. Heating the slab is not the fix. Foam board around the slab's edge keeps the edge from getting as cold. Doors kept shut until the air inside and outside match keep the damp air off the floor. A sheet of plastic under the slab keeps ground moisture out from below. A block press or pan mixer on three-phase power gets it from a converter in the shop. A shop this size takes a building permit, and its wiring, plumbing and heat take their own.

**Applied**

1. Lay a polyethylene vapor retarder under the whole slab, lapped and taped, before the pour.
2. Set 2-inch rigid foam, R-10, around the slab perimeter, 2 to 4 feet down or in from the edge.
3. In spring, keep the doors closed until the inside and outside air temperatures match, then ventilate.
4. Add floor heating only for winter comfort, as a separate decision.
5. Run the shop's load calculation from its own list.
6. List lighting, receptacles, the welder, the compressor, the mixer, the chargers and the converter.
7. Install a rotary phase converter sized to the largest three-phase motor, fed from the shop's single-phase panel.
8. Price a utility three-phase drop only when the three-phase load across every building would justify it.
9. Size the feeder from the house service, or the shop's own service, in the electrical drawings.
10. Put its disconnect at the shop.
11. Pull the building permit for the shop, and the electrical, plumbing and mechanical permits for its systems.
12. Put the shop's planned use to the building official in writing before any side business runs in it.

**Professional**

Condensation forms when warm, humid air meets a slab below the air's dew point. In western Oregon the worst case is spring, when the air warms and carries more moisture while the slab, still cold from winter, lags behind it. GreenBuildingAdvisor's slab discussion recommends a perimeter band of rigid foam, which keeps the slab's edge and near-surface mass closer to the air temperature. It pairs the foam with door discipline that stops humid outdoor air being drawn across a cold floor mid-swing, and a polyethylene retarder under the slab against ground moisture.

In the example cited there, insulating a 2-foot perimeter band rather than the whole slab saved about $4,000 on one job. Rigid 2-inch R-10 board runs near $1.00 a square foot before labor. Those are figures from outside Oregon, a planning anchor until an insulation bid prices this footprint. Slab heating is a comfort choice for winter work, and the condensation problem does not require it.

Many block presses and pan mixers run on three-phase motors, and a rural parcel has single-phase service. Phoenix Phase Converters' and North America Phase Converter's price guides put a 10-horsepower rotary converter at $1,500 to $2,000. Common small-shop sizes, 10 to 30 horsepower, run $2,000 to $4,400. Wiring runs $200 to $400 for a 100-foot run, and a panel upgrade where one is needed $800 to $2,000. A licensed electrician installs the unit in one to three days, and the utility bills ordinary single-phase power.

By contrast, a utility three-phase drop to a rural property runs $15,000 to $75,000 and more. Per foot of line extension that is $100 to $200, on the utility's own schedule. For one or two machines run through a campaign, the converter is the choice. Service for the shop is its own load calculation, planned beside the house's and never as a fraction of it. The converter's draw is added to the single-phase motor loads, the welder and the compressor.

The accessory-structure exemption the legal chapter states covers the first small practice structures and a fabric-covered shelter up to 500 square feet over the yard. Far above it, the shop takes a building permit and its trades their own permits. Nor does the agricultural-building exemption reach a block shop, since manufacturing building material appears on none of the statute's listed uses. A later conversion of a never-reviewed structure brings all of it under review. A home business run from the shop is a change of use the official classifies, asked in writing before it starts.

## 26.11 The block-yard pad

**Simple**

On the block-yard pad, units are pressed, set down to cure under cover, stacked and loaded for the wall. For that the pad is flat, hard and drained. Set on an uneven surface or jolted on a soft one, green block cracks. The pad is level and firm, with a fall of 1 or 2 feet in 100 to shed rain. It sits beside the shop and the driveway, because cement and sand arrive by truck for months. It sits away from the house's concrete turnaround, so its mud and dust stay out of the pours. Over the pressing and curing rows, a fabric-covered hoop house keeps the wet season off the work for a few dollars a square foot.

**Applied**

1. Place the pad on the shop's yard side, a short straight haul from the driveway, off the route to the house pad.
2. Size the hardstand at 2,000 to 3,000 square feet, plus bays for sand, aggregate and covered cement.
3. Grade the pad at 1 to 2 percent cross-fall, and keep the cart paths to the wall under 5 percent.
4. Compact a crushed-rock base over the whole yard in logged lifts.
5. Pour a concrete slab under the press station and the curing rows.
6. Where part of the pad will be enclosed later, lay a vapor retarder under it.
7. Thicken that part to the future floor load.
8. Set anchor bolts or sleeves at the post positions of any structure already planned over it.
9. Cut a swale or berm around the pad toward the site's drainage feature.
10. Keep that flow off the septic absorption and reserve areas.
11. Raise a hoop house over the press station and the first curing rows, 3 feet or more from any lot line.
12. Leave the hoop house's ends open or screened for air.
13. Store cement under the hoop or in the shop, never on the open pad.
14. Keep molds, hand tools and the scale in the locked shop or a job box overnight.
15. Book cement and aggregate deliveries on days without ready-mix or pump trucks.
16. Give the yard's trucks a stand off the turnaround.
17. Draw the pad's reuse on the site plan before the slab is poured.

**Professional**

Three haul routes run through the pad for the length of the campaign. Bulk cement and aggregate come in from the driveway, mixed material moves from the mixer to the press or table, and cured units go out to whichever wall is being laid. Beside the shop and near the driveway, the first two stay short. Away from the ready-mix turnaround, excavation mud stays off the pad and batching dust off wet concrete.

No second access point is needed for a yard. Deliveries are staggered by day instead, and a second driveway built to avoid that scheduling costs more than the scheduling. Handling green units, the weigh-a-block check and the production record, the process that runs on the pad, are the owner-made blocks chapter's.

Area follows from the production target and the cure hold. At 100 units a day held damp on pallets for 7 days before stacking, 700 green units stand on the pad at once. One layer deep on 40-by-48-inch pallets at 15 units each, that is 47 pallets, about 620 square feet of pallets before aisles. Cured stock waiting for the wall stacks four layers high on the same pallets. A wall's worth of 6,000 units then occupies 100 pallets and about 1,300 square feet. Mixer and press station, the scale and the cart aisles add a third to a half again, which is where the hardstand figure comes from. The first production week corrects it.

Green units are set down and never slid, so the curing surface is flat and firm. A slight cross-fall sheds rain without tipping a pallet, and the paths to the wall stay gentle enough for a loaded cart.

Under the whole yard a compacted crushed-rock base carries the loader. Under the press station and the curing rows a concrete slab gives the flat, clean surface on which green units survive handling. That slab is the part worth pouring for reuse, because a slab designed only as a yard rarely matches the anchor and footing pattern a later structure needs. Thickness for a future floor load, a vapor retarder under any part to be enclosed, and anchors or sleeves at known post positions go in at the pour. Afterward they cannot be added.

Compacted and graded, the pad sheds more rain and sheds it faster than the ground it replaced. In the site drainage design it therefore counts as impervious area. Its runoff goes to the same downstream feature as the house pad's and the driveway's, and never to the septic absorption or reserve area. Its disturbed area counts toward the one-acre stormwater threshold with everything else.

A hoop house is a bolt-together steel-arch frame under UV-treated polyethylene, raised by two people with hand tools in days. New Mexico State University's Circular 606, revised in 2019, prices a 14-by-32-foot hoop house at about $2.50 a square foot in materials. It gives the cover up to 12 years with care.

UF/IFAS's 2023 study of a 30-by-84-foot kit priced it at $6.16 to $6.72 a square foot with all components and assumed a 4-year cover replacement. Cover is roof and walls only, so the pad under it is built the same, and the tools and molds still go to the shop at night. It provides a dry roof over the campaign for one or two seasons and replaces no part of the shop.

  Concrete sidewalks, slabs, platforms, driveways and similar work are exempt from the building permit, with any electrical, plumbing or mechanical permit still required.
  Source: ORSC R105.2, Building, item 2 (2023 and 2026 editions).

## 26.12 Practice structures and the barn from owner-made block

**Simple**

From your own block, the first wall you lay is a garden wall. Nothing rests on it, and a bad course is torn down for the price of the mortar. The woodshed's base comes second, and it carries a roof. A small enclosed shed comes third, with openings, a roof tied down and vents that have to work. Last comes a barn or garage with a 10-foot door. Over its opening goes a steel or reinforced-block beam, sized by an engineer from the strength of your own block. Each one adds one thing the house wall will demand. Factory trusses come with their own engineering. A homemade truss does not, and the code gives no owner's path to one.

**Applied**

1. Build a site wall of mortared owner-made units under 4 feet, with no load above it, as the first structure.
2. Lay the woodshed's base and knee wall second, to a plan dimension, with a door opening.
3. Build the generator or pump shed third.
4. Give it four walls, a door, a roof tied to a bond beam and the vents for its use.
5. Build the barn or garage last, with the 10-foot opening engineered before the first course.
6. Give the engineer the opening's clear span, the wall height above it, the roof load and the block's tested strength.
7. Grout and reinforce the jamb cells to the engineer's schedule.
8. Bear the lintel at least 4 inches on each jamb, or more where calculated.
9. Order manufactured trusses for any clear span beyond conventional rafter framing, with their design drawings for the building official.
10. Frame a small roof as conventional rafters within the prescriptive limits, or have a site-built truss stamped.
11. Record each practice structure in the production log with its batch and lot, photographs and the date.
12. Price the generator shed three ways, in block, as a pole-barn kit and as a prefab shed, before building it.

**Professional**

Each practice structure adds one requirement of the house wall at a lower consequence. Over a run and a season, a low garden wall proves the unit itself: dimensional consistency, mortar behavior, plumb and level. A woodshed base adds sustained load on the mortar bond and a foundation interface. An enclosed shed adds openings, a roof connection and the base and head flashing details, with a real safety function in the generator shed's vents.

The barn adds the first engineered element, a lintel over a 10-foot-class opening, at the scale of the house's own openings. Consequence of a mistake sets the order. Each standing structure is also acceptance evidence, a wall of the material two winters old with its production log on file.

Over a 10-foot opening in a block wall goes a steel angle, a fabricated steel beam or a reinforced grouted lintel block. All three are sized for the end reaction the wall and roof above deliver. The design span is the clear opening plus the lintel's depth, not beyond the distance between support centers.

Masonry design references give a minimum bearing length as a starting point. Real bearing length, the jamb reinforcing and the grouted cells follow from the reaction and the unit's tested compressive strength. For an owner-made unit that strength is the program's own number and not a catalog value. TR91B, the Concrete Masonry and Hardscapes Association's lintel design manual, and TMS 402 hold the tables, and the structural chapter owns the calculation.

Trusses ordered from a plant arrive with the drawings the official asks for, and a kit's trusses carry them. A gambrel or scissor shape built in the shop is an engineered design, stamped before it is cut. Otherwise it is an unengineered build whose risk the owner accepts and the official may refuse on any permitted structure. Framed as conventional rafters, a small roof stays inside the prescriptive framing limits, which carry their own wind, exposure and snow ceilings. Even the Structural Building Components Association's span tables describe themselves as guidelines and not a substitute for engineered design.

Three prices, from the project's own inputs, make the comparison. For the block shed they come from the slab, the unit count and the labor hours per unit in the block program. For the pole shed the kit price serves, and for the prefab a dealer's quote. One published anchor exists. Backwoods Home Magazine's account of a pole garage with an attached root cellar and bunkhouse put the garage and bunkhouse at $4,100 and the whole project at $5,200 in 2011 and 2012 dollars. It was a pole-frame build rather than a block one.

## 26.13 The generator shed and the battery shed

**Simple**

A running engine fills a closed shed with carbon monoxide. The shed therefore has an opening low on one wall and another high on the opposite wall. Hot air leaves at the top and pulls fresh air in below, and the exhaust is piped through the wall. Their size comes from the generator's own data sheet. Block walls keep the required distances short, because block does not burn. Noise at the property line is a county or city rule. A heavy steel enclosure with a sound-absorbing lining takes most of the hum out. The battery lives in the house. Only if no room can be found does it go into a shed, insulated, with a small heater.

**Applied**

1. Site the generator to its maker's clearances, with the exhaust end away from the house's openings and the prevailing wind.
2. For Generac's units, keep 18 inches behind it and 3 feet at the front and ends.
3. Keep 5 feet overhead and 5 feet from any opening.
4. Never place it under a wooden deck or platform.
5. Build the shed of block, with a low intake on one wall and a high outlet on the opposite wall.
6. Size both openings to the maker's airflow figure.
7. Run the exhaust through the wall in a listed pipe.
8. Keep those clearances free of shrubs, fuel and stored items.
9. Fit vibration mounts under the unit, and line the shed with a sound-absorbing inner layer behind an impervious outer one.
10. Ask the county or city for its noise ordinance and its property-line limit before choosing the enclosure.
11. Put the battery inside the conditioned house wherever a room allows.
12. Where a shed is the only place, insulate it and paint it a light color.
13. Hold the pack at 41 to 50 °F with a thermostat and heater.
14. Add a vent fan on a thermostat for summer, and put the heater's and fan's watts on the load list.

**Professional**

NFPA 37 sizes a generator room's ventilation to the engine's combustion air and cooling air, per Facility Compliance Hub's summary of the standard. It positions the openings so exhaust is not drawn back into the intake, and it states no fixed square-inch figure. On the manufacturer's data sheet are the cooling airflow in cubic feet per minute and the combustion-air demand. Low intake and high outlet are sized to deliver that flow through the louvers' net free area at an acceptable pressure drop. That is a mechanical calculation made once the model is chosen. Stack effect drives the flow without power: heated air leaves high and draws fresh air in low.

Generac's clearance article applies its reduced wall clearance only to models marked with the SwRI logo. It keeps other models 5 feet from combustible walls and defers to local codes where they are stricter. Placement under NFPA 37, the propane tank's distances and the two-wire start are in the solar chapter.

Oregon's statewide noise rules, OAR 340-035-0035, apply to industrial and commercial noise sources and exempt emergency equipment not run on a regular schedule. A residential standby generator therefore falls under the city or county ordinance where one exists, and those vary, some following the League of Oregon Cities' model ordinance.

CS Diesel Generators' enclosure-level summary puts standard sound-attenuated enclosures at 63 to 89 dB(A) by level and measuring distance. Heavy 12-to-14-gauge steel enclosures reach up to 40 dB(A) below an open unit. Commercial Acoustics' design guide credits a sandwich panel with a further 25 to 30 dB of insertion loss. The panel is an impervious outer layer, a porous inner layer and mass-loaded vinyl, tuned to the 125-to-250-hertz hum. Airborne noise and structure-borne vibration travel separately, and the mounts handle the second. Installed prices for an enclosure and mounts are a quote against the chosen model and the target level.

Lithium iron phosphate cells charge only above freezing and work best near room temperature, and a thermostat holds a shed-housed pack inside that window. In this climate a well-insulated shed has a winter heating load and a small summer one. A thermostatic heater meets the first, and a light-colored exterior and a vent fan meet most of the second. The pack's own charge and discharge heat helps hold temperature once the enclosure is sealed. Insulation value and heater wattage are a heat-loss calculation for the chosen shed, pack and design temperature. The thermostat, heater and fan are commodity parts priced at purchase. Inside the conditioned envelope the pack avoids all of it, which is why the shed is the fallback.

## 26.14 The pump house

**Simple**

Over the well, the small building does two jobs. It keeps the pressure tank, the filters and the pipes from freezing, and it lets a service truck pull the pump when it fails. Freeze protection means an enclosed, heated space. Pulling a pump takes a clear vertical path above the casing for the rig's mast. Two designs meet both. An insulated shed with a small thermostat heater keeps the tank above freezing, and its roof or a wall panel comes off for the rig. Or the tank and pipes go into a pit dug below frost depth under a false floor. There the earth keeps them warm, and the shed above can be plain and cold.

**Applied**

1. Set the pitless adapter and every buried fitting below the frost depth the building department gives for the parcel.
2. Before the well is drilled, choose between an insulated heated shed and a frost-protected pit under a false floor.
3. For a pit, dig below frost depth and lay plastic sheeting under it against ground moisture.
4. Give the pit a low vent and a high vent.
5. Keep the wellhead's vertical path clear with a removable roof section or wall panel.
6. Or set the wellhead outside the shed under a hinged cover.
7. Ask the well contractor how far the service rig's mast must stand from the casing.
8. Draw that clearance on the plan.
9. Put the pump house on the well's service side, reachable by the rig over the driveway base.
10. Record the frost depth used, the pit depth and the clearance in the binder.

**Professional**

The pressure tank's place, in the mechanical room or an insulated pump house with a small thermostatic heater, and the pitless adapter's depth below frost are the water supply chapter's decisions. This plan adds the structure around them. Frost depth is a location-specific value from the jurisdiction's adopted table, varying by elevation and region across Oregon, and the pit's floor goes below it.

Below the frost line, a pit uses the earth's temperature as the freeze protection. The shed above can then be an uninsulated, unheated structure that also stores hoses and tools. Air still has to move through the pit, since gases collect in an enclosed one, and a plastic sheet under it cuts the moisture the ventilation has to carry.

Pulling a stuck pump or a failed tank is vertical work, and a well-service truck's boom or mast positions over the wellhead to do it. The roof framing and walls therefore keep that line clear. Stand-off and mast height are the contractor's equipment, asked before the shed is drawn.

## 26.15 Greenhouse and woodshed

**Simple**

A passive-solar greenhouse keeps its heat overnight. Glass on the south is matched by an insulated solid wall on the north and by water barrels or a dark concrete floor inside. The mass soaks up the day's heat and releases it at night. Proportioned this way, a greenhouse holds the night air well above the outdoor temperature. It still gets vents and a fan, because a sunny day in February can overheat it. Its knee wall is a short run of your own block. A woodshed is the opposite: open to the wind on two sides, off the ground on a block base. Firewood burns well below 20 percent moisture, which takes at least six months of air.

**Applied**

1. Face the glazing south, and make the north wall solid and insulated.
2. Keep the glazed area and the insulated area near equal.
3. Add thermal mass in the sun's path: 4 to 5 gallons of water per square foot of glazing, or a dark concrete floor.
4. Fit ridge vents, operable side vents and a circulating fan, and run them on a thermostat.
5. Build the foundation and knee wall from owner-made block, low, as one of the first practice structures.
6. Where the greenhouse shares a wall with the shop or the house, make that wall concrete or block.
7. Leave no framed cavity open to the greenhouse air.
8. Insulate the greenhouse face of a shared mass wall where condensation would otherwise form on it.
9. Build the woodshed on a one-course block base.
10. Give it a slatted or open upper structure and a roof that overhangs the stack.
11. Leave 1 to 2 inches between wall boards and 2 to 4 inches between rows of wood.
12. Stack split wood with the top covered and the sides open, and keep it at least six months before burning.
13. Check a split face with a moisture meter before it goes in the stove, at 20 percent or below.
14. Count a cord as a stack 4 by 4 by 8 feet.
15. Size the shed for a winter's use plus next winter's wood.

**Professional**

Sierra Greenhouse's design guide (a maker's publication) states the passive-solar proportions as a near-even split between south glazing and an insulated opaque north wall. Mass is stored as water, dark stone or a dark concrete floor. It reports that the proportioned house holds 15 to 20 °F above ambient overnight in a Zone 5 winter. An all-glass house, which absorbs more heat than it retains, swings 25 °F or more. Those figures are national and not calibrated to western Oregon's cloud, and the first winter's thermometer record is the local calibration.

Ventilation is an active system in every passive-solar greenhouse: ridge vents, side vents and a fan. Solar gain in a sealed glazed volume passes plant-damaging temperatures on a sunny day in any month.

Against a shared mass wall, a greenhouse stores heat in the wall and releases part of it to the room behind, the same storage a passive-solar room uses. It also adds a humidity load that a framed wall's cavity cannot tolerate. Against concrete or block the rot risk is low, and condensation on the greenhouse face of an under-insulated mass wall remains. The shared wall is therefore detailed with the envelope chapter's vapor and air-sealing rules. Net heat gain is a building-science calculation for the chosen assembly before it enters the heating budget.

The moisture limit and the seasoning time come from Burn Wise, the U.S. EPA program that ran from 2009 to 2025. Its pages now stand as a historical record. It adds that wood is stored outdoors, off the ground, with only the top covered, since covering the sides traps moisture.

Air reaching the stack from two sides is the design, so an open or slatted upper structure on a block base outperforms a closed shed. One course of owner-made units lifts the bottom row off the ground without a wood sill or post in soil contact, where wood rots. Gaps between slats and between rows are this plan's choice, wide enough for air and narrow enough to keep rain off the ends. Cord count follows the stove's season and a second season drying.

## 26.16 The root cellar

**Simple**

With no electricity, a root cellar keeps potatoes, carrots and cabbage all winter. Root crops keep best just above freezing in damp air, and squash and onions on a cool, dry shelf elsewhere. In the wet Pacific Northwest the design is a concrete box set into a slope and bermed with earth. It has a drain on the downhill side, a north-facing entry, and a floor part concrete and part gravel so the room stays damp. Air moves through it on its own: a low pipe brings cold night air in and a high pipe lets warm air out. A thermometer and a humidity gauge show whether it works. A small fan is added only when the readings miss.

**Applied**

1. Decide the configuration before the foundation is designed.
2. Choose among a standalone bermed cellar, a room in the house's foundation and a corner of a below-grade shop.
3. For a standalone cellar, cut into the slope and berm the exposed sides.
4. Face the entry north and shade the door.
5. Pour the walls in lifts of 4 feet or less.
6. Set walers at 12 inches and brace the form base twice.
7. Have the engineer size the walls, floor and roof for the soil and groundwater pressure, and the reinforcing with them.
8. Lay a perforated drain in gravel on the downhill side, daylighting below the floor.
9. Add a vertical cleanout pipe to a sump point.
10. Keep every wood member out of soil contact, or separate it from the soil with a waterproof membrane.
11. Pour a concrete perimeter and structural floor, and leave a gravel or earth section of the floor open for humidity.
12. Run two vent pipes, each near 12 inches across.
13. Set the intake low on the cool side and the exhaust high through the roof.
14. Hang a thermometer and a hygrometer, and log them through the first year.
15. Add a small fan on the exhaust only when the log shows the targets missed.
16. Store squash and onions in a cool, dry room instead.
17. Where the cellar touches the house's foundation, continue the house's soil-gas retarder and air seal across the shared slab and wall.

**Professional**

University of Minnesota Extension's harvesting-and-storage page, reviewed in 2026, defines root cellar conditions as cold and moist. It puts potatoes at 32 to 40 °F and 95 percent relative humidity, with carrots, beets and cabbage also cold and moist. Onions store cold and dry at 65 percent, and winter squash cool and dry at 50 to 60 °F and 60 percent. No one room provides both sets of conditions, so squash and onions leave the cellar.

Dorothy Ainsworth's account in Backwoods Home Magazine, issue 168 of November and December 2017, is the built example. Her cellar began as a 12-by-16-foot excavation 8 feet deep. Walls went up in low pours between closely spaced walers. They carried 5/8-inch bar on a 16-inch grid, with a 12-inch grid in the floor and a 6-inch grid in the roof. About 21 cubic yards of concrete went in over two months. The whole job cost $5,000, with $2,000 of it concrete, in the dollars of a build about 20 years before the article.

On the downhill side a length of corrugated pipe set vertically waits for a sump pump that in those 20 years was never lowered. A wind-turbine vent on the roof moves the air, and the interior holds 50 to 70 °F year round.

That interior sits above the root-crop range in the Minnesota figures. Cover and drainage keep the structure dry, and the two-duct vent, drawing cold night air in low and venting high, is what lowers a cellar toward that range.

Pacific Northwest root-cellar threads on permies.com add the regional experience. A French drain of perforated pipe in gravel is the standard answer to a wet site. Earth-berming into a slope manages moisture better than full burial in this climate. Buried wood rots at the ground line within a decade, cedar included, so a membrane separates soil from any wood. Split flooring, a concrete perimeter and structural slab with a gravel or earth section left open, is the practiced compromise. It trades between the temperature stability of earth-coupled concrete and the humidity bare ground releases. Its proportion is the owner's.

Stack effect drives the ventilation through two ducts, low intake and high exhaust. Vent size follows the built example as an anchor, and the first year's thermometer and hygrometer record is the test. A fan goes on the exhaust only when the readings show the passive draft falling behind the crop's respiration heat and moisture. A site whose layout gives little draft is the other case.

Three configurations are possible, and each trades cost against control. Standing alone and bermed, a cellar controls its own temperature and humidity and costs the most, with its own excavation and structure. Built as a room in the house's foundation, it shares the excavation and the work and inherits the foundation's insulation strategy. That strategy is designed for the house's comfort and not a cellar's cold, so the shared boundary takes separate detailing. Walled off in a corner of a below-grade shop, it costs least where that space exists and gives the least capacity and control.

Attached to or inside the house's footprint, a cellar also becomes part of the envelope the radon system protects. Its slab, walls and every penetration between the spaces then continue the soil-gas retarder and the air seal under the envelope chapter's radon detailing. Otherwise the cellar becomes the path that bypasses it. Standing alone, it avoids that interaction, and with its temperature control that is why it is the default here.

FEMA funds residential safe rooms through its Hazard Mitigation Assistance grants, built to FEMA P-361 and the 2020 ICC/NSSA 500 storm-shelter standard. The Congressional Research Service puts its share near 75 percent of eligible cost. Whether Oregon or the target county funds a residential safe room is a question for the State Hazard Mitigation Officer. Oregon's tornado exposure is low beside the states where the program concentrates. Built to ICC 500, a cellar is a separate design and cost decision, since the standard's wind and debris-impact requirements are not met by an earth-coupled cellar built well.

## 26.17 The pantry, the freezers and the food reserve

**Simple**

The pantry is sized from a list of what it holds. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints publishes a per-person list for a year's storage. One adult takes 112 large cans of dry food, plus salt, oil and fats. That is about 29 feet of shelf per person. At 50 to 70 degrees the cans keep for decades, and a hot garage or attic shortens that. The oldest can comes off the shelf first, and sloped racks that feed it to the front make the rotation automatic. Chest freezers hold the meat and the garden surplus. In an outage they run on the battery, so their number and size go on the house's power list.

**Applied**

1. Set the household count and the months of reserve, and multiply the handout's one-year figures by them.
2. For each adult-year, plan 54 number-ten cans of grains and 12 of beans.
3. Add 12 cans of dried milk or 28 pouches, and 12 of sugar or honey.
4. Add 6 cans of dried apples, 12 of potato flakes, 3 of dried carrots and 1 of onions per adult-year.
5. Add 8 pounds of salt and 2 gallons of oil per adult-year.
6. Add 9 pounds of shortening, 6 of butter and 6 of peanut butter.
7. Convert the can count to shelf at two cans deep on a 12-inch shelf, 56 cans a row, about 29 feet per adult-year.
8. Store the cans in the pantry at 50 to 70 °F, out of the garage and the attic.
9. Zone the pantry into weekly staples, the deep reserve and home-canned goods on separate shelves, heaviest low.
10. Fit gravity-fed can racks or a rotating drum rack so the oldest stock comes to the front.
11. Reset the rotation when groceries come in, and audit the reserve twice a year.
12. Pack bulk grain in Mylar bags with oxygen absorbers inside food-grade buckets for the long store.
13. Put each freezer's EnergyGuide kilowatt-hours on the house load list, and keep the freezers on the backed-up circuits.
14. Site any canning kitchen in a bay of the shop or a lean-to beside it.
15. Give it water, a drain, a 240-volt circuit and an exhaust fan.
16. Keep a smaller canning operation in the house kitchen.

**Professional**

Food Storage, the handout (PD60004682, version 7/17, 2019), lists per-person quantities for one month and one year with shelf lives of 10 to 30 years. It advises storing at a moderate, even temperature away from attics and garages, because temperature swings shorten shelf life. Its planning intake is 2,000 to 2,400 calories a day. Its figures are in cans rather than pounds.

The Church's long-term storage page adds that a pouch holds 7 pounds of wheat, 6.8 pounds of white rice or 5 pounds of dry milk. A number-ten can measures 6-3/16 inches across and 7 inches tall, which is where the shelf arithmetic comes from. Shelving for the pantry, its design load and its anchors into concrete are the interiors chapter's. Ready.gov asks for a several-day supply of non-perishable food and counts refrigerated food safe for four hours without power, so the Church's list is the long-duration figure.

Gravity racks and zoned shelves make first-in-first-out physical. A new can loads at the back and the oldest rolls to the front, and the weekly staples never draw down the reserve by accident. Heavy canned goods sit low for lifting and against shelf sag over years. Mylar bags with oxygen absorbers inside food-grade buckets are the standard long store for dry staples. Commercial freeze-dried buckets are a purchasable category whose current price per calorie is a quote at purchase time.

Each chest freezer's draw, and the day or two it holds temperature full and closed, are in the load list. Neither the root cellar nor the pantry adds any load at all. That is the system-level return on building all three: every pound of food kept without power is capacity the battery does not have to buy.

Canning at scale puts hours of steam and heat into a room a few weeks a year. A dedicated space outside the main kitchen is a long-standing rural pattern. Here it is a bay of the shop or a roofed lean-to beside it, plumbed and vented, if the household cans at that scale. Its circuits and exhaust are drawn with the shop's service, and the main kitchen carries a smaller operation. Scale is the household's call before the shop's drawings are final.

## 26.19 Problems and solutions

**Simple**

On a site like this, the common failures are placement failures. An aggregate pile sits across the pump truck's path, a shop stands beside the bedroom window, a yard pad takes the excavation's mud. Water causes the rest, in a driveway without a crown, a wall without a drain and a cellar without a vent. For each, the table lists what you see and what to do.

**Applied**

| Problem | What you see | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Driveway ruts under the first mixer truck | Wheel tracks 4 inches deep, base rock pushed into the mud | Excavate the failed length, lay geotextile, rebuild the full heavy-truck section, and stop deliveries until it is compacted |
| Culvert overtops in a storm | Water across the drive, scour at the outlet | Clear the inlet each autumn, armor the outlet, and have the pipe resized where it is undersized |
| Material pile blocks the truck route | The pump truck cannot reach its stand | Redraw the laydown plan each phase, and keep bulk piles beside the yard, off the route |
| Mud on the curing pad | Green units chipped and cracked where carts bogged | Separate the excavation's route from the pad, and give the pad its own swale and a gravel apron |
| Shop slab drips in spring | Puddles on the floor, rust on the tools in April | Keep the doors shut until the air temperatures match, add perimeter foam, run a dehumidifier |
| Container walls run with water | Condensation on the steel inside, mold on stored goods | Closed-cell spray foam on the steel, or board with a sealed vapor retarder, and ventilation |
| Shop too close to the house | Compressor and grinder audible in the bedrooms after move-in | Place the shop for its permanent use, and put the loud work on the far wall with mass between |
| Deer under the fence | Tracks under a dip, a stripped garden row | Fill every dip to ground level, and keep the bottom wire down |
| Gate post leans | The gate drags and will not latch | Reset the post in a concrete footing sized to the gate, or replace a rotted wood post with galvanized steel |
| Swing gate stuck in snow | Drift inside the arc | Change to a slide gate, or clear the arc before the opener strains |
| Gate opener dead in December | Battery flat under fog | A larger panel and battery than the kit's minimum |
| Fence on the wrong line | A survey finds the fence inside the neighbor's land | Remove it within a year of discovering the mistake, as the statute allows, and rebuild on the surveyed line |
| Retaining wall bulges | Face bowed out after a wet winter, water weeping through joints | Excavate the backfill, add the wall rock, fabric and toe drain, and rebuild the bowed courses |
| Generator fumes in the shed | Headache near the shed, soot at the vents | Stop the unit, open the shed, and rebuild the intake, outlet and exhaust to the maker's airflow and clearances |
| Cellar too warm or too dry | Potatoes sprouting, carrots limp | Open the intake on cold nights, add the gravel floor section, and add an exhaust fan when the log shows it |
| Buried wood rotting | A post soft at the ground line within ten years | Keep wood out of soil contact, or membrane between soil and wood |
| Pipes freeze in the pump house during an outage | No water the morning after a cold night without power | Deepen to the pit design, or add insulation and a battery-backed heater circuit |
| Truss cut for a door or a duct | A chord notched or a web removed | Stop, and have the truss maker or an engineer specify the repair |
| Exempt structures questioned | The official asks whether the cluster is one building | Get the written position before the second structure, and keep each inside the limits with space between |

**Professional**

Most rows in the table share two mechanisms, water and placement. Water adds load and removes support. It pushes on a wall built for soil and softens a subgrade under a base built for a lighter truck. It condenses on any mass colder than the air's dew point. Placement decided for the build's convenience becomes a permanent condition after it: the shop's noise, the pile across the route, the pad's mud in the pour. Deer fence, gate post and fence line are the exceptions, and each fails from an omitted detail at the ground: an unfilled dip, an undersized footing, an unsurveyed line.
