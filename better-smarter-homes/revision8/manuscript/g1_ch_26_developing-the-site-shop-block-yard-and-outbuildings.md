# Chapter 26: Developing the Site: Shop, Block-Yard, and Outbuildings

Everything on this parcel besides the house goes up in a fixed order: the driveway first, because every truck for the next several years arrives over it, then temporary power and water, then the shop, then the block-yard, and the house shell once the block campaign is under way. The shop is sized at 1,500 to 2,400 square feet, and its place on the plan is chosen for the decades of use after the build. A small accessory building needs no building permit under the exemption the legal chapter states, which is how the first practice walls and the yard's fabric shelter go up before the house plans are final. Costs are 2026 figures unless a different year is given.

## 26.1 What gets built, and in what order

**Simple**

The driveway comes first, because nothing else can be delivered until a loaded truck can reach the site. Temporary power and water come next, so tools, lights and the mixer have something to run on. Then the shop, or at least a roof and a locked door, so tools and cement stay dry. Then the block-yard and the months of block making. The house foundation starts once the campaign is well along. Making block and digging the foundation at the same time can save a season, but the two share one driveway, one turnaround and one pair of hands. Mud from the excavation ends up on the curing pad, and dust from the mixer settles on wet concrete.

**Applied**

1. Build the driveway to its full base before the first loaded truck, with the turnaround.
2. Bring in temporary power and water, and set the generator and the washout pit where the shop will stand.
3. Raise the shop shell, or a fabric shelter and a locked job box, before buying the block equipment.
4. Grade and pave the block-yard pad, then run the production campaign.
5. Start the foundation when the stock of cured block covers the first lifts of wall.
6. Overlap the campaign with excavation only where the pad and the house pad have separate truck routes.
7. On an overlap, give each weekday to one operation, and book the two sets of deliveries on different days.
8. Redraw the laydown plan at every phase: where each material sits, which route trucks take, and what stays locked.
9. Stack aggregate, sand and cement beside the yard and the shop, never on the route to the house pad.
10. Keep rebar and lumber off the ground on dunnage, under a roof, through the wet season.
11. Lock pipe, wire, conduit and fixtures in the shop or a job box.
12. Stage cured block on a short, all-weather path to the wall, clear of the concrete-truck turnaround.

**Professional**

Each phase in that order supplies the next. The driveway carries the excavator, the aggregate and the ready-mix. Temporary power runs the mixer, the saws and the lights, and temporary water feeds the mixer and the curing cover. The shop stores the tools and the cement, and the yard produces the units the foundation and the walls consume. Running the block campaign and the excavation at once compresses the calendar by up to the shorter of the two durations, since neither waits on the other's output. Interference is the cost. Ready-mix and pump trucks compete with cement and aggregate deliveries for one driveway and one turnaround, excavation mud tracks onto the pad where green units are handled, and dust from dry batching settles on fresh concrete. Where the two work zones and their truck routes are separated, the overlap gains that time. On a single-access site the interference costs more days than the overlap saves.

The owner redraws the laydown plan at every phase, keeping the laydown zone apart from the working footprint so deliveries never block the work. Heavy, weather-tolerant materials that feed the campaign for months, aggregate, sand and cement under cover, sit beside the yard and the shop. Reinforcing bar rusts and lumber swells on bare ground in an Oregon winter, so both ride on dunnage under a roof. Pipe, conduit, wire and fixtures are small, costly and portable, and they stay locked. One pile, placed for convenience early, that later blocks the only truck route to the foundation is the failure the plan prevents, since moving it a second time costs a machine day or a week of hand labor.

### Living on the parcel before the house exists

**Simple**

Whether you may live on the land before the house is finished is a county zoning question, and the answer differs by county. Klamath County requires the house to be built before any accessory structure. It lets a family live in an RV on the parcel under a temporary use permit once the house's building permit is issued. Without that permit, camping there is limited to 21 days in any six months. Some owners build the shop first with a small apartment inside it, so the shop is the parcel's one dwelling until the house is done. A shop built for work was never inspected as a home, and living in it before it is permitted as one is a violation of its own.

**Applied**

1. Ask the county planning department in writing whether any accessory structure may be built before the dwelling, and under what condition.
2. Ask for the temporary-occupancy permit by name, with its fee, its renewal and its trigger.
3. Ask the insurer, before moving onto the parcel, how the policy treats occupancy of an unfinished or uncertified structure.
4. Keep the shop permitted as a shop unless the county's answer leaves the apartment route as the only one.

**Professional**

A dwelling-first rule is a zoning sequence, separate from the building code's permit exemption, which conditions nothing on a house being present. Klamath County's planning FAQ states the rule directly and names the two routes around it: a temporary use permit for an RV, at $450 to apply and $150 a year to renew, and a shop permitted with a small apartment as the parcel's one allowed dwelling, converted back once the house is occupied. Whether the target county follows that pattern is confirmed with its planning department once a parcel is known. Occupying any structure as a dwelling waits on its own certificate of occupancy, under the rules the legal chapter states. Insurance is a separate question, answered by the policy's own wording: whether a homeowner's or builder's-risk policy excludes occupancy of an unfinished structure or requires disclosure of it.

## 26.2 One plan for everything that is not the house

**Simple**

The shop, the yard and the outbuildings go onto the site plan last, after the septic field, the well, the house, the driveway and the sun's path to the panels have taken their ground. They go last because they can move and the others cannot. A building within a few feet of a property line needs fire-rated walls and no windows on that side. A shop placed to save steps during the build sits beside your living room for decades afterward, with its noise and its traffic. Anything tall south of the panels shades them, and anything close against a wall of the house keeps that wall damp and mossy. While the house trench is open, an empty conduit goes to every spot an outbuilding will ever stand.

**Applied**

1. Lock the septic and reserve areas, the well, the hazard zones, the driveway, the house and the solar envelope first.
2. Place the shop, the yard and the outbuildings in the ground that remains.
3. Draw the accessory-dwelling zone and keep every outbuilding out of it.
4. Draw each outbuilding at its full height and footprint, and check its shadow against the array's winter sun lines.
5. Keep the shop, the woodshed, the greenhouse and any tall fence off the house's south, east and west walls.
6. Hold every outbuilding wall 3 feet or more from the property line, or design that wall as fire-rated with no openings.
7. Keep every outbuilding foundation outside the septic setbacks and the well's service clearance.
8. Place the shop where it serves the household after the build.
9. Place the yard between the shop and the driveway, on the far side of the shop from the house's truck turnaround.
10. Where a wanted outbuilding site conflicts with a fixed element, move the outbuilding.
11. Run a spare power conduit and a data conduit to every fixed outbuilding location and to the accessory-dwelling zone while the house trench is open.
12. Oversize the power conduit, or lay a second empty one, and cap and stake both ends.
13. Photograph every sleeve with a tape to two fixed points before backfill, and draw it on the as-built site plan.

**Professional**

Outbuildings are the most movable elements on the plan, so they absorb the conflicts the fixed elements create. The lock order, the septic and well rings and the accessory-dwelling zone are the land chapter's, and three constraints bind an accessory structure once its ground is chosen.

The residential code's exterior-wall table exists to protect a neighbor's building from radiant heat across the lot line, and Oregon's table draws its line closer to the lot line than the model code does. An outbuilding pushed up against the line loses its windows on that side and gains a rated wall, so the plan holds the distance instead. A permit-exempt detached accessory structure is excused from the wall protection, which is one more reason a first practice shed stays inside the exemption. Accessory structures take the county's own zoning setback, set apart from the dwelling's, and the figure is read from the ordinance once a parcel is chosen.

OSU Extension's moss guidance identifies shade and sustained moisture as the conditions that grow moss on roofs, walls and paving, and recommends thinning or removing the vegetation that shades a surface. A shop, a woodshed or a tall fence close to a house wall shades it as a tree does, so outbuildings stand clear of the walls that dry slowest. The array's shadow lines, drawn at the mature height of each tree and the full height of each planned structure, close the ground south of the panels to anything tall.

Distance between the shop and the house trades haul time during the build against noise, fumes and vehicle traffic for the decades after it. The build ends and the proximity does not, so the plan weights the permanent condition. The yard is different: its hardstand has no use after the campaign unless its reuse is designed in, so it sits near the driveway and the shop, where deliveries are short, and on the far side of the shop from the house's turnaround, where its mud and dust stay out of the concrete work.

An open trench costs the width of a second conduit. The same trench reopened after the driveway, the fence and the landscape are in costs excavation, restoration and a week of disruption, and the figure for a specific route comes from the excavation contractor's bid. Power conduit sized only to the load known at the time leaves no room for a larger feeder, and a shop or an accessory dwelling built years on needs a data path as well, so each fixed location gets an oversized power conduit or a second empty one, with a communications conduit beside it. The feeder and its disconnect at a separate building follow the electrical code's rules for a second structure, drawn with the house's service.

**Code**

```
An accessory building near the lot line carries a rated wall with no openings.
  At a fire separation distance under 3 feet: walls rated 1 hour from both sides, no openings, no projections under 2 feet and rated projections from 2 to 3 feet, penetrations per R302.4.
  Source: ORSC R302.1 and Table R302.1(1) (2023 edition).

A permit-exempt detached accessory structure needs no wall protection for its location.
  Detached nonhabitable accessory structures exempted from permits are not required to provide wall protection based on location on the lot, and projections shall not extend over the lot line.
  Source: ORSC R302.1, exception 3 (2023 edition).
```

## 26.3 The driveway base, built once

**Simple**

A gravel driveway built for cars fails under the first loaded concrete truck. The repair means excavating and rebuilding the base, replacing the surface, and losing the delivery day. A car needs 6 to 8 inches of compacted crushed rock. A mixer truck at 66,000 to 80,000 pounds needs 12 to 18 inches on good ground, and more on clay. Angular crushed rock locks together under the roller, and round river gravel rolls apart. The driveway also has to shed water: a crown in the middle, ditches at the sides, and culverts that are cleaned before the autumn rains.

**Applied**

1. Get the subgrade classification before specifying the base, and specify the full heavy-truck section wherever ready-mix and pump trucks will travel.
2. On clay or soft subgrade, lay a woven geotextile on the cut subgrade before the first lift.
3. Place 4 to 6 inches of 1-to-2-1/2-inch crushed stone as the subbase.
4. Place 3 to 4 inches of crusher-run base, then 2 to 3 inches of fine crushed surface rock.
5. Compact every lift at 4 inches or less with a reversible plate or a roller, and log each lift.
6. Build the full section before the excavator arrives.
7. Crown the surface so water runs to the ditches, and cut the ditches to a downhill outlet.
8. Each autumn before the rains, clear the culvert inlets and outlets and the ditch lines.
9. Grade out washboard and edge ruts when they appear.
10. Where the base itself has sunk, add base rock and recompact before grading the surface.
11. Mark any utility conduit crossing under the driveway on the site plan, with its depth.

**Professional**

Base-course depth scales with wheel load. Eger Excavating's and Hello Gravel's driveway guides put a passenger-car driveway at 6 to 8 inches of compacted gravel and a driveway carrying trucks of 14,000 pounds and up at 12 to 18 inches of total compacted base on good subgrade. Both guides build that section as a subbase of larger stone, a crusher-run base and a fine surface course, compacted in thin lifts, and both prefer angular crushed stone, which interlocks under compaction, to rounded gravel. On heavy clay one of them specifies 12 to 16 inches of base and a geotextile separation layer, because without the fabric the base rock works down into a soft subgrade under repeated loads and the section thins from below. The subgrade classification, the culvert sizing and the fish-passage review are the soil chapter's, and the geotechnical engineer sets the final section for the loaded ready-mix truck against the subgrade.

A base built light for early access and then loaded by concrete trucks fails by rutting and by base loss into the subgrade. The repair rebuilds the same base course, replaces the surface and stops deliveries while it is done, so the light base is paid for twice. Staging the base saves money only where cash is unavailable before construction starts and the light base survives the whole heavy-truck phase, which the load-to-depth relationship makes unlikely.

A crowned surface sheds rain to the ditches before it runs down the wheel paths and cuts them. Washboard and edge rutting come from traffic on a surface short of fines or a base short of compaction, and grading restores the crown and the profile. Where the base has failed rather than the surface, grading alone lasts one season, and fresh base rock recompacted under the surface is the repair. A culvert half full of silt backs water against the fill in the first storm, which is why the clean-out comes before the wet season.

## 26.4 The turnaround and the entrance: gate or cattle guard

**Simple**

One graded, based turnaround serves both the fire engine the code plans for and the concrete and pump trucks the build needs. The fire code sets its shape and size, and the concrete truck sets how thick its base must be. At the road the plan puts a cattle guard, a steel grid over a shallow pit, in place of a gate. Cars cross it without slowing, and hoofed animals will not step onto it. It costs more to install than a gate and almost nothing to maintain for thirty years. Where a gate is chosen instead, a solar-powered opener runs it without grid power, and the fire district has to be able to open it in an emergency.

**Applied**

1. Draw the fire turnaround to the shape the fire district accepts, and build it to the heavy-truck base section.
2. Make the turnaround large enough for a ready-mix truck and a pump truck to stand in it at once.
3. Put the turnaround and the yard's delivery stand on opposite sides of the drive where the ground allows.
4. Before choosing a gate or a guard, get the fire marshal's written answer on the opening width and the emergency operation.
5. For a cattle guard, order a 20-foot unit with wing panels, and pour its footings to the maker's drawing.
6. Drain the pit under the guard to daylight, and clean it of silt every autumn.
7. For a gate, choose a slide gate where snow would block a swing gate's arc.
8. Buy a solar opener kit sized above the maker's minimum for the gate's weight, with a second panel and a larger battery for December.
9. Fit the fire department's emergency release the marshal names, and submit the lock method for approval.
10. Keep the approach-permit drawing, the marshal's letter and the guard or opener data in the binder.

**Professional**

The fire code's turnaround geometry, with the driveway's width, grade and surface strength, is in the land chapter. A turnaround is a shape requirement and a pump truck's stand is a load requirement, and the same graded area built to the heavy-truck base meets both. Built to a light residential base, a code-shaped turnaround still fails under the first pour.

A locked gate across the only access stops an engine at 3 AM unless the fire department can open it, which is why the appendix regulates a gate's width, its construction and its lock, and why an electric gate carries an emergency release. The fire code official holds the discretion to modify the access rules where no more than two unsprinklered one- and two-family dwellings are served (Oregon Fire Code 503.1.1, exception 1), so the opening width at this driveway is the district's written answer.

Residential solar openers pair a 10-to-30-watt panel with a 12-volt battery. Ghost Controls and ALEKO sell the kits, and one of them, the JUJIANG C026, pairs a 20-watt panel with two 12-volt 8-amp-hour batteries for a single leaf up to 770 pounds or 16 feet. Makers size panel and battery to the gate's weight and daily cycles and publish no Oregon-winter figure, and a kit sized to its minimum under a sunny-climate assumption runs flat in a western Oregon December. Swing or slide follows the site: a slide gate needs clear run-back along the fence, and a swing gate needs level ground through its arc, which drifted snow can block.

DRGAC Fleet's and Barnworld's price guides put economy steel cattle guards at $1,700 to $2,500 before shipping and footings, mid-grade fabricated guards with skirts and end caps at $3,500 to $5,500 for 14-to-20-foot widths, installed totals at $2,000 to $7,000, and custom or heavy-duty work above $10,000. Wing panels, which stop animals walking around the ends, sell separately. The guard spans a pit deep enough that livestock will not cross, so it needs footings that carry the vehicle load across the span and drainage that keeps the pit from filling with water and silt, which a gate's post footings never need. Those makers put a steel guard's service life past 30 years with little maintenance, against a gate's hinges, latches and opener. The parcel holds no livestock, and the stated priority is low daily friction and low upkeep, which the guard serves at a higher one-time cost.

**Code**

```
A gate across a fire apparatus access road is wide, hand-operable and openable by the fire department.
  At least 20 feet wide for a single gate, swing, slide, lift or pivot type, operable by hand by one person, kept working, with an approved emergency opening on electric gates and an approved lock.
  Source: Oregon Fire Code Appendix D, D103.5, items 1 to 6 (2022 edition, 2021 IFC base).

Powered gates are listed and built to the automatic-gate standards.
  Electric gate operators listed to UL 325, and gates intended for automatic operation designed, constructed and installed to ASTM F2200.
  Source: Oregon Fire Code 503.6 and Appendix D, D103.5, items 7 and 8 (2022 edition).

A security gate across the access road is approved and openable in an emergency.
  Installation approved by the fire code official, with an approved means of emergency operation, both maintained operational at all times.
  Source: Oregon Fire Code 503.6 (2022 edition).
```
