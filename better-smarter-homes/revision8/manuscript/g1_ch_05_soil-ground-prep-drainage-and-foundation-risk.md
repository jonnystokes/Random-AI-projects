## 5.1 What the ground does under a concrete house

**Simple**

Under this house, the ground holds up the footing, the footing holds up the wall, and the wall holds up the roof. Concrete weighs more than wood framing, so the soil carries more load here than under a wood house. Concrete is also stiff. When one corner of the foundation sinks more than the others, the wall cannot bend to follow it, and it cracks. Soft, wet, swelling or filled ground can move even a well-built foundation through every wet and dry season. Soil tests before the design cost hundreds of dollars. Repairs to a foundation already poured cost tens of thousands of dollars.

**Applied**

1. Open a soil file for the parcel before any foundation design starts.
2. Save the desk research in it, with the parcel number and the date on each map or report.
3. Mark the planned building footprint on a site sketch and keep the sketch in the file.
4. Photograph the building area once in dry weather and again after a wet-season rain.
5. Record each groundwater observation with its date, its depth and the weather before it.
6. Add the test-pit logs, auger profiles, field readings and lab reports as each phase produces them.
7. Label every bagged sample with its pit, its depth and the date.
8. Tie each soil decision in the file to the drawing, photo, test, report or inspection behind it.

**Professional**

Foundation design for a concrete house works from four soil properties: the allowable bearing pressure, the expected total and differential settlement, the design groundwater elevation, and the volume change of any fine-grained soil. Lateral support and the frost depth complete the basis. Heavier dead load raises the bearing demand, and stiff concrete and reinforced masonry walls crack at less differential movement than a framed wall tolerates.

Each footing in the record bears on one named material: undisturbed natural soil, engineered fill, improved ground, rock or a deep-foundation element. Presumptive bearing values hold only where the soil classification supports them. Any higher, project-specific value carries its geotechnical basis into the record.

Over a design life measured in centuries, the soil work covers more than first-year bearing. Cyclic wetting and drying, freeze-thaw, seismic shaking and the long-term performance of the drainage all act on the foundation for decades. Because the house's structural design assumes the engineered path, the soil design basis goes onto the engineer's drawings.

Frost depth comes from the jurisdiction's adopted design table. For planning, the land chapter's climate section lists 12 to 18 inches in most valleys and 24 to 36 inches in cold mountain zones.

**Code**

```
Footings rest on undisturbed natural soil or engineered fill.
  Exterior walls bear on continuous concrete or masonry footings supported on undisturbed natural soil or on fill that meets the fill rule.
  Source: ORSC R403.1 (2023 edition).

Exterior footings go below the frost line and at least 12 inches below grade.
  At least 12 inches below finished grade on undisturbed ground, and below the jurisdiction's frost line unless insulated as a frost-protected foundation, designed to ASCE 32, or set on solid rock.
  Source: ORSC R403.1.4 and R403.1.4.1 (2023 edition).

Elements beyond the prescriptive limits are designed.
  Structural elements exceeding the limits of R301 or otherwise not conforming to the code require nonprescriptive design, which may follow the structural specialty code.
  Source: ORSC R301.1.3 (2023 edition).
```

## 5.2 The eight questions the soil evidence answers

**Simple**

Before anyone designs a footing, the ground under it has to pass eight checks. Three concern strength. It has to carry the weight, settle no more than the walls can take, and settle evenly under every corner. Water has to drain out of it. Swelling clay, frost heave and earthquake shaking are the next three. The eighth is the hole itself, which has to stay open while people work in it. Each wrong answer shows up later, as a sunken footing, a cracked slab, a wet basement or a corner that lifts in winter.

**Applied**

1. Log every observation that points to a soil problem, with its location, depth and date. These are the common ones:
   - gray soil and seepage in a test pit at 4 feet
   - wide cracks and slickensides (shiny, grooved faces left where clay has moved) in dry clay
   - brick, wood, ash or old pipe in an auger hole
   - one corner with much softer probe readings than the others
   - diagonal cracks and patched drainage on a neighboring foundation
   - water-loving plants in the low spots
2. Look for a pattern across several observations before acting on any one of them.
3. Choose the response the pattern calls for:
   - move the footprint
   - remove organic or disturbed soil
   - specify engineered fill and verify it
   - change the foundation type
   - add lab tests
   - hire a geotechnical engineer
   - reject the site

**Professional**

| Question | What a bad answer causes | Main evidence | Possible design response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bearing: can the soil carry the load without yielding? | Footings sink, punch into the ground or tilt | Soil classification, test pits, pocket penetrometer, dynamic cone, geotechnical report | Footing sizing, mat design, soil replacement, an engineered or deep foundation |
| Settlement: will the soil compress under sustained load? | Slabs crack, doors bind, walls move | Organic content, soft clay, loose fill, compressible layers, the water table | Over-excavation, preload, controlled fill, deep support |
| Uniformity: is the support the same across the footprint? | One corner moves differently from another, and the differential settlement loads the structure unevenly | Test pits at the corners and center, cone and penetrometer readings compared across the footprint | Removal and replacement of problem zones, a mat or stiffened foundation that bridges the variation, a geotechnical investigation |
| Drainage: where does water go, and how fast? | Basements leak, clay swells, soil under footings softens | Surface flow, mottling, seepage, the water table, nearby wells | Grading, perimeter drains, a capillary break, waterproofing, a sump |
| Shrink and swell: does fine-grained soil change volume with moisture? | Foundations lift, drop or crack with the seasons | Clay plasticity, slickensides, dry-season cracks, Atterberg limits | Moisture control, soil replacement, a stiffened slab, pier and grade beam |
| Frost: can freezing water lift the soil? | Slab edges and shallow footings heave in winter | Frost depth, soil type, water supply to the freezing zone | Footing depth, insulation, drainage, non-frost-susceptible fill |
| Seismic behavior: what happens during shaking? | Loose, saturated soil loses strength (liquefaction) | DOGAMI hazard layers, groundwater, loose sand or silt | Geotechnical analysis, densification, deep support, a different footprint |
| Excavation behavior: can the hole be dug safely? | Walls cave, slump or flood, or the work strips the bearing layer | Soil cohesion, groundwater, depth, nearby loads | Slope, bench, shore, shield, dewater, sequence the work |

Seven results make up the design basis that the foundation designer and the building official read:

- the allowable bearing pressure
- the estimated settlement behavior
- the groundwater elevation used for design
- the expansive-soil classification
- the acceptance criteria for any fill
- the frost-protection assumption
- any limits on excavation or backfill

## 5.3 Desk research before the site visit

**Simple**

Free public maps show much of what the ground is likely to hold before you walk the parcel. Federal soil survey maps name the soil on the land and say how well it drains. State geology maps mark old landslides and ground that can turn to liquid in an earthquake. Logs from wells drilled nearby record the layers the drillers hit and where they found water. None of this replaces digging, but the maps show where to dig first and what to look for.

**Applied**

| Source | What it provides |
|---|---|
| NRCS Web Soil Survey (websoilsurvey.nrcs.usda.gov) | The USDA soil series, drainage class, map-unit description and engineering interpretations for the parcel, free |
| DOGAMI HazVu (oregongeology.org/hazvu) | Mapped Oregon geologic hazards: landslide, liquefaction, earthquake, tsunami, volcanic |
| DOGAMI SLIDO | Mapped landslides and landslide susceptibility |
| County GIS (Lane, Marion, Washington, Multnomah, Clackamas, Deschutes, Douglas and others) | Contours, floodplain and wetland layers, utilities, old roads, tax-lot history |
| FEMA flood maps (msc.fema.gov) or the local equivalent | Floodplain and base flood elevation |
| Oregon Water Resources Department well-log database | Logs of nearby wells: depth, soil layers, water level |
| Oregon Design Criteria Hub (oregon.gov/bcd) | Frost depth, seismic design category, snow, wind and weathering criteria for the site |
| County onsite-wastewater records | Site evaluations on adjacent parcels, which record local soil behavior |
| OSU and SEAO snow load lookup (snowload.seao.org/lookup.html) | Site-specific ground snow load |

1. Run each source in the table against the parcel before the first visit.
2. Save every map, screenshot and exported report with the parcel boundary, the parcel number and the date.
3. Write the expected soil series, drainage class and depth to any limiting layer on the site sketch.
4. Flag the conditions for the field work to check, such as expansive soil, high groundwater, fill, a hazard zone or poor drainage.

**Professional**

Desk research yields an expected profile, and the field pass confirms or rejects each item in it:

- the USDA soil series and its engineering interpretations
- the drainage class, from well drained to very poorly drained
- the mapped seasonal high groundwater
- the depth to a limiting layer, such as a clay seam, bedrock or hardpan that controls drainage
- the mapped landslide susceptibility, liquefaction hazard and flood hazard
- the design values for frost, snow, wind and seismic loads

Regional soil patterns set expectations before the field pass. They are screening expectations, and the parcel's own survey data and test pits replace them.

| Region | Common soil patterns | Common foundation issues |
|---|---|---|
| Willamette Valley lowlands | Silty clay loam (Holcomb, Dayton and Amity series), a seasonal high water table, some organic horizons | Drainage management, possible expansive clay, a high water table in winter |
| Cascade foothills | Stony loam over weathered basalt, rocky in places, seasonal seepage | Bearing that varies across the footprint, rock excavation in places, landslide susceptibility on steeper slopes |
| Coast Range | Wet, organic-rich profiles, fast drainage in some areas and poor drainage in others, high rainfall | Drainage design first, possible old fill with no placement record, a high water table |
| Coastal plain | Sand and silty sand near the shore, peat in places, tsunami and liquefaction overlays | Liquefaction in some mapped zones, salty and acidic soil that corrodes buried metal |
| Deschutes Basin and east of the Cascades | Pumice, scoria and volcanic ash, loess in places, basalt at varying depth | Collapsible loess in some areas, rock at variable depth, frost depth deeper than in the valleys |
| High desert (Harney, Malheur) | Silt-rich loess, clayey alluvium in basins, basalt and tuff bedrock | Frost depth, possible expansive clay in basin sediments, dust and surface erosion |

## 5.4 The soil work in five phases

**Simple**

Soil work runs in five steps, from a first walk over the parcel to the day before the first pour. Two of them come before you own the land, while walking away costs only what the tests cost. Two more turn what the tests found into a foundation design. Step five checks that the soil at the bottom of the trench matches that design. Each step ends in a decision, and the last decision is whether the ground is ready for concrete.

**Applied**

### Before the offer

1. Run the desk research on the parcel.
2. Walk the planned building area.
3. Mark surface water flow, standing water, soft ground, springs and erosion.
4. Look for terraces, rip-rap or an abandoned roadbed, the signs of old grading or fill.
5. Note water-loving plants in low spots and drought-stressed plants on dry knolls.
6. Note the slope, the exposure and the access for trucks.
7. Photograph the site from several angles and record the date.
8. Decide whether to offer, and list the soil conditions the offer must be contingent on.

### After the offer, before closing

1. Order the septic site evaluation if the parcel needs onsite wastewater.
2. Pull the well logs for neighboring parcels to estimate the water source and its depth.
3. Visit after a rain and watch where the water goes.
4. With the seller's permission, dig two or three small test pits in the building area.
5. Take pocket penetrometer readings on any clay the pits expose. This penetrometer is a spring-loaded pin with a dial that reads how hard clay resists.
6. Drive a few dynamic cone profiles to find soft zones. The cone is a pointed steel rod driven by a dropped weight.
7. Bag samples of any clay layer at footing depth for a possible Atterberg test.
8. Ask the building department about each mapped hazard the desk research flagged. Record each answer in the soil file.
9. Where a major hazard is mapped, pay for a one-day geotechnical consultation before closing.
10. Close, or list the price changes and added conditions the findings call for.

### After closing, before the design is final

1. Dig test pits at four to six points across the footprint: the corners, the center and any zone that looks different.
2. Log each pit layer by layer, in the test-pit format.
3. Take penetrometer readings at footing depth in each pit where the soil is cohesive.
4. Drive cone profiles at the same points, down to refusal or bedrock.
5. Bag a sample of each layer at or near footing depth.
6. Send the clay samples to a lab for Atterberg limits and classification.
7. Where fill is planned, add a Proctor test, the lab compaction test that sets fill's density target.
8. Record the groundwater level in each pit, and revisit after rain to find the seasonal high.
9. Mark every zone of concern on the site sketch.
10. Check the triggers for a geotechnical engineer. Where one applies, hire now, before the design starts.

With the soil profile in hand, either the code's presumptive values are confirmed or a geotechnical report sets the design values.

### Design

1. Send the soil profile and any geotechnical report to the structural engineer, who chooses the foundation type from them.
2. On the prescriptive path, choose the foundation type yourself from the same profile.
3. Check that the structural drawings name the geotechnical report where there is one.
4. Put any engineered-fill specification in the permit submittal.
5. Check that the foundation drawings show the frost protection, drainage and waterproofing details.
6. Match the inspection schedule to the construction sequence.

Stamped structural drawings on the engineered path, or a prescriptive design tied to the presumptive values, complete the permit submittal.

### Before the pour

Acceptance criteria for the exposed bearing surface are in the foundation chapter.

1. Proof-roll the building pad by driving a loaded truck or a compactor over it, and mark any spot that ruts or pumps.
2. Excavate to the design footing elevation.
3. Check that the soil at the bottom matches the design assumption, by visual classification and, on clay, a penetrometer reading.
4. Photograph the bearing surface with a scale in the frame.
5. Request the footing inspection.
6. Correct any mismatch, and pour only on a bearing surface that passed.

## 5.5 Reading soil in the field

**Simple**

Gravel, sand, silt and clay each behave differently under a house. Gravel and sand drain fast and carry load well once packed. Holes dug in them cave easily, and loose wet sand can liquefy in an earthquake. Silt feels like flour, weakens when wet and heaves when it freezes. Clay is sticky and holds a shape, and some clays swell hard enough to lift a foundation. Topsoil, peat and old fill come out from under footings, because roots rot and fill can hide debris and loose pockets.

**Applied**

| Field clue | Likely meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Dark soil with roots and visible organic matter | Topsoil or an organic horizon | Strip it and haul it off. Never use it as structural fill |
| Visible gritty grains | Sand or sandy soil | Check its density, its drainage and how the pit wall stands |
| Sticky soil that ribbons between the fingers | Clay | Check its plasticity, drainage and shrink-swell risk |
| Smooth, flour-like feel, weak when wet | Silt | Check its frost susceptibility and how it behaves wet |
| Gray or blue-gray color | Wet or poorly drained ground, possibly seasonal saturation | Investigate the water table and the drainage |
| Red or orange mottling on a gray background | Seasonal wetting and drying as the water table rises and falls | Treat the seasonal high water table as real |
| Buried concrete, wood, trash, pipe or ash, or random bands of soil color | Old fill or disturbed soil | Find its extent, then remove it or design around it |
| Slickensides in clay | Possible expansive clay or old movement surfaces | Sample it for lab tests before relying on the design |
| Strong organic or rotten smell | Organic or airless wet soil | Keep footings off it |
| White crust or efflorescence | Salts or evaporite minerals | Test the soil for chemicals that damage concrete |

**Professional**

Three classification systems describe the same soil, each serving a different trade. USDA texture classes are the soil survey's terms. Engineers and inspectors use the Unified Soil Classification System (USCS), assigned in the lab under ASTM D2487 and in the field under the visual-manual procedure of ASTM D2488. AASHTO's system serves highway work. Field records carry both the USDA terms and the USCS symbol, and the symbol is the input to the foundation design.

| Symbol | Description | Behavior in foundations | Soil group in ORSC Table R405.1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| GW, GP | Well-graded or poorly graded gravel | Excellent bearing when compacted, drains freely | Group I: good drainage, low frost heave |
| GM | Silty gravel | Good bearing, drainage depends on the fines | Group I: good drainage, medium frost heave |
| GC | Clayey gravel | Good bearing, drainage depends on the fines | Group II: medium drainage |
| SW, SP | Well-graded or poorly graded sand | Good bearing when compacted and confined, drains freely | Group I: good drainage, low frost heave |
| SM | Silty sand | Moderate bearing, drainage slowed by the fines | Group I: good drainage, medium frost heave |
| SC | Clayey sand | Moderate bearing, drainage slowed by the fines | Group II: medium drainage |
| ML | Inorganic silt | Poor bearing when wet, frost-susceptible | Group II: medium drainage, high frost heave |
| CL | Lean clay, low plasticity | Moderate bearing, minor shrink-swell | Group II: medium drainage, medium to low expansion |
| MH | Elastic silt, high plasticity | Poor bearing | Group III: poor drainage, high frost heave, high expansion |
| CH | Fat clay, high plasticity | Strong shrink-swell, an expansive-soil concern | Group III: poor drainage, high expansion |
| OL | Organic silt or clay, low plasticity | Not building soil | Group IV: poor drainage |
| OH | Organic silt or clay, high plasticity | Not building soil | Group IV: unsatisfactory |
| Pt | Peat | Not building soil | Group IV: unsatisfactory |

Classification separates what a soil is from how it behaves. Clay can have adequate short-term bearing and a long-term volume-change problem. Sand can drain well and still cave in an excavation or liquefy in an earthquake. Gravel can be excellent natural support or loose, uncontrolled fill.

Each test pit and auger profile is recorded by depth interval, with the contact between layers marked. Sharp transitions between layers indicate fill, and gradual natural layering indicates undisturbed soil. At footing elevation, the bearing layer is the material left after stripping, excavation, replacement and compaction, which is rarely the surface layer.

## 5.6 Test pits, hand augers and hand tests

**Simple**

Most of what the ground holds can be seen and felt without a lab. A test pit, dug by a small excavator or by hand, shows the layers in its wall. A hand auger, a long drill turned by hand, brings up soil from several feet down at many points. Squeezing and rolling a damp ball of soil sorts it into sand, silt or clay. Deeper than 5 feet, a pit can cave in without warning, so its walls are sloped or shored before anyone climbs in.

**Applied**

| Method | Use | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Test pit, dug by mini excavator or by hand | A direct look at the layers, contacts, fill, organics and groundwater | Only as deep as it can be dug safely. The excavation rules apply from 5 feet |
| Hand auger, 4 to 6 inches in diameter, 4 to 10 feet deep | Quick profiles at many points | Only as deep as the auger reaches. Hard going in dense gravel or rocky soil |
| Power auger, truck-mounted | Deeper profiles at fewer points | Needs truck access, and is hired with an operator |

1. Keep out of any pit 5 feet deep or more until its walls are sloped, benched or shored.
2. Keep trucks and spoil piles back from the edge of an open pit.
3. Log each pit and auger hole layer by layer, recording for each layer:
   - its depth interval
   - its color, texture and moisture
   - its consistency
   - organics and inclusions
   - any groundwater
   - the contact with the layer below
4. Photograph each pit wall with a tape measure in the frame.
5. Bag a sample of every layer at or near footing depth for the lab.
6. Run the hand tests in the first table below on each fine-grained layer.
7. Order Atterberg limits on any sample that ribbons more than 2 inches, as the second table sets out.

| Test | How | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Jar test | Shake soil with water in a clear jar, let it settle for 24 hours, and read the layers: sand at the bottom, silt in the middle, clay on top | The rough proportions of sand, silt and clay |
| Dry strength | Form a ball, let it dry, and try to crumble it | High resistance means clay. Low resistance means silt or sand |
| Toughness | Roll a thread to about 1/8 inch (3 mm) at the plastic limit | Tough threads mean higher plasticity and more active clay |
| Dilatancy | Shake a moist pat in the palm and tap the back of the hand | Water rises to the surface of silt and stays hidden in clay |
| Ribbon | Press a moist ball between thumb and forefinger into a ribbon, and measure it before it breaks | Clay content |

| Ribbon length | Indication | Next action |
|---|---|---|
| Under 1 inch | Sandy or silt loam, low clay content | Low plasticity. Nothing about plasticity rules out a prescriptive foundation |
| 1 to 2 inches | Loam or clay loam, moderate clay | Take a penetrometer reading. Add Atterberg limits where the foundation is critical or other warning signs are present |
| Over 2 inches | Clay, silty clay or sandy clay | Order Atterberg limits |

**Professional**

These hand tests belong to the visual-manual procedure of ASTM D2488, and they support the field description while the lab values govern the design. Ribbon length tracks clay content and predicts the USDA texture class. It cannot tell a plasticity index of 25 (medium expansion) from one of 45 (high expansion), because clay mineralogy (kaolinite, illite or montmorillonite) controls plasticity more than the clay percentage does. Rolling a toughness thread comes closer to estimating plasticity, and it still does not replace the lab limits.

Test pits fall under the excavation rules at the same depth as a footing trench. Pit walls that stood in dry weather can fail when a passing truck adds vibration and load at the edge.

Step-by-step procedures for every test, the penetrometers included, are in the testing chapter. Each test has a published standard, and the lab report states the one it followed:

| Standard | What it covers |
|---|---|
| ASTM D2488 | Visual-manual description and identification of soil in the field, including the dilatancy, toughness and dry-strength checks |
| ASTM D2487 | Laboratory classification. Grain size and Atterberg limits give the two-letter USCS symbol |
| ASTM D4318 | Liquid limit, plastic limit and plasticity index. Edition 17e1 was withdrawn in June 2026 |
| ASTM D6913 and D7928 | Sieve and hydrometer gradation, which replaced ASTM D422 when it was withdrawn in 2016 |
| ASTM D4829 | Expansion index |
| ASTM D4546 | One-dimensional swell or collapse of a specimen under load |
| ASTM D698 and D1557 | Standard and Modified Proctor compaction, which give the maximum dry density and the optimum moisture |
| ASTM D1556 and D6938 | Field density by sand cone, the reference method, and by nuclear gauge, the faster common method |
| ASTM D6951 | Dynamic cone penetrometer in shallow pavement applications |
| USDA-NRCS Field Book for Describing and Sampling Soils, version 3.0, and the Soil Survey Field and Laboratory Methods Manual (SSIR-51) | Texture classes, Munsell color, structure and consistence terms for the field description |
| MnDOT User Guide to the Dynamic Cone Penetrometer (Burnham and Johnson, Mn/DOT Report 1993-05) | The most-cited US operating guide for the cone, free from the MnDOT digital library |
| Webster, Brown and Williams, US Army Corps of Engineers Waterways Experiment Station, 1994 | The cone-to-CBR correlation paper whose equations the ASTM annex reprints |

**Code**

```

  Sloping, benching, shoring or a shield, unless the cut is entirely in stable rock, or under 5 feet deep with no sign of a cave-in found by a competent person.
```

## 5.7 The pocket penetrometer and the dynamic cone

**Simple**

Two hand-driven probes rank the soil across the building area. Pushed into a fresh face of clay, the pocket penetrometer reads how hard the clay resists. That reading converts to a safe load per square foot, on clay only. The dynamic cone counts how far a pointed rod sinks each time a weight drops on it. Soft layers show up as a sudden jump in that distance. The cone compares one spot with another and gives no safe load for a footing.

**Applied**

1. Check the penetrometer against soil of known stiffness before each session.
2. Use one operator for every reading on the project.
3. Read at footing depth, on a fresh face of undisturbed soil, never on a shovel-smeared surface.
4. Take three to five readings at each location and record the lowest.
5. Log each reading with its depth, location, moisture and time.
6. On sand, gravel or silty sand, put the penetrometer away and rely on the cone and the classification.
7. Drive the cone at each test-pit location and at the corners of the footprint.
8. Record millimeters of penetration per blow, by depth, and plot the profile.
9. Mark each change in the slope of the plot as a layer boundary.
10. Read each layer against the cone bands in the table below.
11. Keep every cone result out of the bearing calculation. Use it for layers, comparison and calibrated fill checks only.

| Penetration rate | Band | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Under 10 mm per blow | Dense | Confirms a competent layer for a prescriptive footing. Classify the soil to confirm it |
| 10 to 50 mm per blow | Variable | Depends on the material. Use for layer detection and comparison only |
| Over 50 mm per blow at 0 to 3 feet, on natural ground | Loose | Get a geotechnical investigation. Do not rely on prescriptive footings |

**Professional**

Pocket penetrometers read unconfined compressive strength, qu, in tons per square foot. For saturated clay the undrained shear strength, su, is half of qu, with the friction angle taken as zero. Skempton's bearing factor puts the ultimate capacity of a strip footing on saturated clay at 5.14 su, which is 2.57 qu. With a safety factor of 3, the value Bowles, Coduto and Das all use, the allowable pressure is 0.86 qu. Rounded down for field screening, the allowable pressure in psf is 1,500 times the reading in tsf: 1,500 psf at 1.0 tsf and 3,000 psf at 2.0 tsf.

| Reading, qu (tsf) | Consistency | Screening allowable pressure | Meaning for a footing on clay |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 0.25 | Very soft | Under 375 psf | Stop. Redesign or excavate |
| 0.25 to 0.5 | Soft | 375 to 750 psf | Below the residential code's clay value. Engineering required |
| 0.5 to 1.0 | Medium | 750 to 1,500 psf | Can fall short of the clay value. A soils investigation sets the bearing |
| 1.0 to 2.0 | Stiff | 1,500 to 3,000 psf | Supports the presumptive clay value |
| Over 2.0 | Very stiff to hard | Over 3,000 psf | Supports the presumptive value. A higher design value still needs a geotechnical evaluation |

Consistency names follow Terzaghi and Peck's 1967 table. On granular soil the penetrometer reading has no meaning. In sand, gravel and silty sand the tip responds to grain size rather than cohesion, so density-based methods apply.

ASTM D6951 is the standard for the dynamic cone. Its scope covers undisturbed soil and compacted materials, relates the penetration rate to an in-place California Bearing Ratio (CBR), and names pavement work as the usual application. Both leading US references, the MnDOT user guide and the Corps of Engineers work by Webster, Brown and Williams, are pavement references too. Applying the cone to residential footings extends the method beyond its stated purpose.

Two reasons keep the cone's readings out of footing design. Its conversion to a bearing pressure chains two correlations. Cone readings correlate well with CBR, with R² of 0.85 to 0.95 for granular soils and lower for clays, but CBR does not correlate reliably with bearing pressure. For that second step, the Portland Cement Association's rule of thumb is a pavement-design relation extended to footings, and chaining two empirical correlations compounds their scatter. Reproducibility is the second reason: Webster's Corps of Engineers work reported a coefficient of variation near 40 percent, even under controlled conditions.

For screening, the cone does four things well. On the penetration plot, a change in slope marks a layer boundary to within about 1 inch. High penetration rates at shallow depth flag a site for geotechnical investigation. Rates compared across the footprint rank relative strength. With a calibration for the specific material, the cone checks the compaction of engineered fill. Without that calibration it is no density meter, and in no case does it produce a bearing pressure or replace the presumptive table.

## 5.8 Bearing values and the code's table

**Simple**

For each kind of ground, the code lets a footing assume a safe load without a test. The assumed load holds only for soil that matches one of the table's classes. Bedrock carries the most and clay the least. On weaker ground the footing has to spread the load over a wider strip. In the code's footing table, a two-story house with solid concrete walls needs a 29-inch footing on clay and a 14-inch footing on gravel. Claiming more than the table allows takes a geotechnical engineer's report.

**Applied**

1. Classify the soil at footing depth before taking any value from the table.
2. Use the table value for that class, and size the footing from the code's footing table or the engineer's design.
3. Where a penetrometer reading on clay falls under 1.0 tsf, treat the clay value as unproven and get a soils investigation.
4. Where the design needs more than the table value, order a geotechnical evaluation.
5. Check the site against each condition listed below that moves a design off the table.

**Professional**

Table values apply only after the site's actual behavior is verified. Any of these conditions moves a design off the table and onto an engineered basis:

- expansive clay
- engineered fill under footings, or fill with no placement record anywhere under the footprint
- a slope steeper than 1 in 3 next to a footing
- groundwater at or near footing depth
- a mapped seismic hazard such as liquefaction
- an unusual load

Sized for the clay value, a footing overloads any softer clay. On a prescriptive design, the penetrometer's job is to confirm that the clay is at least as stiff as the table assumes.

Footings near slopes steeper than 1 in 3 follow the residential code's slope rule, whose Code entry is in the land chapter. In the model code's slope figure, a building below an ascending slope stands H/2 from the toe, up to 15 feet, where H is the slope height. Above a descending slope, a footing sits back H/3 from the slope face, up to 40 feet.

Those values are as printed in the California Residential Code's Figure R403.1.7.1 and in building guidance from Roanoke County, Virginia, and Agoura Hills, California. Under ORSC R403.1.9.1, where a retaining wall stands at the toe of a slope, the slope height is measured from the top of the wall to the top of the slope.

**Code**

```
Without a complete geotechnical evaluation, footings take the presumptive bearing values.
  Crystalline bedrock 12,000 psf, sedimentary and foliated rock 4,000, GW and GP 3,000, SW, SP, SM, SC, GM and GC 2,000, CL, ML, MH and CH 1,500.
  Source: ORSC R401.4.1 and Table R401.4.1 (2023 edition).

Soil likely to bear less than the clay value takes a soils investigation.
  Where the building official finds in-place soil likely to carry under 1,500 psf, a soils investigation sets its allowable bearing capacity.
  Source: ORSC Table R401.4.1, footnote b (2023 edition).
```

## 5.9 Expansive clay and the Atterberg limits

**Simple**

Some clays swell when they get wet and shrink when they dry, with enough force to lift and crack a foundation. For $50 to $150, a lab test on a bag of the clay gives one number, the plasticity index, that grades the risk. Low numbers mean an ordinary foundation works. Higher numbers mean the foundation is designed around the movement, with deeper supports, replaced soil or steady moisture at its edges. Built on swelling clay without that design, a foundation moves every season.

**Applied**

1. Send every clay sample from footing depth for Atterberg limits.
2. Read the plasticity index against the project thresholds in the table below.
3. Plant new trees on expansive soil outside 20 to 30 feet from the foundation and outside half the tree's mature height.
4. Have the geotechnical evaluation record any large existing tree inside those distances.
5. Where the geotechnical report gives a potential vertical rise, size any void forms from it.

| Plasticity index | Project position |
|---|---|
| Under 15 | Treat as non-expansive. Prescriptive foundations are acceptable |
| 15 to 20 | A screening-level concern. Design the drainage and moisture control, keep irrigation 5 feet or more from the foundation, and consider a thickened-edge slab or a continuously reinforced perimeter footing |
| 20 to 25 | Mitigation is part of the design, and an engineered foundation is likely: footings below the active zone, a drainage envelope, isolation joints, post-tensioning considered |
| 25 to 35 | Engineered foundation required. Over-excavation and replacement with non-expansive fill, lime stabilization, or piers through the active zone |
| Over 35 | Active mitigation: deep piers to a non-expansive stratum, a structural slab on void forms, or a full site-specific engineered design |

**Professional**

Atterberg limits are lab measurements of the moisture contents at the boundaries of soil behavior: the liquid limit, the plastic limit and the shrinkage limit. Of these, the plasticity index, which is the liquid limit minus the plastic limit, is the most useful single number for judging the risk of expansive clay. Holtz and Gibbs published the classification in 1956, with overlapping bands:

| Plasticity index | Swell potential |
|---|---|
| 0 to 15 | Low |
| 10 to 35 | Medium |
| 20 to 55 | High |
| Over 35 | Very high |

In a footnote, the residential code's soil table lists similar bands, low at 0 to 15, medium at 10 to 35 and high above 20. Project thresholds open the mitigation discussion at the code's own expansive line and require an engineered foundation five points higher.

The active zone is the depth of soil whose moisture changes with the seasons. Foundation elements that bear inside the active zone move with the seasonal cycle, and elements that bear below it do not. ACI 332-20, the residential concrete code, has a chapter on design for expansive soils, with sections on scope, general requirements, expansive soil classification and design.

| Option | How it works | Limit or pairing |
|---|---|---|
| Over-excavation and replacement | The swelling soil in the active zone comes out, and non-expansive fill goes in | The geotechnical engineer sets the depth and width |
| Chemical stabilization | Lime or cement treatment lowers the soil's plasticity and swell potential | Paired with piers, void forms or a stiffened slab rather than used alone |
| Drilled piers | Piers carry the load through the active zone to a stable bearing stratum | Residential pier depths run 8 to 30 feet, set by the active-zone depth and the soil profile |
| Void forms | Degradable forms under grade beams and slabs on piers leave a space the heaving soil can fill without loading the structure | The void depth comes from the geotechnical report's potential vertical rise (PVR), never from a generic number |

Volumetric swell depends on the clay minerals and is measured for a specific soil by the ASTM D4546 swell test. No single swell percentage applies across clays, so the design uses the measured value. For the gradation tests in its expansive criteria, the code names ASTM D422. ASTM withdrew D422 in 2016, and labs now run D6913 for the sieve fraction and D7928 for the hydrometer fraction.

Trees dry the soil around their roots and cause localized shrinkage settlement on expansive soil. Published by Springer in 2023, a parametric study of residential footings on expansive soils found significant shrinkage settlement as far from a tree as half its mature height. Most of the root-driven drying was in the top 10 feet of soil. In the planting step, 20 to 30 feet is a planning figure.

Where a required soil report finds highly expansive soil, ORS 455.440 requires the first seller of the house to give the first buyer written suggestions for its care and maintenance. Buyers who do not get them can recover $750.

**Code**

```
Soil meeting the code's four criteria is expansive.
  PI 15 or more, over 10 percent passing a No. 200 sieve, over 10 percent finer than 5 micrometers and expansion index over 20, the first three tests waived when the fourth is run.
  Source: ORSC R403.1.10.1 (2023 edition; R403.1.8.1 in the 2021 IRC).

Foundations on expansive soil are designed to the structural code.
  Footings and slabs on expansive soil follow OSSC section 1808.6, unless the building official approves a system that has performed adequately on similar soil.
  Source: ORSC R403.1.10 (2023 edition).

Compressible or shifting soil is removed from the active zone.
  Removed to a depth and width that keeps moisture stable in each active zone, and not used as fill or stabilized within it by chemical treatment, dewatering or presaturation.
  Source: ORSC R401.4.2 (2023 edition).

A required soil report that finds highly expansive soil is recorded against the land.
  Before issuing the permit, the agency files the report with the plans and records a notice in the County Clerk Lien Record.
  Source: ORS 455.440(1).
```

## 5.10 Other soil problems

**Simple**

Swelling clay is one of ten soil patterns that change a foundation design. Soft or loose ground can be dug out and replaced with packed fill. Wind-blown silt can collapse the first time it is soaked. Loose, wet sand can liquefy in a strong earthquake, and old fill can hide debris and empty pockets. Each pattern has a known design response, and on a few sites the response is to build somewhere else.

**Professional**

| Problem | What it looks like | Design response |
|---|---|---|
| Insufficient bearing | A soft cohesive or loose granular layer at footing depth, penetrometer readings under 0.5 tsf in clay | Move to a deeper bearing layer, over-excavate and replace, widen the footings, or engineer the foundation |
| Excessive settlement | A compressible organic layer, thick soft clay, loose granular soil, settlement cracks in nearby foundations | Over-excavate and replace, preload, surcharge, or a deep foundation |
| Expansive clay | Plasticity index of 15 or more, slickensides, dry-season cracks, seasonal movement in neighboring houses | A stiffened-edge slab, pier and grade beam, over-excavation and replacement, a drainage envelope, the ACI 332 expansive-soil design |
| Collapsible soil | Loose silt or fine sand, wind-deposited loess among them, low density at depth, collapse on wetting | Pre-wet and compact, remove and replace, or a deep foundation |
| Frost-susceptible soil | Silty soil at footing depth in cold zones, frost heave on neighboring houses | Footings below the frost line, a frost-protected shallow foundation, non-frost-susceptible fill |
| Liquefiable soil | Loose saturated sand or silt, a mapped liquefaction hazard, standard penetration test blow counts (N-values) below 15 | Densification by vibroflotation or deep dynamic compaction, a deep foundation to a non-liquefiable layer, another site |
| Variable soil | Mixed fill, old buried foundations, cone readings that vary across the footprint | A geotechnical investigation, removal and replacement of the problem zones, a mat or stiffened foundation that bridges the variation |
| Old fill | Buried debris, ash, scrap or demolition material, uneven colors and densities | Treat it as unknown. Over-excavate to native soil, or run a full engineered-fill program with documentation |
| Unstable slope | A mapped landslide, SLIDO susceptibility, tilted trees, slumped scarps | Geotechnical analysis, a deep foundation, another footprint, or rejection of the site |
| Karst or soluble bedrock | Limestone or evaporite bedrock, sinkholes, sudden ground depressions | A specialized geotechnical investigation, a deep foundation, another site |

Collapsible soil stands firm while dry and loses volume when it is first wetted, so a broken pipe or a wet season can drop a footing. Liquefaction comes from shaking that raises the water pressure between the grains of loose, saturated sand or silt until the soil loses its strength. Under ORSC R403.3, a frost-protected shallow foundation can stay above the frost line with insulation only where the building is kept at a monthly mean of at least 64°F. Unheated spaces such as garages and porches cannot use it. Karst ground over limestone or evaporite rock can open a sinkhole under a footing with no warning at the surface.

## 5.11 When to hire a geotechnical engineer

**Simple**

The owner's field work screens the ground. It finds the soil type, compares one part of the site with another and catches the obvious problems. It cannot prove a bearing value above the code's table or design a foundation around a landslide. Those take a geotechnical engineer, a licensed engineer who specializes in soil and rock. A one-day consultation costs $800 to $2,500, with borings and a slope-stability study priced separately.

**Applied**

Hire a geotechnical engineer when any of these is true:

- the desk research shows a mapped landslide, liquefaction or unstable-slope hazard
- the soil differs from one part of the footprint to another
- the seasonal high water table reaches footing depth or comes near it
- organics, peat or fill with no placement record sit at footing depth
- the plasticity index is 15 or more
- the design needs a bearing value above the table value for the soil
- engineered fill goes under footings, or under a slab that carries structural load
- a basement will stand in groundwater
- a retaining wall retains over 48 inches with its top unsupported, or stands over 24 inches tall and carries a load beyond the soil
- the site is in Seismic Design Category D0, D1 or D2 and the foundation has any nonstandard element
- a soil report from a neighboring site, or a nearby well log, shows problem soil

1. Send the engineer the soil survey, hazard maps, county GIS layers and well logs from the desk research.
2. Send the test-pit logs, auger profiles, penetrometer and cone readings, and photos from the field work.
3. Send the planned footprint and the structural concept.
4. Ask for the report the building official will need, the seven-part design basis with the Atterberg results attached.
5. For a landslide flag found before closing, start with a one-day consultation.
6. Get a quote for borings and a slope-stability study if the engineer calls for them.
7. File the report in the binder, and send a copy to the foundation designer.

**Professional**

This project's trigger list is wider than the code's, because a concrete house tolerates less differential movement than the framed house the code's tables assume. Most of western Oregon sits in Seismic Design Categories D0 to D2 because of the Cascadia Subduction Zone, and in those categories a nonstandard foundation element gets an engineer's design. Once issued, the geotechnical report is referenced on the structural drawings and kept in the binder for every later change to the foundation.

**Code**

```
The building official decides whether questionable soil is tested.
  Where data indicate expansive, compressible, shifting or other questionable soil, the building official decides whether an approved agency tests it by an approved method.
  Source: ORSC R401.4 (2023 edition).

A required soil test states the allowable bearing.
  Where the building official requires a soil test, its recommendations include the soil's allowable bearing capacity.
  Source: ORSC Table R401.4.1, footnote a (2023 edition).
```

## 5.12 The verdict on the site

**Simple**

At the end of the soil work, the site lands in one of four grades. Strong ground is even, drains well and has a documented bearing value. Ground that is acceptable with design works inside the code's tables, provided the drainage and frost protection are right. Conditional ground works only with engineered fill, a different foundation type or an engineer's design. Rejected ground has hazards, or repair costs, beyond what the project budget allows.

**Applied**

| Acceptance level | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Reject | Major hazards or unworkable conditions, with a cost or risk beyond the project's limits | Walk away, or accept extensive geotechnical engineering and its cost |
| Conditional | Workable with documented fill, a changed foundation type or geotechnical engineering | Engage the engineer, budget the fill and foundation work, and carry the conditions into the design |
| Acceptable with design | Workable within the prescriptive code if the drainage and frost protection are right | Follow the standard design path, with attention to drainage and detail |
| Strong | Uniform, well-drained, predictable soil with a documented bearing value | Follow the standard design path, the lowest-risk foundation construction |

1. Compile the desk-research package.
2. Complete the field pass with test pits, auger and probe readings, and the ribbon, jar and dry-strength tests.
3. Send clay samples for Atterberg limits wherever the plasticity is in question.
4. Classify each layer by the field and lab standards.
5. Screen the bearing against the presumptive table, with the penetrometer on clay.
6. Screen for expansive soil against the plasticity thresholds.
7. Evaluate the drainage, slope, frost and seismic exposure.
8. Check the results against the geotechnical-engineer triggers.
9. Assign the acceptance level.
10. Record the decision, with the evidence behind it, in the binder.

## 5.13 Foundation type on a wet site

**Simple**

On a wet site, the foundation type sets how much water pressure the house has to resist. A slab on grade sits on the ground, above most of the water. A crawl space lifts the floor but needs the same drainage under it as a slab. A basement puts walls and a floor down where winter groundwater pushes on them, and it needs the most drainage and waterproofing. Drains can lower the water around the walls, or the walls and floor can be built to resist its full push.

**Applied**

1. Find the seasonal high water table from the test pits and the site evaluation before choosing the foundation type.
2. Weigh the three types against that water level, the cost difference and the value of below-grade space.
3. For a slab on a wet site, raise the pad with compacted engineered fill where needed.
4. Put 4 to 6 inches or more of free-draining aggregate under the slab, and drain it by gravity to daylight where the grade allows.
5. For a crawl space, give the ground the same capillary break and drainage as a slab, plus a sealed ground vapor barrier.
6. For a basement where seasonal groundwater reaches the floor level, add a sump to the passive drainage.
7. Waterproof, rather than dampproof, every below-grade wall on a site with a known high water table.
8. Check the uplift case for any below-grade structure that will stand empty while the water table is above its base.

**Professional**

Groundwater exposure rises from slab on grade to crawl space to basement. Compared with a basement, a crawl space cuts the exposure by the height of its clearance. Where the seasonal high water table reaches or exceeds the floor elevation, a basement is the type most likely to need a sump rather than passive drainage alone.

Prescriptive wall tables are written for soil pressure alone, and standing groundwater adds its own full head to that load. Dampproofing resists vapor and incidental wetting and has no rating against standing hydrostatic head. Waterproofing materials in the code run from hot-mopped felts to liquid-applied synthetic rubber, and the foundation chapter's long-life assembly uses a 60-mil peel-and-stick polymer-modified asphalt membrane.

The footing drain relieves pressure by lowering the water table around the foundation, so the wall and slab never carry full hydrostatic head. Sealing against full head without relief needs the structure, its waterproofing and often its dead weight designed for that pressure, which costs more than relief. Relief degrades slowly as the drain clogs over years, and a crack in a sealed wall admits water under pressure at once.

Buoyant uplift acts on any below-grade structure that stands empty or lightly loaded while the water table is above its base: a pool, an empty cistern or tank, or a basement shell before the house is built on it. Its remedies are added dead weight, such as a thicker base slab or ballast, tension piles or ground anchors, or an under-slab relief layer drained to a sump. Once the house stands on it, the building's dead load resists the uplift, so the exposure falls during construction and in any vault meant to stand empty.

**Code**

```
Foundation walls in groundwater are designed rather than taken from tables.
  Concrete or masonry foundation walls subject to hydrostatic pressure from groundwater require nonprescriptive design.
  Source: ORSC R404.1.1, item 1 (2023 edition).

Below-grade walls are dampproofed, or waterproofed where a high water table is known.
  Waterproofed from finished grade to the higher of the footing top or 6 inches below the basement floor top wherever a high water table or severe soil-water condition is known.
  Source: ORSC R406.1 and R406.2 (2023 edition).
```

## 5.14 The drainage layers around the foundation

**Simple**

Rain reaches this foundation only after getting past several layers of protection. Outside, the ground slopes away from the walls, and gutters catch the roof water and carry it off. A perforated pipe in gravel along the footing collects the water that soaks down. Whatever reaches the wall meets a coating. Under the slab, a gravel bed and a plastic sheet keep ground moisture out of the floor.

**Applied**

- Check the grade with a level for 6 inches of fall or more in the first 10 feet from the wall.
- Slope patios, walks and drives within 10 feet of the house at 2 percent or more away from it.
- Size downspouts at 1 square inch per 100 square feet of roof drained, the Oregon planning convention for the wetter zones.
- Discharge every downspout 5 feet or more from the foundation, onto a splash block, into a piped extension, or into an approved drainage system.
- Check the footing drain against each detail before backfill:
  - at or below the top of the footing
  - 4-inch perforated pipe, holes down
  - on 2 inches or more of washed gravel or crushed rock
  - covered with 6 inches or more of the same stone
  - wrapped in, or covered by, an approved filter membrane
  - surrounded by clean 3/4-inch or #57 stone
- Daylight the drain by gravity wherever the grade gives the fall.
- Where it does not, install a sump, a pit with a pump that lifts the water out, at the low corner of the foundation.
- On a sloping site, add a curtain drain upslope of the house, a shallow interceptor trench 12 to 24 inches deep.
- Run it to cut off surface water and shallow groundwater before they reach the foundation.
- Keep the curtain drain as an addition to the footing drain, never a substitute for it.
- On ground steeper than 3 percent, keep a curtain drain at least 10 feet upslope or 50 feet downslope of the septic absorption area.
- On flatter ground, keep it 20 feet from the absorption area.
- In clay or silt, specify a non-woven geotextile matched to the soil, with a sand buffer between the native soil and the drain stone.
- Check the waterproofing for continuous coverage, sealed laps and detailed terminations at penetrations and corners.
- Under the slab, check the capillary-break stone, the membrane laps and seals, and the soil-gas retarder in the radon counties.

**Professional**

| Layer | What it does | What happens when it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Site grading (R401.3) | Moves surface water away from the foundation | Surface water pools at the foundation, and capillary action draws moisture into the slab edge |
| Roof drainage and downspouts (R801.3) | Collects roof water and routes it away | Concentrated discharge saturates the perimeter soil and loads the footing drain |
| Footing drain (R405.1) | Collects subsurface water around the foundation and discharges it | Hydrostatic pressure builds against the wall, and water enters cracks and joints |
| Dampproofing or waterproofing (R406) | Resists water passing through the foundation wall | Below-grade walls leak, finishes spall, reinforcement corrodes |
| Capillary break (R506.2.2) | Aggregate under the slab interrupts capillary rise from the subgrade | The slab stays damp, finishes fail, vapor drives into the living space |
| Ground cover or vapor retarder (R506.2.3) | A membrane under the slab | Moisture migrates through the slab into the living space |
| Soil-gas retarder and vent (Appendix AF) | Subslab depressurization in the radon counties | Soil gas collects in the living space |

[FIGURE: foundation-section, footing, wall and slab with the falling grade, footing drain, waterproofing and capillary break]

Three grading and roof-water faults keep a foundation wet year after year: a flat or reversed grade, downspouts emptying at the wall, and gutters that are missing, undersized or clogged. On expansive soil the damage comes from changes in moisture rather than from moisture alone. In its homeowner guide, Living With Shrink/Swell Soils, the Geo-Institute traces the differential heave and settlement to wet and dry cycling at the perimeter. Holding the moisture content at the foundation edge near constant is the aim, and grading and downspout control come first.

Sumps add a pump and a power supply to the drainage, and the wet-season storms that raise the water table also bring outages. Gravity discharge to daylight needs neither.

In fine-grained soil, a footing drain fails slowly. Clay and silt particles wash into the drain stone and the pipe perforations until the flow drops. As planning figures, a geotextile not matched to the soil's grain size can clog within about a year in fine-grained soil. Matched non-woven fabric, with a sand buffer between the native clay and the drain stone, extends the drain's working life toward 30 to 50 years. Its apparent opening size, matched to the soil gradation under ASTM D4751 and related standards, governs the fabric choice. Because the code asks only for an approved filter membrane, the product comes from the manufacturer's listing and the inspector's approval.

Under the slab, ORSC R506.2.2 calls for a 4-inch base course of clean graded sand, gravel or crushed stone where the slab is below grade, and none on Group I soils. Oregon's R506.2.3 calls the membrane a ground cover: 6-mil polyethylene or an approved Class I vapor retarder, with joints lapped 12 inches or more.

In Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington and Yamhill counties, ORSC Appendix AF adds passive subslab depressurization: a soil-gas retarder, a 3-inch vent pipe and sealed slab joints. The radon chapter has the soil-gas detail, and the foundation chapter specifies the project's slab stack: 4 inches of clean 3/4-inch or #57 stone under a 10-mil ASTM E1745 Class A membrane.

**Code**

```
Lots drain surface water away from the foundation.
  The grade falls at least 6 inches within the first 10 feet, or drains or swales carry water away where a lot line, wall or slope prevents that fall.
  Source: ORSC R401.3 (2023 edition).

Hard surfaces near the house slope away from it.
  Impervious surfaces within 10 feet of the building foundation slope at least 2 percent away from the building.
  Source: ORSC R401.3 (2023 edition).

Surface drainage goes to an approved collection point.
  Surface drainage is diverted to a storm sewer conveyance or another approved point of collection that creates no hazard.
  Source: ORSC R401.3 (2023 edition).

On expansive or collapsible soil, roof water discharges away from the walls.
  Roof drainage is collected and discharged at least 5 feet from foundation walls, or into an approved drainage system.
  Source: ORSC R801.3 (2023 edition).

The building official can require all roof drainage to be collected.
  Where the building official requires it, all roof drainage is collected and discharged in accordance with the plumbing code.
  Source: ORSC R801.4 (2023 edition, an Oregon addition).

Earth-retaining foundations around below-grade rooms have a footing drain.
  Drains at or below the top of the footing, or below the slab, discharge by gravity or mechanical means into an approved drainage system.
  Source: ORSC R405.1 (2023 edition).

Gravel drains and perforated pipe have minimum dimensions.

  Source: ORSC R405.1, exception, and Table R405.1 (2023 edition).
```

## 5.15 A cistern inside the foundation footprint

**Simple**

A cistern is a tank for stored water. It can be built into the foundation as a basement room or as a vault under the slab. Walls that hold water in are built differently from walls that hold soil back. They follow a stricter concrete standard and are waterproofed on the side the water touches. The tank's design also covers the times it stands empty, because groundwater can push an empty tank upward.

**Professional**

Built into the foundation, a water-storage vault leaves the prescriptive path, whatever the rest of the foundation qualifies for. Wall tables in the code are written for soil held back rather than water held in. Below the water table, an empty vault also takes groundwater pressure, which brings in the hydrostatic rule for foundation walls and full below-grade waterproofing.

Structures built to hold liquid are designed to ACI 350, Code Requirements for Environmental Engineering Concrete Structures, rather than detailed as an extension of the surrounding ACI 332 wall. ACI 350 designs for liquid-tightness as well as strength. ACI's store listing for the 2020 edition and GlobalSpec's summary of it give a load factor of 1.7 for lateral liquid pressure, against 1.4 in ordinary design, with tighter limits on crack width and reinforcement detailing.

The drainage logic reverses for a cistern, because the footing drain relieves water that should not be there and the water in a cistern is meant to be there. Its shell gets dedicated positive-side waterproofing and, where specified, a crystalline admixture that seals minor cracks under sustained water contact. Its detailing keeps the building's footing drain from draining the tank or loading its outer face, and the overflow takes its own discharge route. Wherever the cistern shares a wall with an occupied basement room, that wall takes waterstops at every construction and control joint. The tank's floor is designed as its own slab, thicker and separately reinforced from the footings around it.

The empty tank governs flotation, because a cistern stands empty at known times: before the first fill, after a drawdown, and during inspection or maintenance. Its structural design covers that empty case with dead weight, tie-down anchors or a relief detail sized for it.

Whether to store water this way is decided in the water independence chapter, the water-retaining design is in the structural chapter, and the pour order is in the build-sequence chapter. The tank's base slab sits below the ordinary footing elevation, so the cistern excavation goes deeper and comes first. Its base and walls are placed as a separate, earlier pour with waterstops cast into every joint, before the surrounding footings and walls are formed. One foundation pour becomes at least two coordinated placements.

## 5.16 Retaining walls and terracing for cut and fill

**Simple**

A level pad on a slope needs a wall wherever the cut or the fill ends. The residential code draws the engineering line in two places. Any wall holding back more than 4 feet of soil, with nothing bracing its top, must be engineered. So must a wall over 2 feet that also carries a load beyond the soil, such as a driveway, a parked truck or a building. Below both lines, several wall types can be built by an owner without an engineer's stamp.

**Applied**

| Wall type | Practical height | Position against the code's triggers |
|---|---|---|
| Gravity segmental retaining wall (SRW), unreinforced | Up to 4 to 6 feet | Its mass alone resists the soil. A single tier at 48 inches or less with no surcharge needs no stamp |
| Gabion (stone-filled wire basket) | Up to 6 feet | A gravity wall, $15 to $30 per square foot, less where local stone cuts the hauling |
| Owner-made block gravity wall | Under 4 feet, with no surcharge | The same position as any gravity wall. Above the triggers it is designed as reinforced masonry |
| Geogrid-reinforced SRW or MSE wall | 6 to 50 feet and more | An engineered design at any height, and the economical engineered option as walls get taller |
| Cast-in-place concrete cantilever | Any substantial height | Engineered design and quality-controlled construction at any real retained height |

1. Keep each terrace tier at 48 inches of unbalanced fill or less, with no load on it beyond the soil.
2. Set each upper wall back from the lower one by at least twice the lower wall's height.
3. Allow 8 feet between two 4-foot tiers, plus each wall's base width.
4. Where the setback is less, have the pair engineered as one taller wall.
5. Measure each wall from the bottom of its footing to its top for the local permit floor, which the land chapter states with its exceptions.
6. Keep every cut deeper than 30 inches back from the septic system by the distances in the Code block.
7. Once a wall's height, length and load are known, get a per-wall engineering quote from a local structural engineer.
8. Budget the wall's engineering separately from the whole-house structural fee of $4,000 to $10,000.

**Professional**

Surcharge means any load beyond the retained earth itself: a driveway's vehicle and pavement load, a building pad, stacked block-yard material or parking. The code puts no number on the surcharge trigger, so the local building official applies it case by case. Foundation walls that support the house are outside the retaining-wall rule and follow the foundation-wall rules instead.

Segmental-retaining-wall industry guidance, Allan Block's technical sheet TS1297 and the Masonry and Hardscapes Association's SRW FAQ, sets the zone of influence behind a lower wall at about twice its height. An upper wall inside that zone surcharges the lower one, so two tiers stand as independent walls only when the setback between them is at least twice the lower wall's height. This rule is industry guidance rather than code, and where tiers interact, the stability analysis models them as one wall.

A geogrid-reinforced segmental wall, also called a mechanically stabilized earth (MSE) wall, carries lateral pressure as tension in geogrid layers that reach back into the retained soil, so the reinforced soil mass acts as one gravity block. Published cost comparisons from Concretemetric and Infraconcrete put reinforced and MSE systems 25 to 50 percent below a cast concrete cantilever wall once the wall is taller than about ten feet. Above the code's triggers every material needs the same engineering step, so the choice among systems turns on cost, labor, appearance and available skill. Owner-made block walls above them are designed as reinforced masonry under TMS 402, the discipline the house's above-grade block walls use.

Terracing saves the engineering fee and spends ground, because the setbacks between tiers, each tier's base width and any access space use up horizontal ground that a single engineered wall leaves flat. On a constrained pad or a narrow buildable strip, the single wall recovers more flat area per foot of slope. On an open slope, terraced gravity walls stay within an owner-builder's labor and skill.

## 5.17 Driveway culverts and watercourse crossings

**Simple**

A rural driveway that crosses a ditch or a seasonal creek depends on one culvert, a pipe that carries the water under the road. If the pipe is too small or its outlet is unprotected, a big storm overtops it or scours it out. That washout closes the only way in or out of the property. Pipe size comes from the land that drains to the crossing and the local storm rainfall.

**Applied**

1. Get the county road department's driveway-approach and culvert standard, which sets the design storm and the minimum diameter.
2. Calculate the peak flow by the Rational Method, Q = C x i x A, from the real upstream area and local rainfall.
3. Hire a civil engineer for anything beyond a minor ditch crossing.
4. Before installing or replacing a culvert in a stream, ask ODFW whether native migratory fish are or were present there.
5. Where they are, get a fish-passage plan or a waiver approved before work starts.
6. Ask the Department of State Lands whether the crossing needs a removal-fill permit.
7. Build a headwall at the outlet and armor it with riprap against scour.
8. Each fall before the rains, clear the inlet of debris and check the outlet for scour.
9. Budget $500 to $3,000 per crossing, by size and installation difficulty.

**Professional**

In the Rational Method, Q is the peak flow in cubic feet per second, C the runoff coefficient for the land cover, i the rainfall intensity of the design storm and A the area draining to the crossing. The method needs the real upstream area and local rainfall data, which a site survey and a hydrology calculation supply.

Oregon county standards set floors that override a smaller calculated size. Klamath County requires driveway culverts sized to the 25-year, 24-hour storm, with a 12-inch minimum diameter, and other county road standards use the 25-year storm and sometimes the 50-year storm for a residential crossing. Stormwater magazine's "Ten Rules of Thumb for Culvert Crossings" puts the practical minimum at 18 inches where a defined channel exists. Larger pipes pass debris that clogs smaller ones, and 18-inch corrugated metal pipe is among the strongest standard sizes for its weight.

Deschutes, Klamath and Lincoln counties each publish their own road-approach and culvert standard. For the parcel itself, the final diameter, slope and headwater design come from a hydrology calculation against the controlling county standard.

Under the stream-simulation option of OAR 635-412-0035, the crossing's bed also matches the slope and elevation of the streambed on either side. An open-bottomed structure keeps 3 feet of vertical clearance above the active channel width elevation, and a closed-bottom culvert carries bed material at 20 to 50 percent of its height. Alternative designs can be approved where fish-performance and hydraulic data show they pass fish.

Planning for a second access is in the resilience chapter. Priced at design time, a secondary track uses the unit costs of any gravel road: $4 to $10 per square foot, including site preparation, 6 to 12 inches of compacted gravel base and drainage. For a 12-foot single-lane track that comes to $50 to $120 per linear foot before culverts. Totals for an alternate route depend on its length, terrain and easements, and a road contractor prices them from the actual alignment. A track graded while the machines are on site for the driveway and house pad adds only its own construction cost. Cut after a washout, the same track adds an emergency mobilization.

**Code**

```
Culverts in waters with native migratory fish need ODFW's passage approval first.
  Before a culvert is installed or replaced in waters of the state, its owner gets ODFW's determination of fish presence and, where fish are present, approval of passage or a waiver.
  Source: ORS 509.585(4) and ORS 509.580(1) and (2)(d).

A stream-simulation crossing keeps a bed at least as wide as the channel.
  The bed under or within the crossing structure is equal to or greater than the active channel width, measured outside any constriction upstream and downstream.
  Source: OAR 635-412-0035(3)(a)(A)(i).
```

## 5.18 Site water beyond the foundation

**Simple**

Rain off the roof, the driveway and the work pads needs somewhere to go besides the foundation. A dry well is a buried pit filled with gravel that lets water soak into the ground. Rain gardens are planted low spots that hold runoff while it soaks in. Swales, shallow grassed channels, carry water along the slope. Each one is sized from the area that drains into it, and none of them may empty onto the septic drainfield.

**Applied**

1. Size a dry well to hold the first inch of rain from the area draining to it.
2. Figure 0.623 gallons per square foot of that area, so 500 square feet of roof needs 311 gallons.
3. In a stone-filled pit, count only the gaps between the stones as storage. A hollow chamber of the same size stores more.
4. Expect a common residential dry well to measure 4 feet by 4 feet by 3 feet.
5. Size a rain garden at 10 to 20 percent of the area draining to it.
6. Or divide that area by 6 on clay and by 8 on sand, for a garden about 6 inches deep.
7. By the second method, a 1,200-square-foot roof needs 150 to 200 square feet of garden.
8. Set the rain garden at least 10 feet from the foundation, on 6 to 12 inches of amended soil.
9. Size a swale to its peak flow by the Rational Method, using the county's design storm, a 2-year or 10-year storm for ordinary drainage.
10. Route first-flush water, the first and dirtiest runoff a roof-collection system diverts, into these same features.
11. Keep first-flush water and every other drain off the septic absorption area and its replacement area.
12. Give the block-yard pad its own perimeter swale or berm, aimed at the same downstream feature.
13. Re-check that feature's capacity by the Rational Method against the enlarged total area.
14. Keep vehicles, paving, livestock and stored material off the absorption area and its replacement area.
15. Before the site plan is final, add up every disturbed area (house pad, driveway, septic and block-yard) to see whether the total reaches one acre.

**Professional**

Compacted and graded, a production pad sheds more of the rain, and sheds it faster, than the vegetated ground the original drainage sizing assumed. In the drainage calculation the pad counts as ordinary impervious area, added to the site's contributing area.

First-flush water is dirtier than the rain that follows it. Rainwater-harvesting suppliers Blue Barrel Systems and Rain Brothers put up to 90 percent of a roof's accumulated pollutants in the first 10 to 20 gallons from an average roof. Those pollutants are bacteria from bird and animal droppings and decayed insects, sediment, and metals leached from the roofing, zinc among them. Practice sends first-flush water to non-edible use, an ornamental drip line or the grading swale. Taking it into a food garden is an owner's decision, made with the roofing material in mind.

Saturating the absorption area takes away the hydraulic capacity the drainfield needs to accept effluent, a concern separate from water quality.

DEQ issues the 1200-C construction stormwater permit under ORS 468B.050, and the current permit runs from December 15, 2025 to December 14, 2030. It covers construction that disturbs one acre or more, counting cumulative disturbance and every part of a common plan of development. Clearing, grading, excavating, stockpiling and material staging all count toward the acre. Sites under one acre that belong to a larger common plan take the small-lot permit, at a reduced fee and with no annual fee. Applications go in at least 30 days before work starts, with an erosion and sediment control plan, a land use compatibility statement and current wetland or stream delineations. Work waits for DEQ's written notice of coverage.

The land chapter states the stormwater-permit entry, and the water independence chapter describes the first-flush hardware. Below the state threshold, a county grading or erosion-control ordinance can still apply.

**Code**

```
Drainage stays off the septic absorption area.
  The initial and replacement absorption sites are not subjected to excessive saturation from artificial drainage of ground surfaces, driveways, roads or roof drains.
  Source: OAR 340-071-0220(1)(i).

The absorption area stays unpaved and free of traffic.
  The initial and replacement absorption sites are not covered by asphalt or concrete or subject to vehicular traffic, livestock or other soil-damaging activity.
  Source: OAR 340-071-0220(1)(h).

Septic parts keep fixed distances from buildings and lot lines.
  The absorption area stays 10 feet from any building foundation and from property lines, and the septic tank 5 feet from each.
  Source: OAR 340-071-0220, Table 1, items 9 and 11.

Groundwater interceptors keep their distance from the absorption area.
  On a slope of 3 percent or less, 20 feet, and on steeper ground, 10 feet upgradient and 50 feet downgradient of the absorption area.
  Source: OAR 340-071-0220, Table 1, item 5.
```

## 5.19 Earthwork machines

**Simple**

Most of the digging on this site is excavator work, and most of the moving is loader work. A mini excavator digs deep, precise holes and trenches with straight walls: footings, utility trenches, the septic tank and drainfield. A compact track loader, a skid steer on tracks, moves and spreads material. It travels 7 to 10 miles an hour, against the excavator's 2 to 3. The block-yard pad, the driveway base and the gravel piles are loader work.

**Applied**

| Task | Primary machine | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Footing excavation | Mini excavator | Digging depth and precision. A 3-ton machine reaches 8 to 10 feet |
| Utility trenches | Mini excavator | Clean vertical trench walls and control near existing utilities |
| Septic tank and drainfield installation | Mini excavator | Depth and precision |
| Driveway cut and rough grade | Both | The excavator makes the first cut through undisturbed sloping ground, and the loader finishes the grade and spreads the base |
| Block-yard pad preparation | Compact track loader | Grading and compacting a flat surface is moving and spreading work |
| Bulk material moves (gravel, spoils) | Compact track loader | Travel speed |

1. Rent each machine for the window its tasks fall in.
2. Plan the longer rental for the excavator, which serves the footings, then the trenches, then the septic system and later drainage work.
3. Plan one short rental for the loader, whose tasks cluster in the early site work.
4. Take weekly or monthly rates for sustained use. Weekly rates run 20 to 40 percent below daily rates per day.
5. Estimate total machine-hours from the earthwork takeoff before comparing renting with buying.
6. Consider owning the excavator only if the build spans several seasons.

**Professional**

Ownership, rental terms and the wider tool inventory are in the tools chapter. Whether renting or buying wins depends on the total machine-hours, the regional rental rates and used-equipment prices at the time of purchase. Monthly rental gives the best per-day value for sustained use, and an excavator that serves four phases of the build is the machine most likely to justify a long rental or ownership.

## 5.20 Engineered fill and compaction

**Simple**

Engineered fill is soil or crushed rock placed in thin layers, each packed to a measured density and tested. It raises a building pad, replaces bad soil or makes an even layer for footings. Loose or untested fill is not engineered fill, even when it looks solid, and a footing on it can settle. Placing the fill can be the owner's own work. The density tests have to come from an independent tester for the building official to accept them.

**Applied**

| Fill thickness under a footing | Documentation to plan for |
|---|---|
| Under 12 inches | Visual observation with no formal documentation, where the building department accepts it |
| 12 to 24 inches | Standard or Modified Proctor tests, plus a field density check at each 1-foot lift |
| Over 24 inches, or critical fill under footings, retaining walls or below grade | A fill specification stamped by a professional engineer, a daily inspection log kept by a qualified soils technician, density tests at the specified frequency (one test per 500 to 2,500 square feet of each lift), and a final engineer's letter certifying compliance |

1. Before placing any structural fill, commission a one-day geotechnical consultation for the fill specification.
2. Get the specification's density targets, lift thickness, moisture conditioning and testing frequency in writing.
3. Hire a separate testing agency, or the same geotechnical firm, to verify the in-place density.
4. Strip topsoil and organics from the fill footprint.
5. Proof-roll the exposed subgrade, and mark soft areas for over-excavation.
6. Place the fill in lifts of the specified thickness, 6 to 8 inches loose and 4 to 6 inches compacted unless the specification says otherwise.
7. Bring each lift to within 2 percentage points of optimum moisture.
8. Compact each lift to the specified target, 95 percent of Standard Proctor or 92 percent of Modified Proctor unless the specification says otherwise.
9. Test the density at the specified frequency.
10. Record each test by station, depth, density, moisture and lift number.

**Professional**

No owner can self-certify engineered fill at the larger thicknesses. Its documentation comes from a registered design professional or an AASHTO-certified soils technician, and the building official accepts that third-party record as the evidence that the fill meets the design.

Cone readings relate to Proctor density differently in each material. A penetration rate that means 95 percent of Standard Proctor in a well-graded sandy gravel means something else in a silty clay. MnDOT's method builds a material-specific target from lab Proctor results and cone tests on lab specimens at known densities. Once that calibration exists, the cone is a reasonable check on compacted fill, and without it the cone gives only relative comparison and gross identification.

Engineered fill fails under a footing in five recurring ways:

- placed on uncompacted, unverified subgrade
- placed thicker than the compaction equipment can densify
- placed too dry to reach density, or too wet, so that it pumps and stays weak under load
- made of uncontrolled material, such as crushed concrete with rebar, demolition debris, frozen soil or organic matter
- left without its density verification

**Code**

```
Fill under footings is engineered and tested.
  Fill soils that support footings and foundations are designed, installed and tested under nonprescriptive design.
  Source: ORSC R401.2 (2023 edition).

Fill under a slab is clean, compacted and limited in depth.
  Free of vegetation and foreign material, compacted for uniform support, and no deeper than 24 inches of clean sand or gravel or 8 inches of earth unless approved.
  Source: ORSC R506.2.1 (2023 edition).
```

## 5.21 Protecting the open excavation in the wet season

**Simple**

Rain changes an open hole. A trench wall that stood firm in dry weather can slump without warning once it is soaked. At the bottom, the soil the footing will sit on turns soft when it gets wet and loses strength when it freezes overnight. Concrete poured on it settles. Two things protect the work: walls sloped or braced for their soil, and a clean, firm, unfrozen bearing surface on pour day.

**Applied**

1. Name a competent person, the individual responsible for excavation safety.
2. Have that person classify each soil deposit as Stable Rock, Type A, Type B or Type C before anyone enters.
3. Slope or bench the walls to the classification's maximum angle, or use shoring or a trench shield where sloping will not fit.
4. Where no one has classified the soil, slope the walls at 1 1/2 horizontal to 1 vertical.
5. Have a registered engineer design the protective system for any excavation over 20 feet deep.
6. Reclassify after rain. Saturated soil can drop a class, which flattens the slope or calls for shoring.
7. Cut a diversion ditch or a berm uphill of the excavation to keep surface water out.
8. Keep everyone out of standing or rising water in an excavation until a shield, shoring, monitored pumping or a harness is in place.
9. Before the pour, clear loose soil, mud, standing water, debris and ice from the bearing surface.
10. Where the subgrade softens between excavation and pour, correct it one of three ways:
   - let it dry
   - over-excavate the soft material and replace it with compacted fill or clean aggregate
   - place a mud mat, a lean-concrete leveling course 2 to 3 inches thick with no reinforcement
11. Schedule the footing inspection close to the pour date. Inspections that find mud, ice or standing water fail.

**Professional**

Oregon OSHA's excavation rules protect employees, so they bind from the day a paid worker enters the excavation. Digging alone, an owner faces the same soil, and the project follows the same slopes and water rules.

Water lowers a soil's actual classification below what a dry-season look suggests. A deposit the competent person would call Type A when dry can behave as Type B or Type C when saturated, which is why the classification is repeated after rain.

Mud mats are wet-weather practice rather than a code element. Their use and thickness are a means-and-methods decision that the contractor and the inspector make in the field for the subgrade in front of them.

Concrete International's June 2022 Q&A, answered by the American Society of Concrete Contractors, quotes ACI 318-19 section 26.5.2.1(b). That provision requires standing water to be removed before placement unless a tremie is used or both the licensed design professional and the building official permit otherwise.

Cold-weather placement and the rest of the concrete side of the pour are in the foundation chapter. For residential footings, ACI 332.1R-18 and the commentary to ACI 332-20 accept letting the concrete displace incidental water where the forms let it escape. Where the water cannot escape, it is pumped or drained, because water the concrete cannot displace changes the designed water-cement ratio. In either case the bearing surface is confirmed firm before the pour.

**Code**

```
Each soil deposit is classified before a protective system is chosen.
  A competent person classifies each deposit as Stable Rock, Type A, Type B or Type C, and the class selects the slope, shoring or shield.
  Source: 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P, Appendix A, adopted by OAR 437-003-0001(16).

Excavation slopes are capped by soil type.
  For excavations less than 20 feet deep, Stable Rock vertical (90 degrees), Type A 3/4:1 (53 degrees), Type B 1:1 (45 degrees), Type C 1 1/2:1 (34 degrees).
  Source: 29 CFR 1926.652 and Subpart P, Appendix B, Table B-1, adopted by OAR 437-003-0001(16).

Work in accumulating water takes added protection.
  Support or shield systems, water removal monitored by a competent person, or a safety harness and lifeline protect employees working where water is accumulating.
  Source: 29 CFR 1926.651(h), adopted by OAR 437-003-0001(16).

Footings never bear on frozen soil.
  Footings do not bear on frozen soil unless the frozen condition is permanent.
  Source: ORSC R403.1.4.1 (2023 edition).
```

## 5.22 Backfill against foundation walls

**Simple**

Backfill is the soil pushed back against a foundation wall once the wall is built. It presses sideways on the wall from the first day. Basement walls resist that push through their connections at the top and the bottom. Backfill placed before the floor above is built pushes on a wall whose top is free to move. Such a wall leans in or cracks, and repairing it means digging the backfill out and rebuilding or reinforcing the wall.

**Applied**

1. Wait at least 7 days after the pour, or longer where the structural drawings require it.
2. Brace the top of the wall before any backfill is placed.
3. Where the floor that braces the wall is not built yet, install temporary diagonal bracing.
4. Use heavy timbers bearing at two-thirds of the wall height, about 12 feet apart for an 8-foot wall.
5. Backfill against the wall with free-draining granular material: clean sand, crushed gravel or crushed stone.
6. Reject high-plasticity clay as backfill. Test moderate-clay native soil before using it, and blend it with granular material.
7. Place backfill in lifts 6 to 8 inches loose, and compact each lift before placing the next.
8. Compact the zone against the wall with a hand-guided plate compactor or rammer, and keep vibratory rollers in the outer zone.
9. Where fill rises on both sides of a wall, raise both sides together in even lifts.
10. Photograph the bracing and the lift sequence for the binder.

**Professional**

As planning targets, the zone against the wall is compacted to about 90 percent of Standard Proctor maximum dry density with light equipment, and the outer zone to 93 to 95 percent. Granular backfill compacts predictably, drains to the footing drain and applies a steady lateral pressure. Cohesive backfill swells when wet and shrinks when dry, which works the wall and its connections with a cyclic lateral load.

ORSC R404.1.7 bars backfill until the wall has enough strength and is anchored to the floor above or braced, and it exempts walls holding less than 4 feet of unbalanced backfill from the bracing. That entry and the structural timing are in the foundation chapter. A wall backfilled before it is braced stands, for that time, as a wall without lateral support at the top. The height and soil condition at which that becomes unsafe is a structural question for the wall's designer, not a fixed number.

Both the cure time and the bracing layout come from poured-wall contractor guidance, Armada Poured Walls and Concretemetric among them. Concrete reaches about 75 percent of its design strength in 5 to 7 days, and the strength each wall needs before backfill is on the structural drawings.

**Code**

```
Tall unbalanced backfill makes a foundation wall an engineered wall.
  Concrete or masonry foundation walls holding more than 48 inches of unbalanced backfill without permanent lateral support at top and bottom require nonprescriptive design.
  Source: ORSC R404.1.1, item 2 (2023 edition).
```
