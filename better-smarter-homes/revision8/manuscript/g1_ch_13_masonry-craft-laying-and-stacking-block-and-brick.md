# Chapter 13: Masonry Craft: Laying And Stacking Block And Brick

This chapter describes the hand work of building the two masonry wythes: setting up references, making and matching mortar, laying block and brick, tooling, grouting, and the checks that prove the work. The wall's design (assembly, ties, drainage dimensions, movement joints) is in the Wall System chapter; unit production is in the Owner-Made Blocks And Alternative Concretes chapter; the governing engineering is in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter.

## 13.1 Setting Up: Leads, Lines, And Story Poles

**Simple**

Wall accuracy comes from the reference system, built before the wall. The reference: plumb corner sections, a stretched line between them, and a pole marked with every course height. Each block or brick is then laid to the line. The line replaces per-unit measurement.

**Leads.** A lead is a corner section built up ahead of the wall between corners. The mason checks each course of a lead for plumb and level with a spirit level, and for height against the story pole. The leads fix the wall's geometry; every unit between them follows the line.

**The line and its anchors.** A mason's line stretches corner to corner at the top of the course being laid. Line blocks (L-shaped blocks that hook over the corner unit and hold the line under tension) or line pins (steel pins driven into a head joint) anchor each end. Setting every intermediate unit to the line keeps the course straight and at height without re-measuring each unit. TMS 602 holds walls true to a line within the same tolerances as plumb (§13.13).

**The story pole.** A story pole (a straightedge pre-marked with every course height for the unit and joint thickness in use) transfers course heights to the leads. Re-marking the pole against the actual leads as they rise catches cumulative joint-thickness drift before it becomes visible in the wall. A shop-built pole, a straight length of lumber marked from the project's actual course height, does the same marking work as a manufactured course rule at near zero material cost.

**Manufactured guide-pole systems.** Two purchasable systems replace hand-built leads outright. The Story-Rite system stands on a self-supporting base that adjusts for plumb and presets the course heights, so no corner lead is built at all. The Bon Tool Model C guide is a 2 x 2-inch aluminum pole with brick and modular scales, fitted for both veneer and loadbearing block work, so one system serves both of this wall's wythes. Current delivered prices for either system were not retrievable in the research; the price check against the near-zero cost of a shop-built pole happens before purchase. The case for a guide-pole system is strongest for a solo mason: the pole holds the corner reference continuously, and no second person is available to re-check a hand-built lead while laying proceeds.

**Working the line alone.** Both ends of a solo mason's line must be self-securing, because no helper holds or adjusts the far end. The research located no documented solo-specific line-tensioning technique; the item stays open for a practitioner source (an experienced solo mason or a masonry trade forum). A guide-pole system reduces the repeated walking to re-set the far end, because the pole holds the line's reference position instead of a lead that needs rebuilding and re-checking.

**Applied**

Set up each wall run in this order:

1. Establish the corner reference at both ends: a hand-built lead checked course by course with level and story pole, or a guide-pole system set plumb.
2. Stretch the line at the top of the first course, anchored with line blocks or pins.
3. Lay every intermediate unit to the line. Move the line up one course at a time.
4. Re-check the story pole against the built leads at intervals to catch cumulative joint-thickness drift.

## 13.2 Mortar: Ingredients, Types, And Proportions

**Simple**

Mortar is portland cement, hydrated lime, sand, and water. The cement sets and gives early strength. The lime makes the mix workable, holds water, and keeps the hardened joint slightly flexible. The four types (M, S, N, O) differ mainly in the lime share. More cement means a harder, stiffer joint. More lime means a softer, more breathable one. Which type goes where in this wall is listed in the Wall System chapter.

**The proportion table.** ASTM C270 (the mortar specification) states each type as fixed volumes, cement normalized to 1. BIA Technical Note 8 quotes the table:

| Type | Portland cement | Hydrated lime | Sand |
|---|---|---|---|
| M | 1 | 1/4 | 2-1/4 to 3 times the combined cement-plus-lime volume |
| S | 1 | over 1/4 to 1/2 | same rule |
| N | 1 | over 1/2 to 1-1/4 | same rule |
| O | 1 | over 1-1/4 to 2-1/2 | same rule |

Sand is measured damp and loose. The same table lists masonry-cement and mortar-cement recipes (the cement alone, with sand at the same 2-1/4-to-3 rule) and blends of a Type N masonry or mortar cement with portland cement to reach Type M or S.

**Proportion versus property specification.** C270 contains two governing systems. Under the proportion specification, the recipe above is followed and no laboratory testing is required before or during construction. Under the property specification, a candidate mix is lab-tested first against minimum 28-day compressive strength (350 psi for Type O up to 2,500 psi for Type M, varying by binder), 75 percent minimum water retention, and an air-content cap. Where a project specification names neither, the proportion specification governs by default. The two systems are not interchangeable: a proportion-batched mix typically tests above the property table's number for the same type, and field mortar is not comparable to the lab batch that qualified the mix. For an owner without a materials lab, the proportion route with a consistent measuring container is the working method. A switch to the property route is forced only where the engineer of record designs to a verified mortar strength the standard recipes cannot be assumed to deliver; that requires a testing laboratory, as on a commercial project.

**What lime does; what cement does.** BIA Technical Note 8 assigns the roles directly: portland cement contributes durability, high strength, and early setting; hydrated lime contributes extent of bond, workability, water retention, and elasticity. Lime also seals its own hairline cracks: water and carbon dioxide entering a crack react with the mortar's calcium hydroxide to form calcium carbonate, which closes the crack (autogenous healing). High-lime mortars show greater plastic flow, which lets the masonry accept slight movement. This is the tuning mechanism applied in §13.4: moving from Type M toward Type O trades strength for flexibility, permeability, and self-healing. Only ASTM C207 Type S hydrated lime goes in mortar; Types NA and SA contain air-entraining additives that reduce bond.

**Cementitious systems.** Three ways to supply the binder:

- Field-mixed portland-cement-lime (PCL): the table above. Full owner control of every ratio, and full responsibility for consistent batching.
- Masonry cement (ASTM C91): a factory blend of cement and plasticizing materials, with 8 percent minimum entrained air (21 percent maximum for Type N, 19 for S and M). C91 sets no minimum flexural bond strength, and the model codes assign masonry-cement mortar lower allowable flexural tension than non-air-entrained cement-lime mortar.
- Mortar cement (ASTM C1329): a factory blend with a tighter air cap (14 percent for Types M and S, 16 for N) and a required minimum flexural bond strength (115, 100, and 70 psi for M, S, and N, by the ASTM C1072 bond-wrench test). The code treats mortar-cement and cement-lime mortars alike.

For the brick wythe, where flexural bond carries wind and earthquake load into the anchors, mortar cement or a properly batched cement-lime mix is the documented preference; masonry cement trades its convenience against a lower code-recognized bond value. For the loadbearing block wythe, the reinforcement carries the lateral demand, and masonry cement's workability is a reasonable choice there; running one system for the whole project is also reasonable. The unresolved veneer mortar-type seismic question sits with the engineer of record (Wall System chapter, Mortar And Grout).

**Sand.** ASTM C144 governs mortar sand. Its gradation limits, as commonly summarized (the standard itself is a purchased document):

- 100 percent passing the No. 4 sieve, tapering to 0-5 percent (natural sand) or 0-10 percent (manufactured) passing the No. 200
- no more than 50 percent retained between any two consecutive sieves
- no more than 25 percent retained between the No. 50 and No. 100
- a fineness-modulus drift over 0.20 from the assumed value rejects the sand unless the mix is re-proportioned

The failure modes map one to one:

- Too fine: more grain surface to coat, higher water demand, more drying shrinkage (§13.16).
- Too coarse or gap-graded: a harsh, unworkable board, the same defect as oversanding.
- Dirty (silt, clay, organics): coated grains bond poorly, and the contaminants seed efflorescence and staining.

A jar test (sand and water shaken in a clear jar and left to settle into readable layers) screens a pile for gross fines or silt before it is batched. It does not replace a sieve analysis where documented compliance is required.

**Water.** Clean, potable, free of acids, alkalis, and organic material. Seawater and high-sulfate or contaminated water are excluded: dissolved salts seed efflorescence (§13.16), and chloride from any source corrodes the ties, joint reinforcement, and bars sealed inside the wall (§13.5).

**Batching without a scale.** BIA Technical Note 7B states that sand measurement by shovel should not be permitted. A shovel load varies with the sand's moisture, the person, and the shovel. The drift produces oversanded mortar, harsh and weak-bonding, which the note identifies as one of the most common mortar problems. The fix is a fixed-volume container (a 5-gallon bucket used as one part) used for every material, in every batch, by the same person, for the whole build. Bagged cement and lime arrive at known density (94 pounds per cubic foot for portland cement, 40 for hydrated lime), so the container is calibrated once against a bag and stays in service.

## 13.3 Mixing, Water Content, Board Life, And Buying

**Simple**

Fresh mortar is mixed wetter than intuition suggests. The block or brick pulls water out the moment they touch. A dry, stiff mix loses that water too fast and never bonds well. A batch stays usable for about two and a half hours. After it begins to set, more water cannot revive it.

**Water content and retempering.** BIA Technical Note 8 calls water content the most misunderstood aspect of masonry mortar, and directs mixing with the maximum water consistent with workability, not the minimum. The reasoning is the opposite of concrete practice: the mortar's effective water-cement ratio drops as the unit absorbs mix water, so a deliberately dry mix produces lower bond, not higher durability. Retempering (adding water on the board to replace evaporation loss) is expected and encouraged within board life. Compressive strength drops slightly and the color lightens, but bond strength drops if a drying board is not retempered. Colored mortar is the exception: retempering shifts its color visibly (§13.8).

**Board life.** ASTM C270 requires all mortar used within 2-1/2 hours of mixing. BIA Technical Note 7B tightens the field figure to 2 hours in hot weather, and gives grout 1-1/2 hours, measured from first water. The clock is a proxy; the governing boundary is initial set. A batch that has begun to set, or that stays stiff when worked with a trowel, is discarded. Re-wetting past set breaks the cement's forming crystal structure instead of restoring plasticity.

**Mixing.** Cementitious materials and aggregate mix 3 to 5 minutes in a mechanical batch mixer. The solo builder's equipment options: a mortar tub and hoe (lowest cost, most labor per batch, hardest to hold water content consistent), a heavy drill with a mixing paddle in a bucket (small batches), or a small drum mixer, rented or bought. For weeks of daily mixing, trade practice favors the drum mixer, on fatigue and batch consistency rather than tool cost. The mortar board is scraped clean between batches; dried residue changes the next batch's water behavior.

**Buying the materials.** The research found no reliable current Oregon price table for bagged portland, bagged lime, bulk sand, or pigment. Prices are confirmed with at least two Oregon suppliers (a masonry yard and a big-box retailer) near the purchase date. The structural comparison is documented:

- Site-mix from separate bags plus bulk sand: cheapest per batch of mortar, most labor, highest proportioning-error risk.
- Pre-blended bagged mortar or mortar cement: a higher bag price that removes the cement-lime proportioning error; the documented best fit for a solo builder's daily volume.
- Delivered silo systems: continuous-mix contractor equipment that amortizes only at commercial daily output.

The reasoned middle path, stated as inference rather than a sourced recommendation: pre-blended bags for the cementitious fraction, with sand bought in bulk by the yard and still measured by the fixed container of §13.2.

**Code**

```
Mortar must be used within its board life.
  All mortar used within 2-1/2 hours after mixing; a batch that has begun to set is discarded, not retempered.
  Source: ASTM C270 (rule as quoted by BIA Technical Note 8; the purchased standard was not independently read).
```

## 13.4 Matching Mortar To The Unit

**Simple**

The joint is the wall's sacrificial part. Every masonry wall moves slightly with heat, moisture, and settlement (Wall System chapter, Differential Movement). The movement must be absorbed somewhere. A mortar softer than the unit yields at the joint, and a worn joint can be repointed (§13.15). A mortar harder than the unit pushes the movement into the brick face, and the face cracks or pops off. A broken unit cannot be repaired. Mortar is therefore chosen no harder, and no less vapor-open, than its unit.

**Why the strongest type is the wrong default.** National Park Service Preservation Brief 2 states the principle: mortar is intended to be sacrificial, relieving the wall's stresses in the joint rather than in the units. A mortar harder than the unit may remain intact while the masonry around it deteriorates. Type M is stiffer and less extensible than the lime-richer types. Used on a veneer, it transfers movement stress into the fired brick, which cracks or spalls instead of flexing at the joint. A veneer carries only its own weight; its mortar's functions are bond, load transfer to the anchors, and water shedding, and none of them improves with maximum compressive strength. BIA Technical Note 8 recommends Type N for most veneer work on this basis, reserving Type M for high-load and below-grade conditions where its rigidity is a fair trade.

**The permeability half.** A high-cement joint is also less vapor-permeable than most units. Moisture inside the wall then exits through the unit face instead of the joint. In freezing weather the saturated face spalls; water expanding in the unit's pores has no relief path. Hardness and permeability rise together with cement content, so one wrong type selection produces both failure modes at once.

**Measuring brick suction.** Initial rate of absorption (IRA) is measured in grams of water absorbed per minute per 30 square inches of bed surface (ASTM C67, the laboratory method). Above 30, the brick pulls water from the mortar before initial set, and TMS 602 (as quoted by BIA Technical Note 7B) requires wetting such brick before laying, bringing the as-laid rate under 30. Wetting happens the day before laying, or at latest several hours before, so the units reach saturated-surface-dry: wet inside, dry to the touch. Surface-wet brick is not the goal. Wetting low-absorption brick backfires: the saturated unit cannot absorb the joint's excess water, the joint bleeds, and the brick floats (slides on an unconsolidated joint). For brick under roughly 5 g/min per 30 square inches, the mortar-side correction runs the other way: lower water retention.

**Applied**

The field suction test, from BIA Technical Note 7B:

1. Draw a 1-inch circle on the brick's bed face with a wax pencil (a quarter is a usable template).
2. Place 20 drops of water inside the circle with a medicine dropper.
3. Time the absorption. Over 1-1/2 minutes: lay the brick with normal methods. Under 1-1/2 minutes: pre-wet the packs, or raise the mortar's water retention.

**Reclaimed, soft, and owner-made units.** The tuning tool is §13.2's lime ratio: moving toward Type N or O softens and opens the joint. Genuinely soft units (pre-1930s brick and comparable low-fired material) move past the C270 table entirely, into lime-putty or natural-hydraulic-lime mortar, per the historic-preservation literature. An owner-made block or low-fired brick sits in the same category until testing proves otherwise, because the unit's real strength is whatever the owner's production achieved. The working method for this project's own units: test the block's compressive strength (§13.13's cube tests), then select a mortar type deliberately below it. The documented mistake that inverts the relationship is reaching for a harder mortar because the unit looks weak. The correction runs the other way: a weaker or more variable unit is paired with a softer mortar, not a harder one. Unit production and testing are Owner-Made Blocks And Alternative Concretes chapter subjects; the resulting repointing cycle is a Documentation, Maintenance, And Future Upgrades subject.

**The field cue for a mismatch.** Damage location is the diagnostic. Cracks running through joints with units intact: the wall is behaving as designed, and the eventual fix is repointing. Cracks and spalls through the unit faces, especially early in the wall's life: the mortar is harder or tighter than the unit, and the specification or the batching is wrong. The response is to stop laying, re-check the batch proportions against §13.2, and re-test the unit match per §13.4 before the next course.

## 13.5 Admixtures, Pigments, And Folk Additives

**Simple**

An admixture changes one property of the mortar, usually at the cost of another. A few solve real scheduling problems on a hot or cold day. Some add cost without a documented benefit. A few kitchen substitutes quietly damage the wall. This section lists each family, what it changes, and what it costs in bond or durability.

**The five admixture families.** ASTM C1384 (the mortar-admixture specification), as summarized by BIA Technical Note 8:

- Bond enhancers: raise flexural bond, surface density, and freeze-thaw resistance; intended for smooth, dense units (copings, pavers); never combined with an air-entrainer.
- Set accelerators: cut cement hydration time roughly 30 to 40 percent, shortening the cold-weather protection window (§13.11); they raise short-term strength and can shift color.
- Set retarders: extend board life for hot weather or high-suction brick. A retarded batch is not retempered, may need moist curing, and sets slower than habit expects, so tooling waits for the thumbprint test, not the clock (§13.8). Short-term strength drops.
- Integral water repellents: a matched mortar-plus-unit system, dosed at the block plant and the mixer together. In mortar alone they can inhibit bond and are not recommended with brick. The matched-system products are in the Wall System chapter (Problems And Solutions).
- Workability enhancers: BIA's text calls the benefit subjective, suiting the liking of the mason; a real effect, not a measured performance gain.

Pozzolans (fly ash, slag cement) substitute for part of the portland cement, improving hydration efficiency, long-term strength, pore structure, and workability; BIA's text presents this family without a stated trade-off.

**Air entrainment.** Raising entrained air raises workability and freeze-thaw resistance while lowering compressive and bond strength. The property-specification tables cap it: 12 percent for cement-lime and mortar-cement Types M and S, 14 percent for N and O, tightened to 12 where structural reinforcement is present; masonry cement runs 18 to 20 percent by the same tables. BIA recommends against adding an air-entrainer at all: mortar already carries enough entrapped and entrained air to resist freeze-thaw damage, and masonry cement is already air-entrained by its own standard. The real decision is which cementitious system to buy (§13.2), not how much air to add.

**Accelerators and the chloride prohibition.** Calcium chloride, and every chloride-bearing "antifreeze," is prohibited in grout and not recommended in mortar; BIA Technical Note 8 caps any admixture at 0.2 percent chloride-ion content. Chloride ions strip the passive oxide film off embedded steel, and the ties, joint reinforcement, and bars then corrode where no inspection or replacement is possible. Non-chloride accelerators (calcium-nitrite and calcium-nitrate families) are the industry substitute, used as a supplement to heating and enclosure, never a replacement. An accelerator speeds hydration; it does not lower the freezing point of the mix water, and fresh masonry still gets physical protection (§13.11). No specific product or dose was verified in the research; the manufacturer's current data sheet (Sika, GCP, or Euclid non-chloride lines) governs before anything goes in the mixer.

**Bonding agents.** SikaLatex R (confirmed from the manufacturer's literature) is an acrylic latex used two ways: neat, replacing part of the mix water, for a polymer-modified repair mortar; or mixed roughly 1 part cement to 2 parts sand to a paint consistency as a brushed slurry bond coat. The documented use case is bonding to an already-hardened, low-suction surface (repairs, parging, collar-joint work) that cannot pull water from fresh mortar the way a new absorbent unit does. For ordinary unit laying it adds cost without a documented benefit, because unit suction is the bond mechanism C270 mortar is designed around. Two other circulating product names and a re-emulsification classification were not verified in the research and are not stated as fact here.

**Pigments.** ASTM C979 pigments only, dosed at the minimum quantity that reaches the color. Metallic-oxide pigments cap at 10 percent of cement weight. Carbon black caps at 2 percent, because it sharply reduces strength beyond that. Prussian blue, cadmium lithopone, and zinc and lead chromates are named unsuitable; ordinary paint pigments may not be suitable at all. Color consistency is a process property, not just a dose. The controls:

- cement and pigment premixed in large controlled batches
- one mixing sequence
- constant water content
- constant unit moisture
- no retempering
- constant tooling timing

§13.8 states the realistic palette for a solo mason.

**Folk additives.** Each has a real mechanism, which is why each does real damage:

- Dish soap: an uncontrolled air-entrainer. It buys workability with unmeasured air and takes the bond-strength penalty of the air caps above, steeply and unpredictably.
- Sugar: a genuine retarder with no dose control. An over-retarded batch sets in hours or days, or incompletely, and cannot be retempered.
- Table salt: chloride corrosion of every embedded metal, plus deliberate seeding of the soluble salts efflorescence requires (§13.16). It does not meaningfully protect fresh mortar from freezing.
- Fireclay for workability: mentioned in trade discussion; no source confirming the practice or its effects was located, and it stays out of this project's mix.

**Efflorescence at the source.** The documented materials-side levers are ordinary: cement, lime, sand, and water meeting their ASTM standards; materials stored dry and off the ground; joints tooled tight (§13.8). The Wall System chapter's problems catalog lists the assembly-level levers, including the low-alkali cement option. The one documented conflict: an integral water repellent pursued for efflorescence control must be the matched mortar-plus-unit system above, or it trades bond strength for stain resistance.

**A tested-tradition trial: sticky-rice lime mortar.** Peer-reviewed materials research on 1,500-year-old Chinese construction identified amylopectin (a rice starch) as a functional additive in lime mortar: it inhibits calcium carbonate crystal growth during carbonation, producing a compact microstructure with greater strength and better compatibility than plain lime mortar. Two limits stand before it goes near this project. The documented mechanism is specific to lime carbonation, not portland hydration, so a benefit in a C270 cement-lime mix is unproven. And no modern dosage or trial protocol exists in the sources. Its place, if the owner runs it at all, is a measured trial batch against a high-lime control, evaluated with §13.13's flow, cube, and absorption tests. The century-scale reasoning sits with the Designing For Centuries chapter.

**Ferrocement mortar is a different material.** The ferrocement mixes documented for tanks and thin-shell work (a lime-free 1:1.5-to-3 cement-sand mix, batched by weight at a 0.35-to-0.5 water-cement ratio) serve a continuous steel-mesh shell, not a jointed wall. Neither mix substitutes for the other. The ferrocement application is a Water Independence chapter subject.

## 13.6 Laying The Inner Wythe (CMU)

**Simple**

The block wythe is the structure. It carries the roof, resists earthquakes, and holds the steel and grout. Laying it well means full, even joints, cells kept clean for grout, and every course to the line. Block is heavy, and a misplaced course is slow to redo. The reference setup of §13.1 controls the accuracy; laying repeats it.

**Bedding.** Standard CMU practice mortars the face shells (the two long outer walls of the block); the hollow cores make a full bed impractical. Which webs also get mortar, and where full bedding is required, is set by TMS 402/602 and the stamped drawings. The exact structural bedding rule is inside the purchased standard; the drawings govern. Head joints are formed by buttering the block ends fully and shoving the unit against its neighbor until mortar squeezes out. Mortar thrown into an open joint with the trowel edge ("slushing") leaves voids behind a finished-looking face (§13.7 states the mechanism).

**The laying rhythm.** A uniform mortar bed is spread over only a few units at a time, furrowed lightly if at all. Each unit is set within about one minute of spreading, before the bed loses water and workability, then aligned to the line. Excess mortar squeezed from the joints is cut off with the trowel edge and returned to the board while fresh. Beds beside open cells that will be grouted are beveled away from the cell, so squeezed mortar extrudes toward the face rather than dropping down the core (§13.9 covers cell cleanliness in full).

**Bond pattern and its structural consequence.** Running bond (each course offset half a unit) interlocks the wall so loads spread diagonally as they travel down. Stack bond (units aligned, continuous vertical joints) reads clean and modern, and CMHA TEK 14-06 quantifies its price. An unreinforced stack-bond wall spanning horizontally reaches about 30 percent of running bond's strength. A concentrated load travels down the single column of units below it, with little distribution to the neighbors. The prescriptive recovery is horizontal reinforcement at a minimum of 0.00028 times the gross vertical wall cross-section, satisfied by joint reinforcement at the appropriate spacing or bond beams at 48 inches on center maximum. The bond-pattern decision therefore belongs on the structural drawings, not on the scaffold. The Structural System And Concrete Design chapter states the code entry for stack-bond reinforcement.

**Architectural block.** CMHA TEK 02-03A catalogs the factory finish families, each available in many colors and sizes:

- split face
- ribbed
- scored
- ground face
- sandblasted
- striated
- glazed

Finish and color are appearance selections layered onto ordinary structural CMU manufacture; the wall's structural specification does not change with the face. Owner-made units have their own appearance options, covered in the Owner-Made Blocks And Alternative Concretes chapter.

**Surface-bonded dry-stack, the mortarless alternative.** CMHA TEK 14-22 and 03-05A document a different laying system entirely: ordinary CMU stacked dry in running bond, then coated both faces with fiber-reinforced surface-bonding cement at 1/8 inch minimum thickness. The coating bridges and keys the units together, with strength described as superior to conventionally mortared walls when correctly applied. One condition is absolute: the wall's strength and stability depend entirely on the coating, applied to both sides. A wall coated on one face only lacks its structural system. Design uses f'dm (the dry-stack assembly's own tested compressive strength), a different value from mortared-wall design strength, so the path runs through the engineer of record. For a solo first-time builder it removes the entire mortar-joint skill curve for outbuildings and practice structures. Whether it enters the house wall is an engineering and acceptance question, not a craft one (Owner-Made Blocks And Alternative Concretes chapter, Oregon acceptance path).

## 13.7 Laying The Outer Wythe (Brick)

**Simple**

Brick veneer keeps water out by the quality of its joints. Wind-driven rain gets past brick. The wall design assumes this and drains the leakage through the cavity behind. Joint filling sets how much water gets in and how strong the bond is. The two classic shortcuts look finished and leak: the furrowed bed and the slushed head joint.

**Full joints, and what the shortcuts do.** BIA Technical Note 7B states the rule from laboratory testing and field observation: there is no substitute for proper filling of every joint designed to receive mortar. Improperly filled joints produce leaky walls, lower strength, and freeze-thaw disintegration. For solid brick, full bed joints (mortar across the entire bedding surface) are an inherent requirement of water-resistant brickwork. The bed is spread over a few units and furrowed lightly, if at all; a deep furrow leaves a hollow channel under the unit. Face-shell bedding of hollow brick is permitted only in non-veneer walls; for veneer, full beds are recommended even with hollow units. Head joints are formed by buttering the brick end completely and shoving it against the previously laid brick until mortar squeezes out the top. The squeeze is the visible proof the joint is full. Slushing throws mortar into the open joint, where it bridges and hangs, leaving unbonded voids behind the face. A slushed joint later reads as "separation": it opens under normal wall movement, or passes water outright, because there was never a bonded joint behind the surface. The same mechanism governs collar joints in solid multi-wythe masonry, where slushed mortar hangs partway down. This wall's open drainage cavity replaces the collar joint; the rule matters here only for any solid-masonry sections elsewhere on the property.

**Suction management.** The IRA test and wetting procedure of §13.4 run ahead of the brick, not behind it: packs are tested when delivered and wetted the day before laying where needed. On a hot day the leading edge of spread mortar stays within 4 feet of the last brick laid (§13.11), and each brick is set within about a minute of spreading.

**Bond patterns and their real cost.** Running bond is the default: least cutting, least waste, fastest laying. The header-pattern bonds (Flemish, English, common) alternate or interleave headers and require cut closer units at corners and returns. The documented planning number: at least 10 percent extra brick for a Flemish bond (snap headers, corner closers, breakage), against the smaller allowance running bond needs. The labor increase is documented only qualitatively: more skilled cutting and placement time. No source quantifies the added labor for a solo or two-person owner crew. The honest plan combines the 10 percent waste figure with the solo output planning of §13.14, labeled as an estimate built from two separate general sources. The appearance decision itself is an Aesthetics And Architectural Expression chapter subject.

**Inside corners and intersections.** At an inside corner, the two walls' units run into the joint from opposite directions. The standard coursing keeps any vertical joint from running continuously up the corner. At each course, one wall's units run through to the corner and the other wall's are cut to butt against them (a three-quarter or closure unit). The roles reverse on the next course. A tee intersection uses the same alternating-closure logic, with manufacturer-specific interlocking units where the chosen brick line offers them. This passage is general craft description, not matched to a technical-note source in the research. The coursing detail is confirmed against the brick manufacturer's literature or a working mason before the first inside corner is laid. Outside corners and quoins are covered with the Aesthetics And Architectural Expression chapter's material.

## 13.8 Coursing, Joints, And Tooling

**Simple**

Tooling is the step that seals the wall. Drawing a steel jointer along the half-set joint compresses the surface into a dense skin. The stroke also presses the mortar tight against the brick on both sides. Done at the right moment, it also makes the wall look even. The right moment is a firmness you test with a thumb, not a time on a clock.

**Profile selection.** BIA Technical Note 7B divides the profiles by mechanism. Concave, V, and grapevine joints are compacted by the tooling stroke and shed water; they are the exterior profiles for any climate with driving rain or freeze-thaw. The remaining profiles either leave the outer mortar unconsolidated or create a horizontal ledge that ponds water against the wall:

- weathered
- beaded
- struck
- flush
- raked
- extruded

Western Oregon supplies both halves of the bad combination, wind-driven winter rain and periodic freezes, so the exterior brick wythe gets a concave or V joint; the climate raises the cost of the wrong profile rather than changing which profile is right. Exposed interior CMU faces no rain, so its profile is an appearance choice; running the same concave or V everywhere simplifies the tool kit and the consistency problem.

**Thumbprint hard.** The tooling moment is defined exactly: pressing a thumb into the joint leaves an indentation but transfers no mortar to the thumb. Tooling early smears mortar onto the brick, leaves a rough surface, and reads lighter in color. Tooling late fails to consolidate the surface and reads darker. Because temperature and humidity move the time-to-thumbprint through the day, each section is tooled when it individually passes the thumb test. The worst habit for a solo mason is tooling the whole day's work at quitting time: every section is then somewhere different past its window, and the result is blotchy joint color even when every joint is full.

**Tool and stroke.** The jointer is sized slightly larger than the joint, rounded for concave or angled for V, drawn with firm, even pressure so the mortar visibly compresses and burnishes. The tool's own geometry limits how deep it can press, which is why these two profiles stay consistent over a long run in unpracticed hands; a raked or struck joint depends on a hand-judged depth and angle at every stroke.

**A realistic palette for one mason.** Joint color depends on water content, unit moisture, retempering, and tooling timing, held constant batch to batch (§13.5). A solo mason laying a house over months, through changing weather, holds those variables imperfectly by definition. The combination least dependent on batch-to-batch discipline: natural uncolored mortar, or one metallic-oxide pigment premixed with the cement in large controlled batches, tooled concave or V. More custom color and more hand-judged profile both convert ordinary solo variation into visible patchiness across the finished wall.

**Applied**

The rolling tooling pass, for a solo day:

1. Thumb-test the earliest-laid section of the current run at intervals.
2. Tool each section the moment it passes: no mortar on the thumb, dent remains.
3. Keep laying while later sections approach the window.
4. After tooling, cut off tags (burrs of extruded mortar) with the trowel edge and brush the joints once the mortar has stiffened enough not to smear.

## 13.9 Reinforcement And Grouting In Place

**Simple**

The steel does nothing until grout locks it to the block. Grout is poured fluid. It must flow down the cells, wrap the bars, and fill every corner. The failures are hidden: a void behind a mortar blob, a cell blocked by droppings, a blowout from a too-tall pour. Every prevention happens while laying, hours before any grout arrives.

**What goes where.** Vertical bars stand inside the grouted cells, positioned by wire rebar positioners or tied to the foundation dowels. Cover and lap-splice lengths come from the stamped structural drawings; the exact TMS 402/602 tables are inside the purchased standard, so the engineer's schedule is the working document. Horizontal steel takes two forms with different functions. Joint reinforcement (ladder-pattern welded wire laid in the bed joint, commonly every second course) controls shrinkage and temperature cracking. The Wall System chapter states why the truss pattern is recommended against for this wall. Bond-beam bars are deformed rebar set in a grouted course of U-shaped or knock-out-web units, and work structurally: chords, lintels, shear transfer, seismic ties. Lap splices for every bar are embedded in grout, never left in an ungrouted joint. Placement rules, lift limits, and inspection triggers are sourced in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter; this section is the hand work that satisfies them.

**Grout, and its two placement strategies.** ASTM C476 grout arrives at 8 to 11 inches of slump so it can flow around bars, ties, and mortar protrusions; fine grout (sand only) goes in narrow or congested spaces, coarse grout where the space allows (Wall System chapter, Mortar And Grout). Low-lift grouting places grout in stages as the wall rises; the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter's entry sets the governing numbers (cleanouts required once a pour exceeds 5 feet, 8-foot maximum under ORSC R606.3.5.1). Low pours keep hydrostatic pressure on the young mortar joints small, reach working stiffness in roughly 12 hours so bracing needs stay low, and need no cleanouts; the price is repeated interruption and more bar splices. High-lift grouting builds the wall full height first, then pours in taller lifts: fewer interruptions and splices, but every grouted cell needs a cleanout at the base. The fluid column's hydrostatic pressure is the blowout mechanism when a lift outruns what the fresh joints can hold. The masonry specification's own lift figures are TMS 602 values quoted through industry summaries: a 5-foot-4-inch standard lift, extendable to 12 feet 8 inches only with masonry cured at least 4 hours, slump at 10 to 11 inches, and no intermediate bond beam in the pour. They are confirmed against the primary text or the engineer of record before a high-lift pour is planned.

**Cleanouts.** Where required, cleanouts are formed by omitting a face shell at the base of the cell, at every vertical-bar location and not over 32 inches on center, with a 3-inch minimum opening. These are TMS 602 values via the mason-contractor literature; the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter's code entry defers cleanout sizing to the masonry specification. A high-lift wall is closed and inaccessible, so the cleanout is the only way to remove droppings and confirm the cell is clear before the pour seals it. Before the pour, each opening is cleared, inspected, then sealed and braced to hold the grout's pressure.

**Consolidation.** Every lift over 12 inches is mechanically vibrated, then reconsolidated after the block has absorbed the excess water and before the grout stiffens. The Wall System chapter (Mortar And Grout) states the working window at 10 to 15 minutes after placement. The first pass closes placement voids. The water loss itself opens new shrinkage voids and bridging around bars ("arching"), which only the second pass closes. During the pour, the grout level visibly dropping after vibration is the process working. A lift that shows no settlement at all is the cue that the vibrator never reached it.

**Keeping cells clean while laying.** Every prevention is upstream of the pour:

- Bevel bed joints away from cells to be grouted, so squeezed mortar extrudes toward the face.
- Cut squeezed mortar off fresh instead of letting it drop.
- Remove any protrusion extending more than 1/2 inch into a grout space; fins block flow and shadow voids behind themselves.
- Sight down open cells at intervals during the day, while droppings are still loose.

**Setting lintels and bond beams over openings.** A bond beam runs continuously; a lintel spans an opening; a bond-beam course aligned with an opening head serves as both. The craft at the bearing is load transfer. The masonry-code minimum bearing is 4 inches at each end (CMHA TEK 14-08B and the Lintel Design Manual TR91B). Trade guidance often specifies 6 inches for larger spans; the project's lintel schedule states the governing figure for each opening. Each bearing end is set on a full mortar bed, on solidly grouted jamb cells, never on hollow ones. The grouted cells spread the concentrated end reaction. Undersized or unsupported bearing shows up later as diagonal cracks radiating from the opening's upper corners. A poorly grouted lintel is the highest-consequence void location in the wall: the bar carries tension only through its grout encasement, and a hollow "grouted" lintel is a smaller beam than the one the engineer sized. Lintel selection and sizing are engineering (Structural System And Concrete Design chapter; the veneer's steel-angle span table is in the Wall System chapter). Outbuilding openings in owner-made block are described with the practice structures in the Owner-Made Blocks And Alternative Concretes chapter.

## 13.10 Building In The Ties, Flashing, And Weeps As You Go

**Simple**

The cavity's drainage parts cannot be added later. Flashing, weeps, and ties go in as the courses rise. The other half of the job is negative: keeping mortar out of the cavity. A dropped blob on the flashing, or one bridging the gap, becomes a permanent wet path.

**Tie embedment discipline.** The tie types, schedule, and spacing are specified in the Wall System chapter (Ties And Anchorage). The hand rule at each tie: the leg is pressed into a full mortar bed and covered. Embedment is at least 1-1/2 inches into the brick bed joint, with 5/8 inch minimum mortar cover from the exterior face. A tie laid on bare brick and mortared over on top only, or one short of embedment, is not anchored. It sheds its share of load to the neighboring ties, and the overload propagates tie to tie. The two-piece adjustable ties tolerate coursing offset between the wythes, but the offset allowance is finite (the Wall System chapter's tie table). The craft habit is coursing both wythes off one story pole so the offset never approaches the limit.

**Flashing as laid.** The dimensions are specified in the Wall System chapter (The Cavity): the 8-inch vertical leg terminated into a bed joint or reglet, 6-inch sealed laps, end dams turned up 1 inch at every discontinuity. The outer edge finishes at or past the brick face as a drip. The craft failure the research singles out is placement of the weeps relative to the flashing: weeps go in the joint immediately above the flashing. A weep one or two courses up leaves a permanent head of trapped water standing on the flashing below it.

**Weeps as laid.** Open head joints (a head joint left free of mortar), at least 2 inches high, at 24 inches on center, screened, are the recommended type; the code minimums (3/16-inch diameter at 33 inches maximum) are the floor, sourced in the Wall System chapter. Where appearance rules out open joints, rope wicks go in at 16 inches on center. Each wick runs at least 16 inches into the cavity base along the back of the brick, and finishes flush to 1/2 inch proud of the face to promote evaporation. Tube weeps clog and drain poorly and are discouraged. Weeps stay open through construction; a weep mortared shut during cleanup no longer drains.

**Keeping the cavity clean.** Three workmanship controls, in the order they act:

1. Bevel every cavity-side bed joint away from the airspace, so the squeeze extrudes toward the working face.
2. Draw a cavity board (a batten resting on the ties, pulled up as courses rise) along the gap to catch droppings, and empty it at intervals.
3. During the base courses, leave out roughly every third brick in the course above the flashing; the gaps are cleaning hand-holes for the flashing surface, and the omitted bricks are set with mortar last.

Manufactured mortar-collection mesh in the cavity is a backstop used with these controls, not instead of them; the Wall System chapter states the flashing-height condition the mesh manufacturers attach. A dropping bridge wicks water across the gap to the block by capillary action; a single tie carrying a big enough mortar squeeze does the same, which is why tie beds are struck clean.

**Applied**

The laying-day cavity discipline:

1. Bevel cavity-side beds away from the airspace, every course.
2. Strike tie beds clean; no mortar tail crossing the gap.
3. Pull the cavity board up with each course; empty it before it overflows.
4. Sight down the cavity and check the weeps at intervals during the day, not at quitting time; droppings lift free only while fresh.
5. Close the cleaning gaps above the flashing last, after the flashing surface is verified clean.

## 13.11 Cold- And Hot-Weather Masonry

**Simple**

Fresh mortar is mostly water, and its two hazards are freezing and fast drying. Frozen too early, mortar is broken from inside by ice expansion. The strength never develops. Dried too fast on a hot day, mortar stiffens before the unit is set. The bond never forms. Between 40 and 90 degrees, masonry needs no special procedure. Outside that band, the procedures below apply.

**The cold mechanism and its numbers.** CMHA TEK 03-01C ties the freeze risk to water content: mortar must not be allowed to freeze until its water content falls from the initial 11-to-16 percent range below 6 percent, which is why protection runs on hydration progress, not the calendar. Grout carries more water than mortar, so it gets the longer protection window.

**Cold-weather procedure, below 40°F ambient:**

- Heat the sand or the mixing water so mortar temperature at mixing sits between 40°F and 120°F.
- Lay dry units down to 20°F; thaw wet or frozen units first; never lay on a snow- or ice-covered surface.
- Keep new masonry above 32°F after laying: 24 hours for ungrouted work, 48 hours for grouted, reduced to 24 where the grout's only cement is Type III (high-early-strength) portland. Enclosure with supplemental heat, electric blankets, and infrared lamps are the named methods.
- Non-chloride accelerators shorten the protection window and never replace it; every chloride "antifreeze" is prohibited (§13.5).

**Hot-weather procedure, above 90°F:**

- Shade stored materials; keep mixing water cool in shaded barrels, not in a sun-heated hose.
- Mix smaller batches, 3 to 5 minutes; board life shortens to 2 hours (§13.3).
- Spread mortar no more than 4 feet ahead of the units being laid, and set each unit within about one minute of spreading.
- Watch tooling timing: heat shortens the time to thumbprint-hard, and the window moves through the day (§13.8).

**Wet weather, either season.** Materials and unfinished work stay covered whenever rain is likely; stored materials sit off the ground. A unit with 50 percent or more of its surface wet is unacceptable for laying.

The temperature bands above are CMHA's published summary of the purchased TMS 602 text. The Wall System chapter's weather-windows passage lists a second set of TMS 602 hot-weather submittal triggers from a different industry summary; both sets are confirmed against the primary text or the engineer of record before either is relied on as an exact threshold. The build calendar consequences are in the Build Sequence And Site Coordination chapter.

**Code**

```
Fresh masonry must be protected from freezing until it has hydrated.
  Below 40°F: mortar between 40°F and 120°F at mixing; no laying on snow or ice; wet or frozen units thawed; completed masonry held above 32°F for 24 hours (ungrouted) or 48 hours (grouted; 24 with Type III cement grout).
  Source: TMS 602 cold-weather provisions, as published in CMHA TEK 03-01C (purchased standard not independently read).

Chloride antifreeze admixtures are prohibited in grout.
  No chloride-bearing antifreeze in grout; admixtures in mortar capped at 0.2 percent chloride-ion content.
  Source: TMS 602 via CMHA TEK 03-01C; chloride-ion cap per BIA Technical Note 8 (quoting ASTM C1384 practice).
```

## 13.12 Cutting, Dust, And Skin: Silica And Cement Safety

**Simple**

Cutting or grinding masonry makes crystalline silica dust, which scars lungs permanently. Federal rules list, tool by tool, the required dust control and respirator. Wet concrete and mortar also burn skin chemically, slowly and painlessly at first. Both hazards have exact procedures, and both procedures are short.

**The silica table for this build's tasks.** OSHA 29 CFR 1926.1153 Table 1 assigns each tool its engineering control and respirator class (APF, assigned protection factor: APF 10 is a fitted half-mask class, APF 25 a step above):

- Stationary masonry saw: integrated water feed to the blade; no respirator at any duration.
- Handheld power saw, any blade size: integrated water feed. Outdoors: none up to 4 hours per shift, APF 10 beyond. Indoors or enclosed: APF 10 at any duration.
- Walk-behind saw: water feed; none outdoors; APF 10 indoors or enclosed.
- Handheld grinder, mortar removal (tuckpointing): shrouded grinder with dust collection at 25 cfm or more per inch of wheel diameter, and a respirator at every duration: APF 10 to 4 hours, APF 25 beyond. This is the only Table 1 task with no respirator-free tier at all (§13.15).
- Handheld grinder, other work: water feed or the shrouded collection system; none outdoors; indoors, none to 4 hours, APF 10 beyond.
- Drills, handheld or stand-mounted, including rotary hammers: shroud or cowling with the manufacturer's dust-collection airflow and a 99-percent filter; no respirator at any duration.
- Dowel-drilling rigs (outdoor coring class): shrouded bit with 99-percent collection and filter cleaning; APF 10 at any duration. Indoor coring has no line item of its own in the fetched text; it defaults to the drill line or conservatively to APF 10 until confirmed against the full table.
- Jackhammers and chipping tools: continuous water or the 99-percent shrouded system; outdoors none to 4 hours, APF 10 beyond; indoors APF 10 always.

Two rules sit outside the tool rows. Dry sweeping of silica dust is prohibited where wet methods or HEPA vacuuming are feasible. And task durations stack: performing several Table 1 tasks totaling over 4 hours in one shift puts every task at its over-4-hour respirator tier, which is the normal case for a solo builder doing an hour of grinding and three of sawing in a day.

**Wet cutting on a budget.** The engineering control every saw row requires is continuous water at the blade. Documented shop-built versions on an ordinary circular saw: a small valve tapped into the blade shroud feeding from a garden hose; drip-irrigation line with pinched-nozzle ends; a copper tube crimped to spray the blade through the saw's foot. The parts cost is a few dollars against $2,500 to $10,500 (2026) for professional bridge saws built for production tile work; the mid-priced benchtop wet-saw tier was not priced in the research. Two conditions make a jig legitimate: the flow is continuous and reaches the blade-material contact point, and the electric saw's motor and cord stay out of the spray. Battery and gas saws remove the shock exposure.

**The cutting tool decision.** This project cuts far more block and brick than stone, mostly fit-cuts, corners, and returns:

- A guillotine block splitter ($1,000-3,000, 2026) splits one repeated dimension fast and dustlessly, and handles fine or varied cuts poorly. A documented owner account describes exactly this purchase rarely used. Its use case (production splitting of identical units) is not this project's cut profile.
- A gas cutoff saw (Stihl TS 420 class: $1,679.99 new, roughly $850-1,300 used, 2026; 14-inch blade, about 4 inches of cut depth) is the purpose-built match for sustained block and brick cutting.
- A circular saw with a masonry blade plus a shop-built water feed is the low-cost fallback, adequate for a lower cut volume and shallower cuts.

Diamond-blade selection is a Tools, Equipment, And The Owner-Builder Shop chapter subject: bond matched to the material, segment height as the wear reserve, 4-to-7-inch segmented blades at $20-80 and 10-to-14-inch wet blades at $90-350 and up (2026). The craft-side rule from the research: match the blade to the dominant soft masonry cutting, and keep a separate lower-priced blade or abrasive wheel for the small volume of cured structural concrete.

**Applied**

Wet-cement skin contact, prevention and response. Wet cement reaches pH 12.9 and produces third-degree chemical burns after about 2 hours of contact, usually without pain until the damage is done.

1. Wear liquid-impermeable gloves (nitrile or butyl class) for mixing and pointing. An absorbent leather or cotton glove soaked in mortar holds the caustic paste against the skin and is worse than no glove for sustained contact.
2. Keep sleeves and cuffs closed so mortar cannot lodge inside clothing.
3. On any skin contact with trapped or prolonged wetting: remove the contaminated clothing, glove, or pad immediately.
4. Flush the skin under cold running water for 20 minutes minimum; published guidance runs 20 to 30 minutes.
5. Get medical evaluation for any burn that is visible or painful; cement burns present late and progress from discoloration to ulceration when undertreated.

A single source adds mildly acidified water (vinegar) as a supplementary neutralizing step; it is documented once, never replaces the water flush, and the flush duration is the load-bearing step. A dedicated glove-material comparison for wet cement was not located; the impermeable-versus-absorbent reasoning above is the stated extension of the documented burn mechanism.

**Code**

```
Silica-generating masonry tasks must use the listed engineering control and respirator.
  Handheld saws: water-fed blade; APF 10 beyond 4 hours outdoors, at any duration indoors. Tuckpointing grinders: shrouded dust collection at 25 cfm per wheel-inch plus APF 10 (APF 25 beyond 4 hours), respirator required at all times. Combined Table 1 tasks over 4 hours per shift: every task at its over-4-hour tier.
  Source: OSHA 29 CFR 1926.1153, Table 1.

Accumulated silica dust must not be dry-swept where alternatives are feasible.
  HEPA vacuuming or wet methods replace dry sweeping and dry brushing.
  Source: OSHA 29 CFR 1926.1153(f) housekeeping provisions.
```

## 13.13 Tolerances, Tests, And Build-Day Quality Checks

**Simple**

Masonry has numeric straightness limits. Simple tests prove the mortar before the first real wall. A short list of checks runs during every laying day. The reference for all three is the mock-up panel: a small trial wall in the exact materials and technique. It is built first, tested, and kept as the standard the house walls are held to.

**The dimensional tolerances.** TMS 602's construction tolerances, as summarized by the Mason Contractors Association of America (the standard is purchased; the summary is confirmed against the primary text at plan review):

| Measure | Permitted variation |
|---|---|
| Plumb | ±1/4 inch in 10 feet; ±3/8 inch in 20 feet; ±1/2 inch maximum over full height |
| True to a line | same values |
| Loadbearing columns | ±1/2 inch of plumb, top to bottom |
| Non-loadbearing columns | ±3/4 inch |

These are floors; a project specification may tighten them, never loosen them. The working method that hits them is §13.1's reference system, checked continuously rather than measured after the fact.

**The owner's test bench.** Five standard methods, each with a distinct job:

- ASTM C1437 flow table: consistency by spread percentage. Laboratory mortars run 105 to 115 percent flow; construction mortars run 130 to 150 (higher in hot weather). The field target is the construction range, not the lab number.
- ASTM C780 cone penetration: the field-usable consistency check, a plunger's penetration depth into a cylinder of mortar. This is the solo mason's batch-to-batch water-content instrument (§13.14).
- ASTM C1506 water retention: the lab method behind the 75 percent property-specification minimum.
- ASTM C1072 bond wrench: a lever arm peels the top unit off a stacked prism, reading each joint's flexural bond. The same method qualifies mortar cement's bond minimums (§13.2), and a rented or lab-run version can verify a trial panel.
- ASTM C109 cubes: 2-inch mortar cubes crushed for compressive strength, the method behind the property-specification strength table and the block-versus-mortar comparison of §13.4.

Masonry prism testing (ASTM C1314, the assembled unit-mortar-grout compressive test) is the code-recognized verification that the built assembly reaches the design f'm; it belongs to the structural program (Structural System And Concrete Design chapter and the Testing, Commissioning, And Quality Control chapter).

**The mock-up.** BIA Technical Note 7B's quality system for residential work accepts an as-built dwelling by the same mason as the workmanship reference. A first-time solo builder has none, and the substitute is a real sample panel: a few square feet in the exact planned unit, mortar, joint, and technique. Once accepted, the panel is the project's quality standard for bond pattern, joints, workmanship, and appearance. The panel is where flow targets, tooling timing, and color decisions get settled, and where a joint can be broken open or bond-tested without consequence. The skill path scales the same idea upward: panel, then the permit-exempt practice structures (Owner-Made Blocks And Alternative Concretes chapter, practice structures), then the house. The practice structures double as acceptance evidence for owner-made units; the craft benefit is the progression itself, each stage adding a real foundation, real load, and real weather before the same technique goes into the house wall.

**Applied**

The build-day quality checklist, each item traced to its mechanism:

1. Batch check: run the cone or flow check on each new batch, or match it against the calibrated good batch (§13.14).
2. Joint filling: full beds, buttered-and-shoved heads, squeeze visible (§13.6, §13.7).
3. Tooling: thumb-test before tooling each section; tool at thumbprint-hard, not on the clock (§13.8).
4. Color discipline: same water measure, same unit wetting, no retempering colored mortar, same tooling moment (§13.5).
5. Cavity and cells: sight down both during the day; droppings lift free only while fresh (§13.9, §13.10).
6. Grout pour: fins over 1/2 inch removed before the pour; cleanouts verified clean and sealed; every lift over 12 inches vibrated and revibrated; settlement after vibration is the process working, and a lift showing none was not reached (§13.9).
7. Tolerances: level and story pole on the leads every course; line on every unit between.

## 13.14 Pace, Ergonomics, And Solo Masonry

**Simple**

A professional mason works with a tender: a second person who mixes, stocks units, and moves scaffold. Working alone means doing both jobs in alternation. The wall rises slower than the arithmetic suggests. This section states the real production numbers and what the tender role costs a solo builder. It also lists the setups that protect shoulders, knees, and wrists over months. Body maintenance as a life system (sleep, conditioning, injury history) is an Owner-Builder's Body And Mind chapter subject. This section describes the workstation.

**The production numbers.** Documented tended-mason rates: 250 to 300 bricks a day, including jointing, is the commonly cited average. A good residential day runs 400 to 600. Contractor-reported production highs reach 1,600 modular bricks or 400 to 500 8-inch blocks per man-day. The tender ratio quantifies the support load: one laborer supplies three to four masons at ground level, dropping to one-to-one once the wall reaches 5-foot frame height. A solo mason is both people, with zero parallel capacity: every mix, restock, and scaffold move is subtracted from laying time.

**Planning numbers for a solo first-timer.** No source quantifies first-attempt solo masonry output, and no multiplier is invented here. The defensible plan: initial daily output well under the low professional end (under 250 to 300 bricks, under roughly 85 blocks per day). Output climbs with practice toward, but not necessarily to, that low end. The owner's own tracked output in the first real week is the schedule's planning number; the Build Sequence And Site Coordination chapter's general first-attempt multipliers frame the rest of the calendar.

**The part-time tender case.** The framework, from the ratio data: at height, tending is a full-time role per mason. A hired tender on the highest-volume laying days (walls tall, scaffolds moving often, mortar demand at peak) recovers the difference between a self-tended day and something nearer the tended rate. The hire is weighed against those specific days, not against the whole build.

**Moving material.** Power buggy and motorized-wheelbarrow rentals run roughly $70 to $280 per day (2026; documented points: mud buggy $150/day, $600/week, $1,800/month; motorized wheelbarrow about $70/day; larger buggies $206-276/day). The rental is justified on concentrated high-volume days, a pour with many trips in a compressed window; day-to-day mortar transport rarely limits a solo mason's pace, and a wheelbarrow handles it. The trip-count model needs the site's real haul distance and daily volume, neither fixed yet.

**Working height.** Sustained work above the shoulders or below the knees carries the disproportionate strain; the productive zone is between them. The sequencing that keeps the work there is banded. The mason lays the lowest courses across the whole run, raises the platform, then lays the next band across the run. Building one section full-height instead repeats the worst reaches at every section. This is stated as applied ergonomic reasoning, not a cited masonry study. The platform options: rented or purchased sectional scaffold frames (most flexible, rated at every height), fixed-height steel folding trestles (Bon Tool, 24- and 36-inch, a confirmed US product), or shop-built platforms (unrated; the builder carries the safety margin personally). Stacked pallets are unrated and unstable for sustained wall work. A double-wythe wall needs two independent platforms, one per wythe, since the wythes rise at different rates. The practical comparison is cost and setup time per platform, doubled.

**Scaffold: rent or buy.** Documented ranges (2026): rentals from $150 to $1,500 per month by size and type; a small tower $100-140 per month; a standard 5x7-foot tower to 15 feet, $200-300 per month with platforms and guardrails. Purchase: $200-800 for a basic frame set; one documented used purchase at $85 per section. A multi-month masonry campaign at $200-300 per month crosses the basic-set purchase price by around month three, which is why the sourced guidance favors buying for multi-month projects with storage space. The decision waits on the actual required height and a live quote.

**Knees and wrists.** Knee work splits by task. Hard-cap or gel knee pads serve block laying and footing work: mobile, abrasive contact, where hard PVC shells resist CMU abrasion. A stainless kneeling board or slider ($93-110, category range $28-249, 2026) serves slab finishing, keeping the whole lower leg out of the wet alkaline surface. Pads and boards complement rather than replace each other. For the wrist and forearm, the trowel is sized to the hand and the task (an oversized trowel adds control torque on fine work; an undersized one multiplies strokes). That sizing rule is trade practice, not a cited ergonomic study. The documented lever is the mortar itself: a harsh, oversanded, or stiff batch takes more force on every stroke, all day. The tender's trained eye for consistency is replaced with an instrument: a cone-penetration check (§13.13) on each batch, or one early batch verified good and kept as the visual reference standard.

**The tool kit.** The priced minimum kit for both wythes (2026 retail, checked ranges; several small items were not individually priced and get confirmed in one itemized quote):

| Item | Price found (2026) |
|---|---|
| Laying trowel (London or Philadelphia pattern) | roughly $15-50 at the mid grade (general market range, not a confirmed listing) |
| Pointing trowel | $3.94-4.33 |
| Jointers, one or two sized to the project joint | $3.59-4.06 each |
| Aluminum hawk | $17.25 (10x10-inch) to $20.45 (13x13) |
| Mortar board | shop-built from plywood, near zero |
| Line, line blocks, pins | low-cost stock items, not individually priced in the research |
| Story pole | shop-built, near zero (§13.1) |
| Brick hammer, mason's level | stock items, not individually priced in the research |

Bundled kits center around $101 across a $10-2,483 market span. The planning figure is low hundreds of dollars for a decent complete kit, a range rather than a quote. One project-specific saving is real: a single-project mason tools one joint width and one profile, so one or two jointers replace a professional's full set.

**Fatigue and the quality mechanisms.** The craft failures of this chapter are timing failures: tooling windows missed, boards worked past life, cells left to harden. All of them cluster at the end of long days. Session length and rest planning are Owner-Builder's Body And Mind chapter subjects; the craft-side rule is that the day's last section gets the same thumb test as the first.

## 13.15 Repointing And Repair

**Simple**

A correctly built wall wears at its joints, not its units, over decades (§13.4). Repointing is cutting out the worn mortar and packing in new. It is the planned maintenance that restores the joints. One rule decides whether the repair helps or harms. The new mortar must be no harder than the old wall. A hard modern patch on a softer wall damages the units it was meant to protect. Whether a given crack gets repointed at all is a Wall System chapter decision (Problems And Solutions).

**Cutting out.** The joint is raked out to a minimum depth of 3/4 inch, or 2 to 2-1/2 times the joint width, and always back to sound, well-bonded mortar. Reaching bondable material governs, not the fixed dimension. Hand tools (a joint raker, or chisel and hammer) are the preservation-trade default. A grinder run along the joint widens it into the brick arrises (edges) and leaves kerf marks unless the operator holds dead center. Careful grinder use with a narrow blade is common on long straight runs; the risk it carries is unit damage, the one non-repairable outcome. Grinding mortar is also the single most respirator-intensive task in the silica table (below).

**Preparing and packing.** The cavity is brushed or blown free of dust, then dampened, so the old wall does not strip water from the new mortar the way a dry high-suction brick would (§13.4's mechanism, applied to the wall itself). New mortar packs in lifts of roughly 1/4 inch, each compacted against the back and sides before the next. One deep pack leaves voids and shrinks (§13.16's mechanism, concentrated in a small unconsolidated cavity). The final lift is tooled at thumbprint-hard to match the surrounding profile, which seals the repair and blends it with the original work.

**Matching the mortar.** The repointing mix is as soft or softer, and as vapor-open or more, than both the original mortar and the units, with sand chosen to match color and texture. §13.4's tuning method is the tool, and genuinely soft or historic walls take lime-based mixes. The hard portland patch ranks as the most destructive repair mistake for three reasons at once. It traps moisture behind a tight joint and forces it out through the unit faces. It does not yield to the wall's movement, so stress concentrates into the units. And it is effectively irreversible: removing a patch harder than its bricks damages them again. A too-soft repair wears out early and is repointed again; a too-hard repair converts routine maintenance into unit replacement in a wall whose units may be irreplaceable (an owner-made block run, decades after the molds are gone).

**Cleaning efflorescence.** The removal ladder from CMHA TEK 08-03A, mildest first:

1. Dry brushing followed by a clean-water flush. Most deposits come off here.
2. Light brush sandblasting or a proprietary masonry cleaner, tested first on a hidden area.
3. Muriatic acid as the last resort: 5 to 10 percent solution on plain masonry, 2 percent on colored. Pre-wet the wall so the dry units cannot suck acid inward. Work sections of 4 square feet or less, with roughly five-minute dwell. Scrub with stiff bristle, never wire (wire sheds iron and creates a second stain). Flush immediately and thoroughly.

The whole wall is treated uniformly; partial treatment reads as blotch. Calcium carbonate crusts are the hard case, sometimes needing high-pressure water. Painting or coating waits about a month after an acid wash. Diagnosis of why the wall effloresced comes before any cleaning (§13.16); cleaning a recurring bloom without finding its water source leaves the cause in place, and the deposit returns.

**The silica constraint on repair work.** Tuckpointing grinding is the one task OSHA Table 1 gives no respirator-free tier at any duration: APF 10 up to 4 hours even with the shrouded 25-cfm-per-inch collection system running, APF 25 beyond 4 hours (§13.12). Two planning consequences follow. The respirator is part of the task from the first minute, not an add-on for long sessions. And capping sessions at 4 hours holds the work at the APF 10 tier, so a large repointing scope schedules as multiple short sessions rather than one push. Because repointing is this wall's intended maintenance cycle, the requirement recurs for the wall's whole life. The owner's manual entry for future owners states it (Documentation, Maintenance, And Future Upgrades chapter).

## 13.16 Problems And Solutions

**Simple**

Mortar and joint failures are a short list. Each traces to a specific cause with a specific prevention. This catalog states the mechanism, the prevention, and the fix for each. Assembly-level failures (ties, cavities, movement cracking, coatings) are cataloged in the Wall System chapter. This section describes failures inside the mortar and at the joint.

### Shrinkage Cracking

Two distinct timings, one underlying driver: water leaving paste. Plastic-shrinkage cracks appear within hours, when surface evaporation outruns the fresh mortar's near-zero tensile strength; wind is the multiplier, and covering fresh work is the prevention. Drying-shrinkage cracks develop over days to weeks as the hardened paste loses its remaining excess water. The paste shrinks and the sand does not, so every cause maps to paste and water volume:

- Excess mixing water beyond what absorbent units draw off: more water to lose, more shrinkage. (Mortar against absorbent brick shrinks less than the same mortar in a mold, because the unit removes the excess; this is why maximum-workable-water mixing and correct unit suction, §13.3 and §13.4, are not contradictions.)
- Over-rich mixes: a stronger type than the location needs is more paste, and more paste is more shrinkage (§13.4).
- Too-fine sand: higher water demand for the same workability (§13.2).
- Fast drying: wind and heat convert would-be drying shrinkage into plastic cracking (§13.11).

Wall-scale movement cracking that concentrates randomly through joints marks missing or misplaced movement joints, a design-level cause; the layout is in the Wall System chapter (Differential Movement).

### Spalling And Popping

Three look-alike defects with three unrelated causes:

- Freeze-thaw spalling: water expands roughly 9 percent on freezing; a saturated unit or joint with no relief path fractures from inside. The prevention is dryness (drainage details, §13.10) and compatibility (a joint more vapor-open than its unit, §13.4). Where the mortar is the tighter element, the unit face takes the damage.
- Lime popping: coarse, incompletely slaked lime particles hydrate weeks or months after the joint hardens, expand, and push out a small cone centered on the particle. Prevention is buying ASTM C207 Type S hydrated lime, the properly slaked low-residual-oxide grade, instead of agricultural or unspecified lime.
- Aggregate pop-outs: reactive silica, clay lumps, or organic particles in the sand swell or decompose in the hardened joint and eject a cone from within. Prevention is §13.2's clean, graded sand; the diagnostic difference from lime popping is the particle visible at the crater's center.

### Bond Failure And Separation

Each documented cause pairs with one prevention:

| Cause | Mechanism | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Low water retention | Paste loses water to the unit before it wets and keys the surface | Mix selection and lime content (§13.2); maximum workable water (§13.3) |
| IRA mismatch | Thirsty units starve the bond; sealed or saturated units float | Test and wet per §13.4 |
| No tooling, or off-window tooling | Surface never consolidated against the units | Thumbprint timing (§13.8) |
| Working a set batch | Hydration structure already formed, then broken | Board-life discipline (§13.3) |
| Dusty or dirty units | Paste bonds to the dust film, not the unit | Covered, off-ground unit storage |
| Excess entrained air | Air content displaces bond | No added air-entrainer; system air caps (§13.5) |

A separated joint from any of these looks identical from the face. The autopsy on a failed test-panel joint (§13.13) reveals which one: clean unit faces mean suction or retention failure, a dust film means storage, voids behind the face mean slushing (§13.7).

### Efflorescence

The white crystalline bloom requires three conditions at once: soluble salts in the wall system, water to dissolve them, and evaporation or pressure to move the solution to a surface. Removing any one stops it. Salt sources include the cement, aggregates, and mix water, plus soil contact and some admixtures; the craft-side preventions are clean materials, covered storage, and tight tooled joints (§13.5), and the assembly-side levers are in the Wall System chapter (Problems And Solutions).

The deposit's pattern is the diagnosis:

- Uniform bloom across a new wall in its first year: construction moisture leaving; self-limiting; cleaned per §13.15 when the wall has dried.
- White streaks concentrated below mortar joints, recurring: active water infiltration; the response is the leak diagnosis (flashing, weeps, joint filling), not another cleaning.
- Concentrated at the wall base: capillary rise from ground moisture, pointing at the base flashing and damp-proofing details rather than rain entry.

### The Mistakes That Cause Leaky, Weak Walls

The mortar-and-workmanship failure list, gathered from the sections above as the discipline it implies:

1. Mortar type by location per the Wall System chapter's table, never upgraded to a stronger type for reassurance (§13.4).
2. Every ingredient through the fixed measuring container; no shovel counts (§13.2).
3. Maximum workable water; retemper freely within board life; discard at set (§13.3).
4. Full beds, buttered-and-shoved heads; no furrows, no slushing (§13.6, §13.7).
5. Units laid within about a minute of spreading (§13.6).
6. Tooling at thumbprint-hard, section by section (§13.8).

### First-Timer Mistakes

No source ranks first-time hand-tool mistakes, and this book does not invent a ranking. The two errors the trade literature does call most common: sand batching by shovel (BIA's "one of the most common problems with mortar," the oversanding path) and tooling on the clock instead of the thumb test. Both are process errors rather than dexterity errors: the corrections are the fixed measuring container (§13.2) and the thumbprint test (§13.8), both available to a first-timer from the first batch.

## 13.17 Code And Standards

**Simple**

Three kinds of documents govern this chapter's work. The masonry code and specification (TMS 402 and 602) set tolerances, weather procedures, grouting, and material rules. Both are purchased standards, quoted here through industry summaries. Each summarized number is confirmed against the primary text before it is relied on. ASTM standards define every material and test named in this chapter. OSHA's silica rule is federal law and applies to the work itself. The Oregon code entries for the wall are sourced in the Wall System and Structural System And Concrete Design chapters.

**Code**

```
Mortar and grout must meet their material specifications.
  Mortar proportioned or property-tested per ASTM C270 (types M, S, N, O); grout per ASTM C476, placed at 8-to-11-inch slump.
  Source: ASTM C270; ASTM C476 (proportion and slump values as quoted in BIA Technical Note 8 and industry summaries; purchased standards not independently read).

High-suction brick must be wetted before laying.
  Brick with an initial rate of absorption over 30 g/min per 30 square inches is wetted so the as-laid rate falls below 30.
  Source: TMS 602, as quoted by BIA Technical Note 7B (purchased standard not independently read).

Masonry walls must be built within dimensional tolerances.
  Plumb and true-to-line within ±1/4 inch in 10 feet, ±3/8 inch in 20 feet, ±1/2 inch maximum; loadbearing columns ±1/2 inch of plumb.
  Source: TMS 602 construction tolerances, as summarized by the Mason Contractors Association of America (purchased standard not independently read).

High-lift grouted cells must have cleanouts.
  Cleanout openings of 3 inches minimum at the base of every grouted cell, at each bar location, spaced not over 32 inches on center, cleaned and sealed before the pour; required when a pour exceeds the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter's cleanout threshold.
  Source: TMS 602 cleanout provisions, as summarized in Mason Contractors Association installation bulletins (purchased standard not independently read); pour-height trigger per ORSC R606.3.5.1.
```
