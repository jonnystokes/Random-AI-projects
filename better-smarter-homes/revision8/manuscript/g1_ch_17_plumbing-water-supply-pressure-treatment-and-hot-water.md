## 17.1 The supply system in a concrete house

**Simple**

Clean water travels under pressure from the well to the valve under each sink. Those parts wear out long before the concrete around them. Used water leaves by gravity in larger drain pipes, a separate system. A pressure tank lasts 10 to 15 years, and the walls around it are built for centuries. So the pumps, filters and valves sit in a mechanical room or a closet with a door. Where a pipe crosses a wall, it runs inside a sleeve, a short tube cast into the concrete. Every supply fitting stays outside the concrete.

**Applied**

1. Mark one service room on the floor plan for the well equipment, the treatment train, the water heater and the manifolds.
2. Place every valve, strainer, regulator and backflow device where it opens without tools or demolition.
3. Route horizontal supply piping in an accessible service layer, never cast in structure.
4. Pass pipes through concrete only in the sleeves shown on the penetration plan.
5. Put the main shutoff where it can be reached without tools or a ladder.
6. Label each valve, manifold port and device with the fixture or line it serves.
7. Confirm at the pre-application meeting which edition of the plumbing code the department applies.

**Professional**

Pressure, flow, temperature and water quality govern the potable system, and each is set at a different device.

| Quantity | What sets it |
|---|---|
| Static and flowing pressure | The pump's pressure switch or the booster setpoint, and a regulator where static pressure runs high |
| Flow at the farthest fixture | Pipe size, developed length and the rise from the source, at 0.5 psi lost per foot |
| Thermal expansion | An expansion tank on the building side of any check valve or backflow device |
| Backflow hazard | A device matched to the hazard at each connection |
| Water quality and disinfection | The treatment train and its chlorine contact time |
| Scald | Mixing valves at the heater and at each shower |
| Material compatibility | Pipe chosen for the water's chemistry, temperature and any chlorine residual |

Even where it shares a wet wall or chase with the drains, the supply is designed as its own system. Supply piping is pressure work, and drainage is gravity and vent work. The drain-waste-vent chapter describes the drainage side. Residential energy rules for hot-water piping are in ORSC Chapter 11.

**Code**

```
Plumbing work follows the edition of the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code in force at application.
  The 2026 edition, based on the 2024 Uniform Plumbing Code, effective October 1, 2026, replacing the 2023 edition based on the 2021 Uniform Plumbing Code.
  Source: BCD OPSC adoption page; OAR 918-750-0110.
```

## 17.2 The source and the well log

**Simple**

Your water source sets the treatment, the pressure equipment and the plan for a power outage. City water arrives treated and already under pressure from the utility's mains. Well water arrives raw, and a pump in the well supplies the pressure. When the well is drilled, the driller pumps it for at least an hour and records the flow it holds. That steady flow, the sustained yield, is on the well log. Wells under 5 gallons a minute cannot feed a busy morning directly. Such a well fills a storage tank slowly instead, and a second pump serves the house from the tank.

**Applied**

| Question | Answer from | What it sets |
|---|---|---|
| Public utility or private well | The parcel record and the utility | Permit path, treatment, pressure source and outage plan |
| Sustained flow the source delivers | The well log, or the utility | Pump, tank and pipe sizes |
| Static and flowing pressure at the building | A gauge reading, or the utility | Whether a regulator, a booster or both are needed |
| Water chemistry and bacteria | An accredited laboratory | The treatment train |
| Any nonpotable source on the site | The site plan | Full separation from the drinking water |
| What runs on power | The pump and booster wiring | The outage plan, since every pump stops without power |

1. Pull the well log by tax lot from OWRD's Well Report Query at apps.wrd.state.or.us.
2. Read the sustained yield, the static water level and the drawdown from the log.
3. Record the well depth and the casing diameter for the pump and disinfection calculations.
4. Plan a storage tank and booster when the sustained yield is under 5 GPM.
5. Between 5 and 10 GPM, compare the yield with the peak-hour demand before deciding.
6. At 10 GPM or more, pressurize the house directly from the well.
7. On utility water, get the static and residual pressure at the meter from the utility.
8. Check the source's flow against the fixture schedule before sizing anything downstream.

**Professional**

Public-water design starts from the utility's service size and meter capacity, the static and residual pressure at the meter, and the utility's backflow conditions. Fixture units for the house complete the inputs.

Private-well design starts from the well log the driller files. Sustained yield, static water level and drawdown fix the pump curve and the pressure-tank drawdown. Laboratory results and the site fix the treatment, the disinfection contact time and the outage plan.

Storage with a booster separates a low or intermittent source from the house's peak demand. The well fills an atmospheric tank slowly, and a booster pump, such as a variable-speed constant-pressure unit, feeds the house from the tank. Pressure steadies and the tank holds an outage reserve, and the tank brings its own overflow, backflow and disinfection duties.

Oregon's well-construction standards are OAR chapter 690, division 210. The Land chapter specifies the well's siting, the one-hour yield test, the exempt-use limit and the setbacks from septic systems. The Oregon Health Authority's drinking-water rules, OAR chapter 333, division 61, govern public water systems. A well that serves only the owner's household is outside them, so its testing and cross-connection control come from the plumbing code and the sale-time testing statute.

## 17.3 Testing the water

**Simple**

Before any treatment equipment is chosen, a laboratory tests the well water. Well water can look, smell and taste fine and still carry bacteria, nitrate or arsenic. Those harm people slowly, over years of drinking. Each treatment stage is picked from the result, and the pipe from its corrosion numbers. New wells can test clean and then fail after the first heavy rain soaks the freshly dug ground. Bacteria tests are repeated through the first year for that reason, and again after any major work on the well.

**Applied**

| Tier | Tests | When |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Total coliform and E. coli, nitrate and nitrite, arsenic, pH, alkalinity, hardness, total dissolved solids, total and dissolved iron, manganese, sulfate and hydrogen sulfide | Every new well |
| 2 | Fluoride, lead and copper, radon in water in granitic terrain, uranium and gross alpha, chloride near the coast, tannins and total organic carbon in forested or peaty ground | By local geology |
| 3 | Volatile organic compounds near fuel tanks, industry or landfills. A pesticide and herbicide panel near cropland, vineyards or orchards. PFAS near bases, airports, fire-training areas or biosolids ground. Glyphosate where the pesticide panel omits it | By nearby land use |

1. Send each sample to a laboratory accredited for that analysis.
2. Ship PFAS samples to an accredited laboratory out of state.
3. Ask the laboratory to report the Langelier Saturation Index and the Ryznar Stability Index.
4. Re-sample coliform after the first heavy rain that follows construction.
5. Re-sample coliform after the septic system goes in, and again three months later.
6. After hydrofracturing, chemical redevelopment or other major well work, re-sample coliform on return to service, after the next heavy rain and three months on.
7. File each report with its sample date and tap location.

**Professional**

Arsenic occurs statewide in Oregon's volcanic and geothermal geology, so it is on every panel. Three tests at a sale record a legal minimum and omit the iron, manganese, hardness and pH that select treatment equipment.

Iron is reported as total and dissolved because the dissolved, or ferrous, fraction and any organically bound iron decide which oxidation and filtration media work. Manganese narrows the choice further.

Corrosive water shows as a negative Langelier index or a Ryznar index above 7. That water dissolves copper and shortens the life of brass valve seats and the pressure-tank bladder. Both indices also set the anode chemistry in the water heater and the pH target for treatment.

Construction loosens the ground around the casing, and the first rains can carry surface water down the disturbed annulus. Hydrofracturing and chemical redevelopment reopen that window by widening fractures and moving sediment into the water. Testing recommendations and laboratory guidance for the panels come from the Oregon Health Authority's domestic well safety program.

**Code**

```
A domestic well is tested when the property sells.
  The seller has the well tested for arsenic, nitrates and total coliform on accepting an offer and sends results to the Oregon Health Authority and the buyer within 90 days.
  Source: ORS 448.271.
```

## 17.4 Pump, pressure tank and controls

**Simple**

How often the well pump starts depends on the volume stored in the pressure tank. Inside the steel tank, a rubber bladder separates the water from a cushion of compressed air. That air pushes water out to the taps. When the pressure falls far enough, the pump starts and refills the tank. Each start wears the motor, so a bigger stored volume means fewer starts and a longer pump life. Running dry burns out more well pumps than any other fault. A low-water cutoff stops the pump when the well level drops.

**Applied**

1. Take the sustained yield from the well log.
2. Choose a pump whose output is at or below that yield.
3. Size the pressure tank for 1 gallon of drawdown per pump GPM and a run of at least 1 minute.
4. For a pump of 10 to 20 GPM, size for 1.5 gallons of drawdown per GPM.
5. For a pump of 2 HP or more, size for a run of at least 2 minutes.
6. Choose the next larger tank when the calculation falls between sizes.
7. Set the air precharge 2 psi below cut-in, 28 psi on a 30/50 switch or 38 psi on 40/60.
8. Install a low-water cutoff, either a pressure-sensing well saver, a flow switch or an electrode in the well.
9. Where the output is chlorinated, size a contact tank at peak GPM times 10 gallons, for 10 minutes of contact.
10. Install at least 150 gallons of contact tank for a 15 GPM peak.
11. Record the pump model, switch settings, precharge and tank size in the binder.

When the pump starts at nearly every faucet, find the cause in this order.

1. Turn off the pump breaker, and open a faucet until the gauge reads 0 psi.
2. Press a tire gauge onto the air valve on top of the tank.
3. When air hisses out, compare the reading with the cut-in pressure less 2 psi.
4. When water sprays from the valve, replace the whole tank.
5. Tap the tank from top to bottom, and treat a tank that sounds solid all the way up as waterlogged.
6. With the bladder intact and the charge low, drain the tank and pump the air side back to the precharge.
7. Write each recharge date in the log, and replace a tank that needs a second recharge within a season.

**Professional**

Total dynamic head adds the static water level, the maximum drawdown, the lift to the pressure tank and the friction loss. Each psi of target pressure adds 2.31 feet of head. Submersible turbine pumps are standard in drilled wells, and a stainless-steel body outlasts cast iron in Oregon's mineralized groundwater. No OPSC section requires a low-water cutoff or standby power on a single-family well, so both are project specifications.

Drawdown is a fraction of a tank's volume, and the switch setting fixes the fraction. It is about 29 percent at 30/50 psi and about 22 percent at 40/60 psi, because the same air charge works across a higher pressure band. On a 40/60 switch, a 10 GPM pump needs about 10 gallons of drawdown, which is a 45-gallon nominal tank. Each step up in tank size lengthens the run and cuts the number of starts, and starts set motor life.

Short-cycling pressure tanks have one of three separate faults. A ruptured or waterlogged bladder removes the air cushion, so the pump starts with almost every draw. A precharge that has leaked low shrinks the usable drawdown with the bladder intact. A pump too large for the tank refills the drawdown faster than the design cycle allows.

Bladder tanks last 10 to 15 years on clean water and can fail in 3 to 5 years on acidic or mineral-heavy water. Sediment plugs the fill line and abrades the bladder, iron and scale stiffen it, and chlorine embrittles the rubber until it cracks. Iron filtration and pH correction ahead of the tank are the protection. On an intact bladder, a drain-and-recharge holds a few weeks to three or four months. Air seeping out through the valve, a fitting or the bladder wall sets that interval. In nearly all modern tanks the bladder is sealed in at manufacture, so a ruptured bladder means a new tank.

Variable-speed constant-pressure pumps change speed to match demand and hold one delivered pressure, where a pressure-switch system swings between cut-in and cut-out. Such a pump needs only a small buffer tank and starts softly. On the points that decide a rural installation, the two compare as in the table below.

| | Pressure switch and tank | Variable-speed constant-pressure pump |
|---|---|---|
| Delivered pressure | Swings between cut-in and cut-out | Held near one setpoint |
| Motor starts | One per drawdown cycle | Up to 80 percent fewer, by Oakville Pump Service's figure |
| Energy | Full speed whenever running | 25 to 50 percent less from speed matching, a Department of Energy figure cited by Well Pump Authority |
| Stored water when power or controls fail | The tank's full drawdown | A small buffer tank's volume |
| Installed cost | Lower | $2,500 to $4,500 |
| Part that fails, and its cost | Pressure switch, $250 to $310 | Controller, about $1,800 on a 2 HP system |
| Repair | Parts carried by most rural pump installers | A proprietary controller, serviced by a dealer for that brand |
| Surge exposure | A mechanical switch | Power electronics exposed to lightning-induced surges |

## 17.5 Storage tank and booster

**Simple**

With a storage tank, a slow well keeps up with a busy morning. The well fills the tank at its own rate, all day long. From the tank, a booster pump sends water to the house at steady pressure. Water in the tank is also a reserve for a power outage. Stored water stays drinkable behind a sealed lid and screened vents, with disinfection after any opening. Floats can stick and overfill a tank, so this one stands in a drained pan with a high-water alarm.

**Applied**

- Feed the tank through an air gap on the fill line.
- Specify every part the stored water touches to NSF/ANSI/CAN 61, certified as a system.
- Fit a watertight, vermin-proof, secured cover with downturned, screened vents.
- Provide a gasketed, secured access opening of 22 by 30 inches, or 24 inches across.
- Pipe the overflow to an indirect waste through an air gap, never straight to a drain.
- Put a drain valve at the low point.
- Install level controls and a high-level alarm.
- Stand the tank in a pan or curbed area with an indirect drain.
- Restrain the tank and the booster against earthquake motion.
- Write the disinfection protocol, and run it at commissioning and after any opening, repair or media change.

1. Fit a flow restrictor on the well pump's discharge, set at or below the sustained yield.
2. Start and stop the fill pump with a float switch in the tank.
3. Add a low-level cutoff that stops the pump when the well itself draws down.
4. Dose chlorine at the tank inlet, or hold a residual in the tank with a small feed pump.
5. Where the tank doubles as the contact tank, size its dwell for the contact time.
6. Inspect the tank interior for sediment and biofilm at each disinfection.

**Professional**

An air gap on the fill line protects the whole tank from backflow, because water cannot siphon across open air. Neither a submerged nor a direct inlet gives that protection. Potable-contact certification is specified at the system level under NSF/ANSI/CAN 61 and confirmed in the manufacturer's NSF listing, since a shell's certification does not extend to its liner, gaskets or fittings. The overflow and the pan drain reach the drainage system only through an air gap at an indirect waste, under OPSC 811.0. A tank much above 1,000 gallons, or one outside a mechanical room, brings a stamped design from a registered design professional and the local environmental health office's sign-off.

Raw well water that sits in a buffer tank ahead of treatment loses any chlorine residual it carried, and still water against wetted walls is where biofilm starts. That makes the tank the first stage of the treatment train, with its own disinfection, inspection and residual monitoring. Water held longer than about two weeks keeps a free-chlorine residual from a small feed pump.

A float switch starts and stops the fill pump, and a flow restrictor, sometimes sold as a Dole valve, caps its draw at a fixed rate whatever the pump's rating. With both in place the well never exceeds its logged yield, and the tank meets the household's peak. Tank volume, for a peak-hour shortfall or for days without power, is in the water independence chapter. One configuration described on the permies.com forum throttles a marginal well to 0.5 to 1 gallon a minute, 720 to 1,440 gallons a day, into a buried tank of 1,000 to 2,000 gallons.

**Code**

```
Tanks and equipment are supported and seismically restrained.
  Support follows the plumbing code and the manufacturer's instructions, and seismic restraint follows the building code.
  Source: OPSC 313.1 (2026 edition).
```

## 17.6 Water through a power outage

**Simple**

Every pump in a well system stops the moment the power goes out. During an outage the house runs on stored water, in the pressure tank, any storage tank and the water heater. Fitted inside the well casing beside the electric pump, a hand pump can still lift water from as deep as 325 feet. With a tank set higher than one indoor tap, that tap fills a bucket with no pump at all. Every 2.31 feet of height adds 1 pound per square inch of pressure, so 10 feet gives about 4.

**Applied**

1. Size any generator or inverter for the well pump's starting current, 30 amps or more for a 1 HP pump.
2. Choose a pressure switch and a full drawdown tank when outage reserve and local repair come first.
3. Choose a variable-speed pump only with backup power sized for its standing draw and a dealer nearby for that brand.
4. Where the solar array is DC-coupled, specify a DC-input drive that follows solar output.
5. At well completion, install a deep-well hand pump in the casing beside the submersible.
6. Check the hand pump's rated lift against the static level on the well log.
7. Tee a gravity line off the storage tank's outlet, upstream of every pump and regulator.
8. Run the gravity line on its own pipe to one indoor tap with a valve kept closed.
9. Label the hand pump, the gravity valve and the generator inlet.

**Professional**

A well pump's starting inrush, several times its running draw, sets the size of any generator or inverter that runs it. Starting the motor softly, a variable-speed drive suits a generator, and its controller draws power whenever the system is on. Its small buffer tank holds little water once the power or the controller fails, which is the outage case against it. Gravity from an elevated tank is the one supply path that needs no power at all.

Simple Pump's deep-well hand pump installs beside an existing submersible in 4 to 8 hours and can feed the same pressure tank, so the fixtures keep working. Bison Pumps sells a deep-well unit with a lifetime warranty. The same hand pump serves when the submersible itself fails, a repair that runs $3,000 or more on a deep well. Lowered on a rope, a bucket reaches any depth, brings up small volumes with hard work and cannot feed the pressurized fixtures.

Fixture pressure of 20 to 25 psi under flow needs the tank's base 46 to 58 feet above the highest fixture, by Survipedia's figures, which only a hillside tank or a tower provides. The resilience chapter describes the household's emergency drinking-water plan, gravity filters included. A separate gravity line with a normally closed valve stays out of the pumped system, while a branch sharing its header would need backflow protection of its own. Water at the gravity tap comes from the tank ahead of the treatment stages.

## 17.7 The treatment train

**Simple**

Treatment is a row of tanks and filters, and their order sets whether each one works. Each stage removes something the next stage cannot handle: grit before the ultraviolet lamp, iron before the water softener, chlorine before the plastic pipe. No two wells get the same row, because the water test picks the stages. Treatment for a difficult well costs $1,500 to $8,000 or more. Ultraviolet lamps lose strength before they go dark, so the bulb is replaced every year.

**Applied**

1. Filter sediment first, at 5 to 20 microns.
2. Below pH 6.7, neutralize with a calcite or calcite-and-Corosex tank.
3. Oxidize with metered chlorine or hydrogen peroxide into the contact tank.
4. Use aeration instead for moderate iron with no manganese.
5. Filter iron and manganese with media chosen for the iron form and the manganese load: manganese greensand, Birm, Pyrolox or air injection.
6. Remove tannins in a dedicated anion-exchange tank where organic color or total organic carbon runs high.
7. Soften with sodium-form ion exchange, after iron, oxidation and tannins are handled.
8. Polish with activated carbon to strip leftover chlorine and chloramine.
9. Treat arsenic, nitrate, fluoride or uranium with reverse osmosis at the kitchen tap.
10. Disinfect last, with ultraviolet light sized for 40 mJ/cm² at the end of lamp life.
11. Retest the treated water after commissioning.
12. Label every bypass, flow direction and service interval on the equipment.
13. Replace the UV lamp every 12 months by the calendar.
14. Clean the quartz sleeve at each lamp change and each sediment-filter change.
15. Change whole-house carbon at 10,000 to 20,000 gallons, sooner where iron, hydrogen sulfide or tannins reach it.

**Professional**

Sediment comes first because grit fouls iron media, abrades softener resin and scours the UV sleeve. Iron that reaches a softener coats the resin and cuts its exchange capacity and service life. Ultraviolet light disinfects by a line-of-sight dose, and turbidity, iron carryover and scale on the sleeve absorb that dose before it reaches an organism, so UV sits last. Carbon and softening ahead of reverse osmosis keep chlorine and hardness off the membrane.

Arsenic in low-iron water is handled by titanium-oxide or iron-oxide adsorptive media and point-of-use RO, without a full train. High nitrate goes to nitrate-selective anion exchange or point-of-use RO, the simpler of the two to run. Reverse osmosis rejects water and a softener discharges brine, and both reach the drain through an air gap at an indirect waste, under OPSC 811.0. Residential treatment devices are certified to the NSF/ANSI standard for their function: 42 and 53 for filters, 44 for softeners, 55 for UV, 58 for reverse osmosis and 62 for distillation.

Running a residential UV unit costs about $200 a year, $40 to $60 of electricity and a $145 to $160 lamp. Its output falls before the lamp goes dark, so a lamp that still lights can under-dose. Owners on the Garage Journal forum describe lamps lasting from 18 months to 3 years, and one unit running 6 years with lamp changes as its only service. Scale, iron or biofilm on the quartz sleeve cuts the dose sooner than lamp age does.

Carbon life depends on what the bed adsorbs, not on gallons alone. Chlorine residual from a well is a single, predictable load. Hydrogen sulfide and iron shorten bed life to 3 to 6 months, and tannins coat the carbon and cut its capacity by 40 to 60 percent, per SoftPro's figures.

Seattle's Bullitt Center adds a small chlorine dose after UV to keep a residual through a multistory building's long pipe runs, as a public water system must. Daily draws turn over the water in a continuously occupied house with short runs and flushable dead legs, so UV alone suits it. Weeks of vacancy are the case for a residual.

**Code**

```
Treatment devices are counted in the pipe sizing.
  Pressure loss through each filter, softener or similar device enters the sizing, and without loss data its inlet and outlet are no smaller than the distribution pipe.
  Source: OPSC 610.2 (2026 edition).
```

## 17.8 Problem water and disinfection

**Simple**

Three problems in well water need more than the standard row of filters: bacteria living in the well, acidic water and radon. Chlorine poured down the casing kills bacteria in the water. Slime-forming iron and sulfur bacteria grow on the casing walls, though. They come back within weeks unless the casing is scrubbed. Acidic water dissolves copper pipe and leaves blue-green stains below the faucets. Radon gas dissolved in well water escapes into the indoor air at showers and the washer.

### Bacteria in the well

**Applied**

1. When a coliform test comes back positive, inspect the well cap, the casing and the distance to the septic system before treating.
2. Shock-chlorinate the well, the pressure tank and the house piping together.
3. Retest for coliform after flushing, and shock again if it persists.
4. Design continuous disinfection only for a well that stays positive after the cap, casing and shock are corrected.
5. For iron or sulfur bacteria, shock at 200 to 500 ppm and hold for the longest contact time the method allows.
6. Brush or surge the casing, and circulate the solution with the pump during the hold.
7. When the bacteria return within the season after a correct shock, call the licensed well constructor who built the well.
8. Where shocks do not hold, meter chlorine or hydrogen peroxide continuously into a contact tank ahead of the pressure tank, then filter.

**Professional**

Iron and sulfur bacteria colonize the casing, screen, pump column and formation as a biofilm whose slime shields the inner layers. Organisms in a mature biofilm can need hundreds of times the dose that kills them free-floating. One shock therefore clears the water and the odor for weeks to months while the interior regrows.

General shock disinfection targets about 200 ppm, and stronger solutions lose effectiveness for that purpose, per SpringWell's guidance. Iron and sulfur bacteria take the higher range because dissolved iron consumes chlorine before it reaches them and the slime protects them. Chlorine alone damages only the colony's surface. Brushing, surging or backwashing the casing and screen breaks the colony up, and a well contractor's tools reach where a surface pour does not.

Continuous low-dose injection into a contact tank, followed by iron filtration and UV, holds where iron exceeds about 5 mg/L with active bacteria or where shocks fail within weeks. Hydrogen peroxide oxidizes faster than chlorine and breaks down to water and oxygen without taste or byproducts. It leaves no lasting residual, so a peroxide train ends with UV. Aeration oxidizes iron and sulfide without a feed pump, but it needs a blower and leaves an established biofilm in place. UV treats the water in the pipe and never reaches the colony on the casing.

### Acidic water

**Applied**

1. Read pH, alkalinity and the Langelier index from the laboratory report.
2. From pH 6.0 up to 6.7, install a calcite neutralizer.
3. Below pH 6.0, blend Corosex into the calcite.
4. Below pH 5.0, meter soda ash in proportion to flow.
5. Place the neutralizer ahead of the iron filter and the softener.
6. Size the softener for the hardness the calcite adds.
7. Top up the calcite media on the manufacturer's schedule.

**Professional**

Blue-green staining at fixtures and pinhole leaks in copper well short of its multi-decade service life mark aggressive water. Low pH dissolves the copper, and the dissolved copper deposits as the stain. Calcite dissolves in proportion to the water's acidity and nearly stops between pH 7.5 and 8.0, so it cannot overcorrect, and it adds hardness as it works. Soda ash or caustic soda metered by flow gives tighter control with no added hardness, at the cost of a metering pump, a solution tank and refills.

### Radon in well water

**Applied**

1. Test for radon in water where the well sits in granitic terrain.
2. Choose aeration or granular activated carbon from the result and the outage plan.
3. Before installing carbon, arrange disposal of the spent media as low-level radioactive waste.

**Professional**

| | Aeration | Granular activated carbon |
|---|---|---|
| Installed cost | $4,000 to $12,000, and $6,000 to $20,000 for larger systems | $1,200 to $2,500 |
| Removal | Up to 99 percent, the choice at high concentrations | Suited to lower concentrations and lower water use |
| Power | A blower that runs with water use | None |
| Upkeep | Under $200 a year for checks and blower service | Media replacement |
| Waste | None | Spent media collects radon decay products, a low-level radioactive waste |

Aeration adds a blower load to the outage budget, and carbon adds a periodic disposal duty. The measured radon level sets the equipment's capacity and how fast carbon reaches a disposal threshold. The envelope chapter describes soil-gas radon under the slab.

### Disinfecting the well, the tank and the pipes

**Applied**

1. Calculate casing volume in gallons as 3.14 times the radius squared times the water-column height, in feet, times 7.48.
2. Measure the bleach with OHA's shock chlorination calculator for the dose and contact time chosen.
3. Pour the solution down the well, and recirculate it through a hose from an outside tap until every tap smells of chlorine.
4. Hold it for the full contact time.
5. Flush to a non-sanitary outlet until the chlorine residual reads below 4 mg/L.
6. Take a coliform sample after flushing, and repeat the shock if coliform persists.
7. Disinfect new or repaired house piping before first use, and flush it until the outlets match the supply's residual.

**Professional**

| Job | Dose and hold | Flush and confirm | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Well, tank and house piping together | 10 or 50 mg/L, held 3, 6 or 24 hours by method | Flush below 4 mg/L, then coliform samples | OHA drinking-water operations guidance |
| Owner shock of a household well | 50 to 100 ppm household bleach, recirculated, held 6 to 12 hours | Flush until the odor is gone, coliform test about 3 days later | OSU Extension well-water program |
| Iron or sulfur bacteria | 200 to 500 ppm, the longest hold, with scrubbing | Flush, then coliform and odor checks | SpringWell and SoftPro guidance |
| Storage tank at commissioning and after any opening | 50 to 250 mg/L free chlorine, held at least 24 hours | Drain to a non-sanitary outlet, refill, retest until below 4 mg/L with coliform absent | OHA and OWRD well-owner protocol |

Higher concentration with a shorter hold and lower concentration with a longer one are treated as equivalent paths. Owner shock chlorination is routine on a sound well. For a recurrence within the season, a suspected casing or seal fault, or unfamiliar wellhead hardware, the licensed well constructor who built the well does the work.

**Code**

```
New or repaired potable water piping is disinfected before use where required.
  50 ppm of chlorine held 24 hours or 200 ppm held 3 hours, then flushed until the outlet residual matches the flushing water, repeated while bacteria persist.
  Source: OPSC 609.10 (2026 edition).
```

## 17.9 Pressure control

**Simple**

Pressure is the push behind the water, as distinct from flow, the amount of water that moves. A house can have high pressure and still run a weak shower if a pipe is too small or a filter is clogged. The code caps the pressure standing in the pipes, and above the cap a regulator brings it down. Heated water expands. Once a check valve closes the house off from the main, that expansion has nowhere to go. Inside a small tank, an air cushion absorbs it. Without that tank, the pressure climbs with every heating cycle until the relief valve on the water heater drips.

**Applied**

1. Measure static pressure with every fixture closed.
2. Measure flowing pressure with the worst-case fixtures running.
3. Measure the drop across each filter and treatment device.
4. Measure pressure at the farthest and highest fixture.
5. Where static pressure exceeds 80 psi, install a regulator with a strainer and set it to 80 psi or less.
6. Mount the regulator and strainer above ground or in a drained vault, protected from freezing, with the strainer removable in place.
7. Install the expansion tank on the cold line, downstream of the regulator and on the house side of any check valve or backflow device.
8. Strap or bracket the expansion tank to the wall or a rack.
9. Record the regulator setpoint and the readings before and after it.
10. Record the pump's cut-in and cut-out, or the booster's setpoint.
11. At commissioning, confirm 45 to 60 psi at showers and 60 to 80 psi at tub fills.
12. Confirm 20 to 80 psi, or the appliance's own range, at the washer and dishwasher.

**Professional**

Static pressure above the cap overworks fixture cartridges, valve seats and the water heater's tank. A regulator holds the downstream side at its setpoint, and its strainer catches the grit that would score its seat. Oregon's amendment to 608.2 deletes the model code's strainer exemption for large regulators. It also permits any adequate means of vault drainage in place of a bore-sighted drain to daylight, which matters on a flat site.

Heated water expands, and a check valve, a regulator or a backflow device closes the system against the main. That volume goes into the expansion tank's air cushion. Supported by its piping alone, a full tank works the joints, which is why the 2026 text requires it fastened to the structure. The heater's own relief valve is the last defense and never the expansion control.

A high-pressure trunk with a regulator at each fixture is an alternative engineered design under OPSC 301.5, approved at the building official's discretion, and it multiplies the stubs the dead-leg rule governs. Per-fixture brass regulators cost $25 to $40 each, and a 2-inch copper trunk costs $15 to $25 a foot, before the engineering fee. One regulator and one expansion tank are the default here.

**Code**

```
Static pressure above 80 psi is regulated down.
  An approved ASSE 1003 regulator preceded by a strainer, reducing static pressure to 80 psi or less, mounted accessibly above ground or in a drained vault, protected from freezing.
  Source: OPSC 608.2 (2026 edition, Oregon amendment deleting the strainer exemption).

A regulated system takes an expansion tank on the cold side.
  An NSF/ANSI/CAN 61 expansion tank downstream of each regulator, sized to the manufacturer's instructions and securely fastened to the structure.
  Source: OPSC 608.2 (2026 edition).

A closed system takes thermal-expansion control whatever the heater type.
  An IAPMO/ANSI Z1088 expansion tank, or an approved equivalent, on the building side of any check valve, backflow preventer or other normally closed device.
  Source: OPSC 608.3 (2026 edition).

A storage water heater takes a combination relief valve, and an on-demand heater needs no expansion tank.
  A listed combination temperature and pressure-relief valve on storage water heating equipment, excepting listed instantaneous heaters of 3 inches inside diameter or less.
  Source: OPSC 608.3 (2026 edition).

A designer may substitute a relief valve for the expansion tank.
  Systems designed by a certified plumbing contractor or registered design professional may use an approved pressure relief valve set at 100 psi or less.
  Source: OPSC 608.2 (2026 edition).

Per-fixture regulation is an alternative engineered design.
  Two sets of signed and sealed drawings from a registered design professional, a riser diagram, and the building official's approval.
  Source: OPSC 301.5 (2023 edition); OPSC 608.2.
```

## 17.10 Wet zones and the piping layout

**Simple**

Bathrooms, the kitchen, the laundry and the water heater belong close together when the floor plan allows. Short pipe runs cost less, bring hot water sooner and hide fewer joints inside walls. From a manifold near the heater, one unbroken plastic tube can run to each fixture, with no joint anywhere inside a wall. Each tube has its own shutoff at the manifold. One toilet can be isolated for repair with the rest of the house running. A fitting that leaks behind a framed wall is cut out through drywall. A leak behind grouted block takes a saw to the block.

**Applied**

1. Group the bathrooms, the kitchen and the laundry into a wet zone, with the drains stacked over a common wet wall.
2. Set the water heater beside the largest hot-water loads.
3. Choose the piping topology from the table below before any penetration is formed.
4. Run home-run lines to every fixture whose supply passes through or behind structure.
5. Keep every tee, elbow and coupling in the service layer or at a manifold, where it can be reached.
6. Mark every slab and wall penetration on the plan before the pour.
7. When a fixture group leaves the wet zone, add its pipe length, hot-water wait, insulation and penetrations to the plan.

| Topology | Pipe and fittings | Service | Where it suits this house |
|---|---|---|---|
| Home-run manifold | Most pipe, one continuous run per fixture, no concealed fittings | A shutoff per fixture at the manifold | Any fixture fed through or behind structure |
| Submanifold hybrid | A trunk to a small manifold at each wet-zone cluster, less pipe than full home-run | A shutoff per fixture at the local manifold | A plan with more than one wet zone |
| Trunk and branch | Least pipe, the most fittings, some concealed | Branch shutoffs where fitted | Only where every fitting stays in the service layer |

**Professional**

Home-run piping feeds each fixture one continuous PEX run from a central manifold, with every fitting at the manifold or the fixture. In a worked comparison on the Fine Homebuilding forum, a 2,300-square-foot two-story house took about 1,500 feet of PEX home-run, more than twice a trunk-and-branch layout. A home-run line to a far fixture still holds that fixture's full volume of cooled water, so the topology does not shorten the hot-water wait on its own. Fine Homebuilding's submanifold design keeps most of the per-fixture isolation on far less pipe and drilling. Trunk and branch puts a fitting at every branch, and each one is a future leak point, some behind finishes.

Stacking the drains over the same wet wall is in the drain-waste-vent chapter. The 2026 OPSC includes a new manifold section, 606.5.1, under which every component of a field-built manifold conforms to the water-distribution standards.

## 17.11 Sizing the service and distribution

**Simple**

Pipe size is calculated from the fixtures, their distance from the source and the pressure available, never from habit. Each fixture carries a demand value in the code's table, and the values add up to a load. The farther the water travels, the more pressure the pipe friction takes. Each foot the water climbs costs half a pound per square inch. Too small a pipe starves the shower when the washer fills. Too big a pipe costs more, holds more standing water and lengthens the wait for hot water.

**Applied**

1. List every fixture with its water supply fixture units from Table 610.3.
2. Measure the developed length from the source to the highest, farthest outlet.
3. Subtract 0.5 psi for each foot of rise from the source to that outlet.
4. Subtract the pressure drop through each filter, softener and backflow device.
5. Where a regulator is installed, size against 80 percent of its setpoint, 48 psi for a 60 psi setting.
6. Read the meter and building-supply size from Table 610.4, or run the Appendix M peak-demand method.
7. Check two showers, the dishwasher and the clothes washer running together against each fixture's minimum flowing pressure, 8 to 20 psi by fixture.
8. On a well, size the house piping on the line downstream of the pressure tank or the booster.
9. Size the potable line feeding any irrigation system for the house and the irrigation together.

**Professional**

Two sizing methods run side by side in the 2026 OPSC. In the traditional method the fixture units are added up, the developed length and the elevation loss applied, and the sizes read from the table. Appendix M, a peak-demand method, is built on water-efficient fixtures and the probability of concurrent use, and it yields a smaller meter for the same fixture count. Portland Water Bureau adopted Appendix M for residential meter sizing in 2023. On a long developed length, the margin behind a regulator decides pipe sizes.

On a private well the sizing is layered. Sustained yield caps the pump's flow, the pump curve and total dynamic head give the pressure at the tank, and the tank's drawdown sets the cycle. Where the yield falls below peak demand, the storage tank and booster described earlier sit between well and house, and the house piping is sized on the booster's output. Statewide Code Interpretation 05-01, retained for the 2026 edition, states the division's position on water pipe sizing for wells under 610.7.

**Code**

```
Fixture demand is tabulated in water supply fixture units.
  Table 610.3 values by fixture, such as 2.5 units and a 1/2-inch minimum branch for a 1.28-gallon gravity-tank water closet.
  Source: OPSC Table 610.3 (2026 edition).

The traditional method reads the meter and supply size from a table.
  Minimum meter and building-supply size by fixture units, developed length and available pressure, with a 3/4-inch minimum building supply.
  Source: OPSC Table 610.4 (2023 edition, Appendix A).

The peak-demand method is adopted statewide.
  Appendix M, Peak Water Demand Calculator, is adopted as part of the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code.
  Source: OPSC 102.7.1 (2026 edition); OPSC Appendix M.

A regulated system is sized on reduced pressure.
  Pipe sizes under Table 610.4 are based on 80 percent of the regulator's reduced pressure.
  Source: OPSC 608.2 (2026 edition).

Irrigation piping outside the building is unregulated, and its potable feed is not.
  Exterior irrigation piping behind an air gap or backflow device is outside the sizing rules, and the potable piping that feeds it carries the full domestic and irrigation demand.
  Source: OPSC 610.13, item 6 (2026 edition).
```

## 17.12 Pipe materials

**Simple**

The pipes inside this house are flexible plastic tubing, PEX. Acidic well water eats pinholes in copper within years. PEX is unaffected by acid, bends around corners without fittings and tolerates a freeze that would split copper. Sunlight weakens PEX, so none of it is left exposed. The buried line from the well is black polyethylene, fused into one continuous pipe with no fitting underground. A tracer wire runs beside it so a locator can find it under the yard.

**Applied**

- Specify HDPE PE4710, SDR 9 or SDR 11, fusion-jointed, for the buried service line, with 14 AWG tracer wire alongside.
- Specify PEX-A with expansion fittings for the distribution and the home-run manifolds.
- Switch to PEX-B where the treated water carries a continuous chlorine residual.
- Buy only PEX whose printed designation code reads 5306.
- Keep PEX out of sunlight, in the coil and on the job, and cover any run exposed past 6 months.
- Make the first 18 inches at a storage water heater in copper or stainless, with a flexible connector no longer than 24 inches.
- Put a dielectric fitting at every joint between dissimilar metals.
- Support pipe at the spacing in the code's table or the manufacturer's instructions.
- Leave every fitting reachable from the service layer.

**Professional**

OPSC Table 604.1 permits more materials than this house uses. HDPE PE4710 under AWWA C901 and ASTM F714 resists chlorine, survives a freeze and fuses into a joint-free buried run. Soft copper Type K is the traditional buried line and corrodes in the acidic soils of the Cascade foothills.

PEX-A, crosslinked by the peroxide process, is the most flexible and the most repairable in the field, since one expansion tool makes every joint. PEX-B, crosslinked by silane, keeps the highest chlorine resistance because its process consumes no antioxidant stabilizer, so it is the choice under a continuous chlorine residual. PEX-C carries the smallest stabilizer reserve of the three and is not specified. CPVC embrittles with age and reacts with some caulks, foams and wire jackets, so it is not specified either.

Under ASTM F876 and F877 a designation code of 5306 means Class 5 chlorine resistance and Class 3 UV resistance. Class 3 is a 6-month construction allowance, not a rating for exposure, and the 1306 and 3306 grades are avoided on a chlorinated system.

Aggressive water still attacks brass fittings, valve seats, the pressure-tank bladder and the copper connector at the heater, so PEX removes the pinhole-leak failure and leaves the case for pH correction standing. PEX cast in a slab is standard radiant practice, with the higher-burst PEX-B where rebar drag or a strike edge threatens the tube during placement. Where a metal building supply serves as the electrical ground, 604.10 ties any plastic replacement to a grounding system approved under the electrical code.

**Code**

```
Potable pipe comes from the approved materials table.
  Copper Types K, L and M, CPVC, PEX A, B and C, PE-RT, PEX-AL-PEX, stainless steel, and HDPE for service piping.
  Source: OPSC Table 604.1 (2023 edition).

Plastic tubing stays 18 inches from a storage water heater.
  No PEX, PEX-AL-PEX, PE-AL-PE or PE-RT within the first 18 inches of piping at a water heater, and flexible metal connectors no longer than 24 inches.
  Source: OPSC 604.13 (2026 edition).

Approved plastics may connect directly to a tankless heater.
  CPVC, PE-RT, PEX and PP tubing approved under Table 604.1 may be installed within the first 18 inches at an instantaneous on-demand tankless water heater.
  Source: OPSC 604.13, exception (2026 edition).
```

## 17.13 Water hammer, noise and movement

**Simple**

Water hammer is the bang in the pipes when a fast valve stops moving water dead. A clothes washer's fill valve and a dishwasher's solenoid snap shut in a fraction of a second. The moving water piles up against the closed valve as a pressure spike. Over years the spike shakes pipes, stresses valve seats and works joints loose. A small arrestor at each fast valve holds a cushion of air behind a piston that absorbs the spike. Oregon's code does not require arrestors in a house, and this house has one at each fast valve.

**Applied**

1. Size pipe so water velocity stays low, which cuts both hammer and flow noise.
2. Install an ASSE 1010 mini-arrestor at the clothes-washer box, the dishwasher, the refrigerator ice-maker line and every solenoid valve, including irrigation and treatment regeneration valves.
3. Support pipe at the specified spacing, isolated from masonry and framing with cushioned clamps.
4. Mount booster and recirculation pumps on vibration isolators.
5. Leave each arrestor and pump accessible for replacement.
6. At commissioning, listen with the appliances cycling.

A capped stub of pipe is not an arrestor. Its air dissolves into the water within months, and the stub then transmits the spike.

**Professional**

Under the model code every building with a quick-acting valve takes arrestors, and Oregon's exception exempts houses. Solenoid valves on washers, dishwashers, ice makers and irrigation are the common sources of the spike, so arrestors at those points are a durability specification here. A pipe clamped hard to concrete transmits pump and flow vibration into the slab and walls. Isolation hangers, sleeves with clearance and service-layer routing keep the structure quiet. The 2026 adoption matrix records the review committee's note to consider removing the exception.

**Code**

```
Arrestors are required at quick-acting valves, with a dwelling exception.
  Approved ASSE 1010 or PDI-WH 201 arrestors as close as possible to quick-acting valves, except in one- and two-family dwellings and townhouses.
  Source: OPSC 609.11 (2026 edition, Oregon exception).
```

## 17.14 The heat pump water heater

**Simple**

This house heats its water with a heat pump water heater, an electric tank that pulls heat out of the air around it. At an energy factor of 2.0 or more, it makes at least twice the hot water per kilowatt-hour of a plain electric tank. Its used air comes out cold and dry. So the heater stands in a big room, breathes through ducts, or keeps its compressor outdoors like a mini-split. Recovery is slow, 8 to 15 gallons an hour, so the tank holds a morning's worth: 65 to 80 gallons for four people. Heated at midday on a timer, the tank stores the solar array's surplus as hot water for a fraction of a battery's price.

**Applied**

1. Count the occupants and fixtures, the simultaneous showers and tub fills, the laundry and dishwasher timing, and the electrical service available.
2. Size storage at 1.5 times the peak-hour demand, 30 to 40 gallons for a two-shower morning in a four-person house.
3. Check the first-hour rating on the EnergyGuide label against that peak, with the recovery in heat-pump mode.
4. Specify an 80-gallon tank where a recirculation loop or a midday charging window is planned.
5. Specify a NEEA Northern Climate Tier 2 or higher unit, or a split system with the compressor outdoors.
6. Give an unducted unit 1,000 cubic feet or more of air, or louvered doors into a larger space.
7. Keep intake and exhaust ducts short, straight, sealed and insulated to R-6 where they cross a thermal boundary.
8. Fit a damper that sends the exhaust outdoors in the heating season and into a utility room in summer.
9. Run the condensate by gravity to an indirect waste with no section above the unit's outlet, or through a condensate pump.
10. Set the heater away from bedrooms, with clearance to service and replace it, or choose the split system.
11. Where the heater sits over a finished floor, set it in a pan with a drain to a visible point.
12. Strap the tank in its upper and lower thirds, with the lower strap at least 4 inches from the controls.
13. Run the relief-valve discharge full size, downward, to an air gap at the drain or outdoors 6 inches to 2 feet above grade.
14. Schedule the heating cycle for the solar peak, 10 AM to 3 PM, with a timer, a relay or a CTA-2045 control module.
15. Keep the mixing valves on the outlet whatever the storage setpoint.

**Professional**

A heat pump water heater's compressor cools and dries the air it draws, so an unducted unit in a small closet recirculates its own exhaust and falls into resistance mode. Energy Star's design guidance states a minimum of about 700 cubic feet, a 10-by-10-foot room, and 1,000 cubic feet or ducting for cold-climate reliability. Heat-pump mode needs surrounding air at about 40°F or warmer, and an unheated Oregon garage sits below that for weeks. The Oregon Department of Energy's appliance rule, OAR 330-070-0073, defines the NEEA Northern Climate tiers: Tier 1 at an energy factor of 1.8 configured for unconditioned space, Tier 2 or higher at 2.0 for conditioned space.

The first-hour rating is about 70 percent of tank volume plus the hourly recovery, and recovery in heat-pump mode runs 8 to 15 gallons an hour against 13 to 21 for electric resistance and 20 to 30 for gas. Recovering a 50-gallon tank can take 60 to 130 minutes. Hybrid units engage their resistance elements under heavy draw and lose the efficiency that justified them, so the tank is sized to carry the peak hour in heat-pump mode.

A split system puts the compressor and evaporator outdoors on a refrigerant line through a 3-inch wall penetration and leaves only the tank inside. It removes the cold exhaust and the room-volume constraint, at a higher installed cost and with the outdoor unit derated in cold weather. In heat-pump mode an integral unit runs at 45 to 55 decibels, about a refrigerator. In summer its exhaust, cooled and dried, is a small dehumidification source for a utility room next to living space.

Scheduling the heating cycle to the solar peak stores surplus generation as hot water. A study on the arXiv preprint server found about 15 percent of a household's water-heating energy movable into solar hours. The CTA-2045-A port on many units takes a schedule from a home energy manager or a relay as well as a utility signal, and the solar chapter specifies that control. Energy Trust of Oregon's incentive structure favors these heaters. The 2026 ORSC's heat-pump rule, N1105.8, concerns space heating where split-system air conditioning is installed and says nothing about water heaters. Tankless gas delivers a slug of cold water between draws and stalls below a minimum flow at low-flow fixtures, and it is not specified here.

**Code**

```
A storage water heater is strapped against earthquake motion.
  Straps within the upper third and the lower third of the tank, the lower strap at least 4 inches from the controls, except in one- and two-family dwellings in Seismic Design Category B or C.
  Source: OPSC 507.2 (2026 edition, Oregon amendment).

A water heater over finished space sits in a drained pan.
  A watertight pan at least 1 1/2 inches deep with a drain of at least 3/4 inch to an approved location, its end readily visible, and no relief-valve discharge into the pan.
  Source: OPSC 507.4 (2026 edition).

Relief-valve discharge runs unvalved to a visible, safe end.
  Full size, downward, by gravity through an air gap to the drainage system, onto an on-grade garage floor, or outdoors 6 inches to 2 feet above the ground, untrapped and unthreaded.
  Source: OPSC 608.5 (2026 edition).

Storage capacity follows the first-hour rating table.
  The minimum capacity for a storage water heater is the first-hour rating in Table 501.1(2), and the final installation is approved by the building official.
  Source: OPSC 501.1 (2026 edition).
```

## 17.15 Storage and delivery temperature

**Simple**

Two temperatures matter in a hot-water system, and they are set at two different devices. Water stored warm, between 68 and 122°F, grows Legionella, the bacteria behind Legionnaires' disease. So the tank holds 140°F, hot enough to kill it. Water at 140°F scalds skin in seconds. At the tank a mixing valve cools what leaves it, and inside each shower a second valve caps it again. The water heater's dial sets the storage temperature, and the temperature at each tap comes from its valve.

**Applied**

1. Set the tank at 140°F.
2. Mix down to 124°F at the heater outlet with an ASSE 1017 master mixing valve.
3. Field-set the limit stop on each ASSE 1016 shower valve to deliver 110°F.
4. Fit ASSE 1070 valves at bathtubs, bidets and lavatories, set to 110 to 115°F.
5. Measure the delivery temperature at every fixture at commissioning and record it.
6. Where a heat pump water heater is stored at 130 to 135°F, hold the point-of-use valves and the record of the setpoint.

**Professional**

Legionella grows between about 68 and 122°F and dies quickly above about 140°F, so distribution leaves the heater above the growth ceiling and the fixtures take the temperature down. An ASSE 1016 valve holds its setting within about 3.6°F at 45 psi, which is why the limit stop is set 10 degrees under the code's shower ceiling. Under the fixture sections the water heater's thermostat is not an accepted delivery control, and a heater conforming to ASSE 1084 is an accepted limiting means.

The 2026 OPSC includes a new section on flow-reduction devices listed to ASSE 1062, which cut flow to a trickle within 5 seconds of the outlet passing a preset temperature. They provide scald protection only, no thermal-shock protection, and 408.4.3 bars them from standing in for the shower and tub valves. Appendix N, on water temperature, scalding and Legionella, is printed in the Oregon code for local adoption and is not adopted by the state.

**Code**

```
Showers and tub-showers are limited to 120°F at delivery.
  An ASSE 1016 device, pressure-balancing, thermostatic or combination, with a field-set limit stop, and the water-heater thermostat is not the control.
  Source: OPSC 408.3 (2023 edition; renumbered within 408 in 2026).

Bathtubs, whirlpool tubs and bidets take their own limiting devices.
  ASSE 1070 or CSA B125.3 devices, bidets limited to 110°F and bathtubs and whirlpool tubs to 120°F.
  Source: OPSC 409.4 and 410.3 (2023 edition).

A flow-reduction device never replaces the limiting valve.
  Devices to ASSE 1062 that cut flow on temperature are not a substitute for the balanced-pressure, thermostatic or combination shower valve or the tub temperature-limiting valve.
  Source: OPSC 408.4.3 (2026 edition).
```

## 17.16 One heat source for hot water and the radiant floor

**Simple**

The radiant floor and the hot-water tank each get their own heat source in this house. One combination unit can serve both. When the floor loops draw the tank down, though, the water left for the taps sits in the warm range where Legionella grows. Water that has run through the floor tubing never comes back to the tank you drink from either. A heat exchanger, two sets of channels with a metal wall between, moves the heat into the floor loop. The two waters never touch. The floor loop can then carry antifreeze and corrosion inhibitor with no risk to the drinking water.

**Applied**

1. Give the radiant loop its own buffer tank between the heat source and the floor.
2. Keep the domestic tank at 140°F regardless of radiant demand.
3. Separate the floor loop from potable water with a heat exchanger, in a closed loop.
4. Protect the potable side of the heat exchanger with the backflow device for its hazard.
5. Where one appliance serves both duties, specify a unit listed as a dual-purpose water heater.

**Professional**

A combination appliance shares one heat source between the radiant floor and the domestic tank through a heat exchanger. CSA B214 permits combined service only where the heater is manufacturer-designated for dual-purpose use, and the 2026 OPSC defines such a heater and includes the boiler code among its installation rules. Ordinary storage heaters are not repurposed.

When radiant demand draws a shared tank down, the water remaining for the fixtures idles in the growth range for hours, the condition the storage rule exists to prevent. The buffer tank holds enough thermal mass to carry the floor's long, steady, lower-temperature draw while the domestic tank takes the short, hot, intermittent one.

A closed loop with a heat exchanger keeps glycol and inhibitor out of the potable system, and its only potable interface is the exchanger's connection. An open loop puts floor-circulated water back into the drinking tank, so every component in it needs potable-contact certification and only NSF-listed additives. Buffer-tank and radiant-loop sizing are in the HVAC chapter. Indirect heating from a hydronic boiler commits the house to a boiler, and a dedicated heat pump water heater beside the radiant plant is the simpler pairing.

**Code**

```
A dual-purpose water heater is installed under three sets of rules.
  An appliance intended as a heat source for both space heating and domestic hot water is installed per the plumbing code, the Boiler Code and the manufacturer's instructions.
  Source: OPSC Table 501.1(1), note (2026 edition).
```

## 17.17 Hot-water delivery and recirculation

**Simple**

Hot water standing in a pipe cools, so the first person at a far tap waits while the cold slug drains out. Short runs cut that wait directly, and small-diameter pipe holds less cooled water to drain. A recirculation loop removes the wait by keeping hot water near the fixtures. The loop in this house runs on demand. Pressing a button or passing a motion sensor at the far bathroom starts the pump. Hot water arriving at the return stops it. Running all day, a loop costs $30 to $60 a year in pump power plus the heat lost from the pipe. Its water also idles in the warm range where bacteria grow.

**Applied**

| Strategy | Where it suits |
|---|---|
| Short runs alone | A small wet zone with every fixture near the heater |
| Demand-control recirculation | A button or motion call at the end-of-loop fixtures, with the loop cold until called |
| Timed recirculation | A fixed daily schedule, with more loss than demand control |
| Continuous recirculation | Hot water in 2 to 5 seconds at every tap, at $30 to $60 a year of pump power plus standby loss, and the most bacterial exposure |
| Point-of-use heater | One remote sink, bathroom or guest wing too far to loop |

1. Run the trunk and each branch at the smallest diameter the code and the fixture's flow allow.
2. Hold the standing volume between the heater or loop and each fixture to a half gallon or less.
3. Put a crossover valve at the far fixture and use the cold line as the return during the priming pulse.
4. Fit a check valve between the circulator and the heater.
5. Size a demand pump at a quarter horsepower and 6 to 10 GPM, to fill the loop in 1 to 2 minutes.
6. Size a continuous circulator, where one is used anyway, at 5 to 25 watts and 1 to 2 GPM.
7. Run stub branches as home-run lines from a manifold instead of tees off the loop.
8. On more than one loop, fit balancing valves and a check valve on each dormant branch.
9. Insulate hot-water pipe to R-3, and all recirculation piping and the first 8 feet each side of the heater.
10. Protect weather-exposed insulation from sunlight and moisture.
11. Give every dead leg longer than 1.5 pipe diameters a reachable flushing point.
12. Install a point-of-use electric heater at any fixture too far to loop.

**Professional**

A demand-controlled loop runs the pump until the return reaches setpoint and then sits at room temperature, which holds standby loss and the loop's time in the Legionella range to minutes a day. Cold loop water also slows chlorine attack on PEX. A crossover valve at the far fixture uses the cold line as the return path, so no dedicated return line is needed.

Structured plumbing minimizes the standing volume between the heater and each fixture by running the smallest workable diameters. The Building America Solution Center's target is a half gallon or less, with less than a cup on the shortest runs. The EPA's hot-water distribution guide states waits of 5 seconds on an efficient layout against 90 seconds or more on an oversized one, and the American Society of Plumbing Engineers treats 10 seconds as the benchmark. Recirculation and structured plumbing work together, the loop serving the fixtures on it and small-bore stubs serving the rest.

Stub branches off the loop fall under the dead-leg rule. Under OPSC 309.5 a dead leg is a run with no flow longer than 1.5 pipe diameters, and the flushing point can be a hose bibb, an angle stop, an arrestor or an expansion tank reached through an access. ORSC N1103.5 specifies the pipe insulation, and under the 2026 OPSC, at 609.12, insulation follows the building code. The commercial energy code, OEESC 6.4.3 with ASHRAE 90.1, requires recirculation to shut off on no demand, with a check valve at the circulator, a commercial rule applied here as practice.

**Code**

```
Hot-water piping carries minimum insulation.
```

## 17.18 Backflow and cross-connection control

**Simple**

From the well to the tap, water normally flows one way. A pressure drop from a failed pump or a big draw can reverse it. Left in a bucket of soapy water, a garden hose siphons that water back into the house pipes when the pressure dips. Each backflow device is a one-way check matched to how dangerous the connection is. For a hose bib the check is a screw-on part costing $10 to $20. For a spa or a sprayer that injects fertilizer, it is a tested assembly costing $300 to $800. Nothing can siphon across open air. An air gap, like the one between a faucet and a sink rim, needs no valve and no test.

**Applied**

| Connection | Device |
|---|---|
| Hose bib | An ASSE 1011 hose-connection vacuum breaker, often built into the frost-free bib |
| Landscape irrigation with no pump and no chemicals | A pressure vacuum breaker above ground |
| Irrigation with chemical injection or a pump | A reduced-pressure principle assembly |
| Spa, hot tub or a treated boiler fill | A reduced-pressure principle assembly |
| Softener brine and any makeup to a nonpotable tank | An air gap |
| Yard hydrant | A self-draining sanitary type or an ASSE 1024 dual-check, never an underground stop-and-waste valve |
| Any nonpotable system | An air gap or a reduced-pressure assembly at every makeup connection, with labels |

1. Use an air gap wherever the layout allows.
2. Place every device above ground, reachable, protected from freezing, with room to drain and replace it.
3. Keep any device with an atmospheric vent or port out of pits, below grade and submerged spots.
4. Have each testable assembly tested at installation by a tester on the Oregon Health Authority's certified list.
5. On a public water connection, book the annual test the supplier's cross-connection program requires.
6. On the private well, retest the assemblies annually as maintenance and file each report.
7. Before buying a parcel, check the OHA tester list for the county and the testers' home cities.

**Professional**

A reduced-pressure principle assembly holds a relief valve between two checks, and when either check fouls the relief vents to the floor rather than letting contaminated water through. That is why the code assigns it wherever chemicals or a pump can drive water back under pressure. Having no vent, a double check serves chemical-free connections only, at $100 to $300. A device with an atmospheric port submerged in a flooded pit draws the pit water in, hence the 2026 location rule. The device matrix in 603.5 is tiered by hazard, by what is connected and what could flow back, and no tier turns on whether a tank is buried.

The 2026 OPSC requires the test at installation and no annual schedule, by an OHA-certified tester under 603.4.2. The one-time cross-connection test of a rainwater system is in the rainwater section. Annual testing on a public connection comes from OAR 333-061-0070, which binds public water systems and reaches a customer through the supplier's program, with the report filed within 10 working days. OHA publishes its certified-tester list, searchable by county, at its cross-connection program page and yourwater.oregon.gov. Listing is voluntary, so an empty county result is not proof of no tester, and the program office answers by email. Testers cluster around a county's population centers, which adds drive time to a rural annual test.

**Code**

```
Each hazard class takes its assigned device.
  An ASSE 1011 vacuum breaker at hose bibs, a pressure vacuum breaker at plain irrigation, a reduced-pressure assembly where chemicals or a pump are present, an air gap at brine and nonpotable makeup.
  Source: OPSC 603.5.1 through 603.5.22 (2026 edition).

Plain irrigation takes one of six devices.
  With no pump and no chemical injection: an AVB, PVB, SVB, RP, DC or an IAPMO PS 72 valve.
  Source: OPSC 603.5.6 (2026 edition, Oregon addition of the DC).

Yard hydrants drain above ground or carry a dual check.
  No underground stop-and-waste feature, and an external vertical yard hydrant is self-draining sanitary type or fitted with an ASSE 1024 dual check.
  Source: OPSC 603.5.17 (2026 edition, Oregon exception).

A vented backflow device stays out of pits and below grade.
  No device with an atmospheric vent or port in a pit, underground or submerged, with a double check valve assembly permitted below grade.
  Source: OPSC 603.4.9 (2026 edition, Oregon exception).

A backflow assembly is tested when installed.
  The premise owner has the assembly tested at installation by a backflow assembly tester certified by the Oregon Health Authority.
  Source: OPSC 603.4.2 (2026 edition, Oregon amendment).
```

## 17.19 Rainwater and a second source beside the well

**Simple**

Rain off the roof can flush toilets, run the clothes washer and water the garden, and it stays in its own pipes. In this house the rain lines are labeled, run to their own fixtures, and never join the well water. No automatic valve switches sources. So no valve can stick open and mix them, or stick shut and leave the house dry. You choose the source by choosing the tap. Rainwater arrives slightly acidic and nearly free of minerals, which is why it eats copper and why its treatment differs from the well's. Drinking the rain is possible under a separate code appendix, and this house does not claim it.

**Applied**

1. Assign fixtures to the rain system at design time: toilets, the clothes washer, outdoor spigots.
2. Collect through leaf screens and a first-flush diverter into a watertight, labeled tank with a screened, secured cover.
3. Run the tank overflow to a safe discharge away from the foundation, the drainfield and the neighbor's land.
4. Run the rain system on its own pump and piping, labeled nonpotable along its length and at every outlet.
5. Fit an air gap or a reduced-pressure assembly at any potable makeup into the rain tank.
6. Filter sediment and disinfect with UV, at minimum, for toilet or clothes-washer use.
7. Correct pH on the rain side in its own train, before any point where the two waters could ever meet.
8. Give any rain branch that idles for weeks a flushing point and a shorter inspection interval for its metal fittings.
9. Freeze-protect the rain system, and drain any exposed run before a hard freeze.
10. Where a switching valve is ever installed, specify a manual lever and label the well-only position.
11. Test both systems for cross-connection, isolated from each other, when the rain system is commissioned.
12. Obtain the plumbing permit for any indoor component, designed by a plumbing contractor, a certified rainwater system designer or a registered design professional.

| Architecture | What fails, and how it shows | Pipe and hardware |
|---|---|---|
| Separate systems to the fixture | A fault on one source shows at its own fixtures | The most pipe, labels at every rain fixture, no switching hardware |
| Automatic switching | A valve stuck open mixes sources behind the RP, a valve stuck shut dries the house | A controller, a motorized three-port valve, an RP assembly, a manual override |
| Blend after treatment | A failed stage on one source is masked by the other | One pressure system and one set of devices, two full trains |

**Professional**

Rainwater arrives at about pH 5.6 from dissolved carbon dioxide, with nearly no minerals and no alkalinity to buffer it, so it is aggressive to copper and to calcium-bearing materials. Well water's pH, hardness and alkalinity follow the aquifer and are unknown until the well is tested. A bare or lightly coated concrete roof deck adds alkalinity to its runoff in its early years as the surface carbonates. Blending the two before one correction stage asks that stage to track a moving mix, so each source is corrected in its own train.

Intermittent flow corrodes metal faster than steady flow or permanent stagnation. Each resumption of flow strips the film that stagnant water formed and brings fresh oxidant to the surface, and copper's blue-water pitting occurs under stagnation with no disinfectant residual. A rain branch that idles for a wet season also holds still water in the warm range and freezes before a flowing line in the same cold spot.

WISY's float switches and solenoid valves are sold in the United States by Rainwater Management Solutions, a UL 508A panel shop in Virginia that also builds controllers to order. A motorized three-port valve carries a manual override for a controller or power failure. Its two failure directions have opposite consequences, and the reduced-pressure assembly at the interface holds the cross-connection protection when the valve fails open. The simplest override is a house in which nothing automatic exists to fail.

OPSC Chapter 16 governs nonpotable rainwater, OPSC Chapter 15 other alternate nonpotable sources, and Appendix K, adopted statewide, potable rainwater in a single-family dwelling. The Land chapter states the water-rights side of roof collection, and the water independence chapter specifies the catchment, the tank sizing and the rain treatment.

Appendix K requires design by a registered design professional or a person the authority deems competent, with water quality as the authority determines and frequent testing the owner's responsibility. A nonpotable system treated to the same filtration and UV as the well water carries no claim of potable status, and a house is outside the public-water-system category that the Bullitt Center's rain-to-tap system falls in. Portland and Eugene publish their own rainwater guides under the OPSC framework.

**Code**

```
A private supply joins no other source without approval.
  No water piping supplied by a private water supply system connects to any other source without the approval of the building official, the Oregon Health Authority or other department having jurisdiction.
  Source: OPSC 602.4 (2026 edition).

Nonpotable lines are labeled at every outlet.
  Exposed portions identified per the code, and each outlet posted "CAUTION: NONPOTABLE WATER, DO NOT DRINK."
  Source: OPSC 603.5.11 (2026 edition).

  A licensed plumbing contractor, certified rainwater system designer or registered design professional, except irrigation systems storing 360 gallons or less and single-family systems entirely on the building's exterior.
  Source: OPSC 1601.2 (2026 edition, Oregon amendment).

  Uses such as water closets, trap primers, clothes washers, irrigation and water features are approved by the building official, after complete plumbing plans are approved.
  Source: OPSC 1602.1 and 1602.2 (2026 edition).

  No treatment for aboveground irrigation at 360 gallons or less of storage, or for subsurface or drip irrigation.
  Source: OPSC 1601.5 (2026 edition).

  Source: OPSC 1605.3 (2026 edition, Oregon amendment).
```

## 17.20 Outdoor supply, freeze protection and penetrations

**Simple**

Water that freezes in a pipe expands and splits it. Inside concrete, a split pipe cannot be reached to fix. Supply pipes in this house stay inside the heated shell wherever the plan allows. At the well, the pipe leaves the casing below the frost line through a sealed fitting, so nothing stands above ground to freeze. Hose bibs are the frost-free kind, with the valve seat a foot back inside the warm wall. Electric heat tape keeps a pipe warm by drawing power all winter, 250 to 450 watts for 50 feet of the constant-wattage kind. On a battery system, that draw falls in the weeks when the solar array produces least.

**Applied**

1. Route supply piping inside the conditioned envelope wherever the layout allows.
2. Insulate any pipe in an exterior wall or unconditioned space, and air-seal its cavity so outdoor air cannot reach it.
3. Set the pitless adapter and every buried fitting below the frost depth the county building department gives for the parcel's elevation.
4. House the pressure tank and controls in the mechanical room or an insulated pump house with a small thermostatic heater.
5. Pitch any exposed run to a low-point drain valve, and drain it before a hard freeze.
6. Use heat tape only on a short run that cannot be buried, enclosed or drained, and choose the thermostatic self-regulating type.
7. Fit frost-free hose bibs with integral vacuum breakers at exterior walls.
8. Pass each pipe through concrete or masonry in a nonmetallic sleeve with clearance to move.
9. Coordinate every penetration with the structural, waterproofing, air-sealing and radon details before the pour.
10. Run the outdoor trunk to one isolation valve at the branch point, with a reduced-pressure assembly at any equipment branch.
11. Keep every shutoff, drain and heat-tape connection accessible.

**Professional**

Freeze protection combines routing, insulation, air sealing, a heat source and flow. A pipe in a warm room freezes when outdoor air leaks to it through an unsealed sleeve or rim detail, and insulation alone only slows the loss. The pitless adapter passes the service line through the casing below grade. OAR 690-210-0270 governs the adapter's seal, watertightness and venting and sets no depth, so burial below the local frost line is trade practice.

Clackamas County requires footings below 18 inches between 2,500 and 4,000 feet of elevation and below 24 inches above 4,000 feet, and Klamath County uses 24 inches. The county's design criteria set the parcel's figure, and the same depth protects a buried water fitting.

An insulated pump house fails slowly, since a small space holds its temperature for hours without power. Self-regulating heat cable draws about 3 watts a foot at 50°F and 4 to 5 watts near freezing, and constant-wattage cable draws a fixed 5 to 9 watts a foot. Self-draining piping draws no power and leaves the fixture out of service while drained. Waterproofing and the radon interface at the penetrations are in the envelope chapter, and the pipe protection and the freeze provision are specified here. Workshop sinks, wash stations and any point-of-use heater at those loads are detailed with the site-development work, back to the branch point here.

**Code**

```
Freeze-exposed piping is protected.
  No water, soil or waste pipe outside a building, in an attic or crawl space, or in an exterior wall without adequate provision against freezing.
  Source: OPSC 312.6 (2026 edition).

A pitless adapter is sealed, watertight and vented.
  The casing seal covers the pitless interval in unhydrated bentonite, no cement grout in that interval, the adapter and cap watertight, and a pitless unit vented to the atmosphere.
  Source: OAR 690-210-0270.

  Source: OPSC 603.5.7 (2023 edition); ASSE 1019; ASSE 1052.
```

## 17.21 Inspection, commissioning and records

**Simple**

No wall closes over a supply pipe until that pipe has held test pressure. Before anyone moves in, the whole system is run under real use. A leak found before drywall costs a fitting and an hour. Found after the finishes, the same leak adds demolition and refinishing to the same repair. Records are the part a future owner uses most. They show where every shutoff is, the regulator's setting, which filter cartridge fits, and the water test from the day the house was finished.

**Applied**

Before cover, record and photograph:

- the approved rough-plumbing inspection
- the pressure-test gauge reading, with the date
- pipe material, size and the printed designation code
- every valve, manifold port and shutoff location
- the regulator make and setpoint, and the expansion tank's location
- the water heater's make, model and listing
- each mixing valve's make and setpoint
- each backflow device's make, location and test tag
- the filter and treatment models, media and change intervals
- every buried or concealed pipe before it is covered

At commissioning, run the system under real use and record:

1. Flowing pressure under the worst-case simultaneous draw from the sizing check.
2. Hot-water wait at the farthest fixture, in seconds.
3. Delivery temperature at every fixture.
4. Pressure drop across the treatment train.
5. Any hammer with the appliances cycling.
6. Water-heater recovery and the recirculation balance.
7. Treated-water retest, and a coliform re-sample on a new well.

**Professional**

Final acceptance shows a system that holds pressure, is supported, protected from freezing and corrosion, accessible, labeled, and built to the approved plan and its sizing basis. The testing chapter states the supply test's pressure and duration and the whole-house test program. Measured pressures, temperatures, flows and wait times are recorded at commissioning, so a service call years later starts from what the system delivered at handover. Water-quality reports, the well log, the pump data, the treatment design and the permit record stay in the permanent house file.

**Code**

```
Supply piping is inspected before it is concealed.
  Work remains accessible and exposed for inspection until approved, and plumbing rough-in is approved before cover.
  Source: ORSC R109.1 (2026 edition); OPSC 609.4 (2023 edition).

A water heater is inspected before it is concealed.
  A final water heater inspection after the permitted work is installed, and no appliance or part of it covered until inspected and approved.
  Source: OPSC 503.2 (2026 edition).
```
