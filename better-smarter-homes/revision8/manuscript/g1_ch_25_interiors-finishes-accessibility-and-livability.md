## 25.1 Finish tiers and the access rule

**Simple**

Everything inside the concrete shell sorts into three groups by how long it lasts. Concrete, steel and block are meant to outlast everyone who lives here. Doors, cabinets, tile and wood floors last for decades and are replaced with care. Paint, faucets, appliances and light fixtures wear out on a schedule and are bought to be replaced. No finish in any group hides a part that needs service. Every valve, junction box, drain cleanout and shutoff stays reachable behind an access panel or a removable cover.

**Applied**

| Tier | What it includes | Design rule |
|---|---|---|
| Permanent | Concrete, reinforcement, foundation, masonry | No finish covers a defect, and every part stays open to inspection and repair |
| Semi-permanent | Doors, cabinets, tile, stone, hardwood, built-ins | Install well, record the products, keep replacement possible |
| Consumable | Paint, appliances, faucets, some lighting | Buy for the replacement cycle, and do not overbuild |

1. Assign every interior material on the finish schedule to its tier before ordering.
2. Record samples, model numbers, finish names, paint formulas and photographs in the finish record.
3. Keep every valve, junction box, cleanout and shutoff reachable through an access panel or a removable cover.
4. Keep dampers and radiant manifolds reachable the same way.
5. Mark each access point on the drawings before its wall or ceiling closes.

**Professional**

Each finish is chosen for its tolerance of moisture and heat, its resistance to abrasion and cleaning products, and its fit with the fastener and substrate behind it. Four finish errors turn a sound assembly into a failure:

- vapor-closed flooring laid over a damp slab
- tile laid without movement joints
- a wet room without exhaust
- a shower backer the code does not list

Every interior finish is checked against the shell's air barrier, vapor control, thermal mass and radiant floor, and against its access points and fire separations. Adopted code, approved plans and product listings govern each interior assembly, and a decorative choice never overrides a required one.

## 25.2 Room size, ceiling height, light and air

**Simple**

Oregon's code sets a minimum floor area, ceiling height and window area for every room people live in. Habitable means used for living, sleeping, eating or cooking. Each such room needs enough floor to furnish and a ceiling an adult stands under easily. Each also needs windows for daylight, with part of the glass able to open for air. Bathrooms and laundries may have ceilings four inches lower than living rooms. This house's main rooms have 9-foot ceilings, two feet above the code minimum.

**Applied**

1. Make each habitable room other than the kitchen 70 square feet or more, and 7 feet across.
2. Count no floor under a sloped ceiling below 5 feet, or a furred ceiling below 7 feet.
3. Give habitable rooms and hallways ceilings of 7 feet or more.
4. Give bathrooms, toilet rooms and laundries ceilings of 6 feet 8 inches or more.
5. Under a sloped ceiling, keep half the required floor area at 7 feet or more.
6. In a sloped-ceiling bathroom, keep 6 feet 4 inches over 21 by 24 inches at each toilet and lavatory.
7. Keep that height over 24 by 30 inches in front of and inside each tub or shower.
8. Glaze each habitable room at 8 percent of its floor area, 4 percent openable.
9. Use fixed glass only where whole-house mechanical ventilation runs, and never at an escape window.
10. Give every room with a tub, shower or spa an exhaust fan.
11. Note the 9-foot ceiling height of the main living spaces on the drawings.

**Professional**

The area minimum admits furniture, and the dimension minimum keeps a long, narrow room from meeting the area alone. Under a sloped ceiling, the code counts only floor where an adult stands upright, so an attic room or a bathroom under a roof slope loses usable area on the plan. In a bathroom the code trades height over part of the floor for full height where a person stands or sits at each fixture.

Window glass admits daylight in proportion to its area, and the openable part ventilates the room. Mechanical ventilation moves the air that openable glass would, which is why the code excuses the openable share where a whole-house system or a kitchen's exhaust runs. Escape openings stay operable whatever the ventilation design. Window head heights and surface reflectances for daylight depth are set in the Passive and Bioclimatic Design chapter. Because only glass area counts, a compliant room lit by low windows on one wall stays dim at its far side.

**Code**

```
Habitable rooms meet a minimum area and dimension.
  At least 70 square feet and 7 feet in every horizontal dimension, kitchens excepted; floor under a sloped ceiling below 5 feet or a furred ceiling below 7 feet does not count.
  Source: ORSC R304.1, R304.2 and R304.3 (2023 edition).

Ceiling heights meet the room-type minimums.
  Habitable space and hallways 7 feet; bathrooms, toilet rooms and laundry rooms 6 feet 8 inches; sloped ceilings keep half the required floor area at 7 feet and all of it at 5 feet.
  Source: ORSC R305.1 (2023 edition).

A bathroom may have a sloped ceiling over most of its floor.
  Up to 75 percent of the floor below 6 feet 8 inches if 21-by-24-inch areas at toilets and lavatories and 24-by-30-inch areas at tubs and showers keep 6 feet 4 inches.
  Source: ORSC R305.1 Exception 2 (2023 edition).

## 25.3 Escape openings and window fall protection

**Simple**

Every bedroom needs a second way out in a fire. In this house it is a window or an outside door. It is big enough for an adult to climb out and a firefighter in gear to climb in. Its sill is low enough to reach without a chair. It opens by hand, with no key or tool. In a basement bedroom, the window opens into a well with room to climb out, and a deep well gets a ladder. Upstairs, a window with a low sill gets a stop that keeps a small child from falling out.

**Applied**

1. Give every sleeping room, the basement and any habitable attic an escape opening.
2. Give each basement sleeping room its own.
3. Lead each opening to a public way, or to a yard or court that reaches one.
4. Size each opening to 5.7 square feet net clear, or 5 square feet at grade level.
5. Make it 24 inches high and 20 inches wide by normal operation.
6. Take those figures from the maker's data for the exact unit.
7. Set the bottom of the clear opening 44 inches or less above the finished floor.
8. Choose hardware that opens from inside without a key, a tool or special knowledge.
9. Where a door serves as the escape opening, use a side-hinged or sliding door.
10. Give a below-grade opening an area well of 9 square feet, 36 inches in projection and in width.
11. Fix a ladder or steps in any well deeper than 44 inches, clear of the open window.
12. Drain each well to the foundation drain, unless the soil is free-draining Group I soil.
13. Under a deck or porch, keep a path out 36 inches high and 36 inches wide.
14. Above a lower roof, keep a 36-inch-wide path from the opening to the roof's edge.
15. Fit an opening-control device on any upper window with a low sill.
16. On an escape window, choose a control device that releases to the full escape opening.
17. Photograph each escape opening fully open with a tape across it.
18. File the photo with the window schedule.

**Professional**

The net clear area admits a firefighter wearing a breathing apparatus, and the minimum height and width each admit a person crawling out in one orientation. Meeting the area does not by itself meet the height and width. Casement and awning units lose clear width to their hinges and operators, so the net figure comes from the manufacturer's data for the exact unit, never the rough opening. Sill height is capped at what a child or a smoke-blinded adult reaches without climbing on furniture.

Oregon's October 2024 amendment restored the earlier state wording on where the opening leads, dropping the model code's yard-width test and its separate path exception. Oregon does not adopt the model code's residential sprinkler section, so the basement sprinkler exception applies only where a sprinkler system is installed by choice. Each well drains to the footing drain because standing water blocks the opening it serves.

At upstairs bedrooms the window-fall rule meets the escape rule, and a control device that limits the opening must release to the full escape size. The Lifetime Homes Standard, a UK design checklist, calls for living-room sills low enough to see out while seated. Above the ground floor such a sill brings the fall-protection device with it, and a large pane near the floor becomes a safety-glazing location.

## 25.4 The egress door, landings and hallways

**Simple**

One outside door is the required way out, and the code fixes its size and the floor around it. It swings on hinges, opens wide enough for a wheelchair, and needs no key from inside. Every outside door has a landing on each side, deep enough to stand on while the door opens. Inside, the floor sits almost level with the threshold, so nobody trips going out in the dark. Halls have a minimum width under the same rules.

**Applied**

1. Make one exterior door the required egress door: side-hinged, 32 inches clear at 90 degrees, 78 inches high.
2. Use egress-door hardware that needs no key from inside.
3. Route the path to that door from every room without passing through the garage.
4. Build a landing each side of every exterior door, as wide as the door and 36 inches deep.
5. Keep the floor at the egress door within 1-1/2 inches below the threshold top.
6. Drop the exterior landing up to 8 inches only where the door does not swing over it.
7. Keep the landing at every other exterior door within 8 inches below its threshold.
8. Slope exterior landings no more than 1/4 inch per foot.
9. Build hallways 36 inches wide or more, and 42 inches where a wheelchair user is expected.
10. Set the step-free entry's threshold, landing and grade together on the site plan.

**Professional**

The clear width excludes the door's own thickness and the stop, so the slab must be wider than the clear figure by both. A door one stock size wider than the clear figure clears the requirement, and a slab the size of the clear figure itself falls short. Garages are kept off the egress path because a fire there, fed by fuel and a vehicle, would block the way out.

At the egress door, the threshold limit keeps the step too low to catch a foot. Outside, a deeper drop is allowed only where the door swings inward, so nobody steps out onto a drop with the door swinging into them. Steeper landings shed water faster but tilt a person stepping out with a load. Hallways at the code minimum suit a walking adult. A wheelchair turning into a doorway needs the wider hall the accessibility choices set.

**Code**

```
One side-hinged egress door serves the dwelling.
  Clear width 32 inches between the door face and the stop at 90 degrees open, clear height 78 inches, openable from inside without a key or special effort.
  Source: ORSC R311.2 (2023 edition).

The egress path does not pass through the garage.
  A continuous path from every part of the dwelling to the egress door, which opens to a public way or to a yard or court opening to one.
  Source: ORSC R311.1 (2023 edition).

Every exterior door has a landing on each side.
  As wide as the door, 36 inches in the direction of travel, exterior slope not over 1/4 inch per foot.
  Source: ORSC R311.3 (2023 edition).

Landings at the egress door sit close to the threshold.
  Not more than 1-1/2 inches below the top of the threshold, or 8 inches on the exterior side where the door does not swing over the landing.
  Source: ORSC R311.3.1 (2023 edition).

Other exterior doors have landings within 8 inches of the threshold.
  Not more than 8 inches below the top of the threshold; no top landing needed where three or fewer exterior risers lie outside a door that does not swing over them.
  Source: ORSC R311.3.2 (2023 edition).

Hallways have a minimum width.
  3 feet.
  Source: ORSC R311.6 (2023 edition).

## 25.5 Stairs, handrails and guards

**Simple**

Stairs cause falls when one step differs from the next, so the code limits how much the steps in a flight may vary. It also caps how tall each step is and how shallow its tread. Every stair of four steps or more gets a handrail. Any floor edge more than two and a half feet up gets a guard. Its gaps are too narrow for a small child's head. This house's stairs are drawn gentler than Oregon's limits, with lower steps and deeper treads. Under a stair, an enclosed closet is lined to slow a fire.

**Applied**

1. Draw the stairs with risers of 7-3/4 inches or less and treads of 10 inches or more.
2. Hold every riser and every tread in a flight within 3/8 inch of the others.
3. Measure risers and treads on finished surfaces, allowing for tile or wood treads at the landings.
4. Where a tread is under 10 inches deep, give its nosing a 3/4- to 1-1/4-inch projection.
5. Keep 36 inches clear above the handrail, and 31-1/2 inches below it with one rail.
6. Keep 6 feet 8 inches of headroom above the sloped line of the nosings.
7. Land each flight top and bottom on a floor or landing as wide as the stair.
8. Make each landing on a straight run 36 inches deep.
9. Run a handrail on at least one side of every flight with four or more risers.
10. Set handrails at 34 to 38 inches above the nosings.
11. Use a round rail 1-1/4 to 2 inches across, or an equivalent graspable profile.
12. Hold the rail 1-1/2 inches off the wall, continuous from the top riser to the bottom riser.
13. Guard every walking surface more than 30 inches above the floor or grade below.
14. Make guards 36 inches high, and 34 inches on the open side of a stair.
15. Space guard infill so a 4-inch ball cannot pass.
16. On the open side of a stair, above the second riser, allow up to 5 inches, and 6 at the stair triangle.
17. Light every stair at its treads and landings, with a switch at each floor for six or more risers.
18. Line an enclosed under-stair closet with 1/2-inch gypsum on the walls, the stair underside and any soffit.
19. Log the measured risers and treads of each finished flight.

**Professional**

Oregon's stair text departs from the national model code in three places: a taller riser limit, a shallower tread minimum and a lower start to the handrail range. This house's design values sit inside Oregon's limits and match the model code, which suits the older users the accessibility choices plan for. Uniformity is checked on finished surfaces because a thick tile or wood tread on one landing changes the first and last risers. That allowance is drawn into the rough stair before it is cast or framed.

Nosing projection gives a shallow tread more usable depth for the foot and is not needed once treads are deep. Guards on the open side of a stair are measured from the nosing line, so they run lower than a balcony guard. Their openings stop a small child's head. Under-stair lining keeps a fire in an enclosed closet from burning into the stair that is the way down.

## 25.6 Garage-to-house separation

**Simple**

An attached garage holds a running engine, fuel and stored chemicals. Its shared wall and ceiling with the house are therefore built as a barrier against fumes and fire. Gypsum board on the garage side makes the barrier, thicker and fire-rated under any room above. Only a solid or fire-rated door crosses it, and no garage door opens into a bedroom. Pipes and wires through it are sealed, and any duct through it is steel. If a garage comes later, it gets this barrier when it is built and connected.

**Applied**

1. Cover the garage side of every shared wall and ceiling with 1/2-inch gypsum board.
2. Under a habitable room above the garage, use 5/8-inch Type X board.
3. Fasten that Type X perpendicular to framing at 24 inches or less, nails or screws 6 inches apart.
4. Cover the walls and structure that support the floor above with 1/2-inch gypsum.
5. Hang a solid wood door, a solid or honeycomb-core steel door 1-3/8 inches thick, or a 20-minute rated door.
6. Fit that door with a closer and a latch so it shuts behind every trip.
7. Open no garage door directly into a bedroom.
8. Run any duct through the separation in 26-gauge steel, with no opening into the garage.
9. Keep any vibration isolator in that duct 18 inches or more from the wall.
10. Fireblock every pipe and wire penetration through the separation.
11. Where the separation wall is block or concrete, ask the building official to accept it in place of the gypsum.
12. Photograph the board and its fastening before the garage is finished.
13. For a garage planned for later, mark the separation on the drawings as pending.

**Professional**

Gypsum on the garage side is prescribed protection rather than a rated assembly. The board's type and thickness are the whole requirement, and no fire test is cited. Type X board carries glass fiber in its gypsum core, so it holds together longer in a fire than regular board, which is why it goes where a room sits above.

Oregon's text leaves out the model code's self-closing device on the garage door. Its sprinkler exception reaches the door alone, so wall and ceiling protection stays in a sprinklered house. Fire and fumes are slowed only while the door is shut, which is why this house adds the closer Oregon does not require. Walls perpendicular to the house wall need no protection. Ducts are steel because a plastic or flexible duct would open a path through the barrier when it melts. A future garage is drawn into the plan without its door and board, and the assembly is completed when the connection is made.

**Code**

```
The garage is separated from the house by gypsum board.
  1/2-inch gypsum or equivalent on the garage side; 5/8-inch Type X under habitable rooms above; 1/2-inch on walls and structure supporting the separation floor.
  Source: ORSC R302.6 and Table R302.6 (2023 edition).

Type X under rooms above the garage is fastened closely.
  Perpendicular to framing at 24 inches maximum, with nails or screws at 6 inches on center.
  Source: ORSC Table R702.3.5 (2023 edition).

  Openings from a private garage directly into a room used for sleeping are not permitted.
  Source: ORSC R302.5.1 (2023 edition).

  Solid wood, or solid or honeycomb-core steel, 1-3/8 inches thick, or 20-minute rated; waived where garage and dwelling both have NFPA 13D sprinklers.
  Source: ORSC R302.5.1.1 and its exception (2023 edition, Oregon text).

  No. 26 gage sheet steel or approved material, no openings into the garage, any vibration isolator 18 inches or more from the penetration.
  Source: ORSC R302.5.2 (2023 edition).

  Protected as R302.11 item 4 requires.
  Source: ORSC R302.5.3 (2023 edition).

## 25.7 Accessibility: what applies and what this house adopts

**Simple**

Wide doors and level floors help far more people than wheelchair users. Sprained ankles, walkers after surgery, loads of groceries and new sofas all pass through the same doors. No federal accessibility law applies to a private house a family builds for itself, so these features are this house's own choice. Most cost almost nothing at design time. Doors 36 inches wide cost about what 30-inch doors cost. The same features added after the floors and walls are finished start with tearing out finished work.

**Applied**

| Tier | Features | When it is decided |
|---|---|---|
| Built in every time | The adopted list below | At design, for every plan |
| Weighed against the site and plan | Single-floor living, the elevator shaft, a curbless main entry | With the floor plan |
| Built when a specific need exists | Full roll-in bathrooms, 42-inch halls | When a household member needs them |

The adopted list:

- One step-free entry route into the house.
- 36-inch doors on the ground floor wherever the layout allows.
- Low thresholds, and a ground-floor full or half bath with room to turn.
- A ground-floor bedroom beside that bathroom.
- Grab-bar backing at every toilet, tub and shower, placed on the fastening map.
- A curbless or low-threshold shower with a built-in bench and a handheld shower on a slide bar.
- Comfort-height toilets, lever handles throughout and rocker switches.
- Switches, outlets and controls within reach, with receptacles 18 inches above the floor rather than 12.
- Lighting at entries, stairs, halls, bathrooms and paths sized for older eyes.
- Stacked closets aligned floor to floor, 5 by 5 feet, for a future shaftless elevator.
- A living-room window with a sill a seated person can see over.

1. Check each switch, thermostat and outlet height on the electrical plan against a reach range of 15 to 48 inches.
2. Before the floor plan is fixed, decide whether to buy a plan review from a Certified Aging-in-Place Specialist.
3. Ask that reviewer for a written list of what the review checks, and whether the fee is flat or hourly.
4. Ask occupational-therapy practices whether they review designs before construction, and at what private fee.
5. List any monitoring, fall detection or emergency calling the household wants, for the smart-home design.

**Professional**

### What the law reaches

The Americans with Disabilities Act reaches public accommodations (28 CFR part 36) and state and local government facilities (28 CFR part 35), and a privately owned single-family home is neither. Fair Housing Act design requirements apply to covered multifamily dwellings in buildings of four or more units (24 CFR 100.205), so a detached house is outside them. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act (29 U.S.C. 794) applies only to federally funded housing.

ICC A117.1 is the technical accessibility standard, referenced by the current codes in its 2017 edition, with a 2024 edition published. For this house it is a voluntary reference. Its dimensions are applied by choice and invoke none of the federal regimes, provided no federal or state housing money flows through the project. Oregon has no statewide visitability mandate for a private house, so the site's own jurisdiction is checked for an incentive program.

### The frameworks behind the adopted list

Visitability, as Concrete Change defines it, asks for a step-free entrance, 32-inch clear doors on the ground floor and a ground-floor bathroom with room to maneuver. The City of Redmond's visitability guidance, built on Concrete Change's definition, lists grab-bar backing and reachable controls as optional extras beyond those three. This house's list already includes both.

The UK Lifetime Homes Standard sets 16 criteria. The adopted list covers its criteria for entrances, doors and halls, and the entrance-level bedroom and bathroom. It also covers bathroom wall reinforcement, a future lift and reachable controls. It adds criterion 15, a window sill a seated person can see over. Criteria 1 to 3, parking width, the route from parking and the approach slope, belong to the site plan.

Japanese barrier-free housing guidance places the bathroom directly beside the bedroom to shorten the night trip, the source of the bedroom-beside-bathroom item. It also sets doors at 800 millimeters minimum (31-1/2 inches) and 900 recommended (35-1/2 inches), corridors of 80 to 90 centimeters, floors with no level changes, and handrails in halls and bathrooms as standard. Those widths sit close to A117.1's, which governs single rooms and fixtures and says nothing about which rooms adjoin.

A117.1-2017, as summarized by Steven Winter Associates and MeltPlan, sets a 30-by-48-inch clear floor space for one wheelchair, lengthened to 52 inches in some approaches. Its unobstructed forward reach runs from 15 to 48 inches. Accessible routes are 36 inches wide and may narrow to 32 inches for no more than 24 inches of length.

### Reviews, suites and monitoring

A Certified Aging-in-Place Specialist holds a credential from the National Association of Home Builders, earned through its CAPS coursework. A consultation with a written assessment runs $200 to $500 (LatestCost). The value is a reviewer who has seen many plans and catches interactions, such as a door swing that meets every rule and still blocks a fixture.

An occupational therapist assesses a person instead of a plan: transfers in and out of bed, chair, shower and toilet, gait and balance, and reach and grip. Medicare Part B covers a home-safety assessment after a need arises, with a physician's referral and an in-network provider. Some Medicare Advantage plans carry a home-modification benefit of $500 to $2,500 a year.

An accessory suite for a caregiver or a guest costs less built into the shell than added later. Attached accessory dwellings run $150,000 to $300,000 and garage conversions $80,000 to $150,000 (HomeGuide and Angi). The gap comes from reusing a foundation, walls and roof, and a suite designed into the new shell shares all three.

Fall detection, remote monitoring and voice-activated emergency calling are wants this house states. The smart-home design chooses the hardware to its local-first, privacy-preserving standard, which rules out a cloud-dependent consumer product whatever it offers.

## 25.8 Accessible entries, doors and lifts

**Simple**

Anyone can enter a house with no step at its door, on wheels or on foot. On a slab house the step-free entry comes from grading. Outside, the ground is raised to meet the floor, so no ramp is needed. Over the door, a roof keeps rain off the threshold, and the paving falls away from it. Inside, wide doors and lever handles let a person with weak hands or a walker pass. Stacked closets, one above the other on each floor, keep room for an elevator later.

**Applied**

1. Grade the site so finished paving meets the finished floor at one entry.
2. Fix that elevation on the site plan before the slab is formed.
3. Cover the entry with a roof or porch, and slope the paving away from the threshold.
4. Add a drain channel across the paving at the door.
5. Where the entry cannot meet grade, notch the foundation at the door or build a short ramp.
6. Review the step-free entry and every curbless shower recess on the same foundation drawing pass.
7. Fix the entry's opening width, floor elevation and threshold before its masonry surround is designed.
8. Write every door width into the door schedule by size, such as 2'10" or 3'0", never as "standard".
9. Order those doors from a wholesale door supplier.
10. Where a bathroom is tight, use a pocket door before narrowing a swinging door.
11. Use lever handles or U-shaped pulls on every door, and no round knobs.
12. Where a sliding door serves, choose a pocket door with an accessible pull over a barn door.
13. Give any powered door opener a manual fallback for a power loss.
14. Keep a powered opener off the required egress door unless that door still opens by hand.
15. Stack a 5-by-5-foot closet on each floor in line, for a future shaftless or through-the-floor elevator.
16. Set switches, thermostats and controls between 15 and 48 inches above the floor.
17. Set receptacles 18 inches above the floor.

**Professional**

On a slab-on-grade house, the step-free entrance is made by grading and siting so the exterior finished grade meets the floor at the door. Concrete Change's cost guide (Eleanor Smith, 2012) puts the average added cost near $100 a house when it is planned into lot grading. Every lot is graded anyway, and compacted fill can replace some concrete. Where the entry sits above grade, the same guide gives a notched foundation, used on thousands of basement houses in Bolingbrook, Illinois, at about $500. A short ramp is the other route, credited with the cost of the omitted steps.

A threshold with no dam relies on the roof over it and the fall of the paving for weather protection. The site plan sizes both from the door's orientation and rain exposure. The curbless shower is a different detail, because it needs a recess cast into the slab and the step-free entry has none. Both are fixed before the concrete is placed, so both appear on one foundation drawing review. The entry's masonry surround is composed around the fixed opening, and the Aesthetics and Architectural Expression chapter sets its proportions and accent materials.

Door widths shrink at two points in a project, in design and at purchase. In a tight bathroom, inches get shaved from an adjoining room, and at purchase, a retail-stock door replaces the scheduled one. Retail home centers do not stock 2'10" doors, so the schedule names each width and the order goes to a wholesale supplier. Concrete Change's guide recommends a pocket door in place of a narrower swinging door in the tight bathroom.

Lever handles and U-shaped pulls work with a loose grip or a closed fist, and a round knob needs a pinch and a twist. Pocket doors glide lighter, and barn doors hang heavier and take more effort to close flush (RealCraft). A swinging door makes a wheelchair or walker user back around its arc, the largest usability problem a sliding door removes. A powered opener stops with the power, which is why it stays off a door that must open by hand for egress.

HomeGuide puts shaftless home elevators at $19,000 to $28,000, averaging $22,000. Lifeway Mobility's figures run $40,000 to $75,000, and product type and installation explain the spread. Through-the-floor units run $30,000 to $60,000 or more in HomeGuide's data. Pneumatic vacuum elevators run $35,000 to $100,000 or more. A limited-use, limited-application (LULA) elevator is built for low-rise commercial buildings, and Cibes Symmetry's buying guide names a shaftless or inline-gear unit or a wheelchair lift as the cheaper residential fit. The stacked closets are therefore sized for a shaftless or through-the-floor unit.

## 25.9 Kitchens: layout, cabinets and counters

**Simple**

Kitchens are planned backward from the work done in them. Cooking, cleaning, prep and storage each get a zone with counter beside it. Appliances, sink, hood and outlets are chosen before the cabinets are ordered, because the cabinets are built around them. In this house the wall behind the upper cabinets is concrete or block. Every outlet and every heavy cabinet on it is therefore placed before that wall is built. Stone counters are heavy enough to need a plywood base and cabinets anchored to the floor.

**Applied**

1. Plan the kitchen in zones for cooking, cleaning, food storage, prep and serving.
2. Freeze the appliance models, sink location, hood route, plumbing rough-in and floor thickness before ordering cabinets.
3. Fix every kitchen receptacle, switch, under-cabinet light feed and low-voltage path before the masonry wall behind it is built.
4. Cast a conduit stub through the slab for an island receptacle, or install a listed countertop receptacle assembly.
5. Leave 24 inches of counter on each side of the range and the sink, and 18 inches at the refrigerator's handle side.
6. Hang upper cabinets from a rail screwed to embeds or anchored into the masonry, never from furring strips alone.
7. Set the rail's masonry screws 12 inches apart, biting 1 inch or more into the block.
8. Support a stone or quartz counter on continuous 3/4-inch plywood over the base cabinets.
9. Anchor the base cabinets to the floor.
10. Bracket any counter overhang beyond 6 inches to the fabricator's specification.
11. Choose a cooktop with front or side controls, induction where the household's cooking allows.
12. Fit the sink base with removable doors and panels, so knee space can open later.
13. For a permanent roll-under sink, leave 29 inches of clear height under it and set the plumbing to suit.
14. Decide the canning scale before the kitchen circuits and exhaust are fixed.

**Professional**

The residential code sets no criteria for cabinet attachment, so manufacturer and trade practice govern it. On masonry, a furring strip holds only as much as its own fastening back to the wall, and WOODWEB's cabinet guidance rejects furring alone under a run of uppers. A ledger rail fastened with masonry screws at 12- to 16-inch spacing, closing to 12 under heavy cabinets, carries the load instead. Construction adhesive supplements the screws and never replaces them, because its shear strength falls short of a concrete screw's.

A stone or quartz counter's load runs down through the cabinet box, its substrate and its floor anchorage, so a mass wall behind the run gives it no added support. The one change at a masonry wall is the rear cleat, which takes a masonry anchor instead of a wood screw. Shelving is designed for 30 pounds a square foot, and 50 for books.

Installed costs run $80 to $150 a square foot for quartz, which is nonporous and needs no sealing but tolerates heat poorly. Granite runs $60 to $150, tolerates heat and is resealed every 1 to 3 years. Marble runs $75 to $200 and up, stains and etches, and suits a baking zone better than a whole kitchen. Butcher block serves an island top with regular oiling.

Open shelving exposes dishes and the wall behind them to grease and dust, so both are cleaned more often than a closed cabinet's contents. Shelves hang on rated brackets and closed uppers on a ledger, and both go into the same masonry anchors at different spacings.

Universal Design Project's kitchen specifications separate the features that cost nothing at the build from those that add cost. Front or side cooktop controls, induction (which cuts burn risk) and removable base panels that open into knee space cost nothing extra at the build. A height-adjustable counter section spanning 30 to 42 inches is specialty hardware at real added cost. A permanently open roll-under sink changes the base cabinet, the plumbing height and the storage under it, and a rolling cart is the budget substitute.

In a wood-framed house a misplaced kitchen box costs a drywall patch. Against concrete or block it means coring or chasing masonry, with the risk of striking a bar, a conduit or radiant tubing. The kitchen rough-in therefore freezes before the wall is built, earlier than the cabinet order. The branch-circuit chapter sets the kitchen receptacle and protection rules. The 2023 electrical code made the island or peninsula receptacle optional, required a provision for future installation where none is installed, and ended credit for receptacles below the counter. 
## 25.10 Bathrooms: clearances, hot water and seated use

**Simple**

Hot water stored at 140°F burns skin in seconds. Each tub and shower valve therefore holds what comes out to 120°F. Storage at a higher temperature is still allowed, because the limit sits at the fixture. Each fixture also needs room around it, to stand at the sink, sit at the toilet and step into the shower. Showers big enough for a seat and a handheld spray serve a person who cannot stand for long.

**Applied**

1. Center each toilet and bidet 15 inches or more from a side wall or fixture, and 30 inches from another.
2. Leave 24 inches of clear floor in front of each toilet, lavatory and bidet.
3. Size each shower to 1,024 square inches or more, able to hold a 30-inch circle (32 by 32 inches when square).
4. Leave 24 inches of clear floor in front of each shower entry.
5. Hang each shower door to open to a 22-inch clear width.
6. Leave 21 inches of clear floor along the open side of each tub.
7. Install a pressure-balance, thermostatic or combination valve at each shower and tub-shower.
8. Set each valve's limit stop so the outlet reads 120°F on a thermometer.
9. Log each set point, the date and the measured temperature.
10. Limit tub filler water to 120°F with a limiting device where the valve does not already do it.
11. Fit each bidet with its own limiting device, set to 110°F.
12. Use showerheads rated 1.8 gallons per minute or less.
13. Exhaust each bathroom outdoors at 50 cfm intermittent or 20 continuous, and 80 to 100 cfm with a separate soaking tub.
14. Control each bath fan with a humidity sensor or a timer.
15. Specify comfort-height toilets with 17- to 19-inch seats, and a slide-bar handheld shower.
16. In the accessible bathroom, build a roll-in shower 30 by 60 inches, or 36 by 60.
17. Leave 30 by 60 inches clear at its open face.
18. Frame the roll-in shower's opening 33 inches rough for 32 inches clear.
19. Size the accessible vanity 36 inches or wider per sink, 42 to 48 preferred, with knee space.
20. Hang a tall or tilting mirror that serves seated and standing users.
21. For a composting toilet, reserve the footprint, vent route and service access the chosen model needs.

**Professional**

Fixture spacing comes from two codes at once. The residential code's clearance figure and the plumbing code both apply, and the plumbing code's front clearance is the larger and governs. The model code's figure draws 21 inches of clear floor along a tub's open side. Oregon's version of the figure sends shower clearances to the plumbing code, which sets the shower's area, the circle it must hold and its door opening.

A pressure-balance valve holds the hot-cold mix when pressure drops on one side, as when a toilet flushes. A thermostatic valve holds the outlet temperature itself, and the limit stop caps the handle's hot travel. The limit therefore lives in each valve or in a listed limiting device. An ASSE 1084 water heater limits its own outlet temperature, which is why the 2021 UPC base accepts it in place of a point-of-use device at showers, tubs and bidets.

The plumbing code's lavatory limit covers public lavatories and does not reach a private one. The water-supply chapter details the ASSE 1017 master mixing valve that delivers water stored at 140°F against Legionella at a lower temperature, and the HVAC chapter sizes the bath exhaust.

Roll-in shower dimensions follow the ADA-family figures EZ Able and UpCodes summarize. A clear interior 5 feet long and 30 or 36 inches wide opens onto a clear floor of the same 5-foot length at its open face, which is the usable floor with the curtain open. Showers4less and Pritch Remodeling give 36 inches per sink as the minimum vanity width for seated and standing users, and 42 to 48 as the aging-in-place target.

Composting toilets come in two kinds, self-contained and central, and the Water Independence and DWV designs make the choice between them. Sun-Mar's self-contained models run $1,700 to $1,900, with higher-capacity units up to three times that, and suit smaller or part-time households. Central systems pipe a normal-looking fixture to a larger reactor elsewhere in the building. They run $1,400 to more than $10,000, are emptied about once a year, and suit full-time use by a larger household.

## 25.11 Storage, pantry, mudroom and laundry

**Simple**

Storage in this house is planned space, sized to what it holds. Pantries holding months of food need deep, strong shelves and a way to use the oldest cans first. Mudrooms catch wet boots and coats at the door, so their floors tolerate water. Laundries need a drain, a vent and a pan under the washer. Concrete walls have no studs, so shelves hang from anchor points planned before the wall was built.

**Applied**

1. Size the pantry from a food-storage calculator's output for the household and the months planned.
2. Convert that food list into shelf footprint for the containers you will use, then add aisle space.
3. Design pantry shelves for 50 pounds a square foot of shelf, and other shelving for 30.
4. Use gravity-fed can racks and labeled zone shelves so the oldest stock goes first.
5. Mount wire track shelving at its heavy-duty rating, with standards 16 inches apart or closer.
6. Replace the track's stud screws with masonry anchors sized to each fastener's share of the load.
7. Give each storage area lighting, receptacles where used, and a floor that tolerates water.
8. Exhaust any storage space where moisture gathers.
9. In closets, mount enclosed surface LED or incandescent fixtures 12 inches from storage, and recessed or fluorescent fixtures 6 inches.
10. Where shelves come closer, use fixtures listed for the storage space, and never pendants or bare lamps.
11. Give the laundry a standpipe and trap, a wall-switched light, and a pan with a leak shutoff under the washer.
12. Vent the dryer outdoors, or use a condensing heat-pump dryer.
13. Restrain tall shelving and racks at the top with straps and seismic-rated masonry anchors.
14. Decide where canning happens, in the main kitchen, a separate canning kitchen or an outbuilding, before the circuits are fixed.

**Professional**

Built-in storage reduces the furniture a room needs and keeps circulation clear. The pantry, mudroom and laundry are planned before the final trade and cabinet decisions, because they need water, drains, exhaust or special doors. A mudroom's charging shelf needs its receptacle drawn on the same plan.

Food-storage calculators derived from the LDS calculator size a multi-month supply by the weight and volume of staples per person (The Survival Mom, Build a Stash). None converts that into a room. The household makes the conversion from three inputs: the calculator's output, the containers planned (number-ten cans, five-gallon buckets or commercial shelving), and aisle space. The pantry uses the bookshelf load because canned food and books are both dense loads spread evenly over a shelf.

ClosetMaid rates its wire shelving at 100 pounds per linear foot with standards no more than 16 inches apart, with heavier configurations to 600 pounds per 6-foot shelf and 1,000 pounds when braced to the maker's pattern. Rubbermaid rates FastTrack at 350 pounds per 48-inch shelf. Both systems are designed around drywall over wood studs, and Rubbermaid tells owners of block or brick walls to source their own concrete anchors. Canned goods call for the heavy-duty end of the line and closer, stronger anchors, and a clothes closet works at the base rating. A freestanding unit is the simplest choice wherever floor space allows.

Closet lighting follows NEC 410.16 because a fixture too close to stored goods is a fire risk, and the 2023 electrical code added laundry areas to the rooms that need a wall-switched lighting outlet. The branch-circuit chapter states both rules.

Tall shelving and racks need a top restraint in a seismic zone even when they stand on the floor. BHB Engineers' guidance on bracing nonstructural components calls for anchors prequalified for seismic use in cracked concrete, and an engineer for anything beyond a simple strap. A water heater strap fastened to concrete uses 1/4-inch expansion bolts in place of lag screws, per the California Seismic Safety Commission's water heater bracing guidelines. Cabinet latches sold for earthquakes carry no seismic rating standard. The house-wide bracing checklist belongs to Resilience and Disaster Independence.

Canning at scale puts a sustained heat and steam load on a room, and the siting of a canning kitchen belongs to the outbuilding plan in Developing the Site. The room gets its own ventilation and finishes apart from the main kitchen's. Used a few weeks a year, it is a poor use of year-round conditioned space.

## 25.12 Fastening points planned before the walls close

**Simple**

In a wood house, a cabinet screws into any stud. Concrete and block walls have none. Every heavy thing that will hang on them needs an anchor point planned before the wall is built. Wood strips, plywood pads or steel channels go into the concrete or block as the wall rises. Later, the screw goes into wood or steel instead of a hole drilled in masonry. Each anchor point is photographed and measured before it is covered, and the photos map the wall.

**Applied**

| Embed | Set when | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Pressure-treated wood nailer | Tied into the forms before the pour, face flush with the finished wall | Cabinet rails, shelving, trim |
| Plywood insert | Braced in the forms before the pour, sized to the fixture | One fixture at a known point |
| Steel plate | Anchored in the forms before the pour | Heavy bolted fixtures |
| Strut channel (Unistrut, B-Line) | Cast in, or set in a grouted block cell, slotted face flush or just proud | Shelving and racks that may move later |
| Solid-grouted block cells | Grouted during laying, from a marked coursing diagram | Anchor zones in block walls |

1. List each predictable heavy or repeated load before the structural drawings are final.
2. Include upper cabinets, grab bars, a wall-mounted TV, closet and pantry systems, and any wall-hung toilet or lavatory.
3. Mark each load's embed or grouted-cell zone on the wall elevations, with heights.
4. Run the grab-bar zone 33 to 36 inches above the floor at each toilet, tub and shower, the full width of the fixture.
5. Leave light-duty walls, most bedroom walls and halls, to post-installed anchors.
6. Give the mason a coursing diagram of the cells to grout for fastening, separate from the structural grouting.
7. Have the engineer of record approve every extra grouted cell and every cell left open for a niche.
8. Run small horizontal wiring through the web gaps on courses without bond-beam steel.
9. Run vertical wiring and pipe in cells without vertical bars, and mark each cell on the map.
10. Route larger plumbing up ungrouted cores near baths and kitchens.
11. Form each cast-in niche with a braced blockout, and run its lighting conduit into the same blockout.
12. Run a continuous conduit or raceway path in the formwork or coursing for each cove light.
13. Place under-cabinet light drivers and their feeds with the cabinet layout, before the wall is built.
14. Before a wall is furred or finished, photograph it face by face with a tape measure in the frame.
15. Log each embed, grouted cell, conduit, bar and radiant loop in a spreadsheet by wall and coordinate.
16. Record each block wall's coursing module and course heights.
17. File the photographs in one folder per wall.

[FIGURE: fastening-map-wall, a wall elevation with embeds, grouted cells, conduit runs and a radiant loop marked against the coursing grid, beside its spreadsheet rows]

**Professional**

Interior fastening on concrete and block takes one of two routes. Post-installed anchors (wedge, sleeve, screw and adhesive types) go in after the wall exists, sized from the maker's load tables. Planned embeds go in while the wall is built, so a later screw lands in wood or steel. Heavy or repeated loads justify an embed, and light or one-off loads take an anchor.

An anchor-only house spends nothing on planning and pays at every fixture, years later, in tools, hardware and drilling labor. An embed costs design and coordination up front and leaves ordinary fasteners for the day the fixture goes up. This house uses both, with embeds at the few predictable heavy points and anchors everywhere else.

A stud finder locates steel but cannot tell a grouted cell from an open one or find a plain web (Eng-Tips forum discussion of existing CMU). Infrared thermography distinguishes grouted cells, which heat and cool at a different rate from hollow ones, when a thermographer with the right camera works at the right time of day (The Snell Group). That is a hired service. On walls built under the fastening map no detection is needed, and on an unmapped wall a small pilot hole at the web position calculated from the block module settles the question.

The fastening map is this project's own record, built from what later drilling needs to know. Photographs with a tape in the frame capture everything visible at that moment, so they survive gaps in the written log. A spreadsheet ties each item to a wall and a coordinate, and paper plans marked by hand go missing over a multi-year build.

Cells grouted for fastening are added to the cells the structural design grouts, and only those. Grouting the whole wall adds cost and weight, and leaving a fastening zone hollow puts an anchor into an open cell. Standard block leaves about a 1-inch gap between webs at each course joint, room for wiring and small pipe on a course without bond-beam steel (Frame Frog, CountBricks). Vertical runs use cells without vertical bars and shift sideways within 4 feet before changing cells. A run inside a mapped open cell can be found but not removed without opening the wall, and a removable run needs a surface or furred chase.

Cast-in furniture and the oversized service chase are in the What Concrete Uniquely Enables chapter, and structural embeds, their pre-pour checklist and open-cell niches are in the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter. A cast niche is a braced blockout built into the wall forms and stripped after the pour. Homewyse puts wall formwork at $4.40 to $5.30 per square foot for labor and $4 to $6 for materials, or $2 to $3 to rent forms, and a niche adds to forms already standing. Under-cabinet, in-niche and cove lighting each depend on a formed feature or a wall that closes early, so their conduit and driver locations are drawn into the forms and coursing.

## 25.13 Floors on the radiant slab: choosing the finish

**Simple**

Every floor here sits on a heated slab, so the finish has to let heat through. Polished concrete passes the most, because the slab itself is the floor. Tile and stone pass heat well. Engineered wood and cork work when their makers rate them for radiant heat. Thick carpet blocks about half the heat and is left out of this house. Polishing grinds the top of the slab, so the heating tubes sit deep enough to stay clear of the grinder.

**Applied**

| Rooms | Floor |
|---|---|
| Living areas, kitchen, halls, basement | Polished or stained concrete |
| Wet areas, entry, laundry | Large-format porcelain tile |
| Bedrooms, offices | Engineered wood or cork |

1. Keep cork and floating floors out of the rooms the summer night flush depends on.
2. Confirm each finish carries its maker's radiant-floor rating before ordering.
3. Hold each room's permanent finish at R-1.0 or less.
4. Before the pour, reconcile the tubing maker's minimum cover with the polisher's grind depth on the shop drawings.
5. Seal polished concrete with a penetrating, non-film sealer, and plan a reseal every 3 to 5 years.
6. Choose a matte or honed finish where a floor faces bright windows.
7. Bond tile over the heated slab through a crack-isolation membrane rated to ANSI A118.12.
8. Keep tile surfaces at 85°F or below, and bring the system up no faster than 5°F an hour.
9. Float engineered wood over a radiant-rated underlayment, with no nails into the topping slab.
10. Install cork floating, or glue it only over at least 1 inch of concrete above the tubing.
11. Change floor temperature under cork by no more than 9°F a day, and keep its surface at 85°F or below.
12. Use felt rug pads, or felt-and-rubber pads sold for radiant floors.
13. Keep plastic, nylon, polypropylene and synthetic-rubber pads off the radiant floor.
14. Keep each rug and its pad together under 2.5 tog, about R-1.4.
15. Record each room's finish and R-value for the HVAC designer's loop zoning.
16. Get a written radiant rating from Forbo, Nora or Artigo before choosing linoleum or rubber.
17. Request each resilient product's slip rating, under whatever standard its maker tests to.

**Professional**

Polished concrete keeps the radiant slab coupled to the room, traps no moisture under a second floor, and is refinished in place instead of replaced. Each polishing pass removes 1/16 to 1/8 inch, and a polish takes several passes. The tubing depth set in the radiant design and the planned grind depth are reconciled from the tubing maker's minimum cover and the contractor's cut, before the slab is poured. A diamond-ground polish needs a walk-behind planetary grinder, and an exposed-aggregate wash needs only a pressure washer and hand tools. Slab joint timing and spacing belong to the foundation chapter, and the grinder's rent-or-hire decision to the tools chapter.

Tile and stone suit thermal mass with proper substrate preparation, crack isolation and movement joints. The TCNA Handbook's radiant methods F124, F125, F176 and F189 cap the tile surface temperature and the warm-up rate, and the crack-isolation membrane keeps slab movement from telegraphing through the bond. Through-body porcelain in large formats reduces grout exposure, and epoxy grout in wet areas removes the sealing cycle. Marble and some other stones are heated and cooled slowly because sudden changes can crack them.

Engineered wood is limited to about 80°F at the surface with its maker's certification, and solid hardwood moves too much and is left out. Cork makers iCork Floor and CanCork cap its surface temperature and its daily change in temperature. Warmboard's cork guide requires an inch of concrete over the tubing for glue-down cork, because the adhesive softens when a thin cover overheats. Vinyl products are capped near 85°F, and their plasticizers degrade at sustained higher temperatures. Forbo's Marmoleum data sheets give fire ratings, including a critical radiant flux, which measures resistance to a fire's radiant heat and says nothing about an embedded heating system.

Each added R-1 of finish cuts a radiant floor's output by about a tenth, and carpet cuts it by about half. Makers' caps on total covering run from R-0.5 to R-4.0, depending on the system, and the HVAC design plans on R-2.0 to R-2.5. This house holds its permanent finishes to R-1.0 or less, which leaves room for rugs within any cap.

RugPadUSA rates felt pads safe on radiant floors because heat-pressing leaves no adhesive to melt, and warns that plastic, nylon, polypropylene and synthetic-rubber pads soften or stick. Natural rubber can turn tacky under sustained warmth, so a felt pad, or a felt-and-rubber pad sold for radiant floors, is the safer choice. Rug Pad Corner keeps rug and pad together under 2.5 tog. One tog is 0.1 square meter-kelvin per watt, so the limit is about R-1.4 in US units.

A finish with more R-value lowers surface temperature and output at the same supply-water temperature. Two rooms with different finishes on one loop therefore leave the covered room underheated or the bare one overdriven, and the HVAC designer splits loops and zones at those transitions from the finish schedule.

Cork lasts 15 to 20 years, or 40 and more with care, at $5 to $10 per square foot for material plus $2 to $3 installed floating or $6 to $10 glued (Portuguese Treasures, HomeAdvisor). It takes a water-based polyurethane recoat every 3 to 10 years. Linoleum lasts 25 to 40 years, at an installed price of $2 to $14 that the source shares with vinyl. Rubber lasts 15 to 30 years with little upkeep.

The Tile Council of North America applies the ANSI A326.3 wet test to polished concrete as well as tile, against the same wet-floor minimum. Cork, rubber and linoleum makers test slip resistance to other standards. A polished floor reflects a bright window's image toward the eye, and a matte or honed finish scatters the same light.

## 25.14 Showers and wet areas

**Simple**

Water passes through tile and grout, so the layer that keeps a shower dry is a membrane hidden behind them. Each shower is built as a waterproof basin first and tiled second. Its floor slopes to the drain, and the membrane turns up the walls. Without a curb, a shower needs its basin recessed into the slab when the slab is poured. Soft joints at the corners let the tile move without cracking. Floor tile in a shower has a face rough enough to grip a wet foot.

**Applied**

1. Before the slab is poured, show each curbless shower's recess on the structural drawings.
2. Make the recess equal to the mortar bed, membrane and tile, so the shower floor finishes flush with the room.
3. Build a pre-slope of 1/4 inch per foot to the drain under the membrane.
4. Cover the pre-slope and walls with a bonded sheet or liquid membrane rated to ANSI A118.10.
5. Use a drain made for that membrane, with a bonding flange clamped or bonded to it.
6. Run the membrane across a curbless threshold and 6 inches or more into the room's floor.
7. Use a linear drain where the floor should slope one way.
8. Where a traditional liner is used, turn it up 3 inches above the finished dam or threshold.
9. Keep nails and screws out of the liner below 1 inch above the dam.
10. Flood-test each pan 2 inches deep for 15 minutes before tile, damming a curbless entry temporarily.
11. Finish tub and shower walls in a nonabsorbent surface to 6 feet above the floor.
12. Tile this house's showers to the ceiling.
13. Back wall tile in tub and shower areas with one of the four listed backers.
14. Tape backer-board joints with alkali-resistant mesh.
15. Keep regular and water-resistant gypsum out of the shower's wet walls.
16. Set soft joints at every change of plane and at the perimeter.
17. Add field joints every 8 to 12 feet in showers and other wet or sunlit tile, and every 20 to 25 feet in dry rooms.
18. Choose floor tile rated Interior Wet (0.42 or higher) for wet-prone floors, and Interior Wet Plus for shower floors.
19. Turn the waterproofing up 3 inches above any built-in bench seat, and tie each niche into the membrane.
20. Enclose the shower in tempered glass, 3/8 inch thick, frameless or minimum frame.
21. Finish the whole floor of a bathroom with a curbless shower to take water, base included.
22. File the membrane, drain and tile data with the flood-test photograph.

**Professional**

Grout absorbs and passes water at every joint, so the waterproof layer sits under the tile. A curbless shower needs two slopes in sequence: a pre-slope under the membrane, so water that gets past the tile still reaches the drain, and the finish bed and tile sloped the same way on top. Curbless showers leak at undetailed corners, seams and drain connections, and rarely across the field of tile. The bonding-flange drain clamps or bonds the membrane to the drain body, so the two form one watertight plane without caulk at the drain edge.

The recess depth equals the pre-slope bed plus membrane plus tile, and it is fixed on the structural drawings because it cannot be added after the pour. A statewide interpretation confirms a finished curb is not required, and Oregon's wet-room classification is why the whole floor of a bathroom with a curbless shower is finished to take water.

Regular gypsum board is not among the backers the residential code lists behind tile around tubs and showers. Industry standards sit above the code: ANSI A118.10 for waterproofing membranes, A118.12 for crack isolation, A137.1 and A326.3 for tile and its slip resistance, and the TCNA Handbook methods that membrane warranties cite. The manufacturer's installation instructions govern the warranty for any specific detail.

TCNA's EJ171, as summarized by TileLetter and CTASC, spaces interior field joints 20 to 25 feet apart and closes them to 8 to 12 feet where tile sees direct sun or moisture. Without movement joints, tile cracks across its field as the slab shrinks and heats. ANSI A326.3 classes a tile Interior Wet at a wet DCOF of 0.42 or more, and Interior Wet Plus by the maker's declaration, for floors under standing water such as a shower's (Florida Tile, Crossville). A slip rating is a design minimum and no guarantee, because drainage, surface texture, soap film and footwear decide real slips.

## 25.15 Walls and ceilings: board, plaster and exposed mass

**Simple**

Most walls in this house are concrete or block. Each can stay bare, take a thin finish, or be furred out and covered with board. Bare concrete is a legal finished wall, and this house keeps it where its mass and its look both work. Paint and plaster on a mass wall have to let moisture pass, or water collects behind them. Wherever cold reaches the inside face, at a window edge or a slab edge, the surface runs cold and can grow mold. Wood set against concrete gets a moisture break between them.

**Applied**

1. Choose each wall's finish room by room from bare masonry, vapor-open paint or limewash, plaster, and furring with board.
2. Decide the face of exposed interior block, plain, split, ground, colored or painted, before the block-making method is fixed.
3. Put only vapor-open paint, limewash or plaster directly on a mass wall.
4. Fur masonry with wood strips at least 1 by 2 inches over the solid wall, or with steel studs at least 1-1/4 inches wide.
5. On a below-grade wall, use treated or naturally durable furring, or an approved vapor retarder behind it.
6. Set wood sleepers and wood bases on a slab over 6-mil polyethylene, or use treated or naturally durable wood.
7. Hang 1/2-inch gypsum board on walls framed at 24 inches or less, and on ceilings at 24 inches laid perpendicular.
8. Nail board at 7 inches on ceilings and 8 on walls, or screw it at 12 inches, and 16 on walls framed at 16.
9. Under a water-based ceiling texture at 24-inch framing, use 5/8-inch board or sag-resistant 1/2-inch board.
10. Back paneling thinner than 1/4 inch with 3/8-inch gypsum, on framing 16 inches apart or closer.
11. Check each wall and ceiling finish for a flame spread index of 200 or less and a smoke index of 450 or less.
12. Include fixed acoustic panels and fabric wall coverings in that check.
13. Keep any interior foam behind the code's thermal barrier.
14. Fit an insulated return at each window reveal and a thermal-break strip at each exposed slab edge.
15. Plan acoustic treatment and lighting together with every exposed concrete ceiling.
16. Photograph every wall and ceiling cavity before it closes.

**Professional**

Exposed concrete or block, board on furring, veneer plaster and steel-stud partitions are all valid interior wall paths. The choice follows the thermal-mass goal, acoustics, cost and service access, room by room. An exposed concrete ceiling reflects sound and shows the forming quality, so its acoustic treatment and its lighting are planned with it.

Where the dew point falls inside a mass wall depends on the wall's layer-by-layer R-values and vapor permeances. A finish applied straight to the masonry stays vapor-permeable, so moisture is not trapped at the concrete-to-finish interface. Window reveals, exposed slab edges and ties crossing a cavity wall's insulation are cold spots where indoor humidity condenses and mold grows, even when the rest of the wall performs. The same spots feel cold to the touch and to a person sitting near them in a dry room, so an insulated window return and a slab-edge thermal break serve comfort as well as mold control.

Block face is decided before the block-making method because each face needs its own production step. Split face needs a splitting mold or a splitting pass, ground face a grinding step, and integral color pigment metered in at batching. A painted face is the one finish applied after the wall is built. A warm-toned limewash or stain makes a wall read warmer by eye whatever its surface temperature, a separate effect from textiles. The dew-point location belongs to the Wall System chapter, foam protection and window-return details to the Envelope chapter, and color to the Aesthetics and Architectural Expression chapter.

Oregon requires a rated exterior wall only within 3 feet of a property line, and nowhere else in a detached house. Where one is required, it is a tested design to ASTM E119 or UL 263.

## 25.16 Anchors in finished concrete and block

**Simple**

A screw in concrete or block is only as strong as its anchor and the material behind it. Heavy loads go into solid concrete and grouted block. A hollow cell takes little, because the anchor bites a thin shell with air behind it. Each anchor has a published capacity for each material. Its hole is drilled, cleaned and tightened exactly as the maker says. Drilling into a hidden bar, pipe or heating tube means a structural or plumbing repair.

**Applied**

| Anchor | Base material | Use in this house |
|---|---|---|
| Wedge or sleeve expansion anchor | Solid concrete, solid-grouted block | Heavy fixed loads and equipment, away from edges and cracks |
| Screw anchor (Titen HD or Tapcon type) | Concrete, and solid or hollow block | Ledger rails, furring, TV mounts; hand-tightened in hollow block |
| Adhesive anchor | Cracked or uncracked concrete | Heavy and seismic loads, grab bars into concrete |
| Toggle or plastic plug | Hollow cells and thin shells | Pictures and light shelves only |

1. Choose the anchor class from the base material and the load, and set no load-bearing anchor in an open cell.
2. Read the anchor's ICC-ES evaluation report for its allowable load at the size, embedment and base strength used.
3. Keep the report's edge distance and spacing, and set no expansion anchor beside an edge or a crack.
4. Drill with a hammer drill and the bit diameter the report names.
5. Blow out and brush each hole after drilling, then set the anchor.
6. Tighten with a calibrated torque wrench to the report's value.
7. In hollow block, drive screw anchors by hand, never with an impact wrench.
8. Fasten furring or a ledger with masonry screws at least 1 inch into the block, 12 to 16 inches apart.
9. Treat construction adhesive as a supplement to the screws, never a substitute.
10. Before drilling a wall the map does not cover, scan it with a wall scanner that finds steel and live wiring.
11. Then drill one small pilot hole where the block module puts a web.
12. For a high-consequence hole the map does not cover, hire a ground-penetrating-radar survey.
13. Log each anchor's product, size, embedment, torque and location on the fastening map.

### Hanging specific fixtures

| Fixture | Mount | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Upper cabinets | Ledger rail on embeds or masonry screws | Screws 12 inches apart, at least 1 inch into the block |
| Wall-mounted TV | Masonry anchor kit, holes drilled with a hammer drill | 3/16-by-2-3/4-inch masonry screws for most sets; wedge anchors at 85 inches and up or on a full-motion arm; toggles or epoxy in hollow or crumbling block |
| Grab bar | Through tile into concrete or grouted block | Anchors at full embedment past the tile and its bed, sized for 250 pounds; the bar maker's data sheet sets the embedment |

| Wall-hung toilet or lavatory | Carrier frame in a stud prewall | Frame anchored to the slab, with the wall only bracing it |
| Tall shelving, racks, equipment | Top restraint | Anchors prequalified for seismic use in cracked concrete |
| Art and light decor | Removable adhesive strips or plastic plugs | Light loads only, within the maker's rating |

**Professional**

Capacities in the evaluation reports are allowable loads that already carry the report's safety factor, and the table row for the exact diameter, embedment and base strength is the design value. ICC-ES ESR-3037 gives Simpson's 5/8-inch Wedge-All an allowable tension of 1,130 pounds in 2,000 psi concrete and 2,150 pounds at 4,000 psi.

ESR-1056 tabulates Titen HD screws in hollow, lightweight, medium-weight and normal-weight block at a safety factor of 5.0. It withholds the one-third short-term load increase in masonry and limits installation in hollow block to hand torque near 10 foot-pounds. Hilti's HIT-HY 200 (ESR-3187) is qualified for cracked concrete and seismic service, in members at least its embedment plus 1-1/4 inches thick. No evaluation report covers toggles or plastic plugs, a light-duty consumer class.

FASTO's installation guidance lists over-torque among the most repeated field errors, because it strips threads, crushes the base material or damages the anchor. An expansion anchor set near an edge or into a started crack widens the crack as it expands, until it pulls out. Ferry International adds wrong embedment, short edge distance, the wrong class for the substrate and uncleaned holes. Residential fastening has no routine field pull test, so the checks are the report's hole size, depth, cleaning and torque.

Furring strips alone do not hold a run of upper cabinets, because the strip's own fastening to the masonry limits it (WOODWEB). A furred wall installed under the fastening map is verified by reading its recorded anchor type and spacing, and an inherited one is checked at an opened section.

Grab-bar makers and HUD's Fair Housing chapter on reinforced walls state a 250-pound design force, as Product Assembly Services summarizes them. Its recommended concrete anchor is a 3/16- or 1/4-inch sleeve anchor reaching full embedment in the structure beyond the tile and its bed. Bobrick's grab-bar data sheets give the embedment for each bar and anchor pair, and a bar holds no more than its anchors and wall.

Floating-shelf brackets for concrete are rated at 150 pounds (Stronghold CFS-10), and at 175 pounds for a single 12-inch bracket or 520 pounds for a pair of 8-inch brackets (Right On Bracket). Hovr's heavy line averages near 300 pounds. A 12-by-36-inch shelf of books reaches the limit of a single mid-range bracket, so a full bookshelf takes a pair or a continuous ledger. Masonry TV-mount kits bundle the same anchor classes, so the selection logic does not change (Mount-It!, EchoGear).

Geberit's Duofix carrier is a self-supporting steel frame rated for 880 pounds that stands on and anchors to the floor. The wall around it only encloses it and braces it at the top, and Geberit's instructions call for a framing wall or chase at least 5-1/2 inches deep. Against concrete or block, the carrier therefore goes in a stud prewall built in front of the masonry, with its base anchored to the slab.

Murphy-bed makers require a solid wall, naming concrete among the acceptable ones, and advise on anchors once the wall type is known. Their 50,000-cycle tests cover the lift mechanism and not the wall anchor. A correctly installed anchor keeps its capacity under a static load such as a bookshelf, and the extra margin is for cyclic loads such as a fold-down bed. A kitchen island or a credenza standing on the floor needs no masonry fastening, and a tall freestanding unit still needs restraint at its top against earthquake tip-over. Where floor space is short, as in an entry or a hall, a wall-hung piece on an embed makes the better use of it.

### Finding steel before drilling

A consumer wall scanner such as the Bosch GMS 120 finds steel to about 4-3/4 inches deep in cured concrete and detects live wiring. It shows presence and rough depth only, and cannot separate rebar from conduit. Ground-penetrating radar maps the wall but costs $14,000 to $100,000 to buy or $400 to $1,800 a month to rent (GPRS). A single high-consequence hole is therefore hired out as a survey with a technician and a report.

A struck bar means a structural repair, and a struck radiant loop means draining it and opening the slab to splice the tube. The map is the primary control for every wall built under it, and the scanner backs it up where the map does not reach.

## 25.17 Laying floors over the slab: moisture, gaps and transitions

**Simple**

New slabs hold water for months, and a wood, cork or vinyl floor laid too early traps it. Trapped water softens the glue and lifts the floor. Plastic sheeting under the slab keeps ground water out, so the slab can dry. Before any of those floors goes down, the slab is tested with probes set inside it. Floors also swell and shrink with heat. Every floor gets a gap at its edges, and a transition at each doorway leaves room to move.

**Applied**

1. Confirm the under-slab vapor retarder from the pre-pour inspection photographs before choosing a moisture-sensitive floor.
2. Keep the building ventilated and drying while the slab and mortar cure, with wet materials kept off finish products.
3. Bring the building to its service temperature and humidity for 48 hours before testing.
4. Drill probe holes to 40 percent of the slab's depth, let them equilibrate 24 hours, and read them.
5. Accept 75 percent relative humidity or less, or the flooring and adhesive makers' limit where it differs.
6. Or run calcium-chloride tests for 60 to 72 hours, and accept 3 pounds per 1,000 square feet per day or less.
7. Expect 30 to 90 days after the pour before a slab passes.
8. Where a slab fails and the floor cannot wait, apply a two-part moisture-mitigation coating such as LATICRETE VAPOR BAN E.
9. Let the coating cure before the underlayment or floor goes down.
10. Bring the radiant system to room temperature 10 days before laying cork, and hold 65 to 72°F during installation.
11. Acclimate cork in the room for 48 hours before installation.
12. Leave the maker's expansion gap at every wall.
13. In rooms longer than 30 feet, add field joints filled with foam backer rod or cork strip.
14. Glue down only with an adhesive its maker rates for radiant-floor temperatures, or float the floor.
15. Use glue-down or snap-track transition strips that need no screw into the slab.
16. Record the radiant tubing's position at every threshold on the fastening map.
17. Log each moisture test's location, depth, date, reading and instrument.

**Professional**

Moisture rising through a slab by capillary action and vapor diffusion re-emulsifies water-based adhesives and softens rubber backings, and the floor blisters, loses bond and delaminates (IFTI). The moisture comes from inside the slab, where no installer sees or feels it. ASTM F2170 reads relative humidity inside the slab with probes, and ASTM F1869 weighs the vapor a sealed calcium-chloride dish collects from its surface. ASTM F710, the practice for preparing concrete under resilient flooring, sets the acceptance most flooring makers reference, 75 percent by the probes or 3 pounds per 1,000 square feet per 24 hours by the dish. A maker's own published limit governs where it differs, and IFTI cites one that allows 6 pounds at 75 percent.

The tests are a flooring-warranty requirement that the residential code does not adopt. Skipping them voids the warranty, and IFTI names it the most common cause of resilient-floor failures over slabs. The under-slab vapor retarder prevents the problem by keeping ground moisture out of the concrete, so the slab dries to an acceptable reading and stays there. A topical two-part epoxy or urethane coating is the retrofit for a slab that tests high, at the cost of a step and its cure time (LATICRETE, Polyguard). Construction moisture from curing concrete and mortar leaves through the same slab and walls, and the test reading, never the days since the pour, decides when a floor goes down.

Flooring buckles, cups or delaminates when it has no room to move as the slab cycles between heated and idle. BuildDirect's installer guidance puts field joints, as well as perimeter gaps, in rooms longer than 30 feet. Continental Flooring reports expansion starting near 85°F, adhesive softening at 95 to 100°F, and a risk of permanent damage above 110°F. Glue-down floors over radiant therefore use adhesives rated for the temperature, and a floating floor avoids that failure entirely. A transition strip has to leave the floor's expansion gap open behind it. A screw through a threshold is the fastener most likely to reach radiant tubing, because loops turn close to room boundaries at their tight perimeter spacing.

## 25.18 Sound in the rooms: echo and its treatment

**Simple**

Bare concrete bounces back nearly all the sound that hits it, so a room of exposed concrete rings. Voices carry and blur, and a kitchen's noise fills the house. Soft things fix it. Rugs, curtains, upholstered chairs and mineral-wool panels all absorb sound. A rug also quiets footsteps where it lies. How much a room rings is measured, not guessed. A free phone app times how long a hand clap takes to fade. In a living room the clap should fade in half a second to eight tenths.

**Applied**

1. Furnish each room first, with rugs on pads, lined curtains hung in folds, and upholstered seating.
2. Measure the furnished room with a phone app such as BuzziSpace RT60, RT60 by Studio Six Digital or Reverb Time.
3. Treat a living room that reads above 0.8 second, and a media room above 0.6.
4. Cover 10 to 25 percent of a ringing room's wall and ceiling area with 2-inch mineral-wool panels.
5. Buy fabric-wrapped mineral-wool panels at $5 to $7 per square foot, or $7 to $9 for dense or encapsulated ones.
6. Or build each panel from a 1-by-4 frame around 2-inch Owens Corning 703 or Rockwool Rockboard 60.
7. Wrap it in cotton or linen, stapled from the center of each side outward.
8. Hang panels on French cleats or Z-clips, anchored to the wall for their weight.
9. Under an exposed concrete ceiling, hang baffles or spray an absorptive coating over the seating or cooking zone only.
10. Lay a thick rug or a chair mat at any one spot where footsteps or a rolling chair annoy.
11. Choose between polished concrete and tile for other reasons, since both floors reflect alike.
12. Leave sound masking out unless a speech-privacy problem remains after absorption.
13. Size each room's rugs, curtains and seating for sound once the floor plan is final.

**Professional**

Plain cast concrete reflects about 98 percent of the sound that strikes it (Second Skin Audio), and rooms built of it run long reverberation times. Reverberation time, RT60, is the seconds a sound takes to fall by 60 decibels. SonusGearFlow and HiFiReport put a dedicated listening or home-theater room at 0.3 to 0.6 second and a multi-use living room at 0.5 to 0.8. RT60 by Studio Six Digital measures decay in octave bands from 63 Hz to 4 kHz under ISO 3382, and the free BuzziSpace app models how added absorption would change a measured room.

Carpet over concrete absorbs 0.08 of the sound reaching it at 125 Hz and 0.72 at 2,000 Hz, so it works in the speech range and little in the bass. Drapery absorbs more as it hangs in more folds, and heavy upholstery fabric absorbs more than thin. In the office measurements Rug Goddess cites, carpet alone cut sound levels 2.7 dB and carpet with ceiling absorption cut them 4.1 dB. Furnishing works on its own, and a treated surface adds to it.

A 2-inch mineral-wool board reaches a noise reduction coefficient (the fraction of sound a surface absorbs) of 0.95 to 1.00 at mid and high frequencies, because the core sets the absorption and the wrapping does not (Acoustical Solutions). Home-built panels run near $20 each against $50 to $100 bought, in 1 to 2 hours each with a saw, glue, brads and a staple gun (FixThisBuildThat, Burton Acoustix). Vinyl-backed fabric reflects sound before it reaches the core, so the wrap is cotton or linen.

In most rooms the ceiling is the one continuous hard plane left unfurnished, so an exposed concrete ceiling lengthens the reverberation more than any wall. Spray coatings and hung baffles over the loud zone treat it without covering the rest (SoundProSolutions). Polished concrete and porcelain tile are both dense, nonporous and reflective, and the difference between them is small beside the difference between either and a soft floor. Footfall and dropped-object noise is impact noise, local to the spot, and the Carpet and Rug Institute states a thick rug can eliminate it at the point of contact.

Sound masking raises the background noise with distributed speakers so speech carries less meaning, and it shortens no reverberation. The Network Installers and Telco Data price it at $1.50 to $2.00 per square foot. Speakers cost $75 to $200 and cover 200 to 250 square feet each, and generators run $500 to $1,000 for one zone and $3,000 to $10,000 for several. Installation adds 30 to 50 percent, and an acoustic assessment costs $1,000 to $5,000. Every source prices it for open offices.

## 25.19 Sound between rooms: walls, floors and machines

**Simple**

A heavy wall blocks sound by its weight, and the concrete and block walls here are heavy. The light partitions between inside rooms are not, and a hollow door in one leaks more sound than the wall around it. Floors are a different problem. Footsteps on a bare slab carry straight through to the room below until a springy layer separates the finish from the structure. Machines bolted to concrete send their hum through the whole house, so each one sits on rubber.

**Applied**

1. Place the quiet rooms, the primary suite, an office and a media room, against structural walls at floor-plan time.
2. Rate each mass wall from its weight with the masonry formula before choosing furring.
3. Where a furred mass wall needs more isolation, fill the furring cavity with glass fiber, cellulose or rock wool, never closed-cell foam.
4. Use the deepest furring the room allows, and no empty two-sided cavity shallower than 1 inch.
5. Keep drywall screws short enough that none touches the masonry behind the furring.
6. Seal every electrical box in a quiet wall with joint sealant, and set no boxes back to back.
7. Fill each through-wall opening with fiber or foam, then seal both faces.
8. Fill control joints behind their sealant with mortar, grout, foam or fiber.
9. Carry quiet-room partitions to the underside of the slab above, with a sealed joint that allows deflection.
10. Build quiet-room partitions with insulated cavities on resilient channel or double studs.
11. Hang solid-core doors on quiet rooms, with perimeter gaskets and a drop seal.
12. Over an occupied room, float the finish floor on a sound mat, or place a gypsum topping over Maxxon Acousti-Mat.
13. Turn the radiant system off 24 hours before and after placing a gypsum topping over the mat.
14. Get a quote from a Maxxon-licensed applicator for the mat and topping at the house's tubing spacing.
15. Set the heat pump's outdoor unit on rubber isolation pads over a concrete pad or heavy pavers.
16. Mount the radiant manifold, pumps and ventilator on rubber or spring isolators, never bolted hard to the mass.

**Professional**

CMHA TEK 13-01D rates a concrete masonry wall's sound transmission class from its weight. STC is the single-number rating of airborne sound blocking, and the formula is STC = 20.5 W^0.234, with W the wall weight in pounds per square foot, the best fit TMS 0302 drew across laboratory tests. Its table lists a nominal 8-inch wall at STC 44 to 49 hollow, by unit density from 85 to 135 pcf, and 52 to 57 grout-filled, sand-filled or solid.

For a cavity wall with a clay brick outer wythe, the brick is rated by STC = 19.6 W^0.230 and the two results are interpolated by wythe weight. The document's example, 8-inch block at 33 psf behind 4-inch brick at 38 psf, yields 55 by the block formula and 52 by the brick formula, interpolating to 53. This house's walls are rated once their weights are fixed in the Wall System chapter.

Furring over the masonry changes the rating in both directions. Board glued straight to the masonry adds almost nothing beyond sealing the face. One layer on one side over an empty furring space adds 0.2 STC at a 1/2-inch space and 8.6 at 3-1/2 inches. Board on both sides over an empty space under 0.8 inch deep drops the rating below the bare wall, through the mass-air-mass resonance the TEK calls the drum effect.

The same two-sided assembly over a cavity filled with fibrous insulation runs from a loss of 1.8 at 1/2 inch to a gain of 31.8 at 3-1/2 inches. Closed-cell foam absorbs little sound and gives none of that gain.

Sound flanks a heavy wall through its openings. The TEK's construction notes seal partial penetrations such as electrical boxes with joint sealant and fill through-wall openings with fiber or foam before sealing them. A screw long enough to touch the masonry behind furring carries vibration across the air gap the furring created. Partitions stop at the slab above with a sealed deflection joint, and in a rated wall, listed fire-safing fills the joint and satisfies both the fire and the sound rule.

A bare 4-inch reinforced slab rates IIC 25, and bare slabs run IIC 25 to 35 (Commercial Acoustics, Engineer Fix). Impact insulation class rates how much footfall a floor stops, and the mass that gives a floor its STC does little for it. Only a resilient layer between structure and finish raises IIC.

Maxxon's Acousti-Mat comes in 1/4-, 3/8- and 3/4-inch and SBR versions under a gypsum-concrete topping, the thinner ones saving floor height at some loss of isolation. Maxxon cites the Radiant Panel Association's rule of turning the radiant off 24 hours either side of placing the topping, a sequencing rule and not an output limit. Any underlayment that improves IIC also adds thermal resistance between tubing and room, so the floor's R-value budget from the finish section is spent on it.

Interior stud partitions earn their isolation the same way as furring, with two layers, an insulated cavity and decoupling. The door governs in most partitions, because a hollow-core door undercut for airflow stops far less sound than the wall around it. A rigidly bolted compressor, pump or fan couples its vibration into the mass, as the long screw does for airborne sound. Rubber pads under a condenser's feet carry near 250 pounds a leg for units of 7,000 to 48,000 BTU (RecDec, Senville). A concrete pad or heavy pavers under the unit add damping mass, and spring mounts isolate the low frequencies a rubber pad passes (GreenBuildingAdvisor).

## 25.20 Warmth and comfort in a mass interior

**Simple**

A room can be warm by the thermometer and still feel cold. Hard, bare surfaces and a long echo read as cold to the people in it, apart from the air temperature. The feeling comes from the sound and the surfaces, so soft materials change it. Rugs, hangings and upholstery change the sound and the touch of a room at once. Wood goes where bare feet land, beside the bed and at the shower. Plants help too, on a smaller scale.

**Applied**

1. Put a wood inlay, a wood-topped mat or a built-in wood bench beside each bed and at each shower exit.
2. Lay wood or a mat at the kitchen's standing zone, in front of the sink and range.
3. Hang a large woven textile on one exposed wall in each room that reads cold.
4. Plan a dense planting at a south or west window, over a floor drain or a planter with its own drain.
5. Swap in heavier rugs and curtains for winter and lighter ones for summer, within the floor's R-value budget.
6. Build the textile budget room by room from the final plan, at the unit prices of rugs, drapery and seating.
7. Measure a room that feels cold for echo before adding heat.

**Professional**

Studies of combined thermal and acoustic conditions in Building and Environment find the two interact. In one, temperature stopped affecting overall comfort above 71.3 dBA, and sound level stopped affecting it below 18.5°C. Another finds that acoustic conditions change thermal comfort while leaving thermal sensation unchanged. A radiant-heated room at the right floor temperature can therefore register as cold through its sound and its surfaces, and the remedy is acoustic and textural.

Felt warmth at a surface is explained in the same research by emissivity, with glossy, metallic surfaces reading cool and matte ones warm. Comfort also tracks the temperature of the surfaces around a person, and the Passive and Bioclimatic Design chapter states the thermostat offsets a warm floor allows.

Medieval castles hung wool and silk tapestries to hold warmth at the wall and to shorten the echo of stone halls (teaandideas.com, Dusty Old Thing). A large woven hanging on an exposed concrete wall does the same two jobs at that wall, at less cost and commitment than a panel treatment. Plants absorb sound by deflection, absorption and refraction at their leaves. Broad, dense and soft leaves work best in the green-wall measurements Building and Environment published in 2015. Their transpiration raises local humidity, and humid air absorbs sound slightly better than dry. A planting dense enough to matter needs daylight and drainage, so it goes at a window with a drain below.

Bare feet meet the floor longest at three places: beside the bed at the coldest hour, at the shower exit while wet, and at the kitchen's standing zone, where hardness tires the legs as much as temperature. A small wood surface at each costs a fraction of a wood floor across the room. A heavier winter rug adds thermal resistance over the floor while it heats, the trade-off the finish section's R-value budget polices, and removing it in summer restores the floor for cooling. A whole-house textile cost is built up from the category prices once the room count and sizes exist.

## 25.21 Lighting

**Simple**

Light is planned room by room from the furniture, not from a ceiling grid. Each room gets three layers of light. General light fills the room, task light falls on a counter or desk, and accent light picks out a wall or a niche. Each layer dims on its own. Exposed concrete swallows more light than white paint, so these rooms need more fixtures than a painted house. Older eyes need three to ten times the light of young ones and mind glare more. So this house uses more fixtures at lower brightness, well shielded, with dim night lights along the floor.

**Applied**

1. Plan each room's general light to the living-area baseline near 300 lux (28 footcandles), from the furnished plan.
2. Light kitchen counters to 50 to 75 footcandles, a reading chair to 20 to 50, and a desk to 30 to 50.
3. Treat those figures as floors, and give task fixtures and dimmers the range to reach several times them.
4. Work each fixture's delivered light from its lumens, beam angle and mounting height, never from a count per room.
5. Add fixtures in rooms with exposed concrete or block, which reflect less than painted board.
6. Place more fixtures at lower output, shielded or diffused, instead of fewer bright ones.
7. Add accent fixtures only where a wall, niche or object is meant to be lit.
8. Put each layer on its own dimmable circuit or smart-lighting zone, decided at rough-in.
9. Choose warm white, 2700 to 3500 K, and specify one color temperature per room for both the lighting and the aesthetics plan.
10. Pair every LED fixture with a reverse-phase (ELV, trailing-edge) dimmer its maker lists as compatible.
11. Set each dimmer's low-end trim above the point where its fixtures flicker.
12. For tunable-white fixtures, use drivers with Thread or Zigbee radios that the house's local controller runs, and no 0-10 V control wiring.
13. Run 20 to 50 lux of always-on night lighting low along halls and stairs.
14. Finish daylit rooms at a ceiling reflectance of 80 or higher, upper walls 50 or higher, and floors 20 or higher in matte.
15. Fit sheer and blackout window treatments as each room's glare control through the day.

**Professional**

Waypoint Lighting, Stetra Lighting and Super Bright LEDs reproduce the IES-referenced residential levels. Kitchen counters sit at 50 to 75 footcandles, or 30 to 50 for general kitchen tasks in one of them. A reading chair sits at 20 to 50 and a desk at 30 to 50, with living areas near 300 lux. Delivered footcandles at the work plane come from the fixture's rated lumens, its beam angle and the distance to the task surface, worked against the real room once ceiling heights and furniture exist.

An aging eye needs three to ten times the illuminance of a young one for the same task, and a 60-year-old eye needs about three times a 20-year-old's (Lighting Design for the Aging Eyes, Electrical Contractor Magazine). Aging eyes are also more sensitive to glare, so the remedy is more usable light rather than brighter sources. Night lighting of 20 to 50 lux low in corridors and on stairs is the fall-prevention measure the same guidance adds.

Exposed concrete and block reflect less than light paint, so a room finished in them needs more direct lumen output to reach the same usable level, by an amount worked from each surface's measured reflectance. An exposed mass ceiling bounces less daylight than a white one, so the ceiling finish of a deep room lit from a clerestory is a daylighting decision as well as a look. Warm white, 2700 to 3500 K, is the mainstream, low-cost end of the residential LED market, chosen here to soften exposed concrete's visual coolness. Cooler light at 4000 K and above reads harsh against masonry.

Most LED dimming faults come from pairing a leading-edge (forward-phase, TRIAC) dimmer built for incandescent loads with an LED driver (Repenic, Bees Lighting). The chopped waveform makes the driver misfire, heard as buzzing from its coils. At loads under 10 watts the dimmer cannot hold a stable output, so the driver shuts down and restarts as visible flicker. Reverse-phase dimmers matched to the maker's compatibility list cure most of it, and the low-end trim raises the floor above the cut-out point.

A tunable-white fixture controls color temperature and intensity, two variables instead of one. Hardwired 0-10 V control needs five wires per fixture, and long runs shift color and intensity through resistance (Bees Lighting, MOONS' Industries). DALI-2 avoids the wire count with its own control hardware. Drivers with Matter-over-Thread or Zigbee radios built in need no control wiring, and they are the device class the local-first controller is built to run.

The Passive and Bioclimatic Design chapter sets the daylighting reflectances and shading, and the branch-circuit chapter the switched outlets, the neutral at every switch, closet fixtures and the high-efficacy rule. Glare on a polished floor is the window's specular reflection, which a matte finish scatters. Sheer treatments diffuse daylight while admitting it, and blackout treatments remove it, so the two together are an operable control layered on the exterior shading.

## 25.22 Doors, hardware and safety glazing

**Simple**

Glass anywhere a person could fall into it is safety glass. That means glass in doors, beside doors, around tubs and showers, and along stairs. Tempered glass breaks into small blunt pieces instead of blades, and laminated glass holds together on a plastic layer. Inside the house, bedroom and bathroom locks open from the hall with a coin or a pin, never a key. Interior doors here are solid, on three hinges, with lever handles. An outside door is part of the weather shell, flashed and sealed like a window.

**Applied**

1. Use tempered or laminated glass in every door, and in any pane within 24 inches of a door's edge below 60 inches.
2. Use it in any pane over 9 square feet with its bottom under 18 inches and top over 36, beside a walking surface.
3. Use it in any glass facing a tub, shower, spa or sauna below 60 inches, within 60 inches of the water.
4. Use it in glass below 36 inches beside a stair, a landing or a ramp.
5. Use it at a bottom landing in glass below 36 inches within a 60-inch arc of the last nosing.
6. Use it in every glass guard and railing panel.
7. Check that each pane in those places carries a permanent mark naming its safety standard.
8. Fit bedroom and bathroom doors with privacy sets that release from outside with a coin or a pin.
9. Make no sliding or barn door the only door from a bedroom.
10. Hang solid-core interior doors, 1-3/8 inch thick, on three ball-bearing hinges with lever handles.
11. Use 1-3/4-inch doors with perimeter seals and automatic door bottoms where sound matters.
12. Hang bathroom doors 30 inches wide or more, most doors 32 or more, and 36 where accessibility applies.
13. Frame pocket-door pockets with steel-stud kits.
14. Install exterior doors with flashing, air sealing and a drainage path, as part of the envelope.
15. Confirm any smart lock still opens by hand from inside wherever egress requires it.

**Professional**

Tempered glass breaks into blunt cubes and laminated glass holds its fragments on an interlayer, which is why both pass the impact tests behind the safety-glazing standards. The code lists hazardous locations by the ways people strike glass: walking into a door or the panel beside it, slipping in a wet room, and falling on a stair. Each carries its own height limit. A rail at 34 to 38 inches that resists 50 pounds per linear foot without touching the glass relieves the stair case and the large-window case. Glass set far enough back from a tub or shower is outside the wet-surface rule, and a door light too small for a child's head is outside the door rule.

Interior doors carry no code rating in a single-family house apart from the garage door. Exterior doors meet the energy code's U-factor and air-leakage limits and are flashed to the water-resistive barrier under ASTM E2112, as the Envelope chapter details. Solid cores, wider leaves, steel-framed pockets and acoustic seals are durability and livability choices. In a partition between rooms, the door governs the sound isolation more often than the wall.

**Code**

```

  Fixed and operable panels of swinging, sliding and bifold doors; openings a 3-inch sphere cannot pass and decorative glazing excepted.
  Source: ORSC R308.4.1 (2023 edition).

## 25.23 Interior materials chemistry

**Simple**

Glued wood products such as plywood, particleboard and MDF can give off formaldehyde for years. Federal law caps how much, for everything sold since 2018, and a label on the product proves it. Paints, glues, caulks and floor finishes give off their own fumes. Each one is bought here in its low-emission version, and the same label standard reaches sofas, curtains and rugs. This house changes its air slowly, so a fume that gets in stays until the ventilation carries it out.

**Applied**

1. Specify composite wood, and cabinets and furniture made of it, labeled compliant with the federal formaldehyde rule.
2. Keep every label.
3. Prefer no-added-formaldehyde or ultra-low-emitting composite wood where it is sold.
4. Select paints, sealants, adhesives and flooring certified to the California Section 01350 method or GREENGUARD Gold.
5. Hold flat paints under 50 grams per liter of VOC, and sealants and adhesives under the SCAQMD Rule 1168 limits.
6. Buy upholstery, drapery fabric and rugs certified GREENGUARD Gold, not base GREENGUARD.
7. File each product's certificate with the finish record.

**Professional**

TSCA Title VI (40 CFR part 770) is the binding federal standard. Every composite wood product, and every finished good made of one, carries the mark of a third-party certifier EPA recognizes, and since March 22, 2019 only that certification has counted. The limits equal California's CARB ATCM Phase 2, tested by ASTM E1333 or D6007. Solid wood is unregulated, and phenol-formaldehyde plywood complies by default because it emits far less than urea-formaldehyde board.

Above that floor, the CDPH Standard Method v1.2 (California Section 01350) is the chamber test behind the GREENGUARD Gold and FloorScore labels. SCAQMD Rule 1113 is the content benchmark for coatings, at 50 grams per liter for flat paint, and Rule 1168 for adhesives and sealants. GREENGUARD Gold tightens the formaldehyde and total-VOC limits of base GREENGUARD for use around children, the sick and the elderly. For upholstered goods it certifies the frame, the fabric and the filling together. West Elm, Pottery Barn and Blu Dot sell certified lines in Maharam and Sunbrella fabrics (Arcedior, Blu Dot, Folio Fabrics).

The HVAC chapter sets the ventilation rate and the filtration that work against whatever load the labels leave. Source control through these labels does more for indoor air in a low-air-change house than dilution by ventilation.

**Code**

```
Composite wood products meet the federal formaldehyde emission limits.
  Hardwood plywood 0.05 ppm, particleboard 0.09 ppm, MDF 0.11 ppm and thin MDF 0.13 ppm, for products sold, supplied or made on or after June 1, 2018.
  Source: 40 CFR 770.10 (TSCA Title VI, 15 U.S.C. 2697).
```

## 25.24 Move-in readiness and the finish record

**Simple**

Moving in follows a checklist. Every alarm, handrail, guard, exit and safety valve is tried and found working before the first night. Then the finish record goes into the permanent house file. It holds the paint formulas, the tile and floor products, the model numbers, and the photographs of what is inside the walls. The record shows what is behind each wall before a later repair opens it.

**Applied**

1. Before covering any interior work, photograph each cavity, blocking point, shutoff, valve and cleanout access, electrical box, low-voltage path, radiant loop and waterproofing layer.
2. At move-in, test the smoke and carbon monoxide alarms and confirm they sound together.
3. Pull on every guard and handrail.
4. Open the egress door and every escape opening without a key.
5. Let the garage door swing shut, and confirm it latches.
6. Read each anti-scald valve at 120°F and each bidet at 110°F with a thermometer, and log the readings.
7. File the ventilation balance report and the blower-door result.
8. Trip every GFCI and AFCI device on its test button, and switch on every required lighting outlet.
9. Swing and latch every door, push on every cabinet and counter, and pour water on every tiled floor to watch it drain.
10. Open every access panel to its valve, cleanout, damper or manifold.
11. Start a long-term radon test in the lowest occupied level during the first heating season.
12. File the finish record, with paint formulas, flooring and tile products with their slip data, and cabinet and hardware models.
13. Add the composite-wood labels, slab moisture results, anti-scald set points, spare materials and manuals to it.
14. Add the fastening map, the pre-cover photographs and the signed move-in checklist to the permanent house file.

**Professional**

The final inspection spans the building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical and energy approvals. Alarms, egress, stairs, guards and glazing are among the items checked. Each item on the move-in list is a code requirement belonging to its own system, gathered here so the family walks the house once against one list. The checklist confirms visible quality and hidden-system proof together.

Concrete construction allows no later access to embedded routing. The Documentation chapter sets the binder, its backup rule and the records a sale requires, and the inspection chapter the certificate of occupancy. The pre-cover photographs and the fastening map are what keep a future repair from starting with demolition. Beyond the code minimum, the records that matter later are the tile slip data, the composite-wood labels, the slab moisture results and the anti-scald set points.
