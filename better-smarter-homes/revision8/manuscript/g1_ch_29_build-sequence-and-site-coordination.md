# Chapter 29: Build Sequence And Site Coordination

This chapter sequences the build: how an empty site becomes a dried-in concrete house, how the permits and inspections chain together, how the owner-builder's own work and the licensed specialists are scheduled around each other, and how the project reaches occupancy without losing control of quality. It sequences the work of the technical chapters rather than repeating them; where a step needs a detail (the concrete mix, the waterproofing assembly, the branch-circuit layout), the owning chapter states it.

Three independent timing systems have to align: the Oregon permit chain (land use, then building, then trade, then inspection), the trade-licensing limits on what the owner-builder may install at each stage, and the material curing and conditioning windows set by the standards and the manufacturers. On a rural owner-builder build, the binding order is land-use clearance and septic and well feasibility before the building permit issues, not the reverse.

Phase durations, costs, equipment rental rates, and the current wildfire-hazard-map designation are verified for the actual site, season, and jurisdiction; they are treated as dated facts, not manuscript constants.

## 29.1 The Build As A Dependency Map

The build moves through stages in an order set by dependencies, not by preference. A step done out of order traps the next trade or buries an uninspected assembly, so the sequence is planned before the first load of dirt moves.

**Simple**

A house is built in stages: land, permits, site work, foundation, structure, roof and windows, rough systems, insulation, finishes, and move-in. The order matters because one step done too early can trap the next one. A drain has to slope before finishes hide it. An electrical box has to be located before drywall, and waterproofing has to be inspected before backfill.

**Applied**

Keep the stage map current in the project binder:

| Stage | Goal |
|---|---|
| Land use and feasibility | Prove the parcel can support the house and its water and waste |
| Design and permits | Turn the design into approved documents |
| Site preparation | Make the land buildable and serviceable |
| Foundation | Place and protect the base |
| Structure | Build the concrete and masonry body |
| Roof and openings | Dry in the house |
| Rough systems | Install plumbing, mechanical, electrical, and low voltage |
| Insulation and cover | Close assemblies after inspection |
| Finishes | Make the house livable |
| Occupancy | Prove the required systems are safe and usable |

**Professional**

The sequence is a dependency graph in which downstream permits and inspections cannot close until upstream ones do. Gravity drains need slope before cover, electrical boxes need location before drywall, waterproofing needs inspection before backfill, radiant tubing needs a pressure test and photographs before the topping slab, and windows need egress and rough-opening verification before ordering. This chapter names when to use each technical chapter and how the pieces coordinate in time; the technical chapters state the how.

**Code**

```
Construction proceeds through the required inspection sequence.
  Each stage is gated by the prior inspection's approval; work is not concealed before its inspection.
  Source: ORSC R109 (2023 edition).

The permits chain in a fixed order.
  Land use (ORS 215/197), then building permit (ORSC R105), then trade permits (ORS 479, 693, 447), then inspections (R109), then the certificate of occupancy (R110).
  Source: ORSC R105, R109, R110 (2023 edition); ORS chapters 215, 197, 479, 693, 447.
```

## 29.2 The Permit Dependency Chain

Permits issue in a fixed order, and the building permit cannot issue until the land-use, septic, and well approvals close. On a rural parcel this front-end work takes months, so the schedule is built backward from a target occupancy date with those realities included.

**Simple**

The building permit is not the first permit; it is nearly the last of the front-end approvals. On rural land, the county clears the land use first. The septic evaluation has to pass, and the well has to be feasible. That front end routinely takes months. The schedule starts there, not at the groundbreaking.

**Applied**

Work the chain in order:

1. Land-use clearance from the county or city planning department (rural zoning under ORS 215 and statewide planning under ORS 197), plus any wildfire-hazard-zone designation and its envelope requirements.
2. Septic site evaluation (DEQ OAR 340-071) and well feasibility (OWRD OAR 690) on a parcel without sewer and water.
3. Driveway or approach permit (county, city, or ODOT for a state highway) meeting fire-access standards.
4. Building permit (ORSC R105) with the construction documents (R106), the Stage 1 approvals, and any required PE stamps; plus the erosion-control permit where the disturbance reaches one acre.
5. Trade permits for electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work.
6. Inspections and the certificate of occupancy.

**Professional**

Land use and feasibility routinely take three to nine months in rural Oregon counties, and building-permit plan review runs weeks to months longer in growth-pressured jurisdictions, so no permit is assumed to be obtainable on demand. The wildfire-hazard map has been reissued and challenged repeatedly, and Oregon's R327 wildfire-hardening provisions now apply through local opt-in adoption rather than a statewide map. The parcel's current status is obtained in writing from the county before the envelope is designed; the envelope chapter's wildfire section states the R327 requirements and the adopting-jurisdiction status. The DEQ 1200-C construction stormwater permit applies to a disturbance of one acre or more (or smaller in a regulated area), and the septic site evaluation must be approved before the building permit issues on a parcel without sewer.

**Code**

```
Land-use clearance precedes the building permit.
  County zoning under ORS 215; statewide planning goals under ORS 197; clearance closes before the building permit issues.
  Source: ORS chapters 215 and 197.

Septic and well feasibility close before the building permit on unserved land.
  Septic site evaluation per DEQ rules; well feasibility per OWRD rules.
  Source: OAR chapter 340, division 71; OAR chapter 690 (see the land and water chapters).

Wildfire hardening applies only where the local jurisdiction has adopted it.
  R327 requirements (Class A roofing, ignition-resistant siding, ember-resistant venting) attach by local opt-in; parcel status confirmed in writing at permit application.
  Source: ORSC R327 (2023 edition, Oregon amendment); ORS 455.433 (see the envelope chapter).

Ground disturbance of an acre or more needs the construction stormwater permit.
  DEQ 1200-C erosion-and-sediment-control permit; smaller thresholds in regulated areas.
  Source: DEQ 1200-C program (ORS 468B; OAR chapter 340, division 45).

The driveway meets the fire-access standard.
  Width, vertical clearance, turn radii, grade, and turnaround per the fire code and the approach permit.
  Source: Oregon Fire Code 503 and Appendix D.
```

## 29.3 Permit Lifecycle And The Occupancy Gate

A permit expires if work does not start or is suspended, so extensions are obtained in writing before expiration. Partial approvals let discrete work proceed, and the certificate of occupancy is the legal gate to moving in.

**Simple**

A permit includes deadlines. Work has to start within 180 days, and a 180-day gap in the work can void the permit. A written extension request before the deadline keeps it alive. At the far end, the certificate of occupancy is the legal difference between a construction site and a home.

**Applied**

- Start work within 180 days of permit issuance and avoid a 180-day suspension; request an extension in writing before the permit expires.
- Request inspections through the jurisdiction's portal or phone (same-day requests often by early morning), and document every request and result with dated photos of the inspection record.
- Do not call for an inspection until the work is complete and accessible, to avoid a reinspection fee.
- Use a partial approval (such as a foundation-only permit) to let the pour and cure proceed while superstructure plan review continues, when the plans examiner allows it.
- Treat a temporary certificate of occupancy as discretionary, not a right, and not as a planning crutch.

**Professional**

ORSC R105.5 sets the 180-day commencement and suspension limits and allows the building official to grant extensions of up to 180 days each for justifiable cause; no state reinstatement provision exists, so what happens after a lapse is local policy, and the written extension is the safe path. A temporary certificate of occupancy under R110.4 requires the life-safety items complete (smoke and CO alarms, egress, guards and handrails, working water and waste, working heat, no exposed conductors) plus a written list of remaining work and a deadline. Failing the deadline can trigger revocation, and a building official can deny the temporary certificate even with the minimums met. The certificate of occupancy under R110 requires all inspections signed off, the energy-code documentation filed, the required testing documented (blower door, well-water potability, septic startup where applicable), and any conditions of approval satisfied. Selling before the certificate of occupancy is a disclosure risk under the seller-property-disclosure framework covered in the legal chapter.

**Code**

```
The permit expires on the 180-day clocks.
  Invalid if work is not commenced within 180 days of issuance, or if work is suspended or abandoned for 180 days; written extensions of up to 180 days each for justifiable cause; no state reinstatement provision.
  Source: ORSC R105.5 (2023 edition).

A temporary certificate of occupancy is discretionary and conditional.
  Life-safety items complete, a written remaining-work list, and a deadline; revocable on a missed deadline.
  Source: ORSC R110.4 (2023 edition).

The certificate of occupancy closes the build.
  All inspections approved, energy documentation filed, required testing documented, conditions of approval satisfied.
  Source: ORSC R110 (2023 edition).
```

## 29.4 Owner-Builder Scope And When Worker Rules Attach

The owner-builder is the labor for the bulk of the work, and the licensed specialists are scheduled around that work, not the reverse. The trade exemptions cover the owner's own work on the owner-occupied residence, and the worker-safety rules attach the moment anyone is paid to help.

**Simple**

You may build your own house with your own hands: general construction, wiring, plumbing, and mechanical, all under permits and inspections. A short list of tasks belongs to specialists no matter what: refrigerant work, LP gas, the septic install, and the well. And the day you pay a helper, you become an employer, with the safety rules that come with that.

**Applied**

Assign each task to the right party:

- Owner-builder (on the owner-occupied, not-for-sale residence, with permits): general construction (no CCB license required), and the owner's own electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work.
- EPA-certified HVAC technician: brazing the refrigerant lines, evacuation, charging, and startup of a split-system heat pump (the owner may mount the units and run the line-set conduit).
- Licensed LP-gas installer: all LP-gas work (no homeowner exemption).
- Licensed septic installer and OWRD-bonded well constructor: the septic tank and pipe install and the well construction (no homeowner exemption; the owner may do supervised site work).
- Professional engineer: any structural element outside the prescriptive code (a service purchase; the owner still pulls the permit).
- Before paying any helper: understand that OR-OSHA and the workers'-compensation framework attach to paid workers; route casual labor through a temp agency, or keep it under the casual-labor threshold with eyes open.

**Professional**

The trade exemptions (ORS 479.540 electrical, ORS 693.020 plumbing, and the permit-only mechanical path) parallel the CCB owner-builder exemption established in the legal chapter, and permits are still required. The federal EPA Section 608 certification for refrigerant work does not yield to the Oregon owner-builder exemption, so the certified technician is scheduled for a single startup visit rather than spread across the build.

The worker-safety boundary is statutory. An employee is a person furnishing services for remuneration under the employer's direction (ORS 654.005(4)), so the owner working alone, or alongside licensed independent contractors, is outside Oregon OSHA's jurisdiction, and the moment the owner pays and directs a helper the site is covered. Casual labor stays outside the workers'-compensation system only while the 30-day labor cost stays under the indexed threshold (about $1,000, ORS 656.027(3)) and the work is outside the employer's trade or business. A true volunteer is not an employee, but "remuneration, financial or otherwise" can include in-kind compensation (room and board, labor swaps), and no Oregon OSHA guidance resolves that line, so compensation questions about helpers go to an employment attorney or a free Oregon OSHA consultation. The owner-builder does not schedule the trades the way a general contractor does, because the owner is the trades for most of the work; the external dependencies (the refrigerant technician, the well driller, the septic installer, the surveyor, and the inspector) are the fixed points the owner's own work is planned around.

**Code**

```
The owner's own trade work is exempt from licensing, not from permits.
  Electrical: ORS 479.540(1) (owner-occupied, not for sale). Plumbing: ORS 693.020(1)(a) with ORS 447.060(1)(a). Mechanical: permit-only path under ORSC R105.1.
  Source: ORS 479.540; ORS 693.020; ORS 447.060; ORSC R105.1 (see the legal chapter for the reconciled statement).

Refrigerant work requires federal certification regardless of owner status.
  Brazing, evacuation, and charging by an EPA Section 608 certified technician.
  Source: Clean Air Act Section 608 (EPA).

Septic, well, and LP-gas work have no homeowner exemption.
  Licensed septic installer (OAR 340-071); OWRD-bonded well constructor (OAR 690-210); licensed LP-gas installer (ORS 480.430-480.460).
  Source: OAR chapter 340, division 71; OAR chapter 690, division 210; ORS 480.430-480.460.

Paying a helper makes the owner an employer.
  Employee: services for remuneration under direction (ORS 654.005(4)); employer: one or more employees (ORS 654.005(5)); casual labor outside workers' compensation only under the indexed ~$1,000-per-30-days threshold outside the employer's trade (ORS 656.027(3)).
  Source: ORS 654.005; ORS 656.027(3).
```

## 29.5 Utility Coordination

Utility timelines do not bend for the build schedule, so temporary power, the transformer, and the meter set are applied for early. The trench is dug once for power, communications, and any low-voltage conduit, with locates called before any digging.

**Simple**

The power company works on its own calendar. Temporary construction power can take weeks, and the transformer and meter can take longer, so the applications go in early. Before any digging, the utility-locate call (811) is required by law, even on bare land. One trench carries power and communications together, dug once and photographed before burial.

**Applied**

- Apply early for temporary construction power: a permitted, inspected construction service (up to 100 A under the manufactured-construction-service rule) with a 2-to-8-week utility lead time.
- Coordinate permanent service: the transformer pad and conduit are the customer's responsibility to the utility's spec (commonly 4-inch Schedule 40 PVC, 24 inches or deeper, with tracer wire), and the meter is set after the electrical inspection signs off the service equipment.
- Call 811 at least two business days before any digging; locates are required by law even on raw land.
- Dig one utility trench for power, communications, and low-voltage conduit with the required separations, and photograph it before backfill.
- Install a communications conduit with a pull string from the demarcation to the street even where the house is initially served by satellite or fixed wireless.

**Professional**

The temporary service is a permitted, inspected installation under the OESC and ORS 479, using a manufactured construction service (OAR 918-261-0038) that is exempt from licensing but not from permit and inspection. The permanent service follows the utility's standard for the transformer pad, conduit, service-entrance conductors, and meter base, with a conduit run longer than about 150 feet checked for voltage drop. Underground burial depths follow NEC 300.5 for power and Article 800 for communications, with the typical 12-inch power-to-communications separation, and the 811 locate requirement (ORS 757.541 to 757.571) is non-waivable. The outdoor emergency disconnect and the meter and disconnect placement are detailed in the electrical service chapter.

**Code**

```
Temporary construction power is permitted and inspected work.
  Manufactured construction service up to 100 A; licensing exempt, permit and inspection required.
  Source: OAR 918-261-0038; OESC (2023 edition).

Buried services meet the depth and separation rules.
  Power burial depths per NEC 300.5; communications per Article 800; typical 12-inch power-to-communications separation.
  Source: OESC/NEC 300.5 and Article 800 (2023 edition).

The locate call is required before any digging.
  811 notification at least two business days before excavation, including on raw land.
  Source: ORS 757.541-757.571.
```

## 29.6 Site Preparation And The Survey Line

Site preparation makes the land buildable and serviceable and installs the erosion control, driveway, and address before the structure. The survey rule splits cleanly: an owner may lay out the building from found monuments, and only a licensed surveyor may decide where the boundary runs.

**Simple**

An owner may measure from the existing corner monuments and stake where the house goes. What an owner may never do is decide where the property line itself runs, or replace a missing corner marker. That is licensed surveyor work, and doing it unlicensed is a crime. If a setback is tight enough that an inch matters, or a corner cannot be found, the surveyor answers the question. A boundary survey runs about $1,500-$4,000 (2026).

**Applied**

- Measure and stake the owner's own construction layout (building corners, setback offsets, grades) from found monuments; this is exempt owner work within the parcel.
- Hire a registered professional land surveyor to establish or retrace any boundary, replace any missing corner monument, or resolve any tight setback ($1,500-$4,000, 2026), and document the staking.
- Install erosion control at the start: silt fence at the lower perimeter, inlet protection, a gravel construction entrance, and covers on soil stockpiles left more than about two weeks in the wet season.
- Build the driveway to the approach permit and the fire-access standard (width, vertical clearance, turn radii, grade, and a turnaround for a driveway over 150 feet), with a culvert sized to the design storm where a ditch is crossed.
- Post address numbers at least 4 inches tall, visible from the street, with a separate sign at a long driveway entrance, during construction as well as after.
- Plan phased material staging so lumber, drywall, and finish materials each land protected and out of the access path.

**Professional**

The licensure line sits in ORS 672. Boundary work (establishing, reestablishing, or retracing property lines, or replacing monuments) is the practice of land surveying no matter whom it is done for (ORS 672.005(2)(c)-(d)). The owner exemptions (ORS 672.060(12) and (13)) cover land surveying performed by the owner on the owner's own land affecting exclusively the owner's interests. Measuring from found monuments to place the building at its setback is exempt owner layout; deciding where the line runs affects the neighbor's interests too and falls outside the exemption, and unlicensed boundary surveying is a Class A misdemeanor (ORS 672.991). Erosion control follows the DEQ 1200-C erosion-and-sediment-control plan where the disturbance reaches one acre, and a simplified local version below that threshold. The driveway meets the Oregon Fire Code 503 and Appendix D fire-access dimensions, and a culvert crossing in fish-bearing water triggers ODFW fish-passage review (ORS 509.605 to 509.910). Permanent stormwater management (dispersal, a bioswale or rain garden, or a drywell) is required by the local stormwater code and may need a civil-engineer stamp. Temporary sanitation and jobsite safety obligations under OR-OSHA attach once any paid worker is on site.

**Code**

```
Boundary determination is licensed surveying; owner layout is not.
  Establishing, reestablishing, or retracing boundaries or monuments requires a registered professional land surveyor (any client, including oneself where others' interests are affected); the owner may perform layout within the parcel from found monuments under the owner exemptions.
  Source: ORS 672.005(2)(c)-(d); ORS 672.025(1); ORS 672.060(12), (13).

Unlicensed boundary surveying is a crime.
  Class A misdemeanor; prosecutable within two years of discovery.
  Source: ORS 672.991(1)-(2); ORS 672.045(1).

Erosion control and access precede construction.
  DEQ 1200-C plan at one acre of disturbance (local program below); fire-access driveway per Oregon Fire Code 503 and Appendix D; address numbers at least 4 inches, visible from the street, during construction.
  Source: DEQ 1200-C; Oregon Fire Code 503, 505.1, Appendix D.

A crossing in fish-bearing water gets fish-passage review.
  ODFW review for culverts and crossings in fish-bearing streams.
  Source: ORS 509.605-509.910.
```

## 29.7 Excavation And Trench Safety

Excavation is the most dangerous common construction activity, so a trench 5 feet or deeper gets a protective system and the spoil is set back from the edge. These rules apply to any paid worker on site and are sound practice for solo work.

**Simple**

A collapsing trench wall buries a person in seconds under thousands of pounds of soil. The rules are cheap next to that. Anything 5 feet deep gets sloped or shored, the dirt pile stays back from the edge, and a ladder stays within reach. The trench is inspected every morning. Shallower is not automatically safe; someone competent still has to look.

**Applied**

- Provide a protective system (sloping, benching, shoring, or a trench shield) for any excavation 5 feet or deeper, and a registered-engineer design at 20 feet.
- Treat the under-5-foot relief as conditional: it applies only when a competent person has examined the ground and found no indication of a potential cave-in (or the cut is entirely in stable rock).
- Slope to the soil type (assume Type C granular or wet soil at about 1.5 horizontal to 1 vertical unless the soil is classified stiffer by a competent person).
- Keep spoil piles at least 2 feet from the trench edge, provide a ladder, ramp, or steps within 25 feet of lateral travel for any worker in a trench 4 feet or deeper, and do not enter a trench with accumulated water without controls.
- Have the competent person inspect the excavation daily before each shift and after rain, vibration, or added surcharge load, with authority to pull workers out.

**Professional**

Oregon OSHA adopts the federal excavation standard by reference (Division 3, Subdivision P, 29 CFR 1926.650-.652, plus the Oregon underground-installations rule OAR 437-003-0096). The protective-system requirement has two exceptions: cuts entirely in stable rock, and cuts under 5 feet where a competent-person examination finds no cave-in potential, so the shallow relief is an inspection outcome, not a depth entitlement. The competent person is defined by capability and authority: identifying existing and predictable hazards and being authorized to correct them, with daily and post-event inspections and the duty to remove exposed workers. Maximum allowable slopes for cuts under 20 feet are Type A 53 degrees, Type B 45 degrees, Type C 34 degrees; deeper cuts and any design outside the standard options require a registered professional engineer. Most Oregon residential soils classify Type B or C. Typical foundation footing excavations to about 4 feet keep the spoil setback and the competent-person look; full-height foundation-wall excavations to 8 to 10 feet need sloping or shoring; narrow deep utility trenches need a protective system at 5 feet. The standard applies to any paid worker; for solo owner work it is not a regulatory requirement but is followed because trench collapse is unforgiving. The specific frost depth and footing depth are set by the foundation chapter.

**Code**

```
Excavations 5 feet and deeper get a protective system.
  Sloping, benching, shoring, or shield; exceptions only for stable rock or an under-5-foot cut a competent person has examined and cleared; registered-PE design over 20 feet or outside the standard options.
  Source: 29 CFR 1926.652(a)(1), (b)(4), (c)(4); Subpart P Appendix B (OR-OSHA Division 3/P; OAR 437-003-0096).

Slopes follow the soil classification.
  Type A 53 degrees (3/4:1), Type B 45 degrees (1:1), Type C 34 degrees (1-1/2:1), for cuts under 20 feet.
  Source: 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P, Appendix B, Table B-1.

Egress, spoil setback, and daily inspection are required.
  Ladder, ramp, or steps within 25 feet of lateral travel in trenches 4 feet or deeper; spoil and equipment at least 2 feet from the edge; competent-person inspections daily and after rain or vibration, with authority to remove workers.
  Source: 29 CFR 1926.651(c)(2), (j)(2), (k); 1926.650(b).
```

## 29.8 Equipment, Transport, And Fuel

The build needs earthmoving, compaction, concrete, lifting, and power equipment, and each item is a buy, rent, or hire-with-operator decision. Getting the machine to the site has its own law: Oregon's commercial-license line counts weight, not business purpose, and jobsite fuel has quantity thresholds.

**Simple**

Renting the excavator is often the easy part; towing it is where owners get surprised. Oregon requires a commercial driver license purely by weight. A truck-and-trailer combination at 26,001 pounds or more, with a trailer over 10,000 pounds, needs a Class A license even for personal use. A mid-size excavator on a heavy trailer crosses that line. The practical answers: have the rental yard deliver, or keep the combination under the threshold.

**Applied**

- Match the equipment to the phase: an excavator or compact track loader for earthwork, a plate compactor or rammer for lift compaction, a mixer or pump for concrete placement, a vibrator for consolidation, a telehandler or boom truck for lifting, and a generator sized to the tool load and any well-pump startup.
- Buy tools used across more than one phase with low storage cost; rent equipment used once or twice with high storage and maintenance cost; hire-with-operator for crane, large excavation, and concrete pumping, where the operator carries the certification and insurance.
- Before towing equipment, compute the actual combination weight (truck plus loaded trailer): at 26,001 pounds or more with a trailer over 10,000 pounds, a Class A CDL is required in Oregon regardless of personal use; below it, verify the tow rig's own ratings.
- Have the rental yard deliver anything that puts the combination over the line.
- Haul fuel inside the practical thresholds: standard 5-gallon cans for the owner's own non-commercial use sit outside the hazardous-materials regulations, and a diesel transfer tank of 119 gallons or less stays non-bulk; over 119 gallons in business use brings shipping papers and placards.
- Confirm ROPS and seat belts on rented earthmoving equipment, use the reverse alarm or a spotter where the rear view is obstructed, and get current rental quotes from at least two Oregon yards.

**Professional**

Oregon defines the commercial motor vehicle by weight alone (ORS 801.208): a combination with a gross combination weight rating or actual weight of 26,001 pounds or more, towing a unit over 10,000 pounds, is Class A CDL territory. The statute's only exemptions are emergency vehicles, motor homes, and personal-use recreational vehicles. Unlike the federal definition, Oregon's has no commerce element, so an owner towing their own excavator crosses the line on weight alone, and the actual loaded weight counts where it exceeds the ratings. The farm endorsement substitutes for a CDL only for a farmer moving farm equipment for farm purposes within 150 miles, which does not cover construction hauling.

Fuel transport: an individual hauling fuel for non-commercial purposes in a private vehicle is outside the federal hazardous-materials regulations entirely (49 CFR 171.1(d)(6)). Once the hauling supports a business, the materials-of-trade rules allow up to 8 gallons per package (standard cans) with a 440-pound aggregate, and a diesel transfer tank at or under 119 gallons is non-bulk and effectively outside the regulations. Above 119 gallons in business use, shipping papers, markings, and placards attach.

Equipment safety on site: earthmoving equipment follows the material-handling standard (seat belts per SAE J386; rollover protective structures per Subpart W on loaders, dozers, and tractors; horn and a reverse alarm or spotter where the rear view is obstructed). Excavators, backhoes, and loaders are excluded from the crane standard even when lifting a suspended load with rigging, so an excavator placing pipe stays under the material-handling and excavation rules. A boom truck or crane rated over 2,000 pounds of hoisting capacity triggers the certified-operator regime, which is one reason crane work is hired with its operator. On-site concrete mixing equipment overlaps the make-versus-buy concrete decision in the foundation and concrete-placement chapter, and the block press for owner-manufactured units overlaps the materials chapter; local rental and purchase quotes are the project-level facts confirmed before a budget line is fixed.

**Code**

```
Oregon's CDL threshold counts weight, not commerce.
  Combination GCWR or actual weight 26,001 pounds or more with a towed unit over 10,000 pounds: Class A CDL; single vehicle 26,001 or more: Class B; exemptions only for emergency vehicles, motor homes, and personal-use recreational vehicles; the farm endorsement covers only farm hauling within 150 miles.
  Source: ORS 801.208; Oregon DMV commercial licensing requirements.

Owner fuel hauling sits outside the hazardous-materials regulations; business hauling has thresholds.
  Non-commercial private hauling exempt (49 CFR 171.1(d)(6)); materials of trade: 8 gallons per package, 440-pound aggregate; diesel transfer tank at or under 119 gallons is non-bulk; larger business-use tanks trigger papers and placards.
  Source: 49 CFR 171.1(d)(6); 49 CFR 173.6; 49 CFR 173.150(f); 49 CFR 172.504.

Earthmoving equipment carries its protective equipment.
  Seat belts (SAE J386); rollover protective structures per Subpart W; horn; reverse alarm or spotter with an obstructed rear view.
  Source: 29 CFR 1926.602; 29 CFR 1926.1000 (OR-OSHA Division 3/O).

Excavators are not cranes; cranes over 2,000 pounds need certified operators.
  Excavators, backhoes, and loaders excluded from the crane standard even when rigging suspended loads; hoisting equipment rated over 2,000 pounds requires operator certification, training, and evaluation.
  Source: 29 CFR 1926.1400(c)(2); 29 CFR 1926.1427; 29 CFR 1926.1441 (OR-OSHA Division 3/CC).
```

## 29.9 Concrete And Masonry Placement Sequencing

Concrete placement is governed by the weather window, the cure, and the backfill readiness, and the foundation inspection happens after the reinforcement and embeds are set but before the pour. Masonry has its own mortar and grout timing.

**Simple**

Three things set a pour date: the weather that day, the cure time before the next step, and the inspection that must pass first. Pouring against a failed inspection wastes the load. Backfilling against a green wall cracks it.

**Applied**

- Schedule foundation pours into the practical weather window (roughly March through November in the Willamette Valley without extensive protection; narrower east of the Cascades) and plan hot- or cold-weather protection outside it.
- Call the foundation inspection after the forms, reinforcement, anchor bolts, holdowns, and plumbing rough-ins through the footing are set and before concrete placement, with enough lead time that a failed inspection does not lose the concrete delivery slot.
- Do not backfill against a foundation wall until it has cured to design strength and the first floor is framed or braced to resist lateral earth pressure (typically 7 to 14 days plus a framed first floor for an 8-foot wall).
- Use mortar within 90 minutes of mixing and grout the masonry in the code lift heights, with cleanouts at the base of high-lift pours and temporary bracing on walls over 8 feet.
- Schedule a concrete pump where the truck cannot reach the placement point, and coordinate it with ready-mix delivery for continuous flow.

**Professional**

Hot-weather placement follows ACI 305.1 (concrete at delivery at or below 95°F, evaporation-rate control), and cold-weather placement follows ACI 306R (minimum placement temperatures and protected curing for the durations stated in the foundation and concrete-placement chapter). Formwork supporting structural load is not removed until the concrete reaches the specified strength (ACI 318 and 301), and backfilling too early is a leading cause of foundation-wall cracking. Waterproofing membranes require a cured, dry substrate within the manufacturer's temperature window (commonly a 7-to-14-day minimum cure), and the manufacturer's instructions control under ORSC R102.4. Masonry mortar follows the 90-minute board life of ASTM C270, and grout lifts follow TMS 602 (fine grout to 5 feet, coarse to 4 feet, with cleanouts above those heights). The concrete mix design, reinforcement, curing, and the waterproofing assembly are detailed in the foundation and concrete-placement chapter and the structural chapter; this chapter sequences them in time.

**Code**

```
Weather-window placement follows the concreting standards.
  Hot weather: delivery at or below 95°F with evaporation-rate control (ACI 305.1). Cold weather: minimum placement temperatures and protected curing (ACI 306R).
  Source: ACI 305.1; ACI 306R.

The foundation inspection precedes the pour, and strength precedes loading.
  Inspection after reinforcement and embeds are set, before placement; forms carrying structural load stay until specified strength; backfill after design strength with lateral bracing in place.
  Source: ORSC R109.1.1 (2023 edition); ACI 318; ACI 301.

Masonry follows its mortar life and grout lifts.
  Mortar used within 90 minutes of mixing (ASTM C270); grout lifts fine to 5 feet, coarse to 4 feet, cleanouts above (TMS 602); temporary bracing on walls over 8 feet.
  Source: ASTM C270; TMS 602.
```

## 29.10 Dry-In: Roof And Openings

Drying in the house (roof, windows, and the water-resistive barrier) is scheduled into a single reliable weather window, because an open structure in the rain damages the work already done. Windows are verified for egress before ordering.

**Simple**

Dry-in is the phase the weather controls. The roof, the windows, and the wall wrap turn the structure into a shelter. Until they are on, every rainstorm costs money. So the dry-in is planned as one continuous push inside the dry season, and nothing opens the structure with rain in the forecast.

**Applied**

- Schedule roof tear-off or dry-in into the reliable dry window (mid-June to mid-October in western Oregon), and never open the structure with rain in the 48-hour forecast unless the dry-in is ready.
- Order windows and exterior doors at the framing-decision point (6-to-16-week lead times are verified at order), and confirm each specified unit meets the egress net-clear-opening dimensions before ordering, not just the rough-opening size.
- Flash windows and doors in the shingle-style sequence (sill flashing first, unit, jamb flashing over the flange, head flashing lapped under the barrier above) per ASTM E2112 and the manufacturer's instructions.
- Install kick-out flashings where a roof slope meets a vertical wall, and the ice barrier where the climate requires it.
- Use fall protection for any roof work above 6 feet, and hire crane or boom-truck lifts with their certified operator for truss and heavy-member picks.

**Professional**

Roof coverings and flashings follow ORSC R905, gutters and downspouts follow R903, and the kick-out flashing at a roof-wall intersection is required by R903.2.1; the roofing manufacturer's listed system (underlayment, starter, nailing pattern, ridge ventilation) controls the warranty under R102.4. Window and door installation follows ASTM E2112 with AAMA 711/712 flashing products, and the manufacturer's rough-opening tolerance and fastening pattern govern the installation warranty. Egress openings meet ORSC R310 (5.7 square feet net clear, 24-inch height, 20-inch width, 44-inch maximum sill), and a unit that meets the rough opening but not the operable clear opening is a costly ordering error. Fall protection follows OR-OSHA Subpart M for any paid worker, and a hired boom truck rated over 2,000 pounds runs under the certified-operator regime stated in the equipment section. The envelope control layers and the window and door details are carried by the envelope and interiors chapters.

**Code**

```
Roofing, drainage, and flashings follow the roof chapter of the code.
  Roof covering and flashing per R905; gutters and downspouts per R903; kick-out flashing at roof-wall intersections per R903.2.1; the listed roofing system controls the warranty.
  Source: ORSC R905, R903, R903.2.1, R102.4 (2023 edition).

Fenestration is flashed to the practice standard and verified for egress.
  Installation and flashing per ASTM E2112 and AAMA 711/712; egress units meet the R310 net-clear dimensions as operated.
  Source: ASTM E2112; AAMA 711/712; ORSC R310 (2023 edition).

Roof work by paid workers uses fall protection.
  Fall protection above 6 feet per the residential construction rules.
  Source: OR-OSHA Division 3, Subdivision M (29 CFR 1926 Subpart M).
```

## 29.11 Rough-In Sequence And The Inspection Cascade

After the house is dried in, the rough systems go in from largest to smallest, and the framing inspection cannot proceed until every rough-trade inspection is signed off. A missed inspection stalls the whole cascade.

**Simple**

The rough systems go in by size: big drain pipes first, then ducts, then wires, then data cables. The order exists because a drain cannot bend around a wire, but a wire can bend around a drain. Every rough system gets inspected before the framing inspection, and framing passes before anything is covered.

**Applied**

Sequence the rough-in by clearance demand:

1. Plumbing drain-waste-vent and supply first (the heaviest pipes with the strictest slope), including vent penetrations for the roofer to flash.
2. Mechanical second (ducts, ventilator runs, refrigerant line-set sleeves with the brazing left for the certified technician, condensate, and exhaust).
3. Electrical third (branch circuits routed around the fixed plumbing and mechanical, boxes at finished-wall depth).
4. Low voltage last (structured cabling, coax, and control wiring, routed to keep the required separation from power).

Install firestopping and fireblocking before insulation, air-seal every penetration, and use only insulation-contact-rated recessed lights. Call the rough-trade inspections before the framing inspection, because framing cannot pass until they are signed off.

**Professional**

The inspection cascade under ORSC R109 runs foundation, rough plumbing, rough electrical, rough mechanical, framing (after all rough-ins and firestopping), insulation and air sealing, and drywall where required, then final; some jurisdictions combine steps, which is confirmed locally. The energy-code rough-in enables the later tests: the air-sealing detail sets up the blower-door result, and duct sealing sets up any duct-leakage requirement. The detailed plumbing, mechanical, electrical, and low-voltage designs are carried by their own chapters; this chapter fixes the order and the inspection dependencies, and the schedule is built with buffer because a failed inspection blocks everything downstream.

**Code**

```
The rough-trade inspections gate the framing inspection.
  Foundation, rough plumbing, rough electrical, rough mechanical, then framing after all rough-ins and firestopping, then insulation/air-sealing, drywall where required, then final; local combining confirmed with the jurisdiction.
  Source: ORSC R109 (2023 edition).

Firestopping and fireblocking go in before insulation.
  Draftstopping and fireblocking per the fire-resistance provisions, inspected at framing.
  Source: ORSC R302.11 (2023 edition).
```

## 29.12 Insulation, Slab, And Cover Readiness

Insulation, the radiant topping slab, and the wet-area and fire-rated assemblies are installed and inspected before cover, and the slab moisture is verified before any moisture-sensitive floor. These steps set up the finish work carried by the interiors chapter.

**Simple**

Before anything is covered, it gets tested, photographed, and inspected. The radiant tubing is pressure-tested before the slab buries it. The insulation and air sealing are inspected before drywall hides them. A photograph taken today is the map a repair crew uses in twenty years.

**Applied**

- Pressure-test the radiant tubing before the topping slab (1.5 times operating pressure, held and documented) and photograph the tubing before it is covered.
- Insulate to the climate-zone R-values after the framing and rough-trade inspections, and air-seal the assembly to the blower-door target.
- Test the slab moisture (ASTM F2170 in-situ relative humidity, at or below the flooring manufacturer's limit) before installing any moisture-sensitive floor.
- Install the wet-area backer and waterproofing membrane and the fire-rated garage-dwelling assembly to their listed systems, and the foam thermal barrier over any interior foam.
- Install and photograph the interconnected smoke and carbon monoxide alarms before final.

**Professional**

Insulation R-values follow ORSC N1102.1 by climate zone (the western Oregon valley is generally Climate Zone 4, with higher values at elevation and east of the Cascades), and the air-sealing detail is verified by the blower-door test on the BCD form before the certificate of occupancy. The topping slab, slab moisture testing, and finish-floor readiness are detailed in the interiors chapter, as are the wet-area waterproofing assembly, the fire-rated separation, and the foam thermal barrier. The radiant tubing, insulation, and slab work are sequenced here so the pressure test and the pre-cover photographs happen before the pour and the inspection.

**Code**

```
Insulation and air sealing meet the energy chapter and are verified by test.
  R-values per N1102.1 by climate zone; blower-door result on the BCD form before the certificate of occupancy.
  Source: ORSC N1102.1 and N1104.8.2 (2023 edition).

Slab moisture is verified before moisture-sensitive flooring.
  ASTM F2170 in-situ relative humidity at or below the flooring manufacturer's limit.
  Source: ASTM F2170 (see the interiors chapter).

The protected assemblies are complete before cover and final.
  Garage-dwelling separation (R302.6), foam thermal barrier (R316.4), interconnected smoke and CO alarms (R314/R315) installed and inspected.
  Source: ORSC R302.6, R316.4, R314, R315 (2023 edition).
```

## 29.13 Occupancy, Schedule, And Cost Discipline

Occupancy is a major milestone, not the end of the project, and it is reached through the certificate-of-occupancy checklist. The schedule and cost are tracked against dated quotes and the actual hours the build takes, which for an owner-builder run well beyond a contractor crew's.

**Simple**

An honest schedule uses the real labor pool. A three-person contractor crew dries in a modest house in one to two weeks. A solo owner doing the same work takes six to ten, with weather in between. A first attempt at any task takes about half again as long as the estimating manual lists. An honest schedule also prices the interim-housing and loan-interest months correctly.

**Applied**

- Reach the certificate of occupancy through the life-safety and utility checklist: alarms, egress, guards and handrails, GFCI and AFCI verification, energized permanent power, working water, waste, and heat, commissioned water heater and HVAC, potable well-water test and septic startup where applicable, and address numbers.
- Build the schedule backward from a target occupancy date with the land-use, permit, utility, and inspection lead times included; assume roughly one in four inspections needs re-work on a complex owner-builder build, and add 2 to 8 weeks per plan-revision cycle.
- Track cost against dated quotes (refresh within 30 days of order for materials over about $1,000 and subcontracts over about $5,000) and record the actual hours and cost per phase as the build's own labor database.
- Estimate labor from the published references (residential estimating manuals and builder-association databases) and multiply first-time tasks by about 1.5 for the learning curve.
- Plan the licensed-specialist visits (refrigerant startup, well, septic, LP gas) as fixed points around the owner's own work.

**Professional**

The certificate of occupancy requires all inspections signed off, the energy documentation filed, and the required testing documented, and a temporary certificate is discretionary. A solo owner-builder takes roughly two to four times as long as a contractor crew for the same scope, at a much lower cost per task for owner-installed work, so the schedule is built with the actual labor pool rather than a contractor timeline. Published labor-hour references are starting points adjusted upward for the first occurrence of each task. Phase durations and costs are verified at final scope and treated as dated facts, and the actual per-phase hours and costs are recorded because they become the project's own estimating database for later phases and for the documentation chapter's record. The occupancy documentation, the as-built record, and the maintenance handoff are in the documentation and maintenance chapter, and the inspection gates in detail in the inspection and approval chapter.

**Code**

```
The certificate of occupancy closes on a checklist, not a feeling.
  All inspections approved; energy documentation filed; required testing documented (blower door, well potability, septic startup where applicable); conditions of approval satisfied.
  Source: ORSC R110 (2023 edition); ORSC Chapter 11 (energy documentation).

A temporary certificate is discretionary and conditional.
  Life-safety items complete; written remaining-work list with a deadline; revocable on a missed deadline.
  Source: ORSC R110.4 (2023 edition).
```
