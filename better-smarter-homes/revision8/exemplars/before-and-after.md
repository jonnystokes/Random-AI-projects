# Before and after: the Revision 7 faults and their repairs

Every "before" is from the Revision 7 manuscript. Fault numbers refer to FAULT_CATALOG.md.

## A chapter opening (faults 27, 33)

**Before** (the wall chapter):

> This chapter specifies the assembly of the exterior wall: what gets built, in what order, from
> the first course above the foundation to the bond beam (the reinforced, grouted top course
> that ties the wall together) below the roof. It contains: [seven bullets]. Two walls are
> specified. [...] The engineering of both walls is in the Structural System And Concrete Design
> chapter. The control-layer physics is in the Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance,
> And Radon chapter. The hand skills of laying are in the Masonry Craft chapter. Unit
> manufacture and testing are in the Owner-Made Blocks And Alternative Concretes chapter.

**After:**

> The default wall is one thickness of reinforced concrete block with the insulation on the
> outside and the cladding hung over it, and it is the default because the code has pre-approved
> tables for it. The alternative is a cavity wall, block inside and brick outside with insulation
> and a drained gap between, which no table covers and an engineer designs from the first
> drawing. Both are drainage walls: they assume water gets past the face and give it a way out.
> The engineering for both is in the structural chapter.

## A Simple block (faults 29, 34, and the missing "you")

**Before** (the structural chapter, roof structure):

> The roof structure and the roof's weather surface have different lifespans. The structure is
> designed to last as long as the walls. The weather surface is replaced on a schedule. The
> table compares the three structural options on both lifespans.

**After:**

> Your roof is two things with two lifespans: the structure that holds it up, which should last
> as long as the walls, and the weather surface on top, which you will replace two or three
> times in a century. The design keeps them separate so the second never damages the first. On
> a concrete deck the membrane comes off and goes back on without touching the structure.
> The Oregon roof collapses on record broke at the connections under drifted snow, not in the
> framing, which is why the connections get the engineer's attention here.

## Research narration (fault 23)

**Before** (the owner-made blocks chapter):

> Batch on a cadence, not in bulk. Dry-cast mix loses pressability as it sits; keep batches
> sized so the press consumes each one promptly. No published number fixes the sit-time window;
> establish it by observation and write it into the production log.

**After** (body):

> Batch on a cadence, not in bulk. Dry-cast mix loses pressability as it sits, so size each
> batch to what the press uses in the next few minutes. Find the window for your mix by
> observation in the first week and write it into the production log.

**After** (Open items):

> - **Sit-time window for dry-cast mix.** Unknown: how long a batch stays pressable at this
>   mix and this site's temperatures. Answered by: observation during the first production week,
>   logged. Assumed meanwhile: batches sized to a few minutes of press consumption.

## A run of bold paragraphs (fault 25)

**Before** (the owner-made blocks chapter, mixes section): twelve consecutive paragraphs
opening "**Steel fibers.**", "**Bendable concrete (ECC).**", "**Natural fibers.**",
"**Accelerators.**", "**The early-strength stack.**", "**Supplementary cementitious
materials.**", "**Integral water repellents.**", "**Mortar for owner-made units.**",
"**Crystalline waterproofing admixtures.**", "**Pigments**".

**After:** three subheadings in sentence case ("Fibers", "Accelerators and early strength",
"Admixtures for water and appearance"), a comparison table of the fibers (dose, what it
changes, what it does not change, the test that measures it), and prose under each subheading
that says which of these this project uses and which it does not, before the detail.

## A Professional block that echoed Code (fault 28)

**Before** (the interiors chapter, escape openings):

> **Professional:** ORSC R310 sets the opening at 5.7 square feet net clear (5.0 at grade), 24
> inches minimum height, 20 inches minimum width, and a 44-inch maximum sill...
>
> **Code:** 5.7 square feet net clear (5.0 at grade-floor openings); 24 inches minimum height;
> 20 inches minimum width; sill not more than 44 inches above the floor...

**After** (Professional):

> The opening dimensions come from two bodies: a firefighter in turnout gear climbing in and an
> occupant crawling out. The net clear area is the product that admits the first. The minimum
> height and width each admit the second in one orientation. The sill limit is the height a
> child can climb without a chair. Oregon's amendment restored the 2021 wording on where the
> opening must lead, so the path to a public way is the test, not the model code's yard-width
> qualifier. A sprinklered basement can trade one rescue opening for two means of egress. Both
> configurations are in the Code block.

Code states the numbers once.
