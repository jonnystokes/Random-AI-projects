# Owner-Builder Sale, Lease, Occupancy, and Timing — Resolved

## Top-line for Chapter 4

**There is no statewide bright-line "X-year resale rule" in Oregon.** Your cautious wording is correct. Anyone telling you "you must wait three years to sell" is conflating different regimes or repeating a city's local interpretation as if it were state law. The actual controls are an *intent test*, two *5-year cooldowns* on specific exemptions, and one *75-day post-completion* lien-notice obligation. Below, each of your questions answered with the exact authority.

## What rule actually controls sale or lease timing?

There is no single timing rule. There are four overlapping regimes:

**(a) CCB licensing — intent test (ORS 701.010(5)).** The owner-builder licensing exemption does not apply to anyone who builds, remodels, or arranges construction "with the intent of offering the structure for sale before, upon or after completion. It is prima facie evidence that there was an intent of offering the structure for sale if the person who constructed, remodeled or repaired the structure or arranged to have the structure constructed, remodeled or repaired does not occupy the structure after its completion." [Oregon Legislature](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors701.html) No fixed time period — failure to occupy itself shifts the burden onto the owner to prove there was no intent to sell.

**(b) Structural code exemption — 5-year cooldown (ORS 455.320).** The statewide owner-builder structural exemption (ceiling heights, room sizes, temperature) defines "owner" as a person "which person has not taken advantage of the exemptions under subsection (2) of this section during the five years prior to applying for an exemption under this section." [Oregon Public Law](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_455.320) This is a cooldown between *exemptions*, not between *sales*.

**(c) Rural county exemption — 5-year cooldown (ORS 455.335).** Where a county has opted in under ORS 455.330, "no person shall receive a building permit in that county for an exempt owner-built dwelling and outbuildings more than once every five years." [LawServer](https://www.lawserver.com/law/state/oregon/or-statutes/oregon_statutes_455-335) Same: a cooldown on exemption use, not on resale.

**(d) Lien-notice obligation — 75 days post-completion (ORS 87.007 / 87.093).** Independent of any owner-builder question, anyone selling a residential structure within roughly 75 days after completion must serve the Notice of Compliance with Homebuyer Protection Act, and contractors who sell must serve the Information Notice to Owner on first purchasers within that window. This is a sale-disclosure rule, not a prohibition.

## New construction, substantial improvement, or both?

**Both, but the regimes attach differently.** ORS 701.010(5) covers "constructs, remodels, repairs" [Oregon Legislature](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors701.html) — so new construction *and* substantial improvement are covered by the intent-to-sell test. ORS 455.320(2) explicitly covers "new construction, renovation, remodeling or alteration of an owner-built dwelling or outbuilding." [Oregon Public Law](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_455.320) The rural exemption (ORS 455.330) applies to "owner-built dwellings and outbuildings" generally.

## When does the period start?

Where there *is* a period, the trigger varies and is usually not crisply defined:

- **ORS 455.320 5-year exemption cooldown:** runs five years from the prior *exemption use* (the prior building permit issuance under the exemption), looking *backward* from the new application.
- **ORS 455.335 rural-county 5-year limit:** runs from the *prior building permit issuance* for an exempt owner-built dwelling.
- **ORS 87.007 / 87.093 75-day window:** runs from *completion of construction* (substantial completion is the working definition under ORS 87.045).
- **CCB intent test:** has no clock — it is a fact question evaluated as of the construction period and at sale, with non-occupancy as the trigger for prima facie evidence.

The statute books contain no "certificate of occupancy + N years" or "final inspection + N years" rule for owner-builders. CO/TCO/final inspection do not start an owner-builder resale clock anywhere I can find in ORS Chapter 455, ORS Chapter 701, or OAR Chapter 812.

## 1, 3, 5, or other?

- **1 year:** none for owner-builder resale. (One-year rules exist in unrelated places — e.g., the 12-month bar on a home inspector contracting on a structure they inspected, OAR 812-005-0800(28).)
- **3 years:** none for owner-builder resale anywhere in ORS 455 or 701. Newly enacted HB 3746 reduced *condominium* statute of repose to 7 years and added a 2-year/6-year inspection regime, but that is condo defect law, not owner-builder timing.
- **5 years:** the structural exemption cooldown (455.320) and the rural-county exemption cooldown (455.335). Both are about *exemption frequency*, not resale timing.
- **75 days:** the post-completion lien/disclosure window.
- **6 months:** appears in **Portland city guidance only** as the threshold for whether a homeowner may pull their own electrical permit (Portland.gov: "You can only do the electrical work if you own and occupy the home and are not planning on selling or renting within 6 months." [Portland.gov](https://www.portland.gov/ppd/residential-permitting/home-projects) ). This number is **not in ORS 479.540, OAR Chapter 918, or the BCD's statewide homeowner acknowledgement form**. It is Portland's bright-line interpretation of the statutory phrase "not intended for sale, exchange, lease or rent." Other jurisdictions may use different thresholds or none at all.

## What kind of rule — licensing, exemption, tax, disclosure?

A mix, depending on which regime:

- ORS 701.010 → **contractor licensing** (CCB jurisdiction)
- ORS 479.540 / 693.020 → **trade licensing** (electrical/plumbing)
- ORS 455.320, 455.330–.350 → **structural code exemption + permanent deed-record disclosure**
- ORS 87.007 / 87.093 → **lien-law disclosure**
- HB 3746 / ORS 12.115/12.135 → **statute of repose / construction defect**

Federal tax treatment (capital gains §121 exclusion, IRS treatment of "dealer" property) is a separate analysis the Oregon statutes do not address.

## Legal effect of selling before the period ends?

- **Selling without a CCB license while having had intent to sell:** subject to CCB enforcement under ORS 701.098 and civil penalties under ORS 701.992 and OAR 812-005-0800; loss of standing to bring a claim before the CCB (Tandem Properties confirms this); potential lien-rights problems; potential implied-warranty exposure as a "speculative" builder under OAR 812-002-0680, defined as a structure constructed "in anticipation of or with the intent of selling to another entity during or after construction." [Theglb](https://theglb.com/publiations/2020/3/24/construction-law-cle-chap-9-residential-construcion-8-19)
- **Selling within the ORS 455.320 5-year window after using the exemption:** does not invalidate the prior exemption or void the sale (455.320(4) explicitly says noncompliance with the recording rule shall not affect any conveyance), but bars use of the exemption again on a new permit until 5 years have passed.
- **Selling without serving the 75-day Notice of Compliance with Homebuyer Protection Act:** the ORS 87.007 obligation falls on the seller; failure can affect lien risk to the buyer.
- **Doing electrical work yourself and selling within Portland's 6-month threshold:** Portland city enforcement risk, possible permit revocation, but the underlying statutory test is intent — if you can show no intent to sell at the time the work was permitted, the violation collapses.
- **Renting after building under the rural-county exemption (ORS 455.330):** triggers ORS 455.345 purchaser-notice obligations and the lessee/buyer remedies in ORS 455.350.

## Does intent matter, actual sale matter, occupancy matter?

All three, weighted differently:

- **Intent at the time of construction** is the legal pivot under ORS 701.010(5).
- **Non-occupancy after completion** is the **prima facie evidence** of that intent (statutory presumption — burden shifts to the owner).
- **Actual sale or rental** is *evidence* of intent but not itself the violation; you can violate the statute before any sale occurs.
- **The owner-builder structural exemption (ORS 455.320)** requires that the owner *intend to occupy*; failure to occupy doesn't void the exemption retroactively but undermines the eligibility.

Tandem Properties LLC v. CCB, 184 Or App 28 (2002) is the controlling case: "owners of property who arrange for work to be done on residences that they occupy with the intent of later offering the structure for sale are excluded from the licensing requirements because they are not in the 'business' of developing and selling real property." [Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/oregon/court-of-appeals/2002/a115888.html) Reverse the facts (don't occupy, do intend to sell) and you become a contractor by operation of law.

## Spouses, trusts, LLCs, and other entities?

This is where things get sharp:

- **Spouses:** Oregon is not a community-property state, but transfers to spouses default to tenancy by the entirety under ORS 93.180(1)(b). A spouse on title satisfies "owner" for ORS 455.320 purposes. For the electrical exemption (ORS 479.540), "property is your primary residence...work will be done by the homeowner or an immediate family member. Immediate family members include: mother, father, sister, brother, daughter or son. Extended family does not count by State of Oregon Rules" [Portland](https://www.portland.gov/bds/residential-permitting/residential-electrical-permits/residential-electrical-permits-lights) — spouses are typically treated as a single ownership unit, but Portland's enumeration notably *omits* spouses from "immediate family" for the trade-permit context. The CCB definition under OAR 812-002-0275 is broader: "parents, spouses, sisters, brothers, daughters, sons, daughters-in-law, sons-in-law, or grandchildren." [Oregon Public Law](https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_812-002-0275)
- **Trusts:** A trust can hold title and the trustee can be the "owner" for some purposes, but the owner-builder exemptions assume a *natural person* who intends to occupy. A revocable living trust where the beneficiary-occupant is also the trustee is generally accepted by jurisdictions; an irrevocable trust where the occupant is only a beneficiary is shakier and varies by jurisdiction. There is no statewide bright-line rule on this — local building departments make the call.
- **LLCs:** Property in an LLC name is presumptively a "developer" structure under ORS 701.005(6) ("Developer means a contractor that owns property...with the intent to sell"). An LLC owner-builder is treated as a contractor *prima facie*; the exemption is not designed for LLC ownership. This is a frequent compliance trap and the factual posture closest to Tandem Properties.
- **Married couples filing as an LLC for the project:** worst of both worlds — looks speculative, loses the natural-person owner-occupant posture.

## Unpaid family/friends helping — labor, insurance, tax, workers' comp?

Yes, but narrowly. ORS 455.320(1)(b) defines an owner-built dwelling as one whose "structural components of which are constructed entirely by the owner who intends to occupy the structures or by that owner and friends and relatives of the owner assisting on an unpaid basis." [Oregon Public Law](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_455.320) Note the words *entirely* and *unpaid* — pay anyone and you've left the exemption.

The duties created when you hire help (paid):

- **Workers' compensation (ORS 656.027):** All workers are subject workers unless an exception applies. Two exceptions matter here:
  - *Householder/repair exception (ORS 656.027(2)):* "A worker employed to do gardening, maintenance, repair, remodeling or similar work in or about the private home of the person employing the worker" [Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/oregon/volume-16/chapter-656/section-656-027/) is nonsubject. **Caveat:** Caddy v. SAIF, 110 Or App 353 (1991) limited this to "work similar to listed types and performed in or around an already existing private home" [Oregon Public Law](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_656.027) — meaning *new construction from scratch* on raw land likely falls *outside* this exemption.
  - *Casual labor (ORS 656.027(3)):* nonsubject if total labor cost in any 30-day period is less than the indexed threshold (originally $500, $1,000 in the 2023 ORS edition, indexed annually), AND the work is not in the course of the employer's trade or business.
- **Income tax withholding & unemployment insurance:** if workers are not licensed contractors, the homeowner is treated as their employer (the statewide BCD form spells this out — see below).
- **Liability insurance:** the homeowner-applicant must carry liability sufficient to cover non-employee injuries on the jobsite (the BCD form recommends this explicitly).
- **Federal:** Social Security and federal income tax withholding obligations attach to the homeowner-employer.

The defensible posture for the manuscript: **truly unpaid family/friends helping with structural work fits ORS 455.320; everyone else triggers employer obligations under ORS 656, ORS 316, and federal employment law.** The only safe sub-case for paid help is using actual licensed CCB-endorsed subcontractors and complying with the 2008 amendment requiring permit-required work be under the direction of a licensed general contractor.

## Exact homeowner acknowledgement form text

The statewide BCD form (last modified 1/2/2025) titled *Information Notice to Owners About Construction Responsibilities (ORS 701.325(3))* has the homeowner check the following on page 2:

"I will be performing work on property I own, a residence that I reside in, or a residence that I will reside in. If I hire subcontractors, I will hire only subcontractors licensed with the Construction Contractors Board, and for Plumbing and Electrical Permits subcontractors also licensed with Building Codes Division. If I change my mind and hire a general contractor, I will select a contractor who is licensed with the CCB and will immediately give the name of the contractor to the office issuing this Building Permit." [Oregon](https://www.oregon.gov/BCD/epermitting/Documents/aca/homeowner-acknowledgement.pdf)

A second mandatory check: "I understand that this permit is non-transferable to another person, company, or contractor." [Oregon](https://www.oregon.gov/BCD/epermitting/Documents/aca/homeowner-acknowledgement.pdf)

Two optional checks if applicable: that the applicant will perform their own electrical work in compliance with ORS 479.540, and/or their own plumbing work in compliance with ORS 693.020.

The closing certification: "I have read and understand the Information Notice to Homeowners About Construction Responsibilities, and I hereby certify that I am the homeowner applying for this permit and that the information on this homeowner statement is true and accurate." [Oregon](https://www.oregon.gov/BCD/epermitting/Documents/aca/homeowner-acknowledgement.pdf)

The page 1 disclosures the applicant is acknowledging include: that homeowners using unlicensed labor may be considered an employer subject to Oregon withholding tax, unemployment insurance, BIN registration, workers' compensation insurance, federal Social Security and income tax withholding; that the homeowner is responsible for code compliance and inspection scheduling; and that the homeowner should obtain liability and property damage insurance covering accidents and injuries to non-employees on the site.

The form does **not** contain any "I will not sell for X months/years" attestation. The sale/rent restriction comes from the substantive exemption statutes, not from this form.

## What isn't in the law (worth stating in Chapter 4)

- No statewide statutory minimum holding period for an owner-builder before sale.
- No statutory definition of "primary residence" in the owner-builder context — the test is occupancy + intent, not days-of-occupancy.
- No CO/TCO trigger that starts an owner-builder timing clock anywhere.
- No prohibition on a later-arising change of intent (e.g., job relocation, divorce) — the test is intent at the time of construction. A genuine post-construction change of circumstance is a defense, not a violation.

## Build it, live in it, sell it — Oregon

**Up front, before you build:**
- Title in your individual name (not LLC, not "build-and-flip" trust). Sign the BCD homeowner statement saying you'll occupy.
- Pull all permits (structural, electrical, plumbing, mechanical) yourself or through licensed subs. CCB-licensed GC not required if you're truly the owner-builder.
- Any paid help = workers' comp, withholding, BIN, liability insurance. Unpaid family/friends only = clean.

**During build:** pass all inspections, keep the permit record clean, get final inspection / certificate of occupancy.

**After completion — the only timing rules that exist:**
- **Day 0–75:** if you sell in this window, serve the Notice of Compliance with Homebuyer Protection Act (ORS 87.007). Lien risk window is open.
- **After day 75:** lien-notice obligation drops off.
- **Move in and actually live there.** This is the legal pivot — occupancy rebuts the "intent to sell" presumption in ORS 701.010(5). No statutory minimum, but the longer and more genuinely you occupy, the stronger your position.
- **No statewide waiting period to sell.** Not 6 months, not 1 year, not 3 years.

**Practical safe-harbor most Oregon construction attorneys would give you:** occupy as your primary residence for **12+ months** with utilities, mail, voter registration, and tax filings to match. Federal §121 capital-gains exclusion separately wants **2 of the last 5 years** of ownership-and-use to exclude up to $250K/$500K of gain — that's usually the binding timeline, not state law.

**Then sell normally** as an owner-occupant. No CCB exposure, no exemption clawback, no special disclosure beyond standard Oregon seller property disclosures.

The trap to avoid: don't move out before sale or list while still finishing — that's what triggers the prima facie intent-to-sell finding.










# Owner-Builder Trade Scope — Oregon

## Plumbing (ORS 693.020)

The plumbing exemption is the broadest of the three. "A person working on a building or premises owned by the person, regardless of whether the person holds a license under this chapter, if the person complies with all the rules adopted under this chapter and ORS 447.010 to 447.156 [Oregon Public Law](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_693.020) and ORS chapter 455" is exempt from journeyman-plumber licensure. Note what's *not* in the statute: there's no sale/lease/rent restriction in the plumbing exemption itself (unlike electrical), and no immediate-family extension. The permit and inspection are still required (ORS 693.020(4)).

What this covers in practice: water supply piping, drain/waste/vent (DWV), fixtures, water heaters, sewer line, water service, rain drains, irrigation backflow, plumbed water features. Anything inside walls/ceilings/floors and anything new requires a permit. Same-fixture replacements without altering concealed piping are exempt from permit (faucets, toilets, sinks, hose bibbs, water filters, garbage disposals).

The hard limit: LP gas piping/containers — see below.

## Electrical (ORS 479.540)

Narrower scope. The owner-occupant exemption only applies if "a person is not required to obtain a license to make an electrical installation on residential or farm property that is owned by the person or a member of the person's immediate family if the property is not intended for sale, exchange, lease or rent." [public](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_479.540) The work still must comply with the Oregon Electrical Specialty Code, and a permit and inspection are still required.

What "immediate family" means: per Portland's interpretation of state rules, "mother, father, sister, brother, daughter or son. Extended family does not count by State of Oregon Rules" [Portland](https://www.portland.gov/bds/residential-permitting/residential-electrical-permits/residential-electrical-permits-lights) — though CCB's broader definition under OAR 812-002-0275 includes spouses, daughters/sons-in-law, and grandchildren. For trade-permit purposes, treat the narrower Portland enumeration as the working rule unless your local jurisdiction says otherwise.

Permit-required electrical work: any permanent wiring, new circuits, panel upgrades, fuse-to-breaker conversions, garage-door opener receptacles, low-voltage systems (alarms, AV). Permit-exempt: like-for-like replacement of outlets, switches, fixtures with GFCI installed; light bulbs/tubes; resetting fuses/breakers; replacing a 30-amp-or-less garbage disposal/dishwasher; low-voltage garage door wiring.

The permit itself is non-transferable. If the property is rented, leased, or owned by a landlord, the owner-occupier exemption is gone — must use a licensed electrical contractor.

## Mechanical (Oregon Mechanical Specialty Code via OAR 918-440)

No statewide statutory licensing exemption written for mechanical the way 693.020 and 479.540 carve out plumbing and electrical, but in practice "a homeowner who owns and occupies the house may do any or all of the work (building, plumbing, mechanical {heating and air conditioning}, and electrical)" [Silverton, OR](https://www.silverton.or.us/development/page/homeowner-information) through the BCD homeowner-applicant framework — the controlling concept is that the CCB licensing exemption (ORS 701.010) covers the contracting side, and there's no separate trade license for HVAC the way there is for electrical and plumbing.

Permit required for: installing/altering/repairing any vented heating or cooling system, unvented decorative gas appliances, woodstoves/fireplace inserts/pellet stoves and their venting, ductwork, gas piping between meter/tank and appliances (indoors and outdoors), bath fans, dryer exhausts, kitchen range exhausts, heat pumps, mini-splits, AC condensers. Replacement equipment requires a permit too — there is "no blanket exemption for residential equipment replacement" [Oregonhvacauthority](https://oregonhvacauthority.com/oregon-hvac-permit-requirements) and a like-for-like furnace swap still needs a mechanical permit and final inspection.

Refrigerant containment work on heat pumps/AC requires EPA Section 608 certification regardless of homeowner status. Heat pumps require both a mechanical permit AND a separate electrical permit.

## What requires a licensed trade contractor regardless

**LP gas (any work).** This is the biggest gotcha and worth a sidebar in the manuscript. ORS 480.432 prohibits *any person*, "either as employer or individual," from doing LP gas installation, fitting, or piping work without a State Fire Marshal license. "The State Fire Marshal's licensing law requires anyone, including homeowners, who install, extend, alter, or repair any LP gas appliance or piping, vent or flue connection must be licensed through them." [Building Codes Division](https://www.oregon.gov/bcd/lbdd/pages/oregon-permits.aspx) The only statutory exemptions are (a) a journeyman plumber may do LP gas piping but not tank/container work, (b) certain boiler-licensed pressure-piping installers (same restriction on tanks), and (c) work on manufactured dwellings/RVs during construction or warranty repair. **There is no homeowner exemption for LP gas.** Contrast with natural gas — which the BCD explicitly says "Homeowners or a licensed contractor may legally install natural gas piping." [Woodburn-or](https://www.woodburn-or.gov/faqs?keys=&field_tags_tid=621)

**Septic / on-site sewage disposal (DEQ jurisdiction, OAR 340-071).** Required: a DEQ Sewage Disposal Service installer license. "Alternatively, the property owner or the property owner's regular employee may construct or install a septic system with an issued septic installation permit." [Portland.gov](https://www.portland.gov/ppd/septic-permits) So the owner-builder can install their own septic if they hold the permit, but per OAR 340-071-0650 the system installation must be supervised by a person certified through the Chemeketa CC training course unless the installer is the permittee themselves. Site evaluation, plan review, pre-cover inspection, and certificate of satisfactory completion are all DEQ/county functions and not waivable.

**Water well drilling (OWRD jurisdiction, ORS 537.747).** Either a licensed Water Supply Well Constructor does it, or the landowner gets a Landowner Well Permit. "A property owner who is not licensed under ORS 537.747 who constructs, alters, or abandons a water supply well on their own property shall have a Landowner Well Permit as described in OAR 690-205-0175 for each water supply well on which work is done." [Oregon Secretary of State](https://secure.sos.state.or.us/oard/displayDivisionRules.action?selectedDivision=3183) Requires a $550 permit fee, a surety bond (typical homeowner's insurance agent can write it), construction to the same OAR 690-200 through 690-240 standards as licensed drillers, day-of-drilling notification, four-hour-advance seal-placement notification, well log signed by OWRD staff and landowner, and a $300 groundwater-use recording fee. "OWRD discourages landowners from constructing their own well." [Oregon Department of Human Services](https://sharedsystems.dhsoha.state.or.us/DHSForms/Served/le8316.pdf) Permit is valid 6 months.

**Fire sprinklers.** Oregon's ORSC did *not* adopt mandatory residential automatic sprinklers for one- and two-family dwellings — "The Oregon Residential Specialty Code (ORSC) did not adopt automatic fire sprinkler systems as a requirement for all new construction" [Portland.gov](https://www.portland.gov/ppd/codes-rules-and-guides/bod-15-01-nfpa-13d-sprinklers-garages) . So unlike California and Washington, a typical Oregon SFR build doesn't require sprinklers at all. Where they *are* required (R-3/R-4 occupancies under OSSC, certain adult foster homes, some local jurisdictions adding requirements, NFPA 13D scenarios) the system must be designed by a knowledgeable trained person and installed under permit — and because NFPA 13D systems are typically connected to the domestic water supply and use plumbing-code piping, plumbing licensing rules (ORS 693.020) determine who installs them. There's no separate "sprinkler installer" license like California has.

**Boiler / pressure vessel work (ORS 480.630).** Separate boiler license required for boilers and pressure piping. Water heaters above certain BTU/pressure thresholds may cross into this jurisdiction.

## Service connection and utility-side work

Everything from the utility's meter or service head outward is the homeowner's responsibility (with permits as above). Everything upstream of the meter — service drop, transformer, service pipe to the main — belongs to the utility and is not permitted by the local building department. The exception is the service entrance conductor and service equipment: those are on the homeowner side and require an electrical permit.

For wells, the well itself is OWRD jurisdiction; the piping from the wellhead to the house and the pump system are typically handled under plumbing permits (with carve-outs in ORS 693.020 for potable water pump equipment up to 7.5 horsepower on residential property — owner can do this without any plumbing license).

## Tenant, landlord, employee restrictions

The owner-occupier requirement is strict. ORS 90.145 lets a tenant do repairs, routine maintenance, or cleaning in exchange for rent reduction, but explicitly prohibits the tenant from doing electrical or plumbing installation, maintenance, or repair without proper licensure. A landlord can replace a like 30-amp-or-less appliance (garbage disposal, dishwasher, electric water heater) under ORS 479.540(1)(b), but anything new or substantial requires a licensed electrical contractor. Property managers, landlords' employees, and business entities can't use the owner-occupier exemption for someone else's residence.

## Who pulls which permit

The rule of thumb under ORS 455.050: the entity doing the work pulls the permit and signs for it.

- **Homeowner does the work themselves:** homeowner pulls the permit, signs the BCD Property Owner Statement, takes responsibility for inspections and code compliance.
- **Licensed contractor does the work:** contractor pulls the permit. For electrical, this is mandatory by state law — if a licensed electrical contractor does the work, *they* must pull the permit; the homeowner cannot pull an electrical permit and have an unlicensed person work under it.
- **Mixed (homeowner GCs, hires licensed subs for trades):** the homeowner pulls the building permit and signs the homeowner statement; each licensed sub signs and pulls their own trade permit application. Per the 2008 amendment to ORS 701.010, an owner who directly hires subcontractors for *permit-required* work loses the homeowner-GC exemption unless the work is performed under the direction of a licensed general contractor.

Permits expire if work isn't started within 180 days or is suspended for 180 days. Permits are non-transferable to another person, company, or contractor.

# Certificate of Occupancy — Oregon Residential

## What ORSC R110 and OAR 918-480-0140 actually require

The governing authority is ORS 455.055 (uniform permit/inspection/CO requirements), implemented through OAR 918-480-0140 and reflected in ORSC Section R110. The bottom-line rule from R110.1: "A building or structure shall not be used or occupied in whole or in part, and a change in existing character or use, or a change of occupancy of a building or structure or portion thereof shall not be made, until the building official has issued a certificate of occupancy." [UpCodes](https://up.codes/viewer/oregon/irc-2021/chapter/1/scope-and-administration)

## Final C of O — what's required before issuance

Three things must converge:

**(1) All inspections passed and approved.** Per ORSC R110.3, after the building official inspects and finds no violations of the code or other laws enforced by the department, the building official "shall issue" a certificate of occupancy. The "shall issue" language is mandatory — once compliance is verified, the official does not have discretion to withhold. In practice this means each of the five inspection disciplines must be finaled: building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, and site development (the last includes permanent erosion control, on-site stormwater control, sanitary facilities, and landscape requirements including those mandated by zoning or land-use review).

**(2) Contractor information disclosure.** OAR 918-480-0140(3) is the statewide hard requirement: "Before the certificate of occupancy is issued, the general contractor or owner who was issued the structural permit for construction must provide to the building official the contact information and relevant license information for the general contractor, as well as any electrical contractor, H-VAC contractor and plumbing contractor that performed work on the residential dwelling or townhouse." [Oregon Public Law](https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-480-0140) BCD provides Form 4823 ("Notification of Contractor License Information") for this purpose. Failure to submit will delay issuance.

**(3) The CO document itself contains the statutory minimum content** under R110.3: permit number, address, owner name, use of building, building official's name, code edition under which the permit was issued, and any special stipulations or conditions of the permit.

C of O scope: only required for new residential dwelling units, duplexes, and ADUs. Not required for residential additions, alterations, or accessory structures like garages.

## Temporary C of O — what's required

ORSC R110.4 gives the building official authority to "issue a temporary certificate of occupancy before the completion of the entire work covered by the permit, provided that such portion or portions shall be occupied safely. The building official shall set a time period during which the [TCO is valid]." [Building Codes Division](https://www.oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/Documents/2021orsc-ch1.pdf)

The TCO is discretionary, not mandatory. It's available on request from the property owner or representative when not all permitted work is complete, but only after specific conditions are met. Portland's program guide is the most-cited model and is consistent with how most Oregon jurisdictions handle TCOs:

- **Minimum fire and life safety requirements complete and approved** through the inspection process — the building official defines what counts as minimum on a project basis
- **Temporary erosion control measures in place**
- **All five inspection disciplines agree** the TCO may be issued
- **Issuance fee paid** before the TCO is released
- **Surety/security may be required** for unfinished work — Portland sets surety at 120% of the value of remaining work
- The TCO document lists outstanding items, completion date, and expiration date

## Who decides

The local building official has sole authority. The state sets the floor in OAR 918-480-0140 and ORSC R110, but local jurisdictions administer it. Each jurisdiction (city or county building department) has its own program guide and procedures. Authority is non-delegable to the homeowner, contractor, or any third party. The building official's decision is appealable to the local appeals board under ORSC R112 if the appellant believes the official misapplied or misinterpreted the code.

## Extensions

TCO extensions are allowed at the building official's discretion, prior to expiration. Portland's model: "TCO's are valid for 30 days from the date of issuance" [Portland.gov](https://www.portland.gov/ppd/documents/residential-certificate-occupancy-and-temporary-certificate-occupancy-program-guide/download) and can be extended in 30-day increments with each extension requiring a request before the current TCO expires, a written rationale describing the items needing extension, and a fee equal to the initial issuance fee. There is no statewide cap on the number of extensions, but each extension is at the building official's discretion and PP&D notes that "if after any allowed extensions all outstanding requirements have not been met" [Portland.gov](https://www.portland.gov/ppd/documents/residential-certificate-occupancy-and-temporary-certificate-occupancy-program-guide/download) the city may commence enforcement action.

State-level minimum: ORSC R110.4 just says the building official "shall set a time period" and gives no cap, so this is purely a local matter. Other jurisdictions (Bend, Eugene, Salem, county departments) use comparable 30-day or 60-day windows.

## Does any owner-builder timing rule depend on CO/TCO/final inspection/actual occupancy?

**No statewide owner-builder timing rule keys off any of these events.** This is the answer Chapter 4 should state plainly. Specifically:

- **ORS 701.010 intent-to-sell test:** the trigger is intent at the time of construction, with non-occupancy *after completion* as prima facie evidence. The statute does not define "completion" by reference to CO, TCO, or final inspection. Actual occupancy is what rebuts the presumption — moving in physically, not the issuance of the paper.
- **ORS 455.320 5-year structural exemption cooldown:** runs from prior permit issuance, not from CO.
- **ORS 455.335 rural-county 5-year limit:** runs from prior permit issuance, not from CO.
- **ORS 87.007 / 87.093 75-day disclosure window:** runs from "completion of construction," which under ORS 87.045 is keyed to *substantial completion*, not CO. Substantial completion can precede CO (and frequently does in TCO situations).
- **No 1/3/5/6-year rule keys off CO** anywhere in ORS Chapters 455, 701, or 87, or in OAR Chapters 812 or 918.

What CO/TCO *does* matter for, separately:

- **Legal occupancy:** you cannot legally move in without one or the other. ORSC R110.1 makes this absolute.
- **Federal §121 capital gains exclusion:** the IRS uses an ownership-and-use test (2 of 5 years). The "use" clock starts from when you actually occupy the home as your principal residence. Since legal occupancy requires CO/TCO, the practical earliest start is the date you move in following TCO/CO issuance.
- **Lender disbursement:** most construction-to-perm loans require CO before converting; some accept TCO with conditions.
- **Insurance binding:** homeowners' policies typically require CO or TCO before binding occupancy coverage; vacant-construction coverage falls off when occupancy begins.
- **Property tax assessment:** the assessor may treat substantial completion (often correlated with TCO) as the trigger for adding improvement value to the roll, separate from CO issuance.
- **Address/utilities/services:** USPS, USPS, voter registration, school enrollment all require an occupiable address; CO/TCO is the building department's confirmation the address exists as a residence.

The intent-to-sell defense the manuscript should anchor on is **actual occupancy with utility, mail, voter, and tax-residency evidence** — not CO/TCO issuance. CO/TCO is the *gate* that lets you start the occupancy clock; it isn't the clock itself.

## Documents required before CO (consolidated checklist)

1. Final approved building inspection
2. Final approved electrical inspection
3. Final approved plumbing inspection
4. Final approved mechanical inspection
5. Final approved site development inspection (erosion control, stormwater, sanitary, landscape per zoning/land-use review)
6. **OAR 918-480-0140(3) contractor information form** (BCD Form 4823 or local equivalent) listing GC, electrical, HVAC, plumbing contractor contact + license info
7. Any project-specific deferred submittal closures (engineering, fire suppression if applicable, geotechnical, special inspection reports)
8. Any local jurisdiction add-ons: address verification, school district excise tax certification, system development charge confirmation, septic/well certificate of satisfactory completion if applicable
9. Payment of any outstanding permit, plan review, or surcharge fees

Many jurisdictions also require: as-built plans for septic if owner-installed, well log filed with OWRD if a landowner well, DEQ certificate of satisfactory completion for septic, and SFM tank installation report for any LP gas installations.

# BSH LLC and Brand Separation — Oregon

## Threshold reality check on "engineered"

The single most significant legal exposure in your current model is the word **engineered**. Calling a home "engineered by Better Smarter Homes LLC" — in marketing, on a YouTube video, in a sale listing, or anywhere commercial — implicates Oregon's professional engineering statutes regardless of how you structure the LLC.

ORS 672.007(1) defines practicing or offering to practice engineering as including any person who "by verbal claim, sign, advertisement, letterhead or card or in any other way implies that the person is or purports to be a professional engineer or registered professional engineer" [Oregon Legislature](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors672.html) , or who through any other title implies engineering practice, or who bids to perform or performs the work defined as engineering. ORS 672.020 then makes that practice without registration unlawful.

OSBEELS actively enforces this. Their published disciplinary actions show repeated $1,000-$7,000 civil penalties for unlicensed individuals who stamped, signed, or held themselves out as engineers — including "Wolden had engaged in the practice of engineering as defined in ORS 672.005(1) without a license" [Oregon](https://www.oregon.gov/osbeels/rulesstatutes/Pages/Disciplinary-Actions.aspx) and "Dolatabady falsely represented that he was authorized to practice engineering" [Oregon](https://www.oregon.gov/osbeels/rulesstatutes/Pages/Disciplinary-Actions.aspx) . The Järlström v. Aldridge case (366 F. Supp. 3d 1205, D. Or. 2019) carved out an important First Amendment exception — non-commercial speech describing oneself as an engineer is protected — but the state explicitly preserved the right to regulate "when he's [in] a commercial or professional manner" [UO Matters](https://uomatters.com/2017/12/state-board-fines-oregon-man-500-for-doing-math-without-a-license.html) . Selling a home with "engineered by BSH" branding is unambiguously commercial.

This means: as currently described, your model is at material risk from OSBEELS enforcement before it ever reaches the CCB.

## The fix: relabel the brand and the activity

Three changes make the model legally defensible:

**(1) Drop "engineering" from how you describe the LLC and the work.** "Better Smarter Homes LLC" as the entity name is fine (the words "engineer/engineering" aren't in it). But describing the entity as a "design engineering company" in marketing is the problem. Reframe it as a **design and research company**, or **building science research company**, or **home design and methodology company**. Use **designed by**, **developed by**, **tested by**, **researched by**, **built using techniques from**, **based on research by** — not *engineered by*.

**(2) Don't use a "stamp."** In construction, a stamp/seal has a specific legal meaning — it's the mark of a registered professional engineer (PE) under ORS 672.020(2) or registered architect under ORS 671.020(3) certifying that the document was prepared by or under the supervision of the registrant. Putting a stamp-shaped graphic on plans or marketing materials that mimics a PE seal is exactly what OSBEELS investigators look for. A **brand logo** or a **maker's mark** ("Built by BSH" badge, plaque, or graphic) is different and fine, but it must be visually distinct from anything resembling a professional seal.

**(3) Get a licensed PE involved if you actually want the "engineered by" claim.** Two routes: (a) hire a licensed Oregon PE for each project who reviews and stamps the structural drawings, after which you can legitimately say "structural engineering by [PE name and license number], commissioned by Better Smarter Homes" — this is the cheap, normal path; or (b) become a PE yourself or have a PE as a partner with substantial ownership in BSH and register BSH as an engineering firm under OAR 820 and ORS 672.025/671.041 — the slow, expensive path. Without one of these, the "engineered" claim is unlawful holding-out.

## The architecture side is easier

Architecture has an explicit single-family exemption. Under ORS 671.030(1), "a person from making plans or specifications for, or supervising the erection, enlargement or alteration of, a building, or an appurtenance thereto, if the building... [is] a single family residential dwelling or farm building" [Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/oregon/2005/vol15/671.html) is not practicing architecture. The same applies to structures ≤4,000 sf and ≤20 ft tall. So **BSH can legitimately design, sell, and license single-family home plans without an architect license**, as long as the deliverable is plans-and-specifications for SFRs.

What BSH cannot do without an architect: design multi-family above the SFR threshold, design any commercial structure above 4,000 sf or 20 ft tall, or use the words "architect," "architecture," or "architectural" in business name or marketing without registration. "Home designer" is fine; "residential architect" is not.

## CCB licensing — keep BSH out of the construction stream

The CCB licensing exemption hinges on whether the LLC is a contractor. ORS 701.005 and 701.010 define a contractor as someone who, for compensation or with intent to sell, arranges or undertakes construction for another. The Tandem Properties LLC v. CCB decision (184 Or App 28, 2002) is the warning: an LLC that owns property and arranges construction with intent to sell is a contractor as a matter of law, regardless of how the business is described.

Your stated model — BSH does not buy, build, or sell homes — keeps BSH outside the CCB's contractor jurisdiction *if and only if*:

1. BSH never holds title to land or structures intended for construction
2. BSH never receives compensation tied to construction work performed on a specific structure (designing for someone else's construction project, then getting paid by the project, would put BSH within ORS 701.010's contractor definition unless properly licensed)
3. BSH doesn't supervise or coordinate construction on the homes you personally build (that would constitute construction management under OAR 812-002-0160)
4. BSH's revenue comes from clearly non-contractor sources: YouTube ad revenue, plan sales, courses, books, brand licensing, consulting that is purely advisory and doesn't constitute construction management

## What BSH can safely do

Within those boundaries, the LLC can legitimately:

- Own all design documents, plans, blueprints, video content, photographs, written content, branding, trademarks, domain names, and website material as intellectual property
- License plans to third parties (one-time fee for plan sets — common business model used by many Oregon plan vendors)
- Sell educational material (books, online courses, paid newsletter, paid YouTube tier)
- Run advertising on YouTube and other platforms
- Receive sponsorship and brand-deal revenue
- Provide purely advisory consulting (no construction supervision, no plan stamping, no project coordination)
- Hire licensed PEs, architects, and contractors as needed for specific deliverables it sells to others

What BSH should not do without proper licensing:

- Receive compensation for "engineering" services (PE required)
- Receive compensation for architectural services on non-exempt buildings (architect required)
- Construct, alter, or repair structures for others for compensation (CCB license required)
- Manage construction projects for others for compensation (CCB construction management is contractor work)
- Bid on or solicit construction work
- Use "engineer," "engineering," "architect," "architecture," or any title implying licensure
- Stamp or seal documents with anything resembling a professional seal
- Advertise services using language that implies licensed-professional involvement when none exists

## Marketing language — what to avoid, what to use

**Avoid:**
- "Engineered by Better Smarter Homes"
- "BSH-engineered design"
- "Engineering services by BSH"
- "Professional engineering" anywhere
- "Stamped by BSH" / "BSH-certified" (implies professional certification)
- "Approved by BSH engineering"
- "Engineering specifications" / "engineering analysis" without an actual PE

**Use:**
- "Designed by Better Smarter Homes"
- "Built using Better Smarter Homes design principles"
- "Based on research by Better Smarter Homes"
- "Built to BSH's published methodology"
- "Featured on the Better Smarter Homes channel"
- "Test home for Better Smarter Homes building research"
- If a PE is actually involved on a specific home: "Structural engineering by [PE name, OR license #X], in collaboration with Better Smarter Homes"

For your personal home that you sell as owner-builder, the safest sale-listing language is something like "Custom-built owner-occupied home incorporating design methodology from Better Smarter Homes (better-smarter-homes.com)." This communicates the brand connection without making professional-services claims.

## The structural recommendation

Given your stated model, here's the structure most defensible:

**Better Smarter Homes LLC (Oregon LLC, you as sole member)**
- Purpose: home design research and education
- Revenue: YouTube ads, plan sales, courses, books, sponsorships, advisory consulting
- Assets: trademarks, copyrights, domain names, plan library, video library, brand
- Holds no real estate
- Has no construction contracts
- Treated as pass-through for tax (single-member LLC default)

**You personally (individually, not the LLC)**
- Hold title to the land
- Pull the building permit as homeowner-applicant
- Sign the BCD homeowner statement saying you'll occupy
- Build the home (using BSH's published methodology, which is publicly available content)
- Occupy as primary residence
- Eventually sell as owner-occupant

**Licensed PEs and contractors hired as needed**
- For projects where you want to sell homes with stamped engineering claims, contract with a licensed Oregon PE per project — they stamp the structural drawings, you can describe their work accurately
- For trade work that exceeds your owner-builder scope or for any future homes you might want to sell faster/at scale, hire CCB-licensed subs and a licensed GC

**Two layers of separation make this work:**
1. **Activity separation** — BSH does education and research; you-personally do the construction-and-sale; a licensed PE does any actual engineering. No single entity is doing two regulated activities at once.
2. **Money separation** — BSH revenue stays in the LLC bank account; personal home build expenses come from personal funds; never commingle. If BSH ever pays for materials that go into your personal home, that's an audit flag for both the IRS (improper deduction of personal expenses) and for CCB (BSH is doing construction work without a license).

## What to get from professionals before going further

This is at the boundary where DIY legal research stops being safe. Three professionals worth paying for an hour each:

1. **Oregon construction attorney** — confirm the activity separation passes Tandem Properties scrutiny, draft a license-of-content agreement so BSH IP is properly licensed to you-personally for use on personal builds, advise on USPTO trademark filing for "Better Smarter Homes" wordmark
2. **CPA familiar with multi-entity small business** — set up books for BSH that won't comingle with personal home build, advise on whether to elect S-corp treatment for BSH if revenue grows, plan for capital gains treatment of personal home sale (§121 exclusion), and confirm that personal home costs aren't accidentally taken as BSH deductions
3. **Oregon PE who works on residential** — establish a per-project relationship for projects where you want stamped engineering, get their input on what your designs need from them and whether your structural innovations require formal review under Oregon's "alternative methods" path (ORS 455.060)

OSBEELS in particular is an aggressive enforcement agency with a history of pursuing borderline cases — the Järlström saga shows they will pursue $500 penalties against people for using the word "engineer" wrong. The cost of getting an attorney letter clarifying your safe brand language is very small compared to the cost of a hard cease-and-desist or civil penalty action that drags your YouTube channel into discovery.
