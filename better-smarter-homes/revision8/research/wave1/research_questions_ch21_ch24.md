## Headline findings (read first)

1. **The clean boundary is activity-based, not entity-based, and Oregon's statutes draw it precisely.** CCB licensure attaches to anyone who "undertakes, offers to undertake or submits a bid to do work as a contractor" for compensation (ORS 701.021, https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors701.html), and CCB's own application guidance sweeps in any business that "advertises, offers, bids or arranges to do" construction (CCB application info, https://a1contractorservices.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/08/orlicappinf.pdf). The owner-builder exemption protects the *person* building their own residence with no intent to sell (ORS 701.010; owner-builder exemption items 4–5 of the CCB list, same source). Therefore: the residence stays personally owned and personally permitted under the owner exemption; the LLC owns only intangibles (brand, content, site, channel, design library, courses) and **never advertises, offers, arranges, or bids construction work** — because offering is itself the licensable act, before any hammer swings.
2. **The intent-to-sell trap governs both the person and any portfolio model.** Oregon case law: a property owner developing a structure with intent to sell, not owner occupancy, is acting as a contractor/developer (Tandem Properties, LLC v. CCB, 184 Or App 28 (2002), annotation at https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_701.010), and "developer" is itself a CCB-licensed category (ORS 701.042, same index). A build-occupy-sell-repeat portfolio drifts from exempt owner-builder into unlicensed development; the chapter must present the portfolio vision as philosophy, not a repeatable business method (bullet 15), and route sale-timing to an Oregon attorney. Note: ORS 701.010 was amended by 2025 Oregon Laws ch. 78 with provisions operative July 1, 2027 — re-verify text before publication (https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors701.html).
3. **Oregon's single-family design exemption makes a plan library legally possible — with two hard rules.** Non-architects may prepare technical submissions and provide construction-phase services for single-family dwellings, farm buildings, auxiliary structures, and other buildings ≤4,000 sq ft ground area and ≤20 ft height (ORS 671.030, https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_671.030; practitioner summary, https://www.christiearchitecture.com/when-is-an-architect-required/). Rule one: never use "architect," "architecture," or "architectural" in the brand, titles, or site copy — even website representations violate ORS 671.020 (Twist Architecture v. Board of Architect Examiners, 361 Or 507 (2017), annotation at https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_671.020). Rule two: engineering is a separate practice act (ORS 672) with no equivalent blanket dwelling exemption for offering engineering services to others — structural calcs, load advice, or "engineered" claims for third parties are off-limits (exact ORS 672 exemption text: UNRESOLVED-2).
4. **The stock-plan industry supplies the exact commercial template for the design library.** Established plan houses sell copyrighted plans under single-build licenses, expressly state that stock plans carry **no professional stamp**, that code compliance and any required local stamp are the buyer's responsibility, and that buyers should engage local professionals (Donald A. Gardner copyright/disclaimer page — the model disclaimer language, https://dongardner.com/pages/house-plans-copyright-information; HPA licensing page showing the "retain us separately if your jurisdiction requires a stamp" pattern, https://architects.hpadesign.com/docs/license; single-build license mechanics, https://www.designevolutions.com/stock-house-plans/copyright/; https://www.architecturaldesigns.com/copyright). Selling "plan concepts and reference documents, licensed for one build, not stamped, not verified against your site or codes" is a lawful, established product category; selling "approved construction documents" is not a thing anyone can promise.
5. **Monetization compliance is federal and platform-driven, and the rules are current and citable:** FTC's 2023 Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255) require clear-and-conspicuous disclosure of every material connection (payment, free product, affiliate commission), per post, in the content itself, with platform disclosure tools alone insufficient and civil penalties currently ~$53,088 per violation (FTC FAQ, https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking; FTC press release, https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/06/federal-trade-commission-announces-updated-advertising-guides-combat-deceptive-reviews-endorsements; Disclosures 101, https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers; penalty figure, https://iqfluence.io/public/blog/ftc-influencer-guidelines). YouTube monetization thresholds as of 2026: early tier at 500 subscribers + 3 public videos + 3,000 watch hours or 3M Shorts views/90 days; full ad revenue at 1,000 subscribers + 4,000 valid public watch hours/12 months or 10M Shorts views/90 days, plus policy compliance and review (~1 month) (YouTube Help, https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72851; 2026 tier summaries, https://milx.app/en/trends/youtube-monetization-requirements-for-2026; https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-partner-program-guide/).

## Answers, bullet by bullet

### 1–2. The legal boundary, and what the LLC can safely own.

Structure to publish: **Person** = owner, permit holder, owner-builder exemption claimant, occupant; performs and supervises construction on their own residence. **LLC** = intellectual-property and media company that owns: the brand name and marks; the website, channel, newsletter, and social accounts; the manuscript/book rights; the course and template products; the photo/video archive (assigned or licensed from the person — document the assignment); the design library sold as stock reference plans under Headline 3–4 constraints; affiliate and sponsorship contracts. Owning these creates no CCB issue because none is "work as a contractor" (ORS 701.021, above); no architecture issue if the single-family exemption scope and title rules are respected (Headline 3); no real-estate-developer issue so long as the LLC never owns the land or dwelling or arranges construction on anyone's property (ORS 701.042 context, Headline 2). The LLC must **not** own the house or the building permit — putting the residence into the business entity destroys the personal owner-builder exemption logic, complicates title/insurance/lending, and hands a future disclosure and CCB problem to the sale (Ch. 20 file, Headlines 1–2). Consumer-protection posture: sell information products as information, with the industry-standard disclaimers (Headline 4) — Oregon's UTPA and FTC Section 5 both punish misrepresentation of what a product is, which is why the "not construction documents" framing is load-bearing (UTPA cite: UNRESOLVED-2).

### 3. What the LLC should not do (the prohibited list, each with its statute).

- Contract, offer, advertise, bid, or **arrange** construction work for others (ORS 701.021; CCB "advertises, offers, bids or arranges" language, https://a1contractorservices.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/08/orlicappinf.pdf) — including "we'll manage your build" consulting that shades into arranging.
- Take construction deposits or progress payments of any kind — taking money to build is the definition of contracting for compensation (same sources).
- Build on someone else's land, or on LLC-owned land for sale — developer licensing (ORS 701.042; Tandem Properties, Headline 2).
- Sell plan sets represented as stamped, approved, code-verified, or site-adapted construction documents (Headline 4 disclaimers are mandatory, not decorative).
- Provide engineering services or advice for compensation (calcs, sizing, "will this beam work" answers for a specific third-party project) — ORS 672 practice act (Headline 3, UNRESOLVED-2 for exemption text); the safe lane is describing what *the author's engineer* designed on *the author's* project and telling readers to hire their own.
- Use "architect/architecture/architectural," "engineer/engineering" (as a service), "licensed," "certified," or CCB-number-implying language in marketing (Twist Architecture, Headline 3; ORS 701 advertising rules).
- Perform paid inspections or construction consulting that resembles home inspection — Oregon home inspectors are CCB-certified, and inspection services appear on CCB's license-required list (CCB application info, above; certification specifics UNRESOLVED-2).
- Community/legal hygiene: no individualized professional advice in comments/consults ("your specific footing should be...") — general education about the author's own documented project is the product.

### 4. Oregon LLC mechanics (current, citable).

Formation: Articles of Organization with the Oregon SOS Corporation Division, $100, filed online via Oregon Business Registry (multiple concordant sources: https://bizee.com/llc/oregon/filing-fees-requirements; https://www.legalzoom.com/articles/how-to-start-an-llc-in-oregon). Registered agent: required, physical Oregon street address, may be the owner (https://www.tailorbrands.com/llc-formation/oregon-llc/llc-costs). Annual report/renewal: $100 every year on the formation anniversary, online only, no financial data collected; SOS mails a reminder ~45 days out; ~45-day grace, then administrative dissolution; reinstatement $100 plus missed reports (https://www.statebusinesscompliance.com/blog/oregon-llc-annual-report-2026; https://www.incauthority.com/blog/oregon-llc/). Assumed business name if operating under a different brand: $50, renewed every 2 years (https://bizee.com/llc/oregon/filing-fees-requirements). Name reservation optional, $100/120 days. No Oregon general sales tax; Corporate Activity Tax registration applies once Oregon commercial activity crosses the statutory threshold (structure only — thresholds and applicability to a small media LLC: UNRESOLVED-3 with CPA). Operating agreement: not filed but strongly recommended, and essential here to document the IP-assignment boundary between person and LLC (practice guidance — UNRESOLVED-5). Fees are current as of research date; re-verify against the SOS fee schedule at publication.

### 5–6. CCB rules and the owner-builder/occupancy-intent problem.

Covered in Headlines 1–2; additional publishable specifics: unlicensed contracting penalties run up to $5,000 per offense (https://www.licensetobuild.com/oregon/faq/); the <$1,000 "casual, minor, inconsequential" exemption evaporates the moment a person advertises (https://www.simplywise.com/blog/oregon-contractor-license/; https://www.licensetobuild.com/oregon/faq/) — meaning a monetized channel that also offers small paid help to others is *worse* positioned than a silent hobbyist, a point the chapter should make explicitly. Content, courses, books, sponsorships, and affiliate income are not construction activity and require no CCB license (no source states the negative directly; grounded in ORS 701.005/701.021 definitions — frame as "licensure attaches to construction work and offers of it, which none of these are," and have the manuscript reviewed by an Oregon attorney, UNRESOLVED-1). Consulting is the gray zone: education-only consulting (teaching process, documentation, code-navigation from the author's own experience) sits outside "work as a contractor"; project-specific consulting that directs, arranges, or supervises someone's build slides toward licensure — publish the bright-line list from bullet 3 and route edge cases to counsel (UNRESOLVED-1). Portfolio model: per Headline 2, write occupancy-intent honestly — the exemption exists for a personal residence; documented genuine occupancy, life-driven (not scheduled) sales, and attorney review before any sale are the only defensible framing; never publish target hold periods as compliance advice.

### 7. Tax treatment — publish structure, not numbers.

The research file's own instruction (verify with qualified tax sources before exact claims) is the answer: the chapter must describe the *architecture* and route every number to a CPA (UNRESOLVED-3). Publishable structure with anchors: a single-member LLC is a federal disregarded entity by default (Schedule C pass-through); multi-member defaults to partnership; either can elect corporate/S-corp treatment (Form 8832/2553 mechanics noted in formation guides, https://www.legalzoom.com/articles/how-to-start-an-llc-in-oregon); pass-through profits generally bear self-employment tax, which is the usual motive for a later S-corp election once profit clears reasonable-salary economics (structure only — UNRESOLVED-3); Oregon taxes pass-through income on the personal return and offers a pass-through-entity elective tax with federal SALT-workaround implications (UNRESOLVED-3); Oregon CAT applies above a commercial-activity threshold (https://www.incauthority.com/blog/oregon-llc/; UNRESOLVED-3). Income-type notes for the CPA conversation, not for firm book claims: affiliate/sponsorship/course/consulting/book income are all ordinary business income to the LLC; 1099s arrive from platforms; the **hobby-loss rule (IRC §183)** is the real risk for a channel that runs losses against W-2 income — profit motive documentation matters; home-office, equipment (camera gear), and depreciation follow normal business-expense rules **only** for the media business, and the *house construction itself is never an LLC expense* — that's the boundary from bullet 1 restated in tax terms (all practice-structure statements: UNRESOLVED-3 for CPA verification before publication). The book-safe sentence pattern: "Here is the structure and the questions; your CPA supplies the numbers."

### 8. Insurance — framework, not firm guidance.

Publish as a coverage-conversation checklist for a licensed Oregon agent (UNRESOLVED-4): (a) homeowner/builder's-risk policies typically exclude business activity — filming commercially on the jobsite, hosting visitors for content, or running a business from the property must be disclosed to the property insurer or coverage gaps result; (b) the LLC needs commercial general liability once it has revenue, visitors, or events; (c) media liability/E&O covers the content risks GL doesn't (defamation, IP claims, "I followed your video and got hurt" allegations) — the standard creator-business pairing; (d) volunteers and any paid helpers on the personal build raise workers'-compensation and liability questions that are Oregon-specific and serious (Ch. 17 file covered Oregon OSHA framing; volunteer/casual-labor comp rules: UNRESOLVED-4); (e) umbrella over both; (f) product recommendations/affiliate activity rarely need product liability but the E&O conversation should cover it. Firm numbers, carriers, and whether a given policy covers a given activity: agent territory, never book claims.

### 9. Privacy, filming, and site security.

Practice-guidance list with one Oregon legal anchor to verify: Oregon's recording statute treats in-person oral conversations differently from electronic ones — obtaining consent/informing all participants before recording in-person conversation is the safe rule on a jobsite (ORS 165.540 — verify exact terms and exceptions before publishing as law, UNRESOLVED-2). Editorial rules to publish (UNRESOLVED-5): written releases from every recognizable visitor, worker, and volunteer (standard media practice; template in appendix); do not film inspectors, utility workers, or delivery drivers without asking — and never present an inspector's on-camera comments as an approval; blur or omit house numbers, road names, permit-card close-ups, and geolocatable landmarks; strip EXIF/GPS from published photos; delay-post site content (publish weeks behind reality) so the channel never advertises an unattended site full of tools; never show security-system details, key locations, or family schedules; neighbors: notify, don't feature without releases; children and family members get their own explicit opt-in standard. Site security economics: theft targets (tools, copper, appliances) spike at known project addresses — the delay-post rule plus a locked conex and cameras is the practical answer (Ch. 17 equipment-security cross-reference).

### 10. Advertising, sponsorship, affiliate, and review rules.

FTC 2023 Endorsement Guides govern (Headline 5). Publishable rules: disclose every material connection — money, free product, discounts, affiliate commissions, family/employment ties — whenever a significant minority of the audience wouldn't expect it (16 CFR 255 revision, https://www.commerciallitigationupdate.com/the-ftc-finalizes-changes-to-endorsement-guides); disclosures must be clear and conspicuous, unavoidable, in the content itself (video *and* description; superimposed and spoken for video; repeated periodically in livestreams), not buried in hashtags, "more" folds, or profile pages (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers; https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking); plain language beats jargon — "paid link"/"I earn commissions from links in this post" over "#affiliate"/"#sp" (https://gordonlaw.com/learn/ftc-endorsement-guidelines/); every post is its own ad requiring its own disclosure; honest-experience rules: no claims about products not actually used, no atypical-results framing without typicality disclosure; review integrity: never gate, cherry-pick, or incentivize only positive reviews (FTC final-rule summary, https://perkinscoie.com/insights/update/ftc-finalizes-updated-guidance-influencer-and-consumer-review-programs; note also the FTC's 2024 fake-reviews trade rule — verify cite, UNRESOLVED-2). Sponsorship ethics for a construction channel specifically: never let a sponsor's product override the documented engineering/spec decisions, and say so in the sponsorship policy — this is both FTC-substantiation hygiene and the brand's core credibility asset (practice guidance — UNRESOLVED-5).

### 11. Intellectual-property strategy.

Copyright: content, photos, videos, plans, and templates are protected from fixation, registration not required but enabling statutory damages/attorney fees requires timely registration — register the book and the plan library (17 USC framework via architecture-copyright summaries, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_in_architecture_in_the_United_States; https://www.fasthofflawfirm.com/copyright-protection-architectural-works-designs/; registration-benefit specifics: UNRESOLVED-2). Plans get two protections — the drawings as pictorial/graphic works and the built design as an architectural work — but protection excludes standard features and code-driven elements (same sources), which is why the library's value concentrates in the distinctive documented system, not generic details. Trademark: brand name/logo — clear Oregon SOS registry *and* USPTO before committing (state name availability ≠ trademark clearance, https://www.incauthority.com/blog/oregon-llc/); federal registration when revenue justifies (mechanics: UNRESOLVED-2/attorney). Licensing architecture to publish: plan library under a written single-build license per industry norm (Headline 4 sources give the clause inventory: one dwelling, no resale/redistribution, modification rights defined, no stamp, buyer responsible for code compliance); educational documents and Git repositories can use open licenses deliberately — permissive (MIT/CC-BY) for goodwill assets, NC/ND variants or proprietary for revenue assets — decide per asset class and document it; never open-source the paid library by accident via a repo license (license-selection detail: practice guidance — UNRESOLVED-5). Ownership hygiene: written IP assignment from person to LLC for pre-formation content (UNRESOLVED-1/attorney).

### 12. Sharing plan concepts publicly without crossing professional lines.

Yes, within Headline 3's exemption and Headline 4's framing: publishing concepts, dimensioned reference drawings, technical guides, and templates for single-family dwellings is lawful non-architect activity in Oregon (ORS 671.030), and the national stock-plan industry demonstrates the compliant packaging — explicit statements that documents are unstamped reference material, not verified against any site, soil, climate, or local code; that jurisdictions requiring a stamp will require the buyer to hire a local licensed professional; that mechanical/electrical/plumbing layouts (if omitted) are the buyer's responsibility; and that a local professional review is recommended before construction (Donald Gardner disclaimer set, https://dongardner.com/pages/house-plans-copyright-information; HPA stamp language, https://architects.hpadesign.com/docs/license). Two Oregon-specific cautions to print: the exemption covers the *activity*, not the *title* (Twist Architecture, Headline 3), and out-of-state buyers import their own state's rules — the disclaimer must say "check your state's design-professional and permit requirements," because some states are stricter than Oregon for certain structures (practice guidance — UNRESOLVED-5).

### 13. Platform risks and current rules.

YouTube: thresholds per Headline 5; additional publishable risk facts — monetization requires ongoing policy compliance and channels get demonetized for "inauthentic content" (the renamed repetitious-content rule, with 2026 enforcement emphasis against low-effort/AI-spam content — original documented construction footage is the opposite of the risk profile, https://milx.app/en/trends/youtube-monetization-requirements-for-2026); YouTube retains the right to run ads on non-monetized content, so the audience-building phase earns nothing (https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72851). Structural risk to publish for all platforms: monetization terms, reach algorithms, and account access are revocable at platform discretion — the mitigation is the classic owned-audience stack (website + email newsletter as the canonical home; platforms as distribution), plus export/backups of subscriber lists and content masters (practice guidance — UNRESOLVED-5). Affiliate programs: program-specific terms (cookie windows, payout thresholds, prohibited claims) change frequently — publish the compliance pattern (FTC disclosure + read each program's ToS), not program details (UNRESOLVED-3). Courses/consulting: refund policy, income-claim rules (FTC scrutiny of earnings claims applies to course marketing too), and sales-tax/nexus questions for digital products go to the CPA/attorney list (UNRESOLVED-3).

### 14. Realistic schedule, revenue, audience, and community expectations.

Publish honesty, not projections: only ~3% of channels ever meet even the basic monetization thresholds (https://recurpost.com/blog/youtube-monetization-watch-hours-requirements-explained/), and RPM/sponsorship income varies so widely that any specific income example would be fabricated precision — the manuscript should give the *mechanism table* (ad revenue, sponsorship, affiliate, courses, plans, book, consulting) with effort/risk characteristics instead of dollar figures (editorial rule — no citable revenue baseline exists for this niche; UNRESOLVED-5). Cadence: the defensible claim is that consistency beats frequency and that construction projects supply a natural episodic structure; any specific cadence promise ("weekly for four years while building a house alone") should be reality-checked against the Ch. 17/20 schedule-contingency findings — the build is the product; the channel must survive the build's variance (practice guidance — UNRESOLVED-5). Consulting boundaries: bullet 3/6 bright lines. Community management: moderation policy, no individualized safety-critical advice in comments (redirect to "hire your local professional" as policy), and a documented correction practice when published content turns out wrong — the book's own cite-everything discipline extended to the channel (practice guidance — UNRESOLVED-5).

### 15. Portfolio vision: aspiration vs. life strategy.

Rewrite as risk-managed philosophy, per Headline 2's law: the compliant narrative is "build the home you intend to occupy; let life, not a schedule, determine any sale; understand that intent-to-sell converts an exempt owner-builder into an unlicensed developer (Tandem Properties); repeat only as genuine life chapters, with attorney review at each transition." Strip all build counts, holding periods, sale-timing rules-of-thumb, and financial targets — each one is either (a) evidence of intent-to-sell, (b) an unverifiable financial projection, or (c) legal advice the book can't give. The Ch. 20 findings slot in directly: first-sale permit-statement mechanics, ORS 455.320 recording, disclosure obligations (Ch. 20 file, Headlines 1–2). What CAN stay: the documented-craft thesis — that a meticulously documented, code-anchored build creates durable value in the asset, the records, and the brand simultaneously — which is true, sourced across Chapters 19–20, and requires no legal promises.
