# Chapter 9: Plumbing — Water Supply, Pressure, Treatment, and Hot Water

This is the verification pass for the Chapter 9 bullets. The current code of record for plumbing in Oregon is the **2023 OPSC**, effective February 9, 2024, based on the 2021 UPC. The 2026 OPSC adoption is in progress with anticipated effective date October 1, 2026 — nothing in your manuscript should be written against that until it lands. The **2023 ORSC Chapter 11** governs residential energy provisions; the **2025 OEESC** (commercial) became mandatory July 1, 2025; and the **2026 ORSC** introduces a heat-pump mandate for new construction that affects domestic hot water choices.

---

## 1. Pressure threshold, regulator, strainer, expansion tank, access (OPSC §608)

**The 80 psi rule is the anchor.** Under **OPSC §608.2**, where static pressure in the supply piping exceeds 80 psi, an approved-type pressure regulator preceded by an adequate strainer must be installed and the static pressure reduced to 80 psi or less. Pressure regulators must comply with **ASSE 1003**.

Three Oregon-specific amendments retained from prior cycles, all relevant to your design:

- **Strainer exemption above 1½ inch.** Regulators equal to or exceeding 1½ inch (40 mm) are not required to have a strainer.
- **Vault drainage flexibility.** Where the regulator is in a vault, the vault must have "adequate means to provide drainage" rather than the model code's prescriptive bore-sighted drain to daylight — useful when daylight drainage is geometrically impossible on your site.
- **Expansion tank position.** Oregon prohibits a valve between the expansion tank and the system (boiler-rule alignment); the expansion tank itself must be on the building side of the check valve, backflow preventer, or other normally-closed device.

Access requirements: each regulator and strainer must be **accessibly located aboveground or in a vault**, protected from freezing, with the strainer **readily accessible for cleaning without removing the regulator/strainer body or disconnecting the supply piping**. This is a real design constraint — a regulator buried behind drywall fails inspection.

Expansion tank requirements (OPSC §608.2 and §608.3):

- Required downstream of the regulator on the cold-water distribution.
- Required wherever a check valve, backflow preventer, or other normally-closed device prevents pressure dissipation back into the main — independent of water heater type.
- Must comply with **NSF 61** for potable systems and **IAPMO Z1088** for prepressurized models.
- Must be sized, fastened, and installed per manufacturer instructions.
- **Exception:** instantaneous on-demand water heaters do not require an expansion tank.
- Engineered alternative: a registered design professional may substitute an approved pressure relief valve set ≤100 psi in lieu of an expansion tank.

Pipe sizing where a regulator is installed: **§608.2 requires sizing based on 80% of the reduced pressure** when using Table 610.4. So if you regulate to 60 psi, you size against 48 psi — that 20% margin matters when you run the developed-length math.

**Anchor sentence.** Oregon caps static residential supply pressure at 80 psi under OPSC §608.2; above that threshold a pressure regulator with strainer (strainer-exempt at ≥1½ inch) and an NSF-61 expansion tank are required, both of which must be accessibly installed for service.

## 2. High-pressure trunk with per-fixture PRVs

This is doable but **not on the prescriptive path**. OPSC §608.2 reads: "Such regulator(s) shall control the pressure to water outlets in the building **unless otherwise approved by the Building Official**." That phrase is the only opening for a multi-PRV architecture, and it has to be combined with **OPSC §301.5 Alternative Engineered Design**, which requires:

- Two complete sets of signed and sealed design documents from a registered design professional (RDP).
- Floor plans and a riser diagram showing pipe sizes, grades, loading, and fixture/appliance locations.
- Discretionary approval by the Building Official (jurisdiction-specific, no statewide precedent).

Practical reality: **for BSH this should be flagged as an optional engineered upgrade, not a default methodology.** You'd be committing to (a) a stamped PE plumbing design for every build, (b) a Building Official willing to sign off on alternative engineered design, and (c) per-fixture PRVs that all need to comply with ASSE 1003, be accessible, and be serviceable. The defensible mainstream design is one main regulator + expansion tank, with the high-pressure-trunk concept reserved for the manuscript's "what's possible if you bring in a PE" section.

There is also a Legionella/dead-leg concern. OPSC §309.5 requires dead legs to have an accessible flushing method and not exceed 1.5× the pipe diameter — the model code language explicitly added in 2021. A high-pressure trunk with branched per-fixture PRVs creates more closed-end stub conditions that need flushing design, not fewer.

**Anchor sentence.** A high-pressure trunk with per-fixture PRVs is permissible in Oregon only as an OPSC §301.5 alternative engineered design with PE-stamped drawings and Building Official approval; treat it as a luxury option in the manuscript, not the BSH default.

## 3. Final water-service sizing method

Two methods are concurrent in Oregon under the 2023 OPSC:

**Method A — Traditional WSFU / Table 610.4 (Appendix A).** Tabulate water supply fixture units per OPSC Table 610.3, determine developed length, subtract 0.5 psi per foot for elevation between source and highest outlet, look up minimum meter and building supply pipe size.

**Method B — Appendix M flow demand (new in 2023 OPSC).** A peak-demand methodology that assumes water-efficient fixtures and accounts for probability of concurrent use. Portland Water Bureau formally adopted Appendix M for residential meter sizing in 2023, finding it generally produces smaller, cheaper meters for the same fixture count. Other Oregon jurisdictions are following.

Minimum building supply is **¾ inch** regardless of method.

For a private well + house pressurization system, the sizing math is layered:

1. **Well yield (sustained GPM).** Determined by the well log — request from OWRD's well log query system once drilled. If yield < 5 GPM, you're in storage/booster territory regardless of fixture count.
2. **Pump capacity (GPM at design head).** Match to well yield, accounting for total dynamic head: static water level + drawdown + elevation to pressure tank + friction losses + regulated pressure target.
3. **Pressure tank drawdown.** See bullet 5.
4. **Booster/storage.** Where well yield is below peak demand, an atmospheric storage tank decouples well yield from house demand; a downstream booster pump (often a variable-speed/constant-pressure pump) feeds the house from storage.
5. **House service sizing.** Run Appendix M (or 610.4) on the post-booster line. Subtract 0.5 psi per foot of elevation gain to highest outlet, then size against 80% of the regulated pressure if a PRV is used.
6. **Simultaneous demand check.** Especially relevant for low-flow setups — verify that the worst-case concurrent fixtures (two showers + dishwasher + clothes washer) don't drop below minimum fixture supply pressure (typically 8–20 psi depending on fixture).

**Anchor sentence.** For the BSH build the sizing path is: well yield → pump curve → pressure tank drawdown → optional storage + booster → developed-length and elevation pressure-loss check → OPSC Appendix M (preferred) or Table 610.4 (traditional) for house service and distribution sizing.

## 4. Large indoor potable storage tank — code, health, backflow, drain, vent

Oregon does allow an indoor potable storage/break tank, but it's a layered approval, not a prescriptive permission.

**OPSC §602.4** — no water piping supplied by a private water supply system can be connected to any other source of supply without approval of the Building Official, the Oregon Health Authority (OHA), or other department having jurisdiction. So the moment you add an atmospheric storage tank between well and house, you're triggering an approval pathway that involves OHA Drinking Water Services.

**Cross-connection control under OAR 333-061.** OHA's drinking-water rules (as referenced in OPSC's permitting framework) require cross-connection control between potable systems and any storage. The standard architecture is an atmospheric tank fed via an air gap, then a booster pump downstream — the air gap *is* the backflow protection.

**Materials.** Tank, fittings, gaskets, liner, and any internal coatings must comply with **NSF/ANSI/CAN 61**. System certification (not just tank certification) is the cleaner specification — confirm by manufacturer's NSF database listing.

**Disinfection.** Oregon doesn't have a published HOA-equivalent rule for residential indoor storage disinfection, but the operational standard is in OAR 333-061 and the OWRD/OHA Well Owner's Handbook: super-chlorinate to 50–250 mg/L free chlorine, hold ≥24 hours (some protocols are 12 hours minimum at 50 mg/L), drain to non-sanitary outlet, refill, retest until residual is below 4 mg/L and total coliform is absent. After any opening, repair, or media change, redisinfect. For ongoing free-chlorine residual where stored water exceeds about 14 days, a small chemical-feed pump or chloramination is typical.

**Tank construction details that pass inspection:**

- Vermin-/insect-proof, watertight cover, downturned vents with corrosion-resistant ¼-inch or finer animal screen.
- Manhole/cleanout ≥22"×30" or ≥24" diameter (matching OPSC §508.2 for accessibility), gasketed, secured.
- Overflow piped to a non-sanitary indirect waste with air gap (no direct connection to drainage system).
- Drain valve at low point.
- Liquid-level controls and high-level alarm so a stuck float doesn't dump 1,000 gallons into the floor pan.
- Floor pan / spill containment around the tank with a piped indirect drain (OPSC §811.0 indirect waste).
- Seismic restraint per the building code (OPSC §313.1).

**Practical limits.** Oregon doesn't publish a residential cistern-specific rule the way Ohio does (Ohio's OAC 3701-28-12 is the closest analogue); compliance is built up from OPSC §602.4 + OPSC Chapter 6 cross-connection rules + OAR 333-061 + the manufacturer's NSF 61 listing. Expect the Building Official and the local environmental health office to require a sealed stamped design from an RDP for any tank substantially above 1,000 gallons or anywhere outside a typical mechanical room.

**Anchor sentence.** An indoor potable storage tank is allowed in Oregon under OPSC §602.4 with Building Official + OHA approval, must use NSF 61–certified tank/fittings/liner, must be fed via air gap and discharge overflow indirectly with an air gap, and must be installed with vermin-proof access, secured cover, indirect overflow drain, level controls, and a documented disinfection protocol.

## 5. Well yield, pump selection, pressure tank, low-water cutoff, standby power, contact time

**Well yield** is determined empirically — the driller's 24-hour pumping test on the well log. Anything under 5 GPM sustained is a storage-and-boost build; 5–10 GPM is borderline and demand-dependent; 10+ GPM lets you direct-pressurize for a typical house.

**Pump selection.** Submersible turbine for typical drilled wells; size pump GPM ≤ sustained well yield to avoid drawing the well dry. Pump TDH = static water level + maximum drawdown + elevation lift + friction losses + system pressure (psi × 2.31 for ft of head). Single-phase 230V is standard; consider 3-phase if planning extended runs and a soft-starter inverter. Stainless-steel-body pumps last meaningfully longer than cast-iron in Oregon's mineralized groundwater.

**Pressure tank sizing.** The industry rule of thumb (Water Well Journal / Amtrol):

- Pumps ≤ 10 GPM: 1 gallon drawdown per GPM, 1-minute minimum runtime.
- Pumps 10–20 GPM: 1.5 gallons drawdown per GPM.
- Pumps ≥ 2 HP: 2-minute minimum runtime.

Drawdown is **not** total tank volume. At a 30/50 psi switch, drawdown ≈ 29% of total volume; at 40/60 psi, drawdown ≈ 22%. So a 10 GPM pump on 40/60 needs ~10 gal drawdown ≈ ~45 gal nominal tank. **Always go larger** — oversizing extends pump life dramatically and costs negligibly more. Pre-charge must be set 2 psi below cut-in.

**Low-water cutoff.** Standard practice is a pump-protection device — either a pressure-based "well saver" (turns off pump if drawdown drops pressure below a threshold for a set time), a flow-based switch, or a low-water electrode in the well. None is mandated by Oregon code for a single-family residential well, but the lack is the #1 cause of dry-running pump burnouts. Build it in.

**Standby power.** Not code-required for residential SFR wells. For the BSH 1,000-year reliability principle, the considerations are: (a) 240V well pumps draw substantial starting current — a 1 HP pump can pull 30+ amp inrush — so generator/inverter sizing matters; (b) variable-speed constant-pressure pumps are easier on generators than fixed-speed; (c) gravity-feed off an elevated/atmospheric storage tank is the only fully off-grid solution and aligns with the BSH "function without internet" rule by extension to "function without grid."

**Contact time for disinfection.** If you're chlorinating well output (most Oregon iron/coliform/sulfide treatment paths), CT (concentration × time) requirements come from the EPA Surface Water Treatment Rule analogue — 10 minutes of contact at 1–2 mg/L free chlorine for typical residential conditions, achieved via a contact tank sized to peak flow. Practical sizing: contact tank volume (gal) ≥ peak GPM × 10 min. So a 15 GPM peak demand needs a 150-gal contact tank minimum, larger if you want safety margin or are oxidizing high iron loads. Position contact tank between the chemical injection point and any downstream filtration or carbon polish.

**Anchor sentence.** Pressure tank sized at ~1 gallon drawdown per GPM with at least a 1-minute run cycle, plus a low-water cutoff (well saver or equivalent), plus a chlorine contact tank sized at ≥10 minutes at peak flow, are the three pump-system controls that move the BSH well from "code-compliant" to "actually durable."

## 6. New-build private well water test panel — beyond the ORS minimum

The **ORS 448.271 / OAR 333-061-0307** minimum for real estate transactions is **arsenic, nitrate, and total coliform bacteria** — that's the floor, not the target. For a new-build BSH well, the defensible test panel is layered:

**Tier 1 — Always test (baseline):**

- Total coliform + E. coli (sanitary integrity)
- Nitrate-N + nitrite-N (agricultural, septic)
- Arsenic (Oregon volcanic/geothermal geology; statewide concern)
- pH, alkalinity, hardness, total dissolved solids (treatment design)
- Iron (total + dissolved/ferrous)
- Manganese
- Sulfate, hydrogen sulfide (taste/odor + corrosion)

**Tier 2 — Test based on geography or geology:**

- Fluoride (some Oregon basalt aquifers run high)
- Lead and copper (especially if any old plumbing or lead-content fittings)
- Radon-in-water (granitic terrain — Cascade foothills, parts of southwest Oregon)
- Uranium and gross alpha (some Willamette Valley wells, eastern Oregon)
- Chloride (saltwater intrusion near coast)
- Tannins / TOC (forested, peaty soils — affects oxidation and filtration design)

**Tier 3 — Test based on local risk:**

- VOCs (proximity to fuel tanks, industrial, dry cleaners, landfills)
- Pesticides / herbicides panel (proximity to cropland, vineyards, orchards)
- PFAS panel (proximity to military bases, airports, fire training areas, biosolids application). **Note:** Oregon labs are not currently accredited for PFAS; samples ship out of state (OHA links to an Alaska DEC list).
- Glyphosate (separate from standard pesticide panels)

**Bacteria-resampling cadence:** even a clean initial test should be re-sampled after first heavy rain following construction, after septic install, and again 3 months in. Coliform contamination from construction-disturbed surfaces is the most common false-clean failure.

For BSH long-life design, also baseline **corrosion indices** (Langelier Saturation Index, Ryznar Stability Index) to predict whether the source water is scaling or corrosive — that drives copper-vs-PEX, anode rod chemistry, and treatment pH targets.

**Anchor sentence.** The Oregon RET minimum (As, NO₃, total coliform) is sample-of-record only; for a new-build BSH well, test the full Tier 1 panel plus Tier 2 items appropriate to local geology, plus Tier 3 if site context warrants, and re-test bacteria seasonally for the first year.

## 7. Treatment order for likely Oregon well issues

**Order matters.** Oxidizers ahead of softeners destroy resin; iron ahead of softeners fouls beds; sediment ahead of UV scatters light and tanks dose; carbon ahead of RO protects membranes. The general path:

1. **Pre-sediment filtration** (5–20 µm spun or pleated cartridge) — protects everything downstream.
2. **pH neutralization if pH < 6.7** (calcite/Corosex media tank) — pre-corrects acidic water before iron filtration and softener.
3. **Oxidation contact tank** — chlorine injection (sodium hypochlorite metering pump) or hydrogen peroxide, sized for ≥10 min CT at peak flow. Aeration is an alternative for moderate iron with no manganese.
4. **Iron / manganese filtration** — manganese greensand (regenerated with potassium permanganate), Birm, Pyrolox, or air-injection filters. Choice depends on iron form (ferrous vs. ferric vs. organic), manganese load, and required pH.
5. **Sulfur odor handling** — typically rolls into oxidation + iron filtration; if persistent, separate H₂S-specific treatment (catalytic carbon or iron oxide media).
6. **Tannin removal** — anion exchange (separate tank, salt-regenerated) before softener if TOC/tannins are high. Oxidation alone doesn't remove tannins reliably.
7. **Water softener** — sodium-form ion exchange. Only after iron, oxidation, and tannins. Resin works best at hardness 3–20 gpg, pH 6.7–8, residual iron < 0.3 mg/L.
8. **Activated carbon** — removes residual chlorine and chloramines, protects downstream PEX-A from oxidative attack on hot recirculation legs, removes taste/odor.
9. **Reverse osmosis (point-of-use)** — for arsenic, nitrate, fluoride, uranium, residual contaminants. Whole-house RO is rarely justified; under-sink or kitchen/refrigerator RO is the norm.
10. **UV disinfection** — last stage. UV disinfects but doesn't change chemistry; goes after all filtration so the lamp sees clear water. Sized for ≥40 mJ/cm² at end-of-lamp-life.

**Specialty cases:**

- **Arsenic-only** (low-iron water): titanium-oxide or iron-oxide adsorptive media, then RO at POU.
- **High nitrate**: anion exchange (chloride-form, dedicated nitrate-selective resin) or POU RO. POU RO is much simpler operationally.
- **Bacteriologically positive well**: shock chlorination + investigation of source (cap, casing, septic setbacks per OAR 690-200) before designing around continuous disinfection. Don't treat bacteria as a permanent design problem until you've fixed the well.

**Anchor sentence.** Treatment is sequential — sediment → pH → oxidation → contact → iron/manganese → tannins → softener → carbon → POU RO → UV — and skipping or reordering stages is the most common cause of treatment-system failure on Oregon wells.

## 8. Pipe material strategy

Code-permitted under OPSC Table 604.1 includes copper (Types K, L, M), CPVC, PEX (A, B, C), PE-RT, multilayer PEX-AL-PEX (ASTM F1986, 145 psi @ 180°F), stainless steel, and HDPE for service piping. The OPSC sets the floor; durability strategy sits above it.

**Service line (well/meter to house):**

- **HDPE PE4710** (SDR 9 or SDR 11) for buried service: chlorine-resistant, freeze-tolerant, splice-fusion joints with no in-ground mechanical fittings. AWWA C901 / ASTM F714. This is the BSH-aligned default.
- **Copper Type K** (soft) is the traditional alternative — long life, but susceptible to acidic soil corrosion in the Cascade foothills.
- Tracer wire required: 14 AWG direct-burial per 2021/2023 OPSC change.

**Inside the house — cold and hot distribution:**

- **PEX-A (Engel/peroxide)** — most flexible, highest burst pressure, expansion-fitting joints (Uponor ProPEX) that get tighter as pipe contracts. Lower chlorine resistance relative to PEX-B but rated PEX 5306 (Class 5 chlorine, Class 3 UV) when properly stabilized. Best for radiant, freeze-zone runs, and homerun manifold systems.
- **PEX-B (silane)** — slightly less flexible, generally highest chlorine resistance because the silane process doesn't consume antioxidant stabilizers. Crimp/clamp fittings have a small ID restriction at joints. Best for high-chlorine municipal water or any system with continuous disinfection (your case if you're feeding chlorinated well water).
- **PEX-C (electron beam)** — generally weaker stabilizer reserve and historically more inconsistent geometry. Not recommended for BSH durability targets.
- **Copper Type L** for visible/exposed runs and any branch where mechanical durability matters more than freeze tolerance.
- **CPVC** — code-allowed, but BSH-incompatible at the durability level: brittle with age, intolerant of incompatible materials (some caulks, foam, electrical wire jackets), and well-documented field failures.

**Constraints from OPSC §604.13 (water heater connections):**

- PEX, PEX-AL-PEX, PE-AL-PE, or PE-RT cannot be installed within the **first 18 inches** of piping connected to a water heater.
- **Exception:** CPVC, PE-RT, PEX, or PP approved per Table 604.1 may be installed within the first 18 inches of piping connected to an **instantaneous on-demand tankless** water heater per manufacturer instructions. Storage water heaters require copper or stainless flexible connectors (≤24 inches) for the first stretch.

**UV / concrete / chlorine constraints:**

- PEX is **not approved for continuous UV exposure** — no exposed runs, no outside-wall stretches, and PEX-coil storage must be shaded. Per ASTM F876, Class 3 UV resistance is 6 months; that is a *grace period during construction*, not a design rating.
- PEX through concrete penetrations requires a nonmetallic sleeve at the penetration.
- PEX cast in concrete is permitted (and standard for radiant). Use higher-burst PEX-B for slab work where rebar drag and trowel/strike-edge damage are a risk.
- Chlorine resistance: confirm the four-digit material code on the pipe is **5306** (Class 5 chlorine, Class 3 UV, HDS Class 6). Avoid 1306 / 3306 grades on chlorinated systems.

**Repairability:**

- PEX-A with expansion fittings is the most repairable in the field — a single tool kit handles every joint.
- PEX-B with crimp/clamp is repairable but tool-system specific.
- HDPE service requires fusion equipment — repair options include compression couplings (acceptable but lower durability) or a fusion contractor.

**Anchor sentence.** The BSH default pipe stack is HDPE PE4710 service line with tracer wire, PEX-A homerun manifold inside (PEX-B if continuous chlorination is part of treatment), copper or stainless flexible connectors at storage water heaters per OPSC §604.13, with no PEX exposed to continuous UV and a nonmetallic sleeve at every slab penetration.

## 9. Hot water system type after load research

The 2026 ORSC heat-pump trajectory and the existing Energy Trust incentive structure both point at **heat pump water heaters (HPWH)** as the BSH default. The Oregon Building Codes Division Residential and Manufactured Structures Board approved heat-pump-mandate revisions on Feb. 18, 2026 for incorporation into the 2026 ORSC.

**HPWH considerations specific to Oregon residential:**

- NEEA Northern Climate Specification — Tier 1 (≥1.8 EF) configured for unconditioned spaces; Tier 2+ (≥2.0 EF) configured for conditioned/semi-conditioned spaces. OAR 330-070-0073.
- Cold-air discharge: HPWH cools the room it's in. Either site in unconditioned space (garage, mech room) with adequate volume (usually ≥1,000 ft³) or duct intake/exhaust outdoors.
- Split-system HPWH (compressor outdoors, tank indoors) avoids the cold-room problem and the small-room sizing issue — Energy Trust trained on the "Amazing Shrinking Room" testing of HPWHs in small spaces in 2026.
- Sizing: 65–80 gal HPWH for typical SFR; oversize at 80 gal if you're running a recirc loop or heavy bath usage.

**Multi-tank / hybrid considerations for BSH durability:**

- A primary HPWH + small electric resistance "buffer" or point-of-use tank at long branches works well, especially with demand-control recirc.
- Indirect from a hydronic boiler is durable but commits you to a boiler — not a BSH-aligned simplification.
- Solar thermal preheat (drainback or glycol) feeding an electric resistance backup is durable but maintenance-intensive — the panels and pump assembly are a separate failure surface.
- Tankless gas: not aligned with Oregon's electrification trajectory and has cold-water-sandwich and minimum-flow issues that show up at low-flow water-saving fixtures. Skip.

**Storage capacity sized to peak hour:** standard rule is 1.5× peak hour demand. Two-shower morning peak in a 4-person household runs 30–40 gal; a 65–80 gal HPWH handles that with margin.

**Anchor sentence.** The BSH primary domestic hot water platform should be a NEEA Northern Climate Tier 2+ HPWH (split-system if siting in a small or conditioned space), sized at 65–80 gal, with point-of-use ER buffer tanks only where branch length forces them — aligned with the 2026 ORSC heat-pump direction and Energy Trust incentive structure.

## 10. Storage temperature, delivery temperature, scald limits, ASSE devices

**Storage and delivery are different temperatures and serve different purposes.** Storage must be hot enough to suppress *Legionella pneumophila*; delivery must be cool enough to prevent scald.

**Storage:**

- Legionella growth is suppressed above ~122°F; rapid kill above ~140°F.
- Standard-of-care storage temp: **140°F** for tank water heaters, with ASSE 1017 or 1070 master mixing at the heater outlet to drop delivery to 120°F.
- HPWHs default to ~120–135°F factory; many models can be set higher but coefficient of performance drops as setpoint rises. Best-practice for HPWH: 130–135°F storage, supplemented with point-of-use mixing where needed.

**Delivery (per OPSC §408.3 and §407, parallel to UPC):**

- **Showers and tub-shower combinations:** ≤120°F via ASSE 1016 (P, T, or P/T) at the point of use, with the limit stop field-set per manufacturer instructions. Water heater thermostat is **not** acceptable as the delivery-temperature control.
- **Bidets:** ≤110°F via ASSE 1070 or CSA B125.3 (UPC §410.3 / IPC §424).
- **Bathtubs and whirlpool tubs:** ≤120°F via ASSE 1070 or equivalent.
- **Lavatories** (hand washing): no fixed code maximum in OPSC, but ASSE 1070 to 110°F is the public-health norm (CDC, ASSE white papers).

**Best-practice setpoint for showers (Griffith, ASSE):** field-set ASSE 1016 limit stop to deliver **110°F**, accepting the standard's ±3.6°F variance at 45 psi means actual delivery is 105–115°F. This eliminates scald risk and is what nursing/elder/child-safe design specifies.

**Legionella + scald simultaneous solution:**

- 140°F storage → ASSE 1017 master mixing at heater outlet → 124°F distribution (above the 122°F Legionella growth ceiling) → ASSE 1016 at each shower set to 110°F → ASSE 1070 at lavs/tubs set to 110–115°F.

This is the architecture every reputable hot water system design article (PHC&P Pros, ASSE white papers) converges on, and it lines up cleanly with OPSC and IPC parallel sections.

**Anchor sentence.** Store at 140°F, distribute at ~124°F via an ASSE 1017 master mixer at the heater, deliver ≤120°F at every fixture via ASSE 1016 (showers/tub-showers), ASSE 1070 (lavs/tubs), and field-set shower limit stops to 110°F to eliminate scald risk while staying above Legionella's 122°F ceiling.

## 11. Recirculation strategy

Continuous recirc is energy-wasteful and creates a Legionella surface area at every loop temperature drop. **Demand-control recirc is the default best practice** and is treated favorably under the energy code.

**ORSC Chapter 11 / N1103.5 requirements for residential:**

- Domestic hot water piping insulated to **R-3 minimum** at: pipe not located fully within the conditioned space; the **first 8 feet** of pipe into and out of the water heater; and **all recirculating water piping**.
- Insulation exposed to weather must be protected from sunlight and moisture (no adhesive tape).

**OEESC §6.4.3 / ASHRAE 90.1 parallel for commercial (informative for residential best practice):**

- Recirc systems must turn off automatically when not operational.
- Demand-sensing controls (flow switch in cold-water make-up or return-water aquastat) shall turn off the system when there's no demand even when operational.
- Check valve required between the circulator and the heater.

**Demand-control architecture for BSH:**

- Push-button or motion-sensor activation at each end-of-loop fixture.
- Pump runs only until the return temperature reaches setpoint (typically the loop sits at room temp until called).
- Loop sized for 1–2 minute fill time on call.
- Crossover valve at the far fixture (under-sink, in vanity), not a dedicated return line. Modern systems (Taco D'Mand, ACT D-Mand) use the cold line as the return path during the priming pulse.
- Insulation: R-4 (1" closed-cell) on hot trunk, R-3 minimum on returns; oversize for the long branches.
- Pump sizing: ¼ HP / 6–10 GPM is plenty for residential.
- Ball valves for balancing on multi-loop systems; check valves to prevent thermosiphon backflow on dormant branches.

**Stagnant volume / dead leg risk:**

- OPSC §309.5 — dead legs cannot exceed 1.5× pipe diameter and must have an accessible flushing method.
- Demand-control recirc minimizes this (loop is flushed every call), but stub branches off the loop need flushing design.
- Avoid running long stub branches off a recirc loop; route stubs as homerun PEX from a manifold instead.

**PEX + recirc note:** continuous recirc at high temperature is the worst case for chlorine attack on PEX. Demand-control recirc keeps loop water cold most of the time — that's a strong durability argument layered on top of the energy argument. If you go continuous recirc, lock in PEX-B Class 5 chlorine resistance and a chlorine-stripping carbon filter ahead of the heater.

**Anchor sentence.** Demand-controlled recirculation with R-3 insulation on all loop piping (and the first 8 feet on each side of the heater per ORSC N1103.5), check valve at the pump, and homerun branches off the loop is the BSH-aligned recirc architecture — energy-code-favored, Legionella-quiet, and PEX-friendly.

## 12. Water hammer arrestors

**Important finding for SFR.** OPSC §609.11 requires water hammer arrestors per **ASSE 1010 or PDI-WH 201** at quick-acting valves — *but* the section contains an explicit Oregon-retained exception:

> "**Exception:** This section shall not apply to one- and two-family dwellings or townhouses."

So water hammer arrestors are **not code-required** for the BSH single-family build. They are still strongly recommended at:

- Clothes washer hot/cold supply box (the most common hammer source).
- Dishwasher supply.
- Refrigerator ice-maker line.
- Any solenoid-actuated valve (irrigation interface, water treatment regeneration).
- Toilet supply lines with quick-close fill valves (less critical with modern fluidmaster valves).

Mini-arrestor units inside the supply box (Sioux Chief MiniRester etc.) are inexpensive and replaceable. Engineered air-chamber stubs are not approved substitutes — they waterlog quickly.

**Anchor sentence.** Water hammer arrestors are exempt from OPSC §609.11 in Oregon for one- and two-family dwellings, but installing ASSE 1010 mini-arrestors at every quick-acting valve (washer, dishwasher, ice maker, irrigation solenoid) is durability-preserving best practice.

## 13. Backflow device selection

Per OPSC §603 and the cross-connection table (§603.5.1 through §603.5.21):

- **Hose bibbs**: hose connection vacuum breaker (HCVB) at every hose bib (ASSE 1011), often integral to modern frost-free models. Required at any threaded outlet that could see hose attachment.
- **Lawn / landscape irrigation, no pumps, no chemical injection**: AVB, PVB, SVB, RP, or DC per §603.5.6 — typical residential is PVB above ground.
- **Lawn irrigation with chemical injection or pump**: RP (reduced-pressure principle) — required, no DC substitute.
- **Hot tubs / spas**: RP. Spas are high-hazard due to chemicals (chlorine, bromine, algaecide). Some jurisdictions accept an air-gap fill, but a permanent connection requires RP.
- **Pressure washer feeds (if permanent)**: RP minimum, often air-gap fill into a tank.
- **Water treatment** (softener brine line, chemical injection regenerant): air gap on brine line discharge; RP upstream of any chemical injection point.
- **Rainwater system makeup from potable**: air gap is the gold standard (OPSC Chapter 16 prefers); RP acceptable per §603.5 family.
- **Boiler fill**: RP if any chemical treatment in the boiler (most hydronic systems have inhibitor); DC only for chemical-free systems.
- **Yard hydrants**: §603.5.17 prohibits underground stop-and-waste valves; external vertical yard hydrants must be self-draining sanitary type or fitted with an ASSE 1024 dual-check.
- **Any nonpotable system** (rainwater, gray water, on-site treated): air gap or RP at every makeup connection; identification per §603.5.11 ("CAUTION: NONPOTABLE WATER, DO NOT DRINK"); exposed portions identified with code-compliant signage and color coding.

**Testing:** OPSC §603.4.2 requires backflow assemblies to be tested at the time of installation. Oregon also requires annual testing of testable assemblies (RP, DC, PVB, SVB) by a certified Backflow Assembly Tester — credentialed under OAR 333-061-0070 and reporting to the local water purveyor.

**Anchor sentence.** Default device map for BSH: HCVB on every hose bib, PVB on landscape irrigation (RP if chemicals or pump), RP on hot tub/spa and boiler fill, air gap on softener brine and rainwater makeup, ASSE 1024 dual-check or self-draining sanitary yard hydrants — with annual backflow testing required for any testable assembly.

## 14. Rainwater catchment and nonpotable outdoor water

Two parallel chapters in 2023 OPSC:

- **Chapter 16 — Nonpotable Rainwater Catchment Systems** (mandatory, statewide).
- **Appendix K — Potable Rainwater Catchment Systems** (alternate method approved by BCD; requires Oregon Health Authority involvement; rarely used for SFR).
- **Chapter 15 — Alternate Water Sources for Nonpotable Applications** (broader gray water, on-site treated nonpotable water).

**Permit and design (OPSC §1601):**

- Nonpotable rainwater systems must be designed by a **certified plumbing contractor or registered design professional**.
- Permit required for any system with components, piping, or fittings inside the building.
- **Exemptions:**
  - Aboveground irrigation, ≤360 gallon storage — no RDP design required.
  - Subsurface or non-sprinkled surface irrigation, ≤360 gallon storage — no RDP design required.
  - Single-family dwelling system where outlets, piping, and components are entirely on the exterior — no RDP design required, no permit required.

**Allowed nonpotable end uses (§1601 et seq.):**

- Water closets and urinals (with proper signage and isolation).
- Trap primers for floor drains and floor sinks.
- Clothes washers.
- Irrigation (sub-surface preferred; sprinkler with health-side considerations).
- Water features.
- Cooling tower makeup.
- Industrial processes (commercial).

**Treatment requirements summary:**

- **Filtration** at intake — debris excluder / first-flush diverter mandatory on all systems.
- **Disinfection** scaled to use: subsurface irrigation ≤ flush toilet ≤ surface irrigation ≤ clothes washer ≤ potable. Toilet flushing typically requires sediment + UV minimum.
- Storage tank: durable, watertight, NSF-aware materials; signage per §1603 ("CAUTION: NONPOTABLE RAINWATER, DO NOT DRINK").
- Diverter valves: §1603.21 requires IAPMO PS 59 (2"–4") or IAPMO IGC 352 (6"–12"), with filter upstream.

**Stormwater and water-rights interaction:**

- Rooftop catchment for on-site use does **not** require an OWRD water right per ORS 537.141 (rooftop exemption).
- Catchment that diverts surface flow or impounds runoff *does* require a water right.
- DEQ 1200-C stormwater construction permits trigger at 1+ acre site disturbance — relevant for the build-site grading, not the rainwater system itself.

**Local jurisdiction layer:**

- Some cities (Portland, Eugene) have published their own rainwater harvesting code guides under OPSC framework — verify city-specific guidance before final design.

**Anchor sentence.** Nonpotable rainwater catchment for the BSH build is governed by OPSC Chapter 16, requires a certified plumbing contractor or RDP designer + permit if any system component is inside the building, and the typical SFR end uses (toilets, clothes washer, irrigation) require sediment + UV at minimum with full §1603 signage and IAPMO-PS-59 diverter valves.

## 15. Outdoor hot water / equipment water in Chapter 9 vs. utility/service-bay section

Recommend Chapter 9 carries:

- Trunk run and isolation valving to the utility/service bay (because that's part of the supply distribution architecture).
- Backflow device specification at the trunk's branch into utility-bay services (RP for any equipment connection — pressure washer, brewing, livestock water, vehicle wash, etc.).
- Insulation and freeze-protection methodology for any branch leaving conditioned space (per ORSC N1103.5 and OPSC §312.6).
- Frost-free hose bibb specification (ASSE 1052 / 1019) for exterior fittings, including the dual-check and self-draining requirements per OPSC §603.5.17.

Move to the utility/service-bay section:

- Specific equipment connections (workshop sinks, hose stations, livestock water, equipment wash, brewing).
- Dedicated POU heaters at remote utility loads.
- Outdoor shower / mudroom shower (this is borderline — if it's a primary fixture serving the residence, keep it in Ch 9; if it's a separate utility shower, move it).
- Hot tub mechanical (definitely utility-bay, with the RP backflow specified back-referencing Ch 9).

The BSH 1,000-year and "function without internet" principles argue for keeping the chapter divisions clean: **Chapter 9 = water supply chassis; utility/service-bay = end-use equipment connections.** That keeps the chapter useful as a reference for any subsequent build that wires up different end-uses on the same supply chassis.

**Anchor sentence.** Chapter 9 carries the water supply chassis through the trunk-to-utility-bay branch with backflow, insulation, and freeze-protection specified; specific outdoor equipment connections, point-of-use heaters, and hot tub mechanical move to the utility/service-bay section, cross-referenced back to Ch 9 for the supply boundary.

# Chapter 10: Plumbing — DWV, Sewage, Venting, Slope, Distance, and Fixtures

The current code stack: **2023 OPSC** (Chapters 7–10 cover DWV) and **DEQ OAR 340-071/073 effective January 1, 2026** (DEQ 29-2025, filed October 1, 2025) for onsite wastewater. The 2026 DEQ amendments are a real shift — they implement SB 931 and SB 835 from the 2023 legislative session and they will be the controlling rule set when BSH builds.

## 1. Drain sizing, slope, vent sizing, trap-arm length, cleanout spacing, vent termination, fixture units

These are anchored in OPSC Chapters 7 (Sanitary Drainage), 9 (Vents — note OPSC chapter numbering), 10 (Traps and Interceptors), with sizing tables centralized in OPSC §703 and §906.

**Fixture units (DFU).** OPSC Table 702.1 gives drainage fixture units per fixture; Table 703.2 caps the DFUs on each pipe size. Single-family residential ranges typically: lavatory 1 DFU, kitchen sink 2, shower 2, tub 2–3, water closet 4 (1.6 gpf) or 3 (HET ≤1.28 gpf), clothes washer 3, laundry tub 2. Bathroom group is 5–8 DFUs depending on fixtures.

**Drain sizing (OPSC §703).** Building drain minimums by DFU (typical SFR cases at 1/4"/ft slope): 3-inch drain handles up to 35 DFUs total; 4-inch handles 216 DFUs at 1/4"/ft slope. Most BSH builds will use 4-inch building drain regardless because the cost difference is trivial and it future-proofs against second-phase fixtures. Vertical stacks: 3-inch handles 30 DFUs in 3 stories; 4-inch handles 240 in 3 stories.

**Slope (OPSC §708.1).** Horizontal drainage piping at uniform slope ≥ 1/4"/ft (2%). Where impractical, pipes ≥ 4 inches may slope as low as 1/8"/ft (1%) **only with prior Building Official approval**. Smaller pipes never go below 1/4"/ft. The 1% provision is real but discretionary; don't design against it as a default.

**Vent sizing (OPSC §906).** Vent pipes never less than 1¼ inches; sized per Table 906.1 against developed length and DFU load. Vents over 40 feet developed length must be one nominal size larger. Each vent must be at least one-half the diameter of the drain it serves.

**Trap-arm length (OPSC Table 1002.2).** This is the most-violated dimension in DIY work; commit it to memory:

- 1¼" trap arm: max 2'-6", min 2½" (= 2× diameter)
- 1½" trap arm: max 3'-6", min 3"
- 2" trap arm: max 5'-0", min 4"
- 3" trap arm: max 6'-0", min 6"
- 4" trap arm: max 10'-0", min 8"

**Footnote 2 to Table 1002.2 (the water closet rule):** developed length from top of closet flange to inner edge of the vent must not exceed 6 feet, regardless of whether you use a 3-inch or 4-inch toilet drain. Above-floor toilet vent geometry is the most common rough-in failure.

A trap arm may change direction without a cleanout where the change does not exceed 90°; for trap arms 3 inches and larger, the change may go to 135° without a cleanout.

**Cleanout spacing (OPSC §707.4).** Each horizontal drain run ≥ 100 ft requires a cleanout per 100 ft; cleanout at every aggregate change of direction exceeding 135° (UPC convention; this is more permissive than IPC's 45° rule); cleanout at upper terminal of each horizontal drain; cleanout at base of each waste/soil stack; two-way cleanout at the building drain / building sewer junction (inside or outside the building wall, brought to grade or basement floor). Exception: cleanouts may be omitted on horizontal drain lines < 5 feet long unless serving sinks or urinals.

**Vent termination (OPSC §906.1).** Roof termination is the prescriptive default — vent extends through its flashing and terminates vertically not less than 6 inches above the roof (some jurisdictions amend to 12 inches; verify with local Building Official). At least 10 feet from, or 3 feet above, any openable window, door, opening, air intake, or vent shaft (§906.2). At least 3 feet from any lot line in any direction (alley/street excepted). Frost/snow closure: vent terminals not less than 2 inches in diameter at termination — relevant in BSH's freeze design.

**Anchor sentence.** OPSC §703 / §906 / §1002 / §707 set the prescriptive geometry (1¼-inch min vent, 1/4"/ft drain slope, 100-ft cleanout spacing, 6-foot WC trap-arm cap, Table 1002.2 trap-arm distances, 6-inch min above-roof vent termination with 10 ft / 3 ft offsets from openings) — get these dimensions wrong and the rough-in fails.

## 2. Basement toilet options — and the Venturi-pump concept

Five recognized paths under Oregon code, plus the manuscript's Venturi concept.

**Sewage ejector pump (OPSC §710 — the standard path).** OPSC §710.3 single-family residential ejectors must pass a 1½" diameter solid ball, with discharge piping not less than 2 inches and equipped with a backwater valve and gate valve. Sized in §710.4 to provide sufficient TDH (static lift + friction + system pressure) to lift to the gravity building drain. For a typical basement bathroom group (10–12 DFU), a 1/2 HP ejector at ~25 GPM into a 30–40 gal basin is the standard solution. Watertight, gastight basin; vent ≥1½" sized per Table 703.2; high-water alarm strongly recommended (and required by manufacturer instructions for nearly all listed ejectors). Discharge line: union, check valve, full-way ball or gate valve — installed in that order.

**Macerating toilet system (OPSC §710.13 — alternative to ejector).** Permitted only in one- and two-family dwellings, only when gravity flow is impossible, only one bathroom group per macerating device. Listed to ASME A112.3.4/CSA B45.9. Discharge ≥ ¾", sized per manufacturer; check valve and full-way shutoff in the discharge line. Sump must be watertight and gastight. Macerating sumps may share fixture venting per manufacturer instructions. Brand examples: Saniflo, Liberty Pumps Ascent. Practical limits: discharge runs and lift heights are constrained by manufacturer spec; macerating units are noisier and more service-intensive than ejectors over the BSH 1,000-year horizon.

**Composting toilet (OAR 918-770-0080).** Listed to NSF/ANSI Standard 41. Plumbing permit required. Critical Oregon constraint: **residential composting toilets must be installed only where an approved DEQ graywater disposal system can also be installed**, or where graywater discharges to public sewer. So a composting toilet doesn't eliminate the wastewater system — it just substitutes the blackwater stream. Composting toilets are anchored to the floor with corrosion-resistant fasteners; chamber must be insulated; humus from composting toilets may be buried under ≥12" soil cover near ornamental shrubs, flowers, trees, or fruit trees, but never deposited around edible vegetable beds. For the BSH 1,000-year frame, composting toilets get attractive only if site conditions make conventional septic prohibitive.

**Incinerating toilet.** Falls under NSF/ANSI 41 standard alongside composting toilets (the standard covers "non-liquid saturated waste-treatment systems"). Approval pathway is via the Oregon Building Codes Division as an approved fixture; same plumbing permit. Independent mechanical exhaust through the roof; significant electrical load (3–5 kW). Practical limit: high power consumption and ash disposal — culturally a hard sell, but technically permissible.

**Graywater-only basement (zero blackwater).** If basement has only handwash + sink + maybe a shower (no toilet), basement plumbing routes to the gravity drain or graywater system without ejector. If a toilet is in scope, you need one of the four above paths.

**The manuscript's Venturi pump concept.** No approved path under OPSC. Two specific bars block it:

1. OPSC §710.3 requires sewage ejectors to pass a 1½" solid ball (SFR). A water-jet venturi ejector cannot meet this without first comminuting solids — at which point it becomes a macerating system and must comply with ASME A112.3.4/CSA B45.9, which is a listing for specific products (Saniflo et al.), not a generic device class.
2. OPSC §710.13 macerating systems are listed products. There is no listing standard for "venturi sewage ejector" and no listed manufacturer.

The only path forward is **OPSC §301.5 alternative engineered design**, which would require: a registered design professional's stamped design; demonstration of equivalent performance to the listed methods; explicit Building Official approval (jurisdiction-specific, no statewide precedent); and continuing maintenance accountability. Practically, no Oregon Building Official is going to approve a custom venturi sewage ejector for a single-family residence over the conventional ejector + macerator alternatives.

**Recommendation for the manuscript:** Treat the Venturi pump concept as a research curiosity, not a deployable basement-toilet path. Mention it briefly in a "non-compliant alternatives investigated" section if at all. Default basement toilet path for BSH = listed sewage ejector with high-water alarm and accessible serviceable basin; macerating system as a secondary option for retrofits where gravity drain location forces it.

**Anchor sentence.** Code-recognized basement toilet paths in Oregon are sewage ejector (OPSC §710), macerating toilet (OPSC §710.13, ASME A112.3.4 listing required), composting toilet (OAR 918-770-0080, NSF 41 listed, requires DEQ-approved graywater system), and incinerating toilet (NSF 41 listing); a Venturi-style sewage pump has no listing pathway and would require an OPSC §301.5 alternative engineered design that no Oregon AHJ has signaled willingness to approve.

## 3. Sewer vs. septic decision, lateral/septic approval, distance/slope, pump, backwater, basement protection

**The 2026 sewer-availability test (DEQ 29-2025, effective January 1, 2026).** Implementing SB 931, the new rule reduces the "sewer available" trigger distance from 300 feet to **200 feet** for dwellings needing repairs or alterations, and **limits sewer availability to areas zoned for urbanization**. The municipality determines physical and legal availability; DEQ must deny a septic permit when sewer is "available" under those criteria. Practical consequence for a rural BSH build: if sewer is more than 200 feet from a dwelling needing alterations, or the parcel is outside the urban growth boundary, septic stays the default path even when the city would otherwise prefer connection.

For a new build (not a repair/alteration), the 200-ft test doesn't directly apply — the new-construction sewer-availability framework is broader and case-specific. A new structure within the urban growth boundary and within reasonable lateral reach of a sewer must connect; outside the UGB, septic is presumptive.

**Septic approval sequence (OAR 340-071 et seq.):**

1. **Site evaluation report (SER).** Soil profile, slope, depth to limiting layer, water table, setbacks. Conducted by DEQ agent (county environmental health office in agreement counties). Issued as either site approval or denial; issue with limiting conditions identifies the system type required.
2. **System design / Construction permit (OAR 340-071-0162).** Plans and specs to local agent or DEQ. Some standard subsurface systems can be permitted without a stamped engineer; alternative systems (ATT, sand filter, capping fill, pressure distribution, etc.) require an Oregon-registered design professional or sanitarian.
3. **Pre-cover inspection.** Mandatory before backfilling.
4. **Construction-site compliance certificate (CSC).** Issued after final inspection. **DEQ 29-2025 introduces a start-up inspection requirement before CSC issuance** — that's a new step in the 2026 rules.
5. **Operation and maintenance contract** (where required for ATT/sand filter/RGF systems). Consolidated under new OAR 340-071-0132 in the 2026 rules.

**Distance and slope rules.** Building sewer ≥ 5 feet from any building or structure (OPSC §312.3); minimum 1 foot of cover unless protected; freeze depth applies (OPSC §312.6). Inside the building drain, 1/4"/ft default slope; ≥ 4" pipe may go to 1/8"/ft with Building Official approval. Septic absorption-trench setbacks (OAR 340-071-0220 setback table): 100 feet to wells (greater in some geology), 50 feet to streams/lakes, 10 feet to property lines, 10 feet to building foundations, 10 feet to water lines, with substantial site-specific variation.

**Pump requirements for septic.** When the topography forces an effluent pump (low-pressure dosing, mound system, or any uphill leg from septic tank), DEQ requires a multi-compartment tank with effluent filter, dual pumps recommended for ATT systems, level alarms, and access risers (DEQ 29-2025 standardizes service-access riser requirements).

**Backwater valve triggers (OPSC §710).** Required wherever fixtures are installed below the level of the next upstream manhole or the elevation of the curb at the point where the building sewer joins the main. Swing check or approved backwater valve must be accessible — typically in a masonry pit fitted with a removable cover (§710.6). Backwater valves comply with ASME A112.14.1 or IAPMO IGC 305.

**Basement-fixture protection.** Three layers: (a) the ejector pump system itself (§710.3) for any below-grade fixture that can't gravity-drain; (b) backwater valve on the gravity drain serving the basement when there's risk of main backup; (c) for combined-sewer jurisdictions, additional backflow protection on the building sewer near the property line. The OPSC §710 architecture combined: union, check valve, full-way gate/ball valve at the ejector discharge; backwater valve at the building drain elevation; and (for paranoid durability design) a second normally-closed shutoff at the building sewer / property line.

**Anchor sentence.** Under DEQ's 2026 rules (effective January 1, 2026), the sewer-availability test is 200 feet for repair/alteration dwellings within urban growth boundary zoning; outside that envelope septic is presumptive, with site evaluation → construction permit → pre-cover inspection → start-up inspection → CSC (the start-up inspection is new), and basement fixtures get layered protection via OPSC §710 ejector + backwater valve + accessible service architecture.

## 4. What belongs in Chapter 10 vs. Chapter 9 (rainwater, graywater, alternate wastewater, nonpotable)

The clean split:

**Chapter 9 carries the supply / distribution chassis**, including: nonpotable rainwater catchment supply piping, makeup-water connections, backflow protection at the potable-side interface (RP or air gap), and the trunk into utility/equipment.

**Chapter 10 carries the drainage / disposal chassis**, including:

- **Graywater collection and diversion.** Plumbing-side architecture per OPSC Chapter 15 (Alternate Water Sources for Nonpotable Applications). Diverter valve (OAR 340-053-0100) routes graywater between beneficial reuse (irrigation, toilet flushing) and the sanitary disposal path (sewer or onsite). Diverter valve must be readily accessible, durable, corrosion-resistant. Overflow drain ≥ inlet diameter, gravity to approved sewer or onsite system, no shutoff valve on the overflow.
- **Graywater system permits and DEQ Type classification (OAR 340-053).** Type 1 (untreated or filtered for fats/oils/solids) for subsurface irrigation. Type 2 (chemical/biological treatment, e.g., wetland or technology-based) for surface or restricted irrigation. Type 3 (highest treatment) for indoor toilet flushing.
- **Permit pathway.** Permit required from DEQ for any graywater reuse/disposal system except the laundry-to-landscape (L2L) exemption (clothes washer only, subsurface or surface non-sprinkled, no pump, no storage, no crossing under structures). All other graywater systems need a DEQ permit (general permit #2401 or individual) **plus** a plumbing permit issued under OPSC Chapter 15.
- **Land use (OAR 340-053-0080(17)).** Graywater systems producing < 1,200 gpd connected to approved sewer or DEQ-approved onsite are exempt from a Land Use Compatibility Statement.
- **Composting toilet drainage.** Liquid leachate piping from composting unit to graywater disposal (per OAR 918-770-0080). The drainage side belongs in Chapter 10; the toilet fixture itself belongs in the fixtures chapter.
- **Nonpotable rainwater catchment overflow.** Tank overflow piping must connect to an approved disposal — sewer, onsite system, or DEQ-approved stormwater discharge — through an air-break (no direct connection). This is drainage-side architecture.
- **Sump pump discharge of clear water (foundation drainage, condensate).** OPSC §714.2 prohibits discharge of rain, surface, or subsurface water to the sanitary drainage system without Building Official approval. Foundation drains typically discharge above grade to daylight, French drain, or storm system — not the sanitary drain.

**Manuscript orientation:** Chapter 9 introduces the alternate-source water supply (rainwater collection, treatment, distribution). Chapter 10 carries the corresponding drain/disposal architecture for those alternate sources, the diverter-valve logic, and the DEQ permit pathway. Cross-reference between the two chapters at every diverter point.

**Anchor sentence.** Chapter 10 carries the drainage and disposal half of any alternate-source system: graywater diverter valve and DEQ permit pathway (OAR 340-053), composting toilet leachate piping, nonpotable rainwater overflow, and the sanitary fallback paths — all of which must connect to either an approved sewer or an OAR 340-071 onsite system at the overflow boundary.

## 5. Cleanout clearance, underfloor, access, direction, spacing — full detail

OPSC §707 governs.

**Clearance (§707.9).**

- Cleanouts on piping ≤ 2 inches: clearance ≥ 18 inches × 18 inches in front of the cleanout opening.
- Cleanouts on piping > 2 inches: clearance ≥ 24 inches × 24 inches.
- Under-floor cleanouts: must be extended to or above the finished floor, **or** extended outside the building, **wherever** there is less than 18 inches vertical overall (allowing for ducts, beams, piping obstructions) and 30 inches horizontal clearance from the means of access.

**Direction (§707.5).** Each cleanout opens to allow cleaning in the direction of flow or at right angles to it. Cleanouts must be installed vertically above the flow line of the pipe, except at wye-branch and end-of-line cleanouts.

**Cleanout extension (§707.6).** Each cleanout extension is treated as drainage piping. A 90° cleanout extension must be extended from a wye-type fitting or other approved fitting of equivalent sweep — never from a sanitary tee.

**Where required (§707.4):**

- Upper terminal of each horizontal drain.
- Every 100 feet of total developed length, or fraction thereof.
- Each aggregate horizontal change of direction exceeding 135°.
- Above each urinal fixture connection (regardless of building location).
- At base of each waste/soil stack.
- Near the junction of the building drain and the building sewer (inside or outside the building wall).

**Exceptions:**

- Horizontal drain < 5 ft long, unless serving sinks or urinals.
- Horizontal drainage on slope ≥ 72° from vertical (effectively, near-vertical pipes).
- Above the lowest floor's floor level, except for the building drain and its horizontal branches.

**Two-way cleanout (§707.4).** An approved two-way cleanout fitting at the building drain / building sewer junction (inside the building wall near the connection, or outside at the lower end of the building drain extended to grade) may substitute for the upper-terminal cleanout. This is the BSH-aligned default — easier service from either direction.

**Cleanout sizing (§707.10).** Cleanout same nominal size as the pipe it serves, up to 4 inches. For piping > 4 inches, cleanouts may be 4 inches.

**Access (§707.8).** Each cleanout, unless installed under an approved cover plate, must be above grade, readily accessible, and located to allow rodding to occur in the proper direction. No concealed cleanouts.

**Anchor sentence.** Cleanouts under OPSC §707 require 18×18 clearance for ≤2" pipe, 24×24 for >2", a two-way cleanout near the building drain/sewer junction, cleanouts at every 100 ft and at every aggregate change of direction exceeding 135°, no concealed installations, and underfloor cleanouts extended to floor level or outside whenever clearance falls below 18" vertical / 30" horizontal.

## 6. Sewage ejector — discharge line, valves, basin, alarm, vent, pressure-rated pipe

OPSC §710 in full residential detail:

**§710.3 Pump and discharge.** Single-family residential ejector receiving water closet discharge:

- Discharge capacity ≥ 20 GPM.
- Pass a 1½" diameter solid ball.
- Discharge piping ≥ 2" diameter.
- **Backwater valve and gate valve** in the discharge line.

(Multi-family / non-SFR: 2" solid ball, ≥3" discharge.)

**§710.4 Discharge line.** Of approved pressure-rated material; provided with an accessible backwater or swing check valve and a gate or ball valve. Fittings must be full-way type, not restrict flow, approved for use with pressure discharge piping. Where the gravity drainage line (to which the discharge line connects) is horizontal, the connection is from the **top** through a wye branch fitting. The gate or ball valve goes on the discharge side of the backwater/check valve. The standard sequence (bottom to top from pump): pump → union → check valve / backwater valve → full-way ball or gate valve → vertical riser → wye into top of gravity drain.

**§710.5 Sump.** Watertight and gastight. Vent extended through the roof or connected to the house venting system where permitted. Sump vent ≥ 1½", sized per Table 703.2 for the connected fixtures. Any fixtures discharging into the sump are themselves trapped and vented as required for gravity systems.

**Pneumatic ejector air-pressure relief vent.** Connected to an independent vent stack terminating as required for vent extensions through the roof. Sized to relieve to atmospheric pressure, ≥1½" diameter (some codes ≥2"). Cannot combine with other vents.

**§710.6 Backwater valves.** ASME A112.14.1 or IAPMO IGC 305. Cast-iron, plastic, copper alloy, or other approved bodies; non-corrosive bearings and seats; positive mechanical seal. Must remain open during low flow to avoid solids stranding. Accessible — masonry pit with bolted gasketed cover when not exposed.

**Basin sizing.** Code references manufacturer instructions. Industry rule of thumb: basin volume = pump GPM × 0.75 minutes (45-second runtime target). For 25 GPM ejector, ~19 gallon working volume → 30–40 gallon basin for cycling reasonably.

**Alarm.** Code references manufacturer instructions; high-water float alarm required by virtually every listed manufacturer. BSH-aligned best practice: independently powered alarm with battery backup, audible + visual at a normal-occupancy location (not in the basement utility room where it can't be heard). Some local AHJs require this explicitly.

**Materials for discharge line.** Schedule 40 PVC is the standard residential pressure-rated discharge — must be pressure rated for the maximum discharge pressure of the pump. Cast iron, ductile iron, and copper are also acceptable. PEX is **not** approved for sewage ejector discharge — that's a closed-loop pressure application without thermal cycling, and the listing standard for PEX (ASTM F876) doesn't cover sewage service.

**Anchor sentence.** The OPSC §710 SFR ejector architecture is: 1½"-ball-passing pump, 2"+ pressure-rated discharge with sequence union → check/backwater valve → full-way ball or gate valve → wye into the top of the gravity drain; watertight gastight basin; ≥1½" vent connected to the house vent system; high-water alarm at an occupied location.

## 7. Sidewall vent termination — legality and limits

**Roof termination is the prescriptive path.** OPSC §906.1 requires every vent pipe or stack to extend through its flashing and terminate vertically not less than 6 inches above the roof. (Some cold-climate jurisdictions amend this to 12 inches; verify with the local Building Official.)

**Sidewall venting (§906.1.1 in some UPC editions).** Where sidewall venting is utilized, the vent shall:

- Not terminate under a vented overhang of a building.
- Be located in accordance with §906.2 (the openings clearances): ≥ 10 ft from / ≥ 3 ft above any openable window, door, opening, air intake, or vent shaft; ≥ 3 ft from a lot line in any direction (alley/street excepted).
- Be directed downward and covered with a 3/32" mesh screen to prevent vermin/rodent entry.

The §906.1.1 sidewall provision exists in some UPC editions and was removed/altered in the 2021 model code. **Oregon's adopted 2023 OPSC follows the 2021 UPC base**; sidewall venting is **not a prescribed default** in the OPSC — it is a Building Official discretion call, and the relevant clearance tests in §906.2 still apply regardless of how it's framed.

**Snow and ice (§906.7 model code).** Vent terminals not less than 2 inches in diameter, never smaller than the required vent. Where the building is in a snow-load region, any change in pipe diameter must be made inside the building not less than 12 inches below the roof in an insulated space. This is the rule that makes 1¼" or 1½" vents from individual fixture branches enlarge to 2"+ before exterior termination — you don't want a 1¼" vent terminating outside a snowy overhang where ice closes it.

**Insect screen.** Where required at sidewall (3/32" mesh), screens must be removable for cleaning. UPC traditionally discouraged screens at roof vents because of clogging concerns, but the sidewall geometry requires them.

**Practical Oregon recommendation.** Don't design BSH around sidewall venting as a default. The cost of a roof penetration is low; the geometry constraints (10 ft / 3 ft / 3 ft, no soffit overhang, downward termination, screening) plus AHJ discretion plus Cascadia/coastal weather make sidewall venting a brittle choice. Reserve sidewall vent only for retrofit scenarios where roof access is impossible. For BSH new construction, every drainage stack terminates through the roof per §906.1 — no exceptions.

**Anchor sentence.** Roof termination per OPSC §906.1 is the BSH default; sidewall venting is permissible only under §906.1.1 with strict clearances (10 ft from / 3 ft above openings; 3 ft from lot lines), no termination under vented overhangs, downward direction with 3/32" insect screen, and AHJ discretion — too brittle for the BSH 1,000-year design.

## 8. DEQ onsite wastewater rules effective January 1, 2026

**The rulemaking.** DEQ 29-2025, filed October 1, 2025, effective January 1, 2026. Amendments to OAR 340-071 (onsite wastewater treatment systems) and OAR 340-073 (construction standards). Authorized under ORS 454.625 and 468.020. Implements key provisions of SB 931 (sewer availability) and SB 835 (ADUs) from the 2023 legislative session.

**Major changes affecting BSH-style builds:**

**Sewer availability (SB 931 implementation).**

- DEQ must continue denying septic permits where sewer is "available" — that authority is retained.
- New 200-ft trigger distance (down from 300 ft) for **dwellings needing repairs or alterations** to existing septic systems — closer septic-to-sewer distances now compel connection.
- Sewer availability is **limited to areas zoned for urbanization**. Outside the urban growth boundary, septic remains presumptive even where a sewer line happens to pass nearby.
- Municipalities determine physical and legal availability; DEQ gives weight to that determination.
- Cost-of-repair vs. cost-of-connection considerations passed to municipalities (DEQ doesn't perform that analysis).
- Practical effect for BSH (rural Oregon owner-builder context): if the build is outside the UGB, this section doesn't compel connection. If inside the UGB and within 200 feet of a sewer, expect mandatory connection on any major repair to an existing system. New construction inside the UGB has historically been more permissively required to connect.

**ADUs (SB 835 implementation).**

- ADUs now defined in OAR 340-071-0100.
- Design flow: **300 gpd for 1- to 2-bedroom ADU** (separate from primary dwelling's design flow).
- Two pathways clarified: (a) shared septic system serving primary + ADU, with the system sized for cumulative design flow; (b) standalone separate septic system for the ADU.
- Site evaluation for an ADU on existing-septic property must address whether the existing system has capacity, or whether a major alteration is needed.

**Nutrient loading and treatment.**

- Agents (DEQ or contract county) may require Alternative Treatment Technology (ATT) systems with **higher nitrogen removal** in geographically defined areas where standard systems are inadequate.
- Empowers agents to require above-minimum standards in nitrate-sensitive areas (Lower Umatilla Basin Groundwater Management Area; areas of southern Willamette Valley and Deschutes; coastal lowlands).
- For BSH builds in the affected geographies, expect DEQ or county to specify ATT (e.g., AdvanTex, Norweco Singulair, Bioclere) instead of standard subsurface.

**Variances.**

- Variance applications must demonstrate that the proposed system is **equally or more protective** to public health and the environment as the standard rule.
- Variances may now expressly include **ongoing conditions** like sampling and reporting (i.e., a variance is not a one-time approval but a permit-like obligation).
- Appeal of variance denials is shifted to **contested case hearing** under ORS 183, replacing prior informal appeal pathways.

**Operation and maintenance.**

- All O&M rules consolidated into new OAR 340-071-0132.
- Clarifies maintenance provider and owner responsibilities.
- Adds **minimum maintenance requirements** by system type.
- Expands "Compliance Recovery Fee" definition.
- Introduces **ongoing maintenance contracts** as a permit condition (vs. periodic inspection only).
- Adds **start-up inspection before Construction-Site Compliance certification** — new step in the approval sequence.

**Housekeeping.**

- Major maintenance now includes replacement of distribution boxes and drop boxes (previously these were treated as minor).
- ESERs (Existing System Evaluation Reports) by NAWT-only certified persons must add an SDS (Septic Disposal System) license requirement.
- Agents may now require a new site evaluation if existing records lack sufficient information.
- Removed the standing technical review committee for River Road / Santa Clara, Lane County.

**Practical implications for BSH.**

- New build: site evaluation under 2026 rules; consider ATT systems in nutrient-sensitive areas (factor into both budget and Chapter 10 design pages).
- ADU planning: 300 gpd ADU sizing means a typical primary 4-BR (450 gpd) + 2-BR ADU (300 gpd) = 750 gpd cumulative — pushes drainfield sizing materially.
- O&M contract becomes a permit condition for ATT/RGF/sand-filter systems — factor cost into the long-term ownership model.
- Start-up inspection adds 1–2 weeks to the timeline before CSC.
- Variance pathway is now harder (contested case hearing on appeal) — design to avoid needing one.

**Anchor sentence.** DEQ's January 1, 2026 onsite wastewater rule update (DEQ 29-2025) reduces the sewer-availability trigger to 200 feet within urban-growth zoning, defines ADU sizing at 300 gpd for 1–2 bedroom units, expands agents' authority to require ATT systems in nitrate-sensitive geography, adds a start-up inspection before CSC, requires ongoing O&M contracts for ATT/RGF/sand-filter systems, and shifts variance appeals to contested-case hearings — every BSH septic design conversation after that date references these amendments by name.

## 9. Graywater from a basement handwash station — permitted disposal paths

**Basement handwash sink wastewater is graywater.** OAR 340-053-0070 defines graywater as wastewater from showers, bathtubs, **bathroom sinks**, kitchen sinks, and laundry machines. Graywater excludes toilet discharge, garbage disposal waste, and diaper-soiled water.

**Disposal options:**

**Option A — Route to sanitary disposal (the simple path).** Connect the handwash station drain through the regular DWV system to the building drain, then to sewer or onsite septic. No graywater permit required because nothing is being diverted. This is the BSH-aligned default. The basement ejector (if needed because the handwash is below building drain elevation) handles the lift.

**Option B — Divert to a permitted graywater system.** Requires:

- DEQ graywater permit under OAR 340-053-0080, either General Permit #2401 (registration-based) or an individual permit.
- Plumbing permit under OPSC Chapter 15.
- A graywater diversion valve (OAR 340-053-0100) that allows redirecting flow back to sewer/onsite during winter months, system maintenance, or whenever beneficial reuse isn't desired. Diverter must be readily accessible, durable, corrosion-resistant.
- Overflow drain ≥ inlet diameter, gravity to approved sewer or functioning onsite system, no shutoff valve on the overflow path.
- Setbacks per OAR 340-053-0090 Table 2.
- Annual reporting requirement.

**Option C — Pump basin route.** If the handwash is below grade and you're already running an ejector for blackwater fixtures, you can either (i) tee the handwash into the same ejector basin (treats as combined waste, sewer/septic disposal), or (ii) install a separate small graywater-only sump that pumps to a graywater system aboveground. Option (ii) requires the Option B permit pathway plus DEQ approval of the graywater pump architecture.

**Important distinction — the L2L exemption doesn't apply.** The Type 1 Laundry-to-Landscape (L2L) exemption (no permit needed) is specifically for clothes washer effluent only. Bathroom sink graywater needs the standard permit pathway no matter how small the volume.

**Permit-exempt micro-volumes.** Under OAR 340-053-0080(17), graywater systems producing < 1,200 gpd connected to approved sewer/onsite are exempt from a Land Use Compatibility Statement — that's a paperwork exemption, not a permit exemption. The graywater permit (Option B) is still required for any diversion.

**Recommendation for BSH.** For a basement handwash, the simple path (Option A — discharge to sanitary) is materially better because it avoids the DEQ graywater permit overhead, the diverter-valve maintenance liability, and the annual reporting. Reserve Option B for landscape irrigation cases where the volume justifies the regulatory layer (typically clothes washer or shower volumes, not handwash). If you do want to capture basement handwash for irrigation, route it into a unified graywater collection system that takes other graywater fixtures too — economies of scale apply to permit overhead.

**Anchor sentence.** A basement handwash drain is graywater under OAR 340-053-0070; the BSH-aligned path is to drain it to the regular DWV (Option A — no permit), with an OAR 340-053 permitted graywater system (Option B — DEQ permit + plumbing permit + diverter valve + annual reporting) only when the volume joins a larger graywater capture for landscape irrigation.

## What's settled, what's a design choice, what needs an attorney/PE

**Settled (code or rule):**

- 200-ft sewer availability trigger inside UGB for repair/alteration (DEQ 2026).
- 300 gpd ADU sizing for 1–2 BR (DEQ 2026).
- ATT-eligibility expansion in nitrate-sensitive geography (DEQ 2026).
- O&M contract requirement and start-up inspection (DEQ 2026).
- 6-foot WC trap-arm cap, Table 1002.2 trap-arm distances (OPSC §1002).
- 100-foot cleanout spacing, 135° change-of-direction trigger, two-way cleanout at building drain/sewer junction (OPSC §707).
- 1/4"/ft default drain slope; 1/8"/ft only ≥4" with AHJ approval (OPSC §708.1).
- Sewage ejector architecture (OPSC §710): 1½" solid-ball SFR pump, 2"+ pressure discharge, backwater valve + full-way valve, watertight gastight basin, ≥1½" vent.
- Macerating toilet: ASME A112.3.4 listing required (OPSC §710.13); 1/2-family only, one bathroom group max.
- Composting toilet: NSF 41 listing + DEQ-approved graywater system required (OAR 918-770-0080).
- Roof vent termination ≥6" above roof, 10 ft / 3 ft offsets from openings (OPSC §906).
- Graywater = bathroom sink, shower, kitchen sink, laundry; toilet discharge excluded (OAR 340-053-0070).

**Design choice with engineering judgment:**

- Sewage ejector vs. macerating system selection.
- Composting toilet vs. ejector for blackwater.
- Backwater valve placement strategy (basement vs. building sewer property line).
- Whether to capture basement handwash into a graywater system or route to sanitary.
- ATT system selection in nitrate-sensitive areas.

**Needs PE-stamped engineered design (OPSC §301.5 / OAR 340-071):**

- Any septic system other than a standard subsurface (ATT, sand filter, RGF, pressure dosing, capping fill, mound).
- Variance applications under OAR 340-071-0415 (now subject to contested-case hearing on denial).
- Any non-listed pumping or ejection device (the Venturi-pump concept).

**Needs attorney input (when contractual or sale issues arise):**

- Disclosure obligations on resale of properties with permitted graywater systems or ATT septic — touches the seller property disclosure framework (Chapter on resale).
- Variance conditions running with the land — recordable obligations affect title.
- Sewer-availability disputes between owner and municipality (rare, but the 2026 rules formalize the appeal pathway).

# Chapter 11: Electrical Service, Panels, Grounding, and Load Planning

## 1. Oregon code adoption, NEC base, service sizing, load calc, GES, CEE, SPD, emergency disconnect, panel/clearance, inspection

**Adopted code stack.** 2023 Oregon Electrical Specialty Code (OESC), effective October 1, 2023, based on the **2023 NFPA 70 (NEC)** with Oregon amendments in OESC Table 1-E. Adoption rule: OAR 918-305-0100. The Electrical and Elevator Board has opened the 2026 OESC adoption process; **anticipated adoption October 1, 2026**, which will move the base to the 2026 NEC. BCD 17-2025 (filed December 30, 2025, effective January 1, 2026) added intermediate amendments to the 2023 OESC. So between now and October 2026, BSH builds run on the 2023 NEC + Oregon Table 1-E + the BCD 17-2025 interim amendments. After October 1, 2026, anything pulled under permit transitions to the 2026 NEC base.

**Service sizing — minimum and practical.** NEC 230.79(C) sets a 100 A minimum for single-family dwellings. Practical floor for a modern all-electric home is **200 A**, and dual-EV + heat pump + electric water heating + shop loads commonly push to **320–400 A** without load management. With load management (EVEMS, smart panels), 200 A often holds.

**Load calculation methods (NEC Article 220).**

- **Standard method (220.42).** Sum general lighting (3 VA/sqft under 2023; 2 VA/sqft under 2026) + small-appliance circuits (1500 VA each, min 2) + laundry (1500 VA min) → apply Table 220.42 demand (first 3,000 VA at 100%, 3,001–120,000 VA at 35%) → add fixed appliances → add larger of heating/cooling at 100% (220.60) → add 25% of largest motor (220.50). EVSE per **220.57** at 7,200 VA or nameplate, whichever is larger.
- **Optional method (220.82).** Available where service ≥ 100 A, single 120/240 V 3-wire. Sum total connected load (lighting at 3 VA/sqft, all small-appliance + laundry circuits, all fixed appliances, both heating and cooling — then drop the smaller in step 2). First 10,000 VA at 100%, remainder at 40%; heating/cooling at one of the percentages in 220.82(C). Almost always yields a smaller calculated demand than the standard method.
- **2026 NEC** (relevant once OESC adopts in October 2026): general lighting drops to 2 VA/sqft, first demand tier drops to 8,000 VA, EVSE locked at 100% nameplate continuous, and **EVEMS (625.48) gets a new exception waiving the 125% continuous multiplier** when a listed energy management system actively limits load.

**Grounding electrode system (NEC 250.50, 250.52).** All present electrodes must be bonded together. Acceptable electrodes:

- (A)(1) Metal underground water pipe ≥10 ft in earth (always supplemented by a second electrode — utility could swap to PVC).
- (A)(2) Metal in-ground support structure ≥10 ft vertical earth contact.
- (A)(3) **Concrete-encased electrode (Ufer)** — see below.
- (A)(4) Ground ring ≥20 ft of bare #2 AWG copper, ≥30" deep encircling the building.
- (A)(5) Rod/pipe ≥8 ft, 5/8" rod or 3/4" pipe (galvanized for steel).
- (A)(7) Plate ≥2 sqft surface, ≥30" deep.
- (A)(8) Other listed electrodes.

GEC sizing per NEC Table 250.66. For Ufer-only: GEC never larger than #4 Cu (250.66(B)). For ground-rod-only: GEC never larger than #6 Cu (250.66(A)).

**Concrete-encased electrode (NEC 250.52(A)(3)).** Required to be incorporated into the GES whenever present in new construction (per 250.50, no existing-building escape clause when the building is new). Minimum spec: **20 ft of #4 (½") rebar** (one continuous piece, or multiple pieces tied with steel tie wire, exothermic weld, or other effective means to total ≥20 ft) **OR 20 ft of bare #4 AWG copper conductor**, encased by ≥2" of concrete in horizontal portion of footing or vertical foundation in **direct earth contact**. Multiple CEEs present → only one needs to be bonded (250.52(A)(3) last sentence). For BSH, every footing already has rebar; pour day requires a tail brought above slab to the future panel location.

**Surge protection (NEC 230.67).** Type 1 or Type 2 SPD required at every dwelling unit service — integral to or immediately adjacent to the service equipment. The 2023 NEC expanded the rule to feeders supplying dwelling units (215.18) and to outside branch circuits/feeders (225.42). Also new: 409.70 SPD for personnel-safety control panels. Where the service is replaced, SPD becomes mandatory.

**Emergency disconnect (NEC 230.85, 2023 edition).** All one- and two-family dwellings must terminate service conductors in disconnecting means located at a **readily accessible outdoor location, on the dwelling or within sight (≤50 ft per NFPA Article 100 "in sight")**. SCCR ≥ available fault current. If multiple, grouped. Each disconnect must be one of:

1. A service disconnect, or
2. A meter disconnect integral to the meter equipment **not** marked "suitable only for use as service equipment," installed per 230.82, or
3. Another listed disconnect/breaker on the supply side of each service disconnect, marked suitable for use as service equipment but not marked as exclusively service equipment.

Marking per 110.21(B): red background, white letters, ≥½" high. Replacement triggers the rule (if you change service equipment, the outdoor disconnect becomes mandatory — exception when you only replace meter sockets, service-entrance conductors, or related raceways/fittings).

**The 2026 NEC change to watch.** 230.85 is **deleted in the 2026 NEC** and consolidated into **230.70(A)(1)** — same substantive requirement (outdoor readily accessible, on or within sight of the dwelling) but cleaner code language. When Oregon adopts the 2026 OESC (October 2026), citations move from 230.85 to 230.70(A)(1). Plan and label accordingly for any project pulled after that date.

**Panel location and working clearance (NEC 110.26).**

- Headroom ≥6.5 ft (or height of equipment, whichever is greater).
- Working depth in front of equipment per Table 110.26(A)(1) — Condition 1 (exposed live parts on one side, no live or grounded parts on other): **3 ft**. Condition 2 (live parts on one side, grounded parts on other): 3 ft for ≤150 V to ground, 3.5 ft for 151–600 V. Condition 3 (live parts on both sides): 3 ft / 4 ft.
- Width ≥30 inches or width of equipment, whichever is greater.
- 110.26(C) egress: equipment ≥1,200 A or ≥1.83 m wide → two egress paths. Residential 200/400 A panels don't trigger this.
- 110.26(E) dedicated equipment space — clear vertical space from floor to 6 ft above equipment (or structural ceiling), no foreign systems (plumbing, ducts) intruding into this dedicated space.
- 240.24 — overcurrent devices not in bathrooms (dwelling or guest rooms), not over steps, not in clothes closets.

**Inspection path (Oregon).** OAR 918-305 et seq. governs electrical permits and inspections. Permit types: residential master-electrician supervisor permit (typical contractor), homeowner permit (where ORS 479.540(1)(a) homeowner exemption applies — Jon's pathway for owner-builder work on his own primary residence). Inspections sequence:

1. **Service / temp service inspection** (drop or lateral to meter base).
2. **Underground / trench inspection** (before backfill on any buried raceway).
3. **Concrete-encased electrode inspection** (before pour — this is the one most commonly missed; coordinate with foundation pour).
4. **Rough-in** (before insulation/drywall — boxes, conductors, grounds, bonding).
5. **Final** (cover plates on, devices terminated, panel labeled, SPD installed, emergency disconnect labeled).

Per OAR 918-305-0420(3), the permit must be posted in a conspicuous place near the main electrical panel.

**Anchor sentence.** Oregon runs the 2023 OESC (NEC 2023 + Table 1-E) until the anticipated October 2026 adoption of the 2026 OESC, with mandatory ≥200 A practical service for an electrified home, NEC 250.52(A)(3) Ufer integrated at footing pour, NEC 230.67 Type 1/2 SPD adjacent to service, and NEC 230.85 readily-accessible outdoor emergency disconnect (consolidating into 230.70(A)(1) under the 2026 NEC) — every project's CEE inspection scheduled before concrete pour, every label per 110.21(B) red/white/½".

## 2. Concrete house — grounding, bonding, Ufer, lightning, embedded metal, rebar continuity, corrosion

**Grounding gets simpler and stronger, not harder.** A reinforced-concrete BSH structure has more rebar in continuous earth contact than any wood-frame house — every footing, slab, and structural wall section is a candidate Ufer electrode. NEC 250.52(A)(3) only requires you to bond **one** CEE into the GES even if multiple are present, but you should **deliberately tie the rebar continuity** during pour planning to make the entire concrete shell electrically coherent.

**Bonding architecture for a concrete house:**

- Tie all footing rebar lifts together with steel tie wire, mechanical splices, or exothermic weld (cadweld) to make a continuous low-impedance grid. Long-term durability: exothermic > mechanical clamp > tie wire. For 1,000-year design, exothermic at every major intersection.
- Bring **two GEC tails** above slab from the rebar grid — one to the main service panel location, one stub for future expansion (subpanel, detached structure, future ESS pad). #4 bare Cu, exothermic-welded to rebar.
- Bond all embedded metal — embedded structural steel beams, embedded conduit racks, equipotential mesh under concrete-floor radiant tubing — to the rebar grid. NEC 250.104 bonding of metal piping systems (water, gas, hydronic) where likely to become energized.

**Lightning protection (NFPA 780).** Not required by code for residential, but the BSH 1,000-year frame argues for it on a concrete tall structure. NFPA 780 system: air terminals at roof corners and ridges (10-ft spacing typical), down conductors (Class I copper for structures ≤75 ft), bonded to **dedicated lightning-protection ground rods** (separate from the electrical GES, then bonded together at one point per 250.106). The Ufer electrode is excellent for 60 Hz fault dissipation but **not optimal for lightning impulse** because the impulse impedance of buried concrete differs from steady-state resistance — a dedicated low-impedance ring electrode supplements the Ufer for lightning. Both bonded into a unified GES.

**Embedded metal corrosion concerns.** The historical worry with Ufer grounding: galvanic corrosion of rebar at the GEC connection point if dissimilar metals are in wet contact. Three mitigations:

1. Use the GEC connection above the slab where it stays dry (the 2" concrete cover protects the buried portion).
2. Use bare copper or zinc-coated steel for the GEC tail, not aluminum (aluminum corrodes badly in concrete).
3. Exothermic weld (cadweld) at the rebar-to-Cu transition — no bolt/clamp degradation.

A second concern: **stray DC current** from cathodic protection systems on adjacent buried utilities, or from improperly grounded DC systems (PV strings, ESS) can drive rebar corrosion. Mitigation: properly bonded DC sources with isolation and reference per NEC 690.41/690.43 (PV grounding) and 706.30 (ESS).

**Rebar continuity verification.** BSH design should specify and document continuity testing: ohm-meter from GEC tail to a far-corner rebar exposure, target <0.1 Ω. Document the result on the inspection record. Some Oregon AHJs will require demonstrated continuity testing for a CEE that's not visually verifiable post-pour (Washington's WAC 296-46B-250 requires this explicitly; Oregon AHJs vary).

**Concrete cover and frost.** NEC requires 2" minimum concrete cover over the encased electrode. Oregon frost depths run 12–18" in the Willamette Valley, deeper east of the Cascades — make sure the Ufer-active rebar is below frost depth so the concrete-to-earth interface stays moist year-round (frozen surface soil = high impedance).

**Anchor sentence.** A concrete BSH structure converts the entire footing/slab assembly into a single low-impedance grounding electrode under NEC 250.52(A)(3); BSH practice is to exothermically weld continuity across rebar lifts, bring two #4 Cu GEC tails above slab, supplement with a dedicated lightning-protection ring per NFPA 780, and bond all embedded metal at a single common reference per 250.106.

## 4. Serving utility — meter, transformer, lateral, meter-main, exterior disconnect, trenching, equipment access

**Utility coordination is upstream of NEC.** Pacific Power (PacifiCorp), Portland General Electric, and the rural co-ops (Central Lincoln PUD, EWEB, etc.) each publish their own service rules that govern everything from the service drop point to the meter. NEC starts at the meter base. Utility rules typically govern:

- **Transformer location and type.** Padmount vs. pole. Padmount transformer needs a clear pad with utility-specified setbacks (typically 10 ft from buildings, 5 ft from property lines, 10 ft from windows/doors, vehicle protection if in driveway path). Transformer is owned by the utility; pad and conduit-in/out are owned by you.
- **Service entrance type.** Overhead drop vs. underground lateral. Underground (URD) is BSH default for durability + aesthetics + wildfire. Trenching, conduit, and conductor on the customer side typically by the customer; conductor on the utility side by the utility. Conduit size 3"–4" PVC schedule 40 typical, sometimes schedule 80 under driveways.
- **Meter base specification.** Each utility approves specific meter sockets. PacifiCorp's "URD Standard" lists Milbank, Eaton, Square D specific models. Get the spec sheet from your utility's service planner before ordering.
- **Trench depth.** NESC (IEEE C2) governs utility-side trench depth (typically ≥30" cover for primary, ≥24" for secondary). NEC 300.5 governs customer-side: Table 300.5(A) — direct burial of dwelling-unit branch circuits (≤150 V to ground) ≥18" cover; under driveways/parking ≥24"; service-entrance underground ≥24"; rigid metal conduit ≥6"; PVC conduit ≥18".
- **Meter-main combo.** Most modern Oregon services use a meter-main combo (meter socket + main breaker + load center in one outdoor enclosure) at the property. This becomes the service equipment AND satisfies the NEC 230.85 emergency disconnect in one device. Inside the building, the panel becomes a feeder-fed subpanel (4-wire feeder with separate ground and isolated neutral; 250.32 separate building rule).

**BSH-aligned setup (rural Oregon):**

1. Padmount transformer at edge of parcel near the easement/road, on a pad you pour to utility spec.
2. Underground service lateral in 3" Schedule 40 PVC from transformer to meter-main, ≥24" cover, with red marking tape buried 12" above conduit per OAR 952 (One-Call locate rules).
3. Meter-main combo (200 A or 400 A) on exterior of house or on a service post within 50 ft of the dwelling, satisfying NEC 230.85 outdoor emergency disconnect.
4. Subfeed (4-wire) to indoor distribution panel(s).
5. Separate dedicated trenches or shared trench (with required separation per NEC 300.3 and utility rules) for: (a) electric service, (b) communications (telephone/fiber/coax), (c) graywater/septic if applicable. Utility typically requires 12" horizontal separation from communications, more from gas.

**Equipment access (utility side).** Utility needs **clear, unobstructed access** to the meter, transformer, and any in-ground vault for life of service. Document this in any landscaping/site plan. Locked gates → utility key access required by easement.

**Anchor sentence.** BSH service entrance: utility-spec padmount transformer on customer-poured pad, 3" PVC underground lateral at ≥24" cover with locate tape, exterior meter-main combo within 50 ft of dwelling that doubles as the NEC 230.85 emergency disconnect, 4-wire subfeed to interior panels — every dimension cleared in a pre-construction utility service planning meeting before trench day.

## 5. Generator transfer, interlock, neutral switching, bonding, inlet, fuel, exhaust, standby vs portable

**Three generator architectures, each governed differently:**

**Architecture A — Portable + interlock kit (NEC 702 optional standby).**

- Wall-mounted generator inlet box (NEMA L14-30P for 30 A / 7.5 kW or NEMA 14-50P for 50 A / 12 kW) on the **outside** of the building. Indoor inlets are explicitly disallowed because of CO and combustion-air concerns.
- Inlet feeds a backfeed breaker in the main panel.
- **Mechanical interlock kit** on the panel that prevents the main breaker and the backfeed breaker from being simultaneously ON. Listed for the specific panel model (Square D QO interlock won't fit Eaton BR, etc.).
- NEC 702.4 sizing: generator output ≥ calculated load it serves.
- NEC 702.7 marking: caution label at service equipment identifying type and location of standby source.
- Portable generator operated outside, ≥20 ft from doors/windows/openings (CDC/manufacturer guidance — not a code number, but a defensible practice). Many OFC/IFC AHJs adopt 20 ft as a hard rule.
- Backfeed breaker should be the same brand/model series as the panel (UL 67 listing dependency).

**Architecture B — Permanently installed standby generator with automatic transfer switch (NEC 702 + 445).**

- Generac, Kohler, Cummins, Briggs & Stratton air-cooled units (8–24 kW) or liquid-cooled (≥22 kW).
- Listed automatic transfer switch (ATS) — service-rated ATS can replace the meter-main and become the service disconnect (satisfies 230.85), or load-side ATS placed downstream.
- Outdoor placement on engineered pad. NFPA 37 separation: ≥5 ft from any building openings/combustible walls (manufacturer often specifies more — Generac 18 kW air-cooled = 18" from any wall, 60" from any window/door/opening).
- **Fuel: LP gas or natural gas.** LP requires either utility connection or onsite tank (typically 250–500 gallon). LP regulator at tank with second-stage regulator at generator. **No homeowner exemption on LP work in Oregon (ORS 480.432) — must use a licensed LP installer.** Natural gas service requires utility coordination.
- Exhaust clearances per manufacturer (typical ≥36" from building openings, ≥10 ft from openings into garage, never under overhangs without forced ventilation).
- Annual exercise required; battery maintenance.

**Architecture C — Whole-house battery ESS as primary backup, generator as deep backup (NEC 706 + 702).**

- ESS (Tesla Powerwall 3, Enphase IQ Battery, Franklin aPower) with built-in transfer relay or paired with Span/Lumin/IQ System Controller.
- Generator becomes a deep-backup source feeding the ESS during multi-day outages.
- This is the architecture I'd default to for BSH 1,000-year design — fewer moving parts, no fuel logistics, runs every day for self-consumption optimization (unlike a generator that sits idle).

**Neutral switching (NEC 250.30 separately derived systems).**

- **2-pole transfer switch** (does NOT switch neutral): generator neutral remains bonded to the service neutral; generator is **not** a separately derived system. This is the most common residential setup. **The generator's bonding jumper must be removed** to avoid a second N-G bond (parallel neutral path through the equipment grounding conductor — unsafe).
- **3-pole transfer switch** (DOES switch neutral): generator becomes a separately derived system, requires its own grounding electrode, generator's internal N-G bond stays in place. Required for some commercial setups; uncommon in residential.

**Get this right or you have parallel neutral currents flowing through the EGC, which causes nuisance GFCI/AFCI tripping at best and shock/fire risk at worst.**

**Generator bonding & grounding.**

- Permanent standby: NEC 250.30 — if separately derived (3-pole TS), needs grounding electrode within 50 ft. If not separately derived (2-pole TS, the residential default), bonded through the service GES.
- Portable: NEC 250.34 — equipment grounding conductor through the cord and inlet to the service ground; generator frame bonds to neutral at the generator (per OSHA-required factory bonding) only when used as a stand-alone source (no inlet connection). When connected to a building's wiring system through an inlet, the building's N-G bond at the service is the only N-G bond.

**Anchor sentence.** BSH generator architecture defaults to ESS-primary + generator deep-backup; if generator-only, choose between portable + listed mechanical interlock + outdoor inlet (NEC 702 optional standby) or permanent ATS-equipped standby (NEC 702 + 445 + NFPA 37 setbacks + LP licensed installer per ORS 480.432) — and either way, use a 2-pole non-neutral-switched TS with the generator's internal N-G bond removed unless a separately derived system is intentionally being created.

## 6. Battery ESS — location, listing, working space, fire separation, ventilation, disconnects, exterior vs interior

**Governing standards.**

- **NEC Article 706** — Energy Storage Systems. Applies to all permanently installed ESS >50 V AC or >60 V DC. Threshold for code applicability: **>1 kWh capacity**.
- **UL 9540** — listing standard for complete ESS (battery + inverter + controls as a system). NEC 706.5 requires ESS to be listed; UL 9540 is the recognized listing.
- **UL 9540A** — large-scale fire test methodology used to justify reduced spacing under NFPA 855.
- **NFPA 855** — Standard for Installation of Stationary Energy Storage Systems. Governs siting, spacing, ventilation, fire suppression, and HMA (Hazard Mitigation Analysis) for systems above threshold.
- **NEC 480** — Storage Batteries (lead-acid, traditional batteries). Largely superseded by 706 for modern lithium ESS, but parts apply for installation rules.
- **NEC 706.7** — emergency shutdown function required for one- and two-family dwellings; readily accessible disconnect.

**Listing.** For BSH, only UL 9540-listed self-contained ESS units. Powerwall 3, Enphase IQ Battery 5P/10P, Franklin aPower 2, FranklinWH, Sol-Ark + EG4 (some configurations), LG Chem RESU. Demand the UL 9540 cert from the manufacturer's spec sheet — not just UL 1973 (battery cell standard) or UL 1741 (inverter standard) alone.

**Location — interior vs exterior.**

- **Exterior wall mount** is the BSH default for fire safety, thermal management, and ease of replacement. Powerwall 3 and Enphase IQ Battery are listed for outdoor installation in temperatures −20 °C to 50 °C (Tesla) / −15 °C to 55 °C (Enphase). Western Oregon climate is well within range.
- **Interior installation in a garage** is permitted for residential listed ESS, with NFPA 855 setback requirements (typically 3 ft from doorways into living space, 3 ft from other ESS units, 5 ft from windows/exits, 36" from combustible walls unless tested to UL 9540A for reduced clearance).
- **Habitable spaces (bedrooms, living rooms) are prohibited** for ESS installation. Utility rooms, mechanical rooms, basements with appropriate fire separation are permitted under 855 with HMA review.

**Aggregate energy thresholds (NFPA 855, dwelling units).**

- Single-family dwellings: aggregate ≤40 kWh per unit (some editions 20 kWh per location), with each unit ≤20 kWh (some manufacturers' UL 9540A test reports allow more).
- Above threshold → HMA required, potentially fire suppression, dedicated room with fire-rated separation.
- Two Powerwall 3 units (27 kWh nominal) is well under threshold. Three or four pushes you toward HMA.

**Working space (NEC 110.26).** Same rules as panels: 3 ft front clearance, 30" wide, 6.5 ft headroom. Service-side disconnect access for maintenance.

**Fire separation (NFPA 855).**

- If interior: 1-hour fire-rated wall between ESS room and habitable spaces (Type I or II construction; for BSH concrete walls, this is automatic — concrete construction inherently exceeds 1-hour rating).
- Spacing between units per UL 9540A test report; typically 3 ft if not tested for less.
- Smoke detection in ESS area, interconnected with dwelling alarm system per NFPA 855.

**Ventilation.** Lithium-ion ESS for residential typically does not require active ventilation per NFPA 855 (unlike vented lead-acid); confirm via UL 9540 listing. If ESS includes hydrogen-venting cells (rare in residential lithium), ventilation per IEEE 1635 / ASHRAE Guideline 21 applies.

**Disconnecting means (NEC 706.8 — and now 706.15 in 2023 NEC).**

- ESS disconnect within sight of the ESS or remote-operable from a readily accessible location.
- Emergency shutdown required for dwellings — typically a button outside the building, marked per 110.21(B). Often integrated with the listed ESS controller.
- DC disconnect between battery and PCS. AC disconnect between PCS and main panel. Both lockable.
- Plaque or directory at the emergency disconnect identifying all energy sources (NEC 230.85(D), 706.15) — this gets BSH a nice-and-tidy outdoor labeled placard listing "Service / PV / ESS / Generator" with their respective disconnect locations.

**Inspection requirements.**

- Plan submittal: UL 9540 listing certification, one-line diagram showing ESS as separate system with own disconnect, ventilation plan, labeling plan, fire detection plan, emergency shutdown documentation.
- Permit: electrical permit (BCD electrical inspector) + sometimes a separate fire department review (rare for ≤20 kWh residential, common for larger).
- Inspection timing: rough-in for conduit/conductors, final at energization.
- Commissioning: NEC 706.7 commissioning required for ESS, **except** for one- and two-family dwellings (which BSH is). Manufacturer commissioning still typically required by warranty.

**Anchor sentence.** BSH ESS architecture: UL 9540-listed self-contained unit (Powerwall 3, Enphase IQ Battery, Franklin aPower) mounted on exterior concrete wall or in mechanical room with concrete fire separation, aggregate ≤20 kWh per unit and ≤40 kWh per location to stay below NFPA 855 HMA threshold, NEC 706.15 emergency shutdown labeled per 110.21(B) outside the building, and an outdoor placard directory listing all energy source disconnects per 230.85(D).

## 7. Solar — rapid shutdown, interconnection, labeling, disconnect, fire access, structural, utility approval

**Rapid shutdown (NEC 690.12).**

- **Outside array boundary** (1 ft in all directions from PV array): controlled conductors to ≤30 V within 30 seconds of initiation.
- **Inside array boundary**: ≤80 V within 30 seconds, OR a listed PV Hazard Control System (PVHCS) per UL 3741.
- **2023 NEC exceptions**: ground-mount arrays where conductors don't enter a building used for human occupancy are exempt; non-enclosed detached structures (carports, trellises) exempt.
- **Initiation device**: single readily accessible switch per PV system, located outside, marked per 690.56(C). For dwellings, often combined with the emergency disconnect placard.
- **Compliance pathways**: (a) microinverters (Enphase IQ8, Hoymiles) — module-level conversion, no high-voltage DC on building, inherently compliant; (b) DC optimizers + string inverter (SolarEdge, Tigo + most string inverters) — module-level shutdown via PLC; (c) listed PVHCS (UL 3741) using string design without module-level electronics. For BSH 1,000-year reliability, **microinverters win** — fewer DC arc-fault risks, individual module replaceability, no high-voltage DC anywhere on roof.

**Interconnection (NEC 705).**

- **120% busbar rule (705.12(B)(3)(2))**: PV breaker + main breaker in a panel ≤ 120% of busbar rating. Example: 200 A panel with 200 A main → max 40 A PV backfeed breaker.
- **Sum rule (705.12(B)(3)(1))**: PV breaker + main breaker ≤ busbar rating (no 120% allowance). More restrictive but allows PV breaker anywhere on busbar.
- **Supply-side connection** (705.11): PV interconnection upstream of main service disconnect. Always allowed; sized per service rating. Useful when 120% rule is exceeded.
- **PCS (Power Control System) per 705.13**: a listed system that monitors and controls the combined output of multiple sources to not exceed busbar rating dynamically. Smart panels (SPAN, Lumin) qualify and are increasingly the BSH-aligned answer.

**Labeling.**

- 690.13(B) PV system disconnect.
- 690.54 PV system AC point-of-interconnection labeling: max AC operating current, max AC operating voltage.
- 690.56 placard at service equipment identifying PV system.
- 705.10 directory at each service equipment showing all sources.
- All per 110.21(B): durable, weatherproof, contrasting color, ≥3/8" letters minimum (often interpreted ≥½").

**Disconnect location.**

- AC disconnect at point of interconnection — typically a fused or non-fused outdoor disconnect adjacent to the meter or main panel.
- DC disconnect (string inverters only) at the inverter or rooftop combiner.
- Rapid shutdown initiator at the service equipment or other readily accessible outdoor location.
- For BSH: a single labeled equipment cluster at the meter-main location (PV AC disconnect, ESS disconnect, generator inlet, emergency disconnect placard) — first-responder-friendly architecture.

**Fire access (IFC / ORSC R324).** Oregon Residential Specialty Code R324 (PV Systems) governs roof access pathways for residential PV:

- **Hip roof:** modules ≥18" from ridge.
- **Single ridge:** access pathway ≥36" wide on both slopes (modules can be installed up to ridge if pathway provided on the other side).
- **Two parallel ridges (multiple roof planes):** pathway between planes.
- For ground-mount: setbacks per local fire code; 10 ft access lane around array typical.

**Structural coordination.** ORSC R301 + ORSC R324.6 (where adopted): roof-mounted PV must not exceed structural capacity of the roof framing. New construction designed for PV: include 5–7 psf dead load + wind/snow per ASCE 7 for the planned PV footprint. Existing-roof retrofit: structural assessment required if approaching framing capacity.

For BSH new construction with concrete roof or heavy-timber roof, structural capacity is rarely the limiting factor. For ground-mount: pile/ballast foundation engineered for soil + wind. **Engineering required** for ground-mount > a certain threshold (varies by AHJ, typically anything fixed-anchor needs PE stamp).

**Utility approval (Oregon).**

- Net metering enrollment with utility (PGE Schedule 7, Pacific Power Schedule 135, etc.).
- Interconnection agreement signed before energization.
- Utility witness inspection or commissioning sign-off.
- For systems >25 kW: distribution impact study, possibly transformer upgrade.
- Single-family residential systems typically clear in 4–8 weeks.

**Anchor sentence.** BSH-aligned PV: microinverter architecture (inherent NEC 690.12 compliance, no rooftop DC), interconnected via either the 120% busbar rule or a smart-panel PCS per NEC 705.13, with the AC disconnect / rapid shutdown / labeling stack co-located at the outdoor meter-main cluster, ORSC R324 ridge setbacks observed for fire access, and utility net-metering interconnection agreement signed before energization.

## 8. EV charging — load management, future conduit, location, outdoor rating, dynamic load control

**Load math summary (re-iterating from §3):**

- NEC 220.57: EVSE at 7,200 VA or nameplate, whichever larger. 48 A Level 2 = 11,520 VA.
- NEC 625.42 (2023): EVSE is a continuous load → branch circuit and OCPD sized at 125% of nameplate (48 A EVSE → 60 A breaker / circuit).
- 2026 NEC: same continuous treatment unless NEC 625.48 EVEMS is installed; with EVEMS, the 125% multiplier is waived and the circuit is sized at the managed maximum.

**Future conduit strategy (BSH 1,000-year discipline).**

- Stub a **2" EMT or rigid PVC conduit** from the main panel area to **every plausible EV location**: garage, carport, driveway parking pad, future ADU. Empty conduit costs ~$50/ft; retrofitting through finished walls costs ~$500/ft.
- Multiple conduit paths to support: (a) primary EV at home; (b) second EV / future EV; (c) Tesla destination charger or higher-amperage equipment up to 100 A (sized for 4/0 Cu 80 A continuous).
- Pull strings in conduits from day one. Label both ends with destination and intended ampacity.
- For ADU EV charging: a dedicated 60 A subfeed to the ADU panel that handles EV + appliances, with EVEMS at the ADU.

**Charger location.**

- Garage wall: most common, easiest weatherproofing (chargers indoor-rated). 18–48" mounting height, accessible to either side of vehicle.
- Exterior wall: needs NEMA 3R or NEMA 4 rated charger for outdoor installation. Wall Connector (Tesla Universal), Enphase IQ EV Charger, Wallbox Pulsar Plus all rated for outdoor.
- Detached pedestal: for parking pads where wall mount not practical. Concrete bollards / vehicle protection per NEC 110.27 if subject to physical damage.

**Outdoor rating.**

- NEMA 3R minimum (rain-tight) for outdoor.
- NEMA 4 (watertight) for exposed locations (driveway pedestal in heavy weather).
- NEMA 4X (watertight + corrosion-resistant) for coastal Oregon.

**Dynamic load control / load management.** This is the BSH-aligned answer to service-size pressure. Three architectures:

1. **Charger-integrated load monitor**. Emporia, Wallbox Pulsar Plus, ChargePoint Home Flex monitor whole-home load via CT clamps and curtail charging when total exceeds a threshold. Cheap (~$300 add-on for CT module). Limited to single-charger installations and not always recognized by AHJs as a 625.48 EVEMS.
2. **Smart panel with built-in EVEMS**. SPAN Panel, Lumin, Schneider Pulse, Eaton Energy Hub. Manages all EV chargers + other large loads. Listed per 625.48 / 750.30. AHJ-friendly. ~$3,500–$6,000 added cost over standard panel.
3. **Utility-side demand response**. Some utilities (PGE, EWEB) offer EV-time-of-use programs that incentivize off-peak charging via rate structure rather than hardware. Doesn't reduce calculated load on a permit application but does reduce utility bill.

**Service-size impact of EVEMS.** For BSH:

- Without EVEMS, dual 48 A EV chargers add 2 × 11,520 × 1.25 = 28,800 VA → push to 320–400 A service.
- With EVEMS curtailing aggregate EV draw to 48 A combined, dual chargers add only 11,520 × 1.25 = 14,400 VA → fits comfortably under 200 A service alongside heat pump + HPWH + induction + shop.

**Recommendation.** BSH baseline = SPAN smart panel as the main panel, two future-conduit EV stubs (garage + driveway/ADU), one Wall Connector or Enphase EV Charger installed day one, one stub left empty for future second EV. Total electrical service: 200 A with EVEMS-managed peak.

**Anchor sentence.** BSH EV strategy: SPAN-class smart panel as the main panel doubling as NEC 625.48 EVEMS to keep dual-EV future-load within a 200 A service envelope, future-conduit (2" PVC + pull strings) stubs to garage / driveway / ADU positions on day one, and outdoor-rated NEMA 3R+ chargers for any non-garage installation.

## Controlling Code Authority

**Settled position:** Residential electrical in Oregon is governed by the **2023 Oregon Electrical Specialty Code (OESC)**, which is the **2023 NEC (NFPA 70)** as amended by **OAR 918-305-0105 Table 1-E**. Effective October 1, 2023, the 2023 OESC consists of the 2023 edition of NFPA 70 (NEC) and as further amended by the division in OAR 918-305-0105 Table 1-E. There is no integrated codebook — you must use the 2023 NEC text alongside the Oregon amendment table. The BCD electrical page links to both; the 2023 NEC is available free read-only at nfpa.org with a free account.

One critical housekeeping note: effective January 1, 2025, the 2023 OESC Table 1-E was amended to include errata that corrects errors and aligns section references for accuracy. The January 2025 errata version is the operative Table 1-E. Any citation to Oregon electrical rules should reference Table 1-E as amended effective January 1, 2025, not the October 1, 2023 original. Additionally, effective January 1, 2026, the 2023 OESC is amended to require that microgrid equipment and distribution on the customer side of the service point must be installed in accordance with the applicable sections of the current code.

Oregon does not amend most NEC residential dwelling provisions. The amendments in Table 1-E primarily modify GFCI and AFCI provisions and some commercial scope questions. Everything in this chapter that doesn't cite an Oregon-specific modification is 2023 NEC baseline.

## Conductor Ampacity and Wire Sizing

**Governing section:** NEC Table 310.16 — Ampacities of Insulated Conductors with Not More Than Three Current-Carrying Conductors in Raceway, Cable, or Earth.

**Copper baseline (60°C termination column, most residential breakers):**
- 14 AWG → 15A
- 12 AWG → 20A
- 10 AWG → 30A
- 8 AWG → 40A
- 6 AWG → 55A (adequate for 50A breaker)
- 4 AWG → 70A

**Temperature column rule:** For circuits 100A and less, the value is taken from the 60°C column. For larger circuits, the 75°C column applies. The 90°C column is used only for calculating derating — it is not the as-installed ampacity for conductors with 60°C or 75°C terminations.

**Continuous loads:** Conductor and breaker must be sized at 125% of the continuous load before applying Table 310.16. A 16A continuous load requires a 20A circuit (16 × 1.25 = 20A).

**Derating:** More than three current-carrying conductors in a raceway requires ampacity adjustment per Table 310.15(B)(3)(a): 4–6 conductors = 80% of tabulated value; 7–9 conductors = 70%; 10–20 conductors = 50%. Neutral conductors carrying only unbalanced current do not count toward the fill derating calculation, but a neutral carrying harmonic-current in certain circuits does count — verify in each application.

**Aluminum wire:** Aluminum is code-acceptable for 6 AWG and larger; it is not recommended for 14 AWG or 12 AWG branch circuits due to historical connection reliability issues. Aluminum is commonly used for service entrance conductors and large feeders when properly terminated — not for small branch circuits. Aluminum conductors require AL-rated terminals and anti-oxidant compound. For BSH, aluminum should not appear in any branch circuit description in the manuscript without a specific note about terminal compatibility.

**Minimum conductor size:** Under the 2023 NEC, 14 AWG copper is the minimum for branch circuits (NEC 310.5 as applicable to branch circuits). Oregon Table 1-E has no departure from this.

## Voltage Drop

Voltage drop guidance in NEC 210.19(A) is an informational note — not a mandatory requirement. It recommends keeping branch circuit voltage drop under 3% and total drop (feeder + branch) under 5%. At 120V that is 3.6V branch / 6V total.

This is the key point for the manuscript: voltage drop compliance is advisory, not code-enforceable. An inspector cannot fail a rough inspection for voltage drop. However, BSH's 1000-year reliability standard makes the 3% recommendation functionally mandatory for long runs. The practical impact: a 20A circuit at 120V serving a load at the end of a 50-foot run in 12 AWG copper is generally fine; at 100 feet it may exceed 3% drop depending on load, and upsizing to 10 AWG for the run is the appropriate BSH response.

**Should Chapter 12 include practical wire-size-and-distance tables?** Yes — but label them clearly as BSH design guidance applying the NEC informational note, not as binding code requirements. The authoritative source for calculating these is NEC 210.19(A) applied with the voltage drop formula (VD = 2 × K × I × L / CM where K = 12.9 for copper at 75°F). Include a caveat that long-run sizing must account for derating before applying voltage drop — the conductor must first pass the ampacity test, then be checked for voltage drop. The Mike Holt Enterprises NEC commentary and the NFPA 70 Handbook are the appropriate citation sources for any tables, not third-party calculators.

## AFCI Protection — 2023 NEC with Oregon Amendments

**Baseline 2023 NEC (Section 210.12(B)):** All 120-volt, single-phase, 10-, 15-, and 20-ampere branch circuits supplying all outlets in kitchens, family rooms, dining rooms, living rooms, parlors, libraries, dens, bedrooms, sunrooms, recreation rooms, closets, hallways, laundry areas, and similar rooms and areas (including finished basements) must be AFCI protected.

**Oregon amendments to AFCI (Table 1-E, critical):** Oregon makes significant carve-outs from the 2023 NEC AFCI list. AFCI protection shall not be required on branch circuits supplying receptacles located in hallways, kitchens, or laundry areas and GFCI protected receptacles installed in dining rooms. And separately: AFCI protection shall not be required for branch circuits that serve an appliance that is not easily moved or that is fastened in place.

So in Oregon:
- **AFCI required:** Bedrooms, living rooms, family rooms, parlors, dens, libraries, sunrooms, recreation rooms, closets, finished basements — all outlets (receptacles, lighting, switches) on 120V 10/15/20A circuits.
- **AFCI not required (Oregon amendment):** Kitchens (receptacles), hallways, laundry areas, dining room GFCI-protected receptacles, circuits to fixed/fastened appliances.
- **Fire alarm exception:** AFCI protection shall not be required for an individual branch circuit supplying a fire alarm system installed in accordance with 760.41(B) or 760.121(B), provided the branch circuit is installed in metal raceway, metal auxiliary gutter, steel-armored cable, Type MC or Type AC cable.

**AFCI methods:** Combination AFCI breaker is the dominant field approach. The code allows six methods (NEC 210.12(A)(1)-(6)), but methods 2-6 have significant practical limitations including wiring distance caps (50 ft for #14, 70 ft for #12 from panel to first outlet for branch/feeder + outlet-branch combination) and the fact that no listed "system combination" products currently exist for methods 3-5.

**The 10A circuit inclusion:** The 2023 NEC adds 10A circuits to the AFCI requirement, tied to the introduction of 14 AWG copper-clad aluminum (CCA). This is not practically relevant to conventional residential construction using copper conductors — 10A breakers serving receptacles are not permitted by NEC 210.23(A)(2). Ignore for the manuscript unless BSH uses CCA conductors.

## GFCI Protection — 2023 NEC with Oregon Amendments

**The governing structure:** NEC 210.8(A) for dwelling units. The 2023 NEC expanded GFCI significantly; Oregon amended specific items. GFCI must be installed before the equipment it protects (i.e., at the breaker or at the outlet).

**Dwelling unit locations requiring GFCI (2023 NEC + Oregon Table 1-E):**

The Oregon Table 1-E keeps NEC items (1)–(4) unchanged: bathrooms, garages and accessory buildings, outdoors, crawl spaces at or below grade. Then Oregon modifies several items:

- **(5) Basements:** Oregon amendments item (5) to: "Unfinished portions or areas of basements not intended as habitable rooms." This is a significant departure from the 2023 NEC, which would require GFCI in all basements. Oregon limits basement GFCI to unfinished, non-habitable portions. Finished basements do not require GFCI for general receptacles under the Oregon amendment.
- **(6) Kitchens:** Oregon Table 1-E item (6) reads: "Kitchens — where the receptacles are installed to serve the countertop surfaces." This reverts to the pre-2023 NEC language. The 2023 NEC expanded kitchen GFCI to all kitchen receptacles; Oregon narrows it back to countertop-serving receptacles only.
- **(7) Food prep areas:** Oregon keeps the NEC 2023 expansion: "Areas with sinks and permanent provisions for food preparation, beverage preparation, or cooking." This covers wet bars, butler's pantries, and similar spaces beyond the formal kitchen.
- **(8)–(12):** Oregon keeps items (8) through (12) and Exception No. (1) unchanged — covering sink proximity (within 6 ft), boathouses, bathtub/shower stall proximity (within 6 ft), dishwashers, and indoor damp locations.
- **Security system exception:** A receptacle supplying only a permanently installed premises security system shall be permitted to omit ground-fault circuit-interrupter protection if the receptacle is labeled as "not GFCI protected."
- **HVAC exception:** GFCI protection shall not be required for listed HVAC equipment. This exception shall expire September 1, 2026. This is important — as of September 1, 2026, HVAC equipment receptacles will require GFCI.

**Sump pump:** The Oregon-amended Table 1-E provision on sump pumps: A permanently installed sump pump shall be permanently wired or shall be supplied by a receptacle that is ground-fault circuit-interrupter protected. A single receptacle supplying a permanently installed sump pump shall not require ground-fault circuit-interrupter protection. Read carefully: a duplex receptacle at a sump pump requires GFCI; a single-outlet receptacle at a sump pump does not. This is the Oregon-specific carve-out for dedicated single receptacles serving sump pumps.

**Dryers and large appliances (2023 NEC 210.8(D), Oregon position):** The 2023 NEC added explicit GFCI requirements for clothes dryers, wall-mounted ovens, counter-mounted cooking units, and microwave ovens. Oregon's Table 1-E does not appear to have adopted 210.8(D) in full — the Oregon kitchen amendment covering countertop receptacles suggests Oregon limited kitchen appliance GFCI to countertop circuits. Confirm this specific item with the BCD code specialist line (503-378-4133) before writing it into the manuscript, because the interaction between 210.8(D) and Oregon's kitchen amendment is ambiguous from available public sources.

## Kitchen Small-Appliance Circuits

**Two 20-ampere circuits minimum:** NEC 210.11(C)(1) requires at least two 20-ampere small-appliance branch circuits (SABCs). The two or more 20-ampere small-appliance branch circuits required by 210.11(C)(1) shall serve all wall, floor, and countertop outlets covered in 210.52(A) and (C), and receptacle outlets for refrigeration equipment. The small-appliance branch circuits shall have no other outlets. The refrigerator can share a SABC; it does not require a dedicated circuit, though a dedicated circuit is best practice.

**Exceptions within SABCs:** NEC 210.52(B)(1) Exception No. 2 permits a receptacle outlet serving a specific appliance to be supplied from an individual branch circuit rated 15A or greater — meaning a dedicated circuit for a microwave, garbage disposal, or dishwasher is permitted in addition to the two SABCs, as long as the SABC rules still cover the countertop receptacles.

**Countertop spacing — wall surfaces:** No point along the countertop wall line may be more than 24 inches from a receptacle. Maximum center-to-center spacing between receptacles is therefore 48 inches. A countertop wall space 12 inches or wider requires a receptacle. No receptacle is required directly behind a range, counter-mounted cooking unit, or sink. In the 2023 NEC, if a required receptacle cannot be installed in the wall areas due to obstructions, it may be installed as close as practicable to the countertop area being served, but the total number of required receptacles cannot be reduced and all must be located on or above the countertop or work surface, not more than 20 inches above.

**Countertop receptacle height:** Required receptacle outlets for countertops must be installed on or not more than 20 inches above the countertop. The 2023 NEC eliminated below-countertop receptacles for serving countertop surfaces.

**Island and peninsula (critical 2023 NEC change):** In the 2023 NEC, installing a receptacle at a kitchen island or peninsular countertop or work surface is completely optional. If a receptacle outlet is not provided, provisions shall be provided at the island or peninsula for future addition of a receptacle outlet. This reverses the 2020 NEC requirement. If a receptacle is installed, it must be on or above the countertop, no more than 20 inches above. No below-countertop receptacles for island service. The "provisions" requirement for future installation is intentionally vague — some AHJs will want a junction box stub; confirm expectations with the local building department.

**Dishwasher:** Dedicated circuit, no specific ampacity specified in code (15A minimum in practice). GFCI status: see discussion above regarding 210.8(D) and Oregon amendments — verify before writing.

**Garbage disposal:** Typically on a 20A dedicated circuit or a split-receptacle circuit under the sink shared with dishwasher. NEC does not mandate a dedicated circuit for disposal specifically; the dedicated-circuit convention is best practice, not code.

**Appliance garage:** The NEC does not specifically address appliance garages. Receptacles inside an appliance garage rendered not readily accessible by the appliance occupying the space are not required outlets — they do not count toward the countertop 24-inch spacing requirement. Whether those receptacles need GFCI depends on Oregon's countertop-surface GFCI rule: if the receptacle serves the countertop work surface, it requires GFCI; if it only serves the appliance inside the garage, the GFCI question depends on whether it is "serving the countertop surface." Discuss with your AHJ before the rough-in.

## Receptacle Spacing — General Dwelling

**General wall receptacle spacing (NEC 210.52(A)):** No point along any wall space 2 feet or wider may be more than 6 feet from a receptacle. Receptacle outlets must be installed in each wall space 2 feet or wider. This is the standard room perimeter rule.

**Bathroom:** A receptacle outlet shall be installed within 3 feet of each bathroom sink measured from the outside edge of the sink. The receptacle shall not be installed more than 12 inches below the top of the sink. GFCI required.

**Exterior:** At least one receptacle outlet shall be installed on the front and back exterior of the dwelling that is accessible from grade, no greater than 6½ feet above grade. Decks, balconies, and porches accessible from inside a dwelling unit shall have at least one receptacle outlet.

**Hallways:** Hallways that are 10 feet in length or more shall have at least one receptacle outlet. No GFCI required in hallways; no AFCI required under Oregon amendment.

**Foyers/entries:** Foyers greater than 60 square feet shall have a receptacle outlet on wall spaces greater than 3 feet in width.

**Tamper-resistant receptacles:** All nonlocking 15- and 20-ampere, 125-volt receptacles in dwelling units must be tamper-resistant per NEC 406.12(A). This applies throughout — every general-use outlet in the home.

**Weather-resistant receptacles:** Required for all 15- and 20-ampere receptacles installed in wet or damp locations — exterior, garages (where exposed to weather), and similar. NEC 406.9(A) and (B).

**In-use covers:** NEC 406.9(B)(1) requires in-use covers for 15- and 20-ampere receptacles in wet locations that are to maintain protection with a cord plugged in. Exterior receptacles require in-use ("bubble") covers by default.

## Lighting Outlets and Switching

**General room lighting (NEC 210.70(A)(1)):** At least one listed wall-mounted control device to control lighting outlet(s) shall be installed in every habitable room, kitchen, laundry, and bathroom near the entrance to the room. The 2023 NEC added laundry areas to this list. A switched lighting outlet is required at all stairways, garages, hallways, and exterior side of all outdoor entrances.

**Closet lighting:** NEC 410.16 governs permitted fixture types in clothes closets. Incandescent fixtures are prohibited. Permitted: surface-mounted or recessed incandescent with a completely enclosed light source, surface or recessed fluorescent, LED. Clearances: surface-mounted incandescent or LED enclosed — 12 inches from the nearest point of storage; pendant fixtures and bare lamps are not permitted.

**Damp and wet locations:** NEC 410.10. Luminaires in wet locations must be listed and labeled for wet locations. Damp-location fixtures (covered porches, carports, unenclosed soffits) must be listed for damp. The distinction matters — a "damp location" fixture is not adequate for a fully exposed exterior fixture that receives direct rain.

**Dimmer/smart switch considerations:** Smart switches that replace standard switches typically require a neutral conductor at the switch box. Pre-2010 wiring commonly used switch loops without neutrals; the 2011 NEC required neutrals at switch locations for new construction (NEC 404.2(C)). The 2023 NEC maintains this requirement. For a BSH build with all-new wiring, design every switch box with a neutral — the 14/3 or 12/3 cost is negligible against the lifetime smart-switch compatibility it provides.

**Box fill:** NEC 314.16. A standard 18 in³ single-gang box holds approximately: 14 AWG — 9 conductors max; 12 AWG — 8 conductors max (2.25 in³ per 14 AWG, 2.5 in³ per 12 AWG, with additional volumes for devices, clamps, and equipment grounding conductors). Calculate box fill on any multi-circuit junction before specifying box depths, particularly at 4-way switch locations.

**Lighting energy efficacy (ORSC Chapter 11):** ORSC N1107.2 requires that in kitchens, bathrooms, laundry rooms, dining rooms, and living rooms, a minimum of 90% of permanently installed luminaires must use high-efficacy lamps. LED fixtures universally meet this requirement. The "permanently installed" qualifier means hard-wired fixtures — not table lamps or floor lamps.

## Dedicated Circuits

**Code-required dedicated circuits in a dwelling:**
- Electric range/cooktop: 50A, 240V (NEC 422.10 and 210.19)
- Electric dryer: 30A, 240V
- Dishwasher: 15 or 20A (best practice: 20A dedicated)
- Garbage disposal: 15 or 20A (best practice: 20A dedicated)
- Refrigerator: Not required to be dedicated by code; can share SABC; best practice is dedicated 20A
- Microwave (built-in): Best practice is dedicated 20A; not code-required unless manufacturer specifies
- HVAC equipment: Per manufacturer (heat pumps typically require 240V dedicated circuits sized to equipment; mini-splits typically 20-50A depending on capacity)
- EV charger: 240V, 50A typical for Level 2 charging (NEC 625.40)
- Water heater: 30A, 240V for electric resistance; size per equipment

No NEC provision mandates a dedicated circuit for smoke alarms, though some jurisdictions and some IBC provisions recommend it. The standard practice is to power smoke alarms from a non-switched lighting circuit in a frequently used area.

## Smoke Alarms — ORSC R314

**Locations required (ORSC R314.3, new construction):**
- In each sleeping room
- Immediately outside each sleeping area
- On each additional story including basements and habitable attics

Where more than one smoke alarm is required, all alarm devices shall be interconnected so that the actuation of one alarm will activate all alarms in the individual unit. Wireless listed alarms may be used to satisfy interconnection without physical wiring.

**Power source:** New construction requires hardwired, 120V AC with battery backup. Listed per UL 217. NFPA 72 governs placement and testing; the NEC governs the electrical installation.

**AFCI and smoke alarms:** Single-station smoke alarms connected to 120V branch circuits are "outlets" under NEC Article 100 and are subject to AFCI requirements wherever the circuit serves an AFCI-required location. The Oregon Table 1-E Exception No. 1 for fire alarm circuits (Article 760) applies to low-voltage fire alarm systems with control panels — not to standard 120V residential smoke alarms. Plan all smoke alarm circuits as AFCI-required circuits in Oregon.

**Wiring method:** Three-conductor NM (14/3 or 12/3) between alarms is the standard residential approach. Black = hot, white = neutral, red = interconnect signal conductor (causes all alarms to sound when one activates), bare = equipment ground.

**No dedicated circuit required:** Smoke alarms are not required to be on a separate or dedicated branch circuit. Installers typically power them from a general lighting circuit serving a frequently used area. Check with local AHJ — some jurisdictions have local requirements.

## Carbon Monoxide Alarms — ORSC R315 and ORS 476.725

**Locations:** CO alarms must be installed outside each sleeping area and on every level of the dwelling, including basements. ORSC R315 adds: in each bedroom or within 15 feet of each bedroom door.

**Trigger:** Required whenever the dwelling has fuel-burning appliances, fireplaces, or attached garages. A BSH home with a propane backup generator, LP-fired water heater, or attached garage will require CO alarms.

**New construction requirement:** Hardwired and interconnected in new construction. In new constructions, CO alarms must be hardwired and interconnected. Battery-only is not sufficient for new construction.

**Listing standard:** UL 2034. Combined smoke/CO alarms meeting both UL 217 and UL 2034 satisfy both requirements at shared locations.

## Low-Voltage Wiring — Chapter 12 vs. Smart-Home Chapter Split

**What belongs in Chapter 12:**
- The legal separation requirement: Class 2/3 circuits shall not share a raceway or enclosure with power circuits without barriers (NEC 725.136). The practical rule for BSH: low-voltage and line-voltage in separate conduits, always, with one limited exception for equipment-connection scenarios.
- Class 2 circuits operate at up to 30 volts and 100 VA. Class 3 circuits allow slightly higher voltage and current. Separation from line-voltage wiring is required to prevent induced voltage from creating shock or fire hazards.
- The conduit-sharing prohibition: Class 2 or Class 3 circuit conductors shall not be strapped, taped, or attached by any means to the exterior of any conduit or other raceway as a means of support, except where the raceway contains power supply conductors for electrically controlled equipment and the Class 2 cable solely connects to the equipment control circuits.
- Cable type requirements by location: plenum-rated (CMP) in air-handling spaces, riser-rated (CMR) in vertical shafts, general-purpose (CM) in other areas per NEC Article 800 for communications (Ethernet/coax) and Article 725 for control/signaling.
- PoE (Power over Ethernet): Addressed in 2023 NEC through Article 840 and 725.144. Standard 4-pair communications cables (Cat5e/6/6A) may carry PoE per NEC 840.160 up to 60W. Above 60W, additional thermal ratings apply. CCA (copper-clad aluminum) conductors are not permitted for Ethernet per Article 800.

**What belongs in the smart-home chapter:**
- Specific product selections (Ethernet category, coax type, fiber specification)
- Structured wiring design (home run topology, patch panel location, MDF/IDF)
- Camera systems (IP camera power via PoE, NVR placement, storage considerations)
- Access control topology (reader wiring, lock power, controller placement)
- Energy monitoring (CT-based monitoring wiring, EV charger integration)
- BSH's "no internet required" design philosophy and how it informs the low-voltage architecture

The bright line: code requirements for installation method and separation belong in Chapter 12. Product selection and system design logic belong in the smart-home chapter, with a cross-reference back to Chapter 12's separation and cable type rules.

**2023 NEC article reorganization note:** The 2023 NEC moved Class 1 circuits to new Article 724, and moved all cable listing requirements for Class 2 and Class 3 to new Article 722. Article 725 now covers only circuit requirements, not cable listings. If any earlier-edition NEC commentary is used as a reference, verify that article numbers have not shifted.

## Practical Wire-Size-and-Distance Tables — Recommendation

Yes, Chapter 12 should include practical tables. The correct source framework:
- **Ampacity:** NEC Table 310.16 — cite this directly by edition (2023) and table number
- **Voltage drop:** NEC 210.19(A) informational note recommendation of 3% branch / 5% total — cite as BSH design standard (note it is advisory, not mandatory)
- **Formula basis:** V_drop = (2 × K × I × L) / CM, where K = 12.9 for copper, I = amperes, L = one-way run length in feet, CM = circular mils

Label the tables as "BSH design guidance applying NEC 210.19(A) informational note" — not as code requirements. Include a note that any specific BSH circuit deviating from the table requires a project-specific calculation. Do not cite a third-party calculator or blog — derive the table from the NEC formula with the sourcing explicit.
