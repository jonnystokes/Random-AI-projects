## 2026-10-27  What does ORSC R703.8.4.1 require for veneer tie spacing and wall area, and does it reduce the area in high seismic categories?
Finding: R703.8.4.1 Size and spacing: "Veneer ties, if strand wire, shall be not less in thickness than No. 9 U.S. gage [(0.148 inch) (4 mm)] wire and shall have a hook embedded in the mortar joint, or if sheet metal, shall be not less than No. 22 U.S. gage by [(0.0299 inch) (0.76 mm)] 7/8 inch (22 mm) corrugated. Each tie shall support not more than 2.67 square feet (0.25 m2) of wall area and shall be spaced not more than 32 inches (813 mm) on center horizontally and 24 inches (635 mm) on center vertically." Exception: "In Seismic Design Category D0, D1 or D2 or townhouses in Seismic Design Category C or in wind areas of more than 30 pounds per square foot pressure (1.44 kPa), each tie shall support not more than 2 square feet (0.2 m2) of wall area." The printed metric value for the vertical spacing, 635 mm, equals 25 inches, the TMS 402 value BIA TN 28D gives ("Space ties at a maximum of 32 in. (813 mm) horizontally and 25 in. (635 mm) vertically"); the code's inch value is 24. The 2-square-foot exception is 75 percent of 2.67, the TMS 402 SDC D reduction the answer file (items 2 and 5) had only from a course summary.
Source: ORSC R703.8.4.1 (2023 edition), UpCodes Oregon viewer, retrieved 2026-10-27; BIA Technical Note 28D (June 2018), Summary of Recommendations, gobrick.com/media/file/28d-tn28d.pdf, retrieved 2026-10-27.
Confidence: high for the text. The 24/25 discrepancy is resolved in the code's favor: an inspector reads the inch value.
Used in: 11.3 Professional, 11.10 Code.

## 2026-10-27  Do the residential code's veneer anchorage rules address a masonry backing?
Finding: R703.8: "Anchored stone and masonry veneer shall be installed in accordance with this chapter, Table R703.3(1) and Figures R703.8(1) and R703.8(2). These veneers installed over a backing of wood or cold-formed steel shall be limited to the first story above grade plane and shall not exceed 5 inches (127 mm) in thickness." R703.8.4: "Masonry veneer shall be anchored to the supporting wall studs with corrosion-resistant metal ties embedded in mortar or grout and extending into the veneer a minimum of 1-1/2 inches (38 mm), with not less than 5/8-inch (15.9 mm) mortar or grout cover to outside face. Masonry veneer shall conform to Table R703.8.4(1)." Table R703.8.4(1) lists only wood-stud and cold-formed-steel-stud backing. Its footnotes: "An airspace that provides drainage shall be permitted to contain mortar from construction"; adjustable pintles with not fewer than 1 W2.8 leg and a 1-1/4-inch maximum offset (airspace to 4-5/8 inches), or 2 legs, 1-1/4-inch offset and 2 inches maximum from the inside face of brick to the end of the pintle (over 4-5/8 to 6-5/8 inches); backing attachment by eyes of W2.8 wire, a barrel of 1/4-inch outside diameter, or a plate 0.074 inch thick and 1-1/4 inches wide. R606.14.1: "Anchored masonry veneer installed over a backing of wood or cold-formed steel shall meet the requirements of Section R703.8." R606.1: "Masonry construction shall be designed and constructed in accordance with the provisions of this section, TMS 402, TMS 403 or TMS 404." The tie spacing (R703.8.4.1), opening (R703.8.4.1.1), flashing (R703.8.5) and weephole (R703.8.6) rules carry no backing condition; the tie-type and air-space table is written for stud backing, and the masonry standard covers a block backing through R606.1.
Source: ORSC R703.8, R703.8.4 and Table R703.8.4(1), R606.1, R606.14.1 (2023 edition), UpCodes Oregon viewer, retrieved 2026-10-27.

## 2026-10-27  Does the residential code's ban on Type N mortar in high seismic categories reach an anchored brick veneer?
Finding: Not under the residential code. R606.2.8.3: "Mortar for masonry serving as the lateral-force-resisting system in Seismic Design Categories D0, D1 and D2 shall be Type M or S Portland cement-lime or mortar cement." R606.12.3.4 (applies in D0 and D1, and in D2 through R606.12.4): "Type N mortar or masonry cement shall not be used as part of the lateral force-resisting system." R606.12: "These requirements shall not apply to glass unit masonry conforming to Section R607, anchored masonry veneer conforming to Section R703.8 or adhered masonry veneer conforming to Section R703.12." BIA TN 28D (Mortar): "Type N or S mortars are typically used in brick masonry veneer walls, with Type N preferred in veneer or non-loadbearing applications and Type S preferred in loadbearing applications. Mortars with air content of less than 12 percent are recommended for their bond strength and resistance to moisture penetration ... the TMS Code requires reduced allowable flexural tensile stresses for assemblies constructed with masonry cement mortars or air-entrained portland cement-lime mortars. In addition, the TMS Code prohibits the use of all Type N mortars in Seismic Performance Categories D and E and permits the use of masonry cement mortars only if the masonry elements are fully grouted." ("Seismic Performance Categories" is pre-2000 terminology.) The residential-code side of answer item 11's open question is settled; the TMS 402-22 reading stays with the engineer.
Source: ORSC R606.2.8.3, R606.12, R606.12.3.4, R606.12.4 (2023 edition), UpCodes Oregon viewer, retrieved 2026-10-27; BIA TN 28D p. 5.

## 2026-10-27  What do the residential code's grout rules say about mortar used as grout, consolidation, cleanouts and fine versus coarse grout (answer items 13, 14, 15)?
Finding: R606.2.12: "Grout shall consist of cementitious material and aggregate in accordance with ASTM C476 or the proportion specifications of Table R606.2.12. Type M or Type S mortar to which sufficient water has been added to produce pouring consistency shall be permitted to be used as grout." R606.3.5.1: "Grout shall be a plastic mix suitable for pumping without segregation of the constituents and shall be mixed thoroughly. Grout shall be placed by pumping or by an approved alternate method and shall be placed before any initial set occurs and not more than 1-1/2 hours after water has been added. Grout shall be consolidated by puddling or mechanical vibrating during placing and reconsolidated after excess moisture has been absorbed but before plasticity is lost. ... Grout shall be poured in lifts with a maximum height of 8 feet (2438 mm). Where a total grout pour exceeds 8 feet (2438 mm) in height, the grout shall be placed in lifts not exceeding 64 inches (1626 mm) and special inspection during grouting shall be required. If the work is stopped for 1 hour or longer, the horizontal construction joints shall be formed by stopping all tiers at the same elevation and with the grout 1 inch (25 mm) below the top." Table R606.3.5.1, grout space dimensions and pour heights (pour height: minimum width of grout spaces between wythes; minimum cell dimensions for hollow units): fine grout 1 ft: 0.75 in, 1.5 x 2 in; 5 ft: 2 in, 2 x 3; 12 ft: 2.5 in, 2.5 x 3; 24 ft: 3 in, 3 x 3. Coarse grout 1 ft: 1.5 in, 1.5 x 3; 5 ft: 2 in, 2.5 x 3; 12 ft: 2.5 in, 3 x 3; 24 ft: 3 in, 3 x 4. Notes: the grout space dimension is the clear dimension between masonry protrusions, increased by the horizontal projection of the horizontal bars in the space; vertical reinforcement not more than 6 percent of the grout space area. R606.3.5.2 Cleanouts: "Mortar that projects more than 1/2 inch (12.7 mm) into the grout space and any other foreign matter shall be removed from the grout space prior to inspection and grouting. Where required by the building official, cleanouts shall be provided in the bottom course of masonry for each grout pour where the grout pour height exceeds 64 inches (1626 mm). In solid grouted masonry, cleanouts shall be spaced horizontally not more than 32 inches (813 mm) on center. The cleanouts shall be sealed before grouting and after inspection." R606.3.5.3: vertical bars held at top and bottom and at intervals not over 200 bar diameters; cells containing reinforcement filled solid; at least 1/4 inch of grout between units and bars. Corrections to the answer file: item 13 says packing cores with mortar gives an inspector grounds to reject because mortar is not an ASTM C476 material; the code permits Type M or S mortar thinned to pouring consistency, and what fails is stiff mortar that cannot be placed and consolidated as R606.3.5.1 requires. Items 14 and 15 call the fine/coarse thresholds paywalled and put cleanouts at 5 feet; the code table gives the thresholds, and the cleanout trigger is 64 inches where the building official requires cleanouts.
Source: ORSC R606.2.12, R606.3.5.1, Table R606.3.5.1, R606.3.5.2, R606.3.5.3 (2023 edition), UpCodes Oregon viewer, retrieved 2026-10-27.

## 2026-10-27  What does BIA Technical Note 18A state for expansion-joint spacing, placement and sealants (answer item 24 had the formula only from summaries)?
Finding: TN 18A (May 2019): "for most unrestrained brickwork, the total extent of movement can be estimated as the length of the brickwork multiplied by 0.0009"; Se = wj ej / 0.09, with wj the joint width ("usually 3/8 in. (10 mm) to 1/2 in. (13 mm)") and ej the percent compressibility of the least compressible of sealant, backer and filler; the worked example (1/2-inch joint, 50 percent) gives "278 in. or 23 ft, 2 in. (7.06 m)"; "Vertical expansion joint spacing should not exceed 25 ft (7.6 m) in brickwork without openings and 20 ft (6.1 m) for brickwork with multiple openings." Corners: "The preferred location is within 2 ft (600 mm) of the corner on either side"; within about 10 feet of at least one side; the two distances from a corner to the first joints sum to no more than the straight-wall spacing (10 and 15 feet at a 25-foot spacing). Joints at offsets and setbacks, wall intersections, changes in wall height, changes of backing or support, and where wall function or exposure changes, extended to the top of the brickwork including parapets. Parapets: additional joints halfway between full-height joints so spacing at the top is no more than 15 feet, continued to a horizontal joint; if that cannot be done, widen the joints or place continuous joint reinforcement at 16 inches in the parapet. Horizontal joints immediately below shelf angles, 1/4-inch minimum space. Sealant ASTM C920 Grade NS, Use M, Class 50 minimum compressibility recommended (Class 25 alternate), field adhesion testing before construction; backer rod about 25 percent larger than the joint, not bonded to the sealant; sealant depth about half the joint width, 1/4 inch minimum. Bond breaks of building paper, flashing or 4 to 6 mil polyethylene between brickwork and dissimilar materials, foundations and slabs. Where brickwork has no shelf angles: joints around elements rigidly attached to the frame that project into the veneer (windows, doorframes), copings that allow independent vertical movement of the wythes, adjustable anchors. Aesthetics: color-matched sealant (brick color in running bond, mortar color in stack bond), sand rubbed into fresh sealant, joints at inside corners or beside quoins and reveals; joints hidden behind downspouts and toothed (zipper) joints are not recommended. Punched openings concentrate stress at their corners.
Source: BIA Technical Note 18A, Accommodating Expansion of Brickwork, May 2019, gobrick.com/media/file/18a-tn18a.pdf, downloaded and text extracted 2026-10-27.
Confidence: high. The formula and the 20/25-foot rules are now primary.
Used in: 11.8.

## 2026-10-27  What are TMS 602's hot- and cold-weather requirements (answer items 29 and 30 had them from contractor summaries)?
Finding: CMHA TEK 03-01C reproduces the TMS 602 tables. Normal range 40 to 90°F. Cold: material heating below 40°F; at 20°F and below, grout aggregates and water heated to give grout at 70 to 120°F; heat the masonry to 40°F before grouting; windbreaks or enclosures above 15 mph wind; enclosure and heat above 32°F at the coldest band; water and aggregates never above 140°F. Protection: mean daily 25 to 40°F, cover with a weather-resistive membrane 24 hours; 20 to 25°F, insulating blanket 24 hours (48 for grouted masonry unless the grout's only cement is Type III); 20°F and below, masonry held above 32°F for 24 hours (48 grouted, unless Type III) by heated enclosure, electric blankets or infrared lamps. Hot (Table 2a, ambient): "Above 100°F (37.8°C) or above 90°F (32.2°C) with a wind speed greater than 8 mph (12.9 km/hr)": sand piles damp and loose, mortar and grout below 120°F, mixer and boards flushed with cool water, retemper with cool water, use mortar within 2 hours; above 115°F, or 105°F with wind over 8 mph: also shade materials and mixing equipment and use cool mixing water (ice melted before it meets the other materials). Table 2b (mean daily): above 100°F, or 90°F with wind over 8 mph, "Fog spray all newly constructed masonry until damp, at least three times a day until the masonry is three days old." The spread-no-more-than-4-feet and set-within-one-minute rules "is no longer a requirement in the current document but the concept still merits consideration." Correction: the answer file and the Revision 7 text call the 100/90 line a "submittal" trigger; TMS 602 makes it a preparation and construction requirement.
Source: CMHA TEK 03-01C, All-Weather Concrete Masonry Construction, cmha.org/resource/tek-03-01c/ (PDF via the page's download link), downloaded and text extracted 2026-10-27.
Confidence: high for the tables as TEK 03-01C prints them; the TMS 602 edition the TEK reproduces is not stated on the extracted pages.

## 2026-10-27  What does CMHA's grout note add on fine and coarse grout, admixtures and self-consolidating grout?
Finding: TEK 09-04A: slump 8 to 11 inches per TMS 602 and ASTM C476; 2,000 psi minimum at 28 days; "Building codes and ASTM Specifications do not recognize any appreciable compressive strength difference between fine and coarse grouts. The choice of grout type therefore depends primarily on the minimum clear dimensions of the grout space, the grout pour height and construction economics. Coarse grout is typically more economical to produce." Site-mixed grout mixed at least 5 minutes in a mechanical mixer. Admixtures accepted by the architect or engineer; antifreeze prohibited by C476; no chlorides. Shrinkage-compensating admixtures "cause a slow, controlled grout expansion that is intended to offset grout shrinkage due to the initial water loss ... especially useful for high-lift grouting". Retarders in hot weather keep grout workable "for placement, consolidation and reconsolidation". Self-consolidating grout "does not require consolidation or reconsolidation", uses superplasticizers with or without viscosity modifiers, is measured by slump flow ("Typical slump flow spreads for SCG range from 20 to 30 in. (508-762 mm). Indications of bleeding or segregation should not be seen in the flow spread"), and was accepted by "using the grout demonstration panel option in Specification for Masonry Structures". The note's references date from 2005; ASTM C476's current SCG acceptance values were not read.
Source: CMHA TEK 09-04A, Grout for Concrete Masonry, cmha.org/resource/tek-09-04a/ (PDF via the page's download link), downloaded and text extracted 2026-10-27.
