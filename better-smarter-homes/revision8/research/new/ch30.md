## 2026-10-27  Current text of Oregon's heat rule, OAR 437-002-0156
Finding: Scope (1): "This standard applies whenever an employee performs work activities, whether in indoor or outdoor environments, where the heat index (apparent temperature) equals or exceeds 80 degrees Fahrenheit." (4) "Ensure that a sufficient supply of drinking water is immediately and readily available to exposed employees at all times, at no cost, when the heat index in the work area equals or exceeds 80 degrees Fahrenheit." (4)(b) "Supply each employee with enough drinking water to enable them to consume up to 32 ounces per hour." Note to (4): caffeine-free electrolyte drinks "are acceptable substitutes, but should not completely replace required water supplies." (5) High-heat practices apply when controls "do not reduce an employee's exposure to a heat index of less than 90 degrees Fahrenheit." (5)(a): a cell phone "may be used for this purpose only if reception in the area is constant and reliable." (5)(b)(A) "Regular communication with employees working alone, such as by radio, cellular phone, or other alternative means;" (B) "Create a mandatory buddy system; or" (C) "Implement other equally-effective means of observation or communication." (5)(e) written rest-break schedule, one of three options. Table 1 (employer-designed minimum): 90 or greater, 10 minutes every two hours; 100 or greater, 15 minutes every hour. Table 2 (simplified): 90 or greater, 10 minutes every two hours; 95 or greater, 20 minutes every hour; 100 or greater, 30 minutes every hour; 105 or greater, 40 minutes every hour. (6): "When employers are performing Construction activities, they must also comply with 29 CFR 1926.50." (7) written acclimatization plan, employer-designed or the CDC/NIOSH plan in Appendix A. Appendix A Table 4.1 (from NIOSH 2016-106): exposure increased over 7 to 14 days; new workers no more than 20 percent of the usual duration on day 1 and no more than 20 percent more each day; experienced workers 50, 60, 80 and 100 percent over days 1 to 4; unfit individuals take about 50 percent longer; a week or more away loses the adaptation, regained in 2 to 3 days. History: "OSHA 3-2022, adopt filed 05/09/2022, effective 06/15/2022", no later entry.
Source: Oregon Secretary of State, OAR division 437-002, secure.sos.state.or.us/oard/displayDivisionRules.action?selectedDivision=2052, downloaded 2026-10-27 (rule text and history); the table values from the rule-text PDF at oshacademy.com/courses/training/645-heat-injury-illness-prevention-employee/documents/437-002-0156.pdf (the SOS page prints the table headings only); Cornell LII mirror for cross-check; Oregon OSHA fact sheet FS-91 (10/23).
Confidence: high for the text and history (official database); high for the table values (two copies agree on the headings and the PDF prints the rows).
Used in: 30.11 Simple, Applied, Professional and Code; 30.8 Applied (water).

## 2026-10-27  Current text of Oregon's wildfire-smoke rule, OAR 437-002-1081
Finding: Scope (1): employers "whose employees are or will be exposed to wildfire smoke where the ambient air concentration for fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is at or above 35.5 µg/m3 (Air Quality Index value of 101 for PM2.5)." At 35.5 µg/m3: engineering and administrative controls to bring exposure below it unless functionally impossible, and NIOSH-approved filtering facepiece respirators provided for voluntary use. "Whenever employee exposure to PM2.5 is at or above 200.9 µg/m3 (AQI 251) ... ensure that employees wear appropriate NIOSH-approved filtering facepiece respirators", under the Appendix A program in lieu of medical evaluation and fit testing. "Whenever employee exposure to PM2.5 is at or above 500.4 µg/m3 (AQI 501)": respirators and a complete 29 CFR 1910.134 program. Monitoring at the start of each shift and as needed, from Oregon DEQ, EPA AirNow, the Interagency Wildland Fire Air Quality Response Program or an equivalent, smoke advisories, or a direct-reading PM2.5 device; the 5-3-1 visibility index (Appendix B) only where none is available. A two-way communication system and annual training. History: "OSHA 4-2022, adopt filed 05/10/2022, effective 07/01/2022", no later entry.
Source: Oregon Secretary of State, OAR division 437-002 (as above), downloaded 2026-10-27; Cornell LII mirror.
Confidence: high.
Used in: 30.11 Applied, Professional and Code.

## 2026-10-27  Do the smoke rule's AQI numbers still match the concentrations on AirNow?
Finding: No, at the upper tiers. EPA's fact sheet on the 2024 PM NAAQS revision: "The AQI updates will become effective May 6, 2024." Previous PM2.5 breakpoints: Unhealthy (151-200) 55.5 to 150.4; Very Unhealthy (201-300) 150.5 to 250.4; Hazardous (301+) 250.5 to 350.4 and 350.5 to 500. New: Unhealthy 55.5 to 125.4; Very Unhealthy 125.5 to 225.4; Hazardous 225.5 and above, the two Hazardous bins combined. The rule's pairs (200.9 µg/m3 with AQI 251; 500.4 with AQI 501) fall on the old scale; on the current scale 200.9 µg/m3 reads inside Very Unhealthy well above 251, and an AirNow reading of 251 means less smoke than 200.9 µg/m3. The rule was not amended after the change (history above). The concentration is the binding number.
Source: EPA, "Final Updates to the Air Quality Index (AQI) for Particulate Matter, Fact Sheet and Common Questions," epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-02/pm-naaqs-air-quality-index-fact-sheet.pdf, downloaded 2026-10-27.
Confidence: high for the breakpoints; the reading of the rule is the writer's.
Used in: 30.11 Professional and Applied.

## 2026-10-27  Construction noise limit, 29 CFR 1926.52, and the NIOSH limit
Finding: 1926.52(a): protection is provided when sound levels exceed Table D-2 (A-scale, slow response); (b) feasible controls first, then hearing protection; (d)(1) a hearing conservation program where levels exceed the table. Table D-2: 8 hours 90 dBA; 6 hours 92; 4 hours 95; 3 hours 97; 2 hours 100; 1.5 hours 102; 1 hour 105; 0.5 hour 110; 0.25 hour or less 115. Source: osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.52 (curl, 2026-10-27); cdc.gov/niosh/noise/about/noise.html (2026-10-27).

## 2026-10-27  How OSHA adjusts a hearing protector's NRR, and what double protection adds
Finding: OSHA Technical Manual, Section III, Chapter 5 (noise), Appendix on HPD attenuation: single protector with A-weighted data, "Estimated Exposure (dBA) = TWA (dBA) - (NRR - 7)"; with the 50 percent safety factor used for field estimates, "TWA (dBA) - [(NRR - 7) x 50%]", worked example "98 dBA - [(25-7) x 50%] = 89 dBA". Dual protection: take the higher-rated protector, subtract 7, "Add 5 dB to this field-adjusted NRR to account for the use of the second hearing protector", and "using such double protection will add only 5 dB of attenuation"; with the safety factor, "TWA (dBA) - {[(NRRh - 7) x 50%] + 5}".
Source: osha.gov/otm/section-3-health-hazards/chapter-5 (curl, 2026-10-27).
Confidence: high. This replaces the answer file's commercial calculator as the source for item 17.
Used in: 30.6 Professional and Applied.

## 2026-10-27  Is this house "residential construction" under OSHA's fall-protection directive?
Finding: STD 03-11-002 interprets "residential construction" for 1926.501(b)(13) as end use as a dwelling plus "traditional wood frame construction materials and methods", characterized by "Framing materials: Wood (or equivalent cold-formed sheet metal stud) framing, not steel or concrete; wooden floor joists and roof structures. Exterior wall structure: Wood (or equivalent cold-formed sheet metal stud) framing or masonry brick or block." A house with masonry block walls and wood floors and roof qualifies; this house's concrete floors and roof do not, so a hired crew's employer works under the general 1926.501(b) provisions without the residential fall-protection-plan alternative. Correction to answer item 75, which gives the masonry walls as the reason; masonry walls are expressly included, and the concrete framing is what excludes.
Source: osha.gov/enforcement/directives/std-03-11-002 (curl, 2026-10-27).

## 2026-10-27  Lifting-equation multipliers, and a worked block lift
Finding: CCOHS tables (metric): load constant 23 kg (51 lb). HM: 25 cm or less 1.00, 30 cm 0.83, 40 cm 0.63, 50 cm 0.50. VM: 0 cm 0.78, 30 cm 0.87, 50 cm 0.93, 70 cm 0.99, 100 cm 0.93, 150 cm 0.78. DM: 25 cm or less 1.00, 40 cm 0.93, 55 cm 0.90, 100 cm 0.87. AM: 0 degrees 1.00, 30 0.90, 45 0.86, 60 0.81, 90 0.71. FM by time between lifts (one hour or less / over one hour): 1 min 0.94/0.75; 30 s 0.91/0.65; 15 s 0.84/0.45; 10 s 0.75/0.27. CM: good 1.00; fair 1.00 standing, 0.95 stooping; poor 0.90. Worked example, writer's arithmetic from those values: a block lifted from a ground pallet (hands at 30 cm, held 30 cm out, raised 100 cm, one lift every 15 seconds for under an hour, stooping, fair grip): 23 x 0.83 x 0.87 x 0.87 x 1.00 x 0.84 x 0.95 = 11.5 kg (25 lb). The same lift from a pallet raised to 70 cm, held within 25 cm, raised 55 cm, standing: 23 x 1.00 x 0.99 x 0.90 x 1.00 x 0.84 x 1.00 = 17.2 kg (38 lb). The same ground-pallet lift kept up for over an hour: FM 0.45, 6.2 kg (14 lb). A 38 lb block is 17.2 kg.
Source: ccohs.ca/oshanswers/ergonomics/niosh/calculating_rwl.html (2026-10-27); the method is the Revised NIOSH Lifting Equation (Waters et al., NIOSH Applications Manual, Publication 94-110).
Confidence: high for the table values; the inputs are illustrations chosen by the writer, stated as such in the text.
Used in: 30.2 Professional.

## 2026-10-27  NIOSH hydration and acclimatization advice for heat

## 2026-10-27  Kneeling and knee osteoarthritis: the study figures from the abstracts
Finding: Verbeek J and others, "Occupational Exposure to Knee Loading and the Risk of Osteoarthritis of the Knee: A Systematic Review and a Dose-Response Meta-Analysis," Safety and Health at Work, 2017: "The incremental OR per 5,000 hours of kneeling was 1.26 (95% confidence interval 1.17-1.35, 5 studies, moderate quality evidence)"; "For lifting, there was no exposure dose-response per 100,000 kg of lifetime lifting (OR 1.00 ...)". ## 2026-10-27  The masonry exoskeleton field study, full citation
Finding: Al-khiami MI and Lindhard SM, "Field comparison of rigid and soft passive back-support exoskeletons: physical exertion, comfort, and adoption among masonry workers," Safety Science, 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.ssci.2026.107138: fifteen masons at five sites over two days, structured interviews; the soft BISKO suit reduced perceived fatigue 50.53 percent and perceived exertion 32.57 percent, against 27.39 and 14.76 percent for the rigid HAPO Back. ## 2026-10-27  Hand-arm vibration trigger times, recomputed
Finding: The daily exposure A(8) = a x square root of (T / 8 hours), the definition in Directive 2002/44/EC; the time to reach a limit L at vibration magnitude a is 8 hours x (L / a) squared. At the action value 2.5 m/s2: 9 m/s2 gives 37 minutes, 12 gives 21, 20 gives 7.5, 25 gives 4.8; 4 gives 3 hours 8 minutes, 6 gives 1 hour 23 minutes. At the limit value 5 m/s2: 20 gives 30 minutes, 9 gives 2 hours 28 minutes, 25 gives 19 minutes. These match the HAVS Log table the answer file cites (item 19) at every endpoint.
Source: the writer's arithmetic on the directive's definition (as cited in answer item 19: legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2002/44); HAVS Log, havslog.co.uk/blog/havs-time-limits-trigger-times/.
