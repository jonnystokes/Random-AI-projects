## 2026-10-29  How does OWRD describe the down-hole heat-exchange exemption?
Finding: OWRD's handout "Exempt Water Uses in Oregon" (undated, current enough to state "Beginning Jan. 1, 2026, commercial gardens not exceeding a half-acre and using no more than 3,000 gallons per day will qualify as exempt") lists, under ORS 537.545, "Down-hole heat exchange purposes: An open loop heat exchange hole to provide source heat." It also states: "Each exemption is separate, and one well may serve multiple exempt uses." Its surface-water list includes "Rainwater: Collection and use of rainwater from an artificial impervious surface (like a building's roof)" and the natural-spring exemption "as determined by the Department".
Source: oregon.gov/owrd/WRDReports/ExemptUseHandout.pdf (downloaded and text-extracted 2026-10-29).
Confidence: high for the quoted wording. The two-well withdraw-and-reinject accounting is not addressed by the handout.
Used in: 18.3 Professional; Open items; cross-chapter flag for the HVAC chapter.

## 2026-10-29  What do ORS 537.545, 537.515 and 537.783 say that this chapter relies on?
Finding: ORS 537.545(1)(e) exempts "Down-hole heat exchange purposes" with no quantity, beside (1)(d) "Single or group domestic purposes in an amount not exceeding 15,000 gallons a day" and (1)(f) "Any single industrial or commercial purpose ... not exceeding 5,000 gallons a day". ORS 537.515(4): "'Geothermal fluid' means any ground water used for its thermal characteristics that is encountered in a well with a bottom hole temperature of less than 250 degrees Fahrenheit or any other fluid that is circulated within a well with a bottom hole temperature of less than 250 degrees Fahrenheit and used for its acquired thermal characteristics." ORS 537.783(3): "A water pollution control facilities permit shall be obtained from the Department of Environmental Quality under ORS 468B.050 before reinjection is commenced. The Department of Environmental Quality may, by agreement with the Water Resources Commission, waive this requirement for reinjection into the reservoir from which the fluid came where adequate standards and tests have been adopted". ORS 537.141(1)(h) reads as the Land chapter's entry states it.
Source: revision6_code_library/ors/ors537.html, read 2026-10-29.
Confidence: high.

## 2026-10-29  Which DEQ rule authorizes a residential heat-pump return well, and what does it require?
Finding: OAR 340-044-0018(1) authorizes an injection system by rule when, among other conditions, "(b) The owner or operator submits inventory information under OAR 340-044-0020 and registers the system with the Director". Section (2)(b) lists Class V systems so authorized, including "(B) Low-temperature geothermal fluid return wells to the same or equivalent-quality aquifer, decided case by case" and "(C) Heat pump and air conditioning return wells: 'Wells returning fluids to the supply aquifer after use for non-contact heating or cooling'". OAR 340-044-0015(4): after the rule's effective date no one may construct or operate an allowable injection system without a Director-issued permit unless the system is authorized by rule under 340-044-0018.
Source: oregon.public.law/rules/oar_340-044-0018 and oar_340-044-0015 (Secretary of State text, accessed May 26, 2025 per the page), read 2026-10-29.
Confidence: high for the summary; the page was read through a summarizer, with short quotations.

## 2026-10-29  How does OAR 690-230-0130 treat household heating and cooling from groundwater?

## 2026-10-29  What is Oregon's definition of gray water, and what do the graywater rules require?
Finding: ORS 454.605(8) defines gray water as "shower and bath waste water, bathroom sink waste water, kitchen sink waste water and laundry waste water" and states it does not mean toilet wastes, garbage wastes or waste water contaminated by soiled diapers. OAR 340-053-0080: (2) no construction, installation or operation without a DEQ permit; (5) the system connects to an approved sewerage system or a functioning onsite system under OAR 340-071; (8) kitchen-sink water without primary treatment, dishwasher and garbage-disposal water, diaper wash water, and water with chemicals, oily residues or hobby wastes go to the sewer or onsite system; (11) the design plan's contents; (13) the O&M manual; (16) property-line crossings under a written agreement with state access; (17) no land use compatibility statement for a system under 1,200 gallons a day connected to a sewer or approved onsite system. OAR 340-053-0090: (1)(e) irrigation sites on stable geology, no slopes over 45 percent, no frozen or saturated soil, groundwater at least 4 feet below the discharge at irrigation time, irrigation only when evapotranspiration exceeds precipitation; (2) Type 1 stored no more than 24 hours, discharge covered by at least 2 inches of soil, mulch or compost, no surfacing, ponding or runoff; (3) Type 2 at BOD5 and TSS of 10 mg/L or less, sampled once a year at 300 gallons a day or less; (4) Type 3 total coliform median 2.2 per 100 mL and no sample over 23. OAR 340-053-0100: (3) the diversion valve easy to reach, labeled, durable, corrosion-resistant and watertight; (4) no direct potable connection; (5) tank rules including an overflow at least the inlet size, by gravity, with no shutoff, and the label "Caution — Non-potable Water — Not Safe to Drink". OAR 340-053-0070 defines the three types; OAR 340-053-0060 scopes the division. Table 1 (design flows) and Table 2 (setbacks) are not reproduced on the oregon.public.law or SOS pages.
Source: oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_454.605 and rules oar_340-053-0060, -0070, -0080, -0090, -0100; secure.sos.state.or.us/oard/view.action?ruleNumber=340-053-0080 (last amended DEQ 13-2019, effective 05/16/2019); all read 2026-10-29.
Confidence: high for the summarized text, read through a summarizer with short quotations.

## 2026-10-29  What does DEQ General Permit 2401 require, and what are the setbacks?
Finding: The posted permit (effective date June 1, 2017, issued under ORS 468B.050) covers Type 1 graywater, subsurface irrigation only, "from a single family residence or residential duplex with no more than four bedrooms", collecting no more than 300 gallons a day, connected to a sewer or onsite system. Condition 9 setbacks, in feet, for the point of discharge to landscape and for a storage or surge tank: groundwater supplies and wells 100 and 50; springs 100 and 50; surface water excluding springs 50 and 50; stormwater management structures 10 and 10; UIC systems 10 and 10; property boundaries 2 and 5; building structures 0 and 0. Condition 11: no slopes over 45 percent, no frozen or saturated soils, irrigation only when evapotranspiration exceeds precipitation, no surfacing, ponding or runoff. Condition 8: storage no more than 24 hours. Condition 10: discharge covered by at least two inches of soil, mulch or compost. In lieu of the annual fee the permittee may submit an annual report. DEQ's homeowner guide (last updated 6/24/13, by Ron Doughten) gives Tier 1 (2401) fees of $50 new and $40 annual and Tier 2 (2402) fees of $534 and $50, with the Tier 1 annual fee waived four years in five on an annual report, and gives Type 2 discharge setbacks of 50 feet from wells and springs, 25 from surface water, 10 from stormwater structures and UICs and 2 from property lines. DEQ's fee pages show a water-quality fee increase effective November 2026; the new 2401 and 2402 figures were not retrievable.
Source: cms.oregon.gov/deq/FilterPermitsDocs/2401permit.pdf; oregon.gov/deq/FilterPermitsDocs/GraywaterGuideHomeowners.pdf (both downloaded and text-extracted 2026-10-29); oregon.gov/deq/wq/regulation/Pages/table70fees.aspx (effective Nov. 1, 2026, tables not rendered).

## 2026-10-29  What does Oregon OSHA's confined-space rule set for oxygen and retrieval?
Finding: OAR 437-002-0146 (Division 2, Confined Spaces) defines a permit-required confined space by a hazardous atmosphere or its potential, engulfment, entrapment or another serious hazard; a hazardous atmosphere includes oxygen below 19.5 percent or above 23.5 percent; testing with calibrated direct-reading meters before entry and continuous monitoring during it; the attendant stays outside, counts entrants and performs non-entry rescue; entrants wear a chest or full-body harness with the retrieval line attached at the center of the back near shoulder level or above the head; a mechanical retrieval device is required for vertical spaces more than 5 feet deep. The rule does not prescribe a fixed test sequence.
Source: oregon.public.law/rules/oar_437-002-0146, read 2026-10-29.
Confidence: high for the summary. The oxygen-first, flammables-second, toxics-third order in the chapter's Applied steps is the federal 1910.146(d)(5)(iii) sequence, which the osha.gov and ecfr.gov pages refused to serve this writer; it is stated as practice, not cited to the Oregon rule.
Used in: 18.9 Code, Applied and Professional.

## 2026-10-29  What does the Texas rainwater manual give for first flush, yield and materials?
Finding: Texas Water Development Board, The Texas Manual on Rainwater Harvesting, third edition (2005): first-flush rule of thumb "divert a minimum of 10 gallons for every 1,000 square feet of collection surface"; an Australian vendor "recommends that between 13 and 49 gallons be diverted per 1,000 square feet"; "one to two gallons of first-flush diversion for each 100 square feet"; "a rain intensity of one-tenth of an inch of rain per hour is needed to wash a sloped roof. A flat or near-flat collection surface requires 0.18 inches"; standpipe volumes "3" diameter PVC ... allow 33" length of pipe per gallon; 4" diameter pipe needs only 18" of length per gallon; and a little over 8" of 6" diameter pipe"; standpipes "must be emptied and cleaned out after each rainfall event" and the water "may be routed to a planted area"; "approximately 0.62 gallons per square foot of collection surface per inch of rainfall"; "Most installers assume an efficiency of 75 percent to 90 percent", 85 percent in its worked balances; clay and concrete tile "may contribute to as much as a 10-percent loss"; composite shingles "are not appropriate for potable systems"; "For potable water systems, lead cannot be used as gutter solder"; copper flashings discolor fixtures; gutter materials half-round PVC, vinyl, pipe, seamless aluminum and galvanized steel; the U.S. Virgin Islands cistern ratios of 10 and 15 gallons per square foot.
Source: twdb.texas.gov/publications/brochures/conservation/doc/RainwaterHarvestingManual_3rdedition.pdf (downloaded and text-extracted 2026-10-29).
Confidence: high.

## 2026-10-29  Owner-built cistern accounts: what did they cost and what went wrong?
Finding: Dorothy Ainsworth, "Build a 6,500-gallon concrete water tank for $1,500", Backwoods Home Magazine issue 101 (September/October 2006): a 12-by-12-by-6-foot tank, 8-inch walls and floor, 1:3:5 mix, forms of 5/8-inch plywood on 2-by-4 frames, vertical rebar at 2-foot intervals in the wall cavities and none in the slab, a gabled roof over a screened pony wall, a troweled Thoroseal and Acryl 60 interior coat about 1/4 inch thick; cost about $750 in 1983 and "adds up to $1,500" at the article's 2006 prices. Dancing Rabbit Ecovillage, "How to Build a Rainwater Catchment Cistern" (undated): an 8-foot-deep, 8-by-12-foot excavation, wall forms of 1/2-inch plywood on flat 2-by-4s that bulged, a second ready-mix truck, about 50 percent more concrete than estimated, about 1,000 gallons of capacity lost, #6 rebar on an approximate 1-foot grid set into holes drilled in the floor slab, mesh in a 4-inch floor pad, a keyway added after the pour, and the stated fix of a 2-by-4 framework on 16-inch centers with sheathing at least 3/4 inch thick. Rain Brothers, "Concrete Cistern pH": new tanks at pH 10 to 11, "two or three full empty-fill cycles" to reach "a safe pH level of 9 or below", then 6 to 8; a bleach scrub (a cup of unscented bleach in a 55-gallon drum) and pump-out before service.
Source: backwoodshome.com/build-a-6500-gallon-concrete-water-tank-for-1500/; dancingrabbit.org/building/how-to-build-a-rainwater-catchment-cistern/; rainbrothers.com/concrete-cistern-ph (all read 2026-10-29).

## 2026-10-29  Which studies back the sizing, hygiene and behavior figures?
Finding: Imteaz, Ahsan, Naser and Rahman, "Reliability analysis of rainwater tanks in Melbourne using daily water balance model", Resources, Conservation and Recycling 56(1):80-86 (2011): a two-person household reaches 100 percent reliability with a 150 to 300 square meter roof and a 5,000 to 10,000 liter tank, and a four-person household cannot reach 100 percent even with 300 square meters and 10,000 liters. The 2018 Journal of Cleaner Production paper the answer file cited (S0959652618317839) could not be opened or located by title. Vivek, Malghan and Mukherjee, "Toward achieving persistent behavior change in household water conservation", PNAS 118(24) e2023014118 (2021): a five-week habit intervention cut use 15 to 25 percent without volumetric pricing, persisting more than a year. Virginia Water Resources Research Center special report (1998, VWRRC_sp199803) on Dickenson County cisterns: more than 65 percent of cisterns tested for coliform failed the federal standard, the only parameter that failed, with maintenance the cause (from the search-index summary; the scanned PDF has no text layer). NC State Extension, "Mosquito Control for Rainwater Harvesting Systems": larvae in 18 percent of systems with adequate overflow and effective screening against 44 percent with inadequate overflow, 4 percent with both inlet screening and adequate overflow (from the search summary; the page refused direct fetch). Alameda County Mosquito Abatement District guideline: cisterns "completely enclosed", lids sealed, "Cover all inlets, outlets, and vents with fine mesh screening (1/16 of an inch)". U.S. Virgin Islands Code Title 29 section 308: 10 gallons per square foot of roof for one-story and 15 for two-or-more-story dwellings, with an overflow at least the combined inlet area (confirmed through search results citing the Justia text; Justia itself refused the fetch). EPA WaterSense: 82 gallons a person a day at home, outdoor use above 30 percent of household use on average; toilets "nearly 30 percent of an average home's indoor water consumption", older toilets up to 6 gallons a flush and labeled ones 1.28 or less. CDC boil-water guidance: a rolling boil for 1 minute, 3 minutes above 6,500 feet.
Source: researchers.westernsydney.edu.au record of the Imteaz paper; pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8214704; vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/bitstream/handle/10919/49478/VWRRC_sp199803.pdf; content.ces.ncsu.edu/mosquito-control-for-rainwater-harvesting-systems; bawsca.org ACMAD_Guidelines.pdf; law.justia.com Virgin Islands Title 29 section 308; epa.gov/watersense/statistics-and-facts and /residential-toilets; cdc.gov boil-water advisory key points (all 2026-10-29).
Confidence: high for the Imteaz, PNAS, EPA, ACMAD and CDC figures; medium for the NC State and VWRRC figures (search summaries).
Used in: 18.6, 18.9, 18.14, 18.15.

## Cross-chapter flags

- **2026-10-29. Chapter 23, HVAC, section 23.3 Professional and its second Code entry.** The text states that the down-hole heat-exchange exemption, ORS 537.545(1)(e), "covers fluid circulated inside a well without withdrawal; it is a closed-loop-in-well configuration, not the open-loop path". OWRD's own handout defines the exemption as "an open loop heat exchange hole to provide source heat", and OAR 690-230-0130(2) places household heating and cooling inside the domestic exemption. Chapter 23's Code entry also omits (1)(e) from its exempt-use ceiling. The two chapters should agree; this chapter states the statute's three categories and the handout's wording and carries the two-well accounting as an Open item. Evidence: oregon.gov/owrd/WRDReports/ExemptUseHandout.pdf; ors537.html in the code library; the flag already filed on 2026-10-27 in research/new/_cross-chapter-flags.md.
- **2026-10-29. Chapter 4, Legal, section 4.5 ("Cistern sizing that depends on that answer is in the water independence chapter") against chapter 3, section 3.11 ("the fire reserve and stored-water plan in the resilience chapter") and the chapter 17 writer's routing of answer items 14 and 19 (NFPA 1142 sizing, takeoff heights) to Resilience.** Three chapters point the fire-reserve calculation at two different owners. This chapter states the tank-side rule (a protected lower volume with separate takeoffs, NFPA 22, the sprinkler water tests) and names the resilience chapter for the NFPA 1142 calculation and the district's role. The writers of chapters 4 and 34 should align the pointers.
- **2026-10-29. Chapter 19, DWV, section 19.11 table, and chapter 22, Solar, Open items.** The DWV table gives an incinerating toilet's load as "3 to 5 kW electrical load"; research/answers/ch18_water-independence.md item 72 gives "on the order of 1-2 kW while incinerating"; the solar chapter leaves the figure open. No source for either number is cited. This chapter states "several kilowatts, read from the product's rating" and an Open item. The DWV rewrite should source or drop its figure.
- **2026-10-29. Chapter 17, Plumbing Supply, answer file item 20 and section 17.5.** The answer file's "$1,500" owner-poured 6,500-gallon cistern is Dorothy Ainsworth's 2006 Backwoods Home figure ($750 in 1983), not a 2026 figure. Chapter 17's text does not carry the number; this chapter carries it with its year. Evidence: backwoodshome.com, issue 101 (September/October 2006).

Chapter 17, Plumbing Supply, section 17.19 Professional, last sentence.** Under the brief v5 lint gate "sentences naming 2+ other chapters", the sentence "The Land chapter states the water-rights side of roof collection, and the water independence chapter specifies the catchment, the tank sizing and the rain treatment" now FAILs (one sentence, two chapters). - **2026-10-29.
