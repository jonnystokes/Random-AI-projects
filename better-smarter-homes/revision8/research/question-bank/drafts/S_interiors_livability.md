## Part 1: Gathered Understanding

### 1. Fastening And Mounting On Concrete And CMU

The core problem: a wood-framed house lets an owner drive a screw into a stud almost anywhere; a concrete or CMU house does not have studs, so every hung object needs either a masonry anchor chosen for the actual substrate, or an embed planned into the wall before it is finished (in some cases before it is poured or laid at all).

**Anchor classes and where each belongs (BuildToolHQ, CraftingWithConcrete, Eugene Fasteners, all unverified figures, general trade consensus across sources):**
- Thread-cutting screws (Tapcon and similar): light-to-moderate loads, reported under roughly 200 lb per fastener in solid concrete, brick, or block; removable; fast to install with a hammer drill and matched masonry bit; the common choice for shelving, conduit clips, furring strips, and anything that may need to come off later.
- Wedge and sleeve anchors (expansion anchors): heavier structural loads, reported over 200 lb; a 1/2-inch wedge anchor at 3-inch embedment in 2,000 psi concrete is reported to exceed 2,000 lb ultimate tension (unverified, single-source figure, needs an engineering-grade check before any manuscript use). Wedge anchors are effectively permanent; sleeve anchors are partially removable. Only work reliably in solid material; in a hollow CMU cell they can spin or fail to expand against anything solid.
- Chemical/epoxy (adhesive) anchors: preferred near an edge or in cracked concrete, and reportedly the right choice where a hollow CMU cell is grouted solid at the specific fastening point; slower to install (cure time) and typically not removable.
- Hollow-wall anchors (toggle bolts, and specifically masonry toggles such as the type reported as "Hilti Toggler" for 1/4-inch bolts): the correct choice specifically where the block behind the finish is hollow and ungrouted; expansion and wedge anchors are reported to fail or under-perform in this condition.
- Plastic plug anchors: reported as adequate for lighter fixtures such as a grab bar's mounting screws (1/4-inch plug anchor with a No. 12 stainless screw is one specific combination found), though multiple sources flag plug/plastic anchors and even Tapcons as inconsistent for a full-body-weight-rated grab bar and recommend a caulk-in fastener, a lead anchor, or a sleeve-type anchor (brand example found: Dynabolt) instead for that specific application.

**The CMU-specific problem: face shell, web, and cell.** A hollow CMU unit is strong where the fastener engages the face shell over a cross web or a solid, mortar-filled section, and weak or unreliable in an open cell. A finished interior wall (furred, drywalled, tiled, or painted directly) hides which parts are solid and which are hollow. This is a real, recurring failure mode: an anchor set into an open cell spins, pulls, or never seats. Locating a solid bearing point behind a finished CMU wall without exploratory drilling is an unresolved practical question this file flags for research (candidate answer directions: a magnetic stud/block finder tuned for masonry, matching anchor locations to the block coursing module recorded at construction, or the "fastening map" concept below).

**Embeds planned before the wall closes (the pre-pour or pre-finish opportunity a wood-framed house does not have, and a concrete or CMU house gets exactly once):**
- Wood nailers or full plywood inserts cast into a concrete wall, or built into a CMU cavity, at rooms and heights where heavy items are already known to be going (a kitchen wall of cabinets, a great-room TV wall, a primary-bath grab-bar zone): once the concrete or block sets around them, ordinary wood screws work at that location for the life of the house.
- Steel plates or embedded unistrut/channel, the same technique already used in this project's structural and solar-mounting embeds (Structural System And Concrete Design's PV attachment-plate concept, extended to an interior, non-structural use): a fixed hard point for heavy or repeatedly-loaded fixtures (a wall-hung toilet, a fold-down bed, a heavy floating shelf).
- Sleeves or empty conduit left through a wall for a future fastener bolt, a cable pass-through, or a plumbing/electrical stub, coordinated with the same pre-pour planning discipline the project already applies to electrical and plumbing rough-in.
- Furring strips (pressure-treated lumber, fastened to the masonry with Tapcon-class screws at roughly 16 inches on center, with a moisture gap) are the traditional way to get a nailable surface over a whole wall, but multiple trade sources are explicit that furring alone is not trusted for upper cabinets or heavy wall-hung loads unless the furring's own attachment is verified; a furred wall for lower cabinets (which bear on the floor) is a different, lower-risk case than a furred wall for upper cabinets (which hang entirely from the fastener).

**Specific fixtures and what they actually need:**
- Wall-mounted TV: match the mount's rated capacity to the TV weight with a reported 1.5x safety buffer as the practical rule installers use; the mount's own lag bolts still need a real anchor or embed behind the finish, not just a screw into thin furring.
- Kitchen wall cabinets: through the back rail into solid backing at every stud-equivalent crossing is the wood-framed rule; on masonry the equivalent is a real anchor (Tapcon-class for lighter loaded cabinets, sleeve/wedge for anything heavier) directly into solid block or concrete, or into a pre-planned embed, not into furring alone.
- Wall-hung toilets and lavatories: proprietary in-wall carrier systems (named examples found: Geberit, Viega) are rated for high loads (one manufacturer figure found: 880 lb) but are designed and marketed around a stud or furred-and-studded wall cavity that hides the tank and frame; installing the same carrier against solid masonry is reported as workable (bolted to the concrete floor and braced to the wall) but several sources note it more commonly means furring out a new stud wall in front of the masonry specifically to house the carrier, which changes room depth and trim. This is a genuine open question for this project: verify the specific carrier manufacturer's masonry-wall installation instructions before assuming a carrier bolts straight to CMU.
- Grab bars: code-adjacent design load is a 250 lbf force applied at any point in any direction (a figure repeated across multiple installation guides, tracing to ICC/ANSI accessibility hardware testing, not yet verified against the actual ORSC/ANSI citation this project uses elsewhere for accessibility). On tile over a concrete or CMU wall, several sources warn that Tapcon-class screws alone are not reliable for this load and recommend a longer, larger-diameter masonry anchor engaging solid material behind the tile, not just the tile-and-thinset layer.
- Floating and blind shelves: purpose-made masonry floating-shelf brackets exist as a named product class, with one specific example found rated to 150 lb for a 10-inch bracket on concrete (unverified, single manufacturer's own figure); installation is the same masonry-anchor logic as everything else in this section, with the added note that a fastener should land on brick or block, not on a mortar joint alone, for real strength.

**Documentation and the drilling-into-a-finished-wall problem.** Once a concrete or CMU wall is finished, drilling into it to add or move a fastener risks hitting embedded rebar, electrical conduit, or (in this project specifically) cast-in radiant floor or wall tubing. The professional tool for this is a rebar/utility scanner: cover meters (pulse-induction, reported accurate to about plus-or-minus a quarter inch to the object's center) for straightforward rebar location, or full ground-penetrating radar for a harder case (post-tension cable, deeper or denser reinforcement), which can also locate conduit and embedded heating tubing but needs trained interpretation and is expensive to rent or hire. The stakes are real: one industry figure found (unverified, needs a second source) puts the cost of a single accidental hit on a structural element as high as several thousand dollars in repair, and tens of thousands of dollars if a post-tension cable is severed. The project's own existing pre-cover photograph discipline (already stated in the manuscript's move-in and records section) is the direct, lower-cost prevention: a recorded "fastening map" showing embed locations, solid CMU cells, conduit runs, and radiant tubing layout before any wall is finished removes the guesswork a scanner is otherwise needed to resolve later.

### 2. Acoustics Of Hard Interior Surfaces

**Why a concrete or masonry house is acoustically different.** Hard, flat, non-absorptive surfaces (bare concrete, CMU, tile, polished floors, large windows) reflect sound instead of absorbing it, so a room built from them reverberates more than an equivalent wood-framed, drywalled room furnished the same way. This is not a defect; it is a direct, physical consequence of the material choice this project already makes for other reasons (mass, durability, fire resistance), and it needs its own design response rather than being discovered after move-in.

**Real owner and builder reports (forum and trade-source accounts, illustrative and unverified as data but consistent across sources):** an empty concrete room reported to sound "like filming in a commercial bathroom"; a homeowner's stated worst design mistake was pairing an open floor plan, polished concrete floors, and stone counters, producing a room where two people's conversation and kitchen chopping noise sounded busy and loud; a concrete shop reported as reverberant when new and empty, and reported as noticeably deadened once wall studs, OSB paneling, and a machine-filled lumber rack were added, which is a real-world illustration that mass alone does not fix reverberation, furnishing and surface treatment do.

**Reverberation time and treatment.** No single livable-room RT60 (reverberation time) target number was found in this pass and is flagged as an open research item; what is well documented is the treatment method: adding soft, porous, or engineered-absorptive material to some fraction of the hard surface area measurably shortens reverberation. Reported options, cheapest to most involved:
- Area rugs, especially large and thick, ideally with a sound-absorbing pad: repeatedly cited as the single highest-value, lowest-cost first move on a hard floor.
- Heavy drapery (velvet or thick cotton specifically named): softens window reflections and adds absorptive mass to a wall plane without a structural change.
- Upholstered furniture, in quantity and placement, functions as distributed absorption; an empty hard room and the same room furnished are reported as measurably different acoustic experiences.
- Plants are reported (in passing, in more than one source) to measurably help sound absorption in addition to their other value, a genuine two-benefit, low-cost, non-permanent option.
- Acoustic panels (purpose-made, fabric-wrapped, felt, or mineral-wool-core) rated by NRC (Noise Reduction Coefficient, 0 to 1.0, the fraction of sound energy absorbed): dense felt or fiberglass-backed panels reported at NRC 0.85 to 1.00; cork alone reported at roughly NRC 0.3 to 0.7 depending on thickness and mounting (one source's cork-plus-wool-felt composite figure was reported much lower, NRC around 0.20, which conflicts with the plain-cork figures found and is flagged as needing reconciliation before any manuscript use). A reported rule of thumb: 25 to 35 percent of a room's wall area covered in NRC 0.75-plus panels gives a clearly noticeable improvement.
- DIY fabric-wrapped acoustic panels (a wood frame, a core of mineral wool or rigid fiberglass insulation board, wrapped in breathable fabric such as cotton or polyester, stapled to the back) are a real, owner-buildable alternative to purchased panels, at a fraction of the cost, and let the household choose a fabric that matches the interior design rather than an off-the-shelf acoustic-panel look.
- A furred, insulated, and drywalled ceiling under an exposed structural concrete deck (a "drop" of a few inches using metal furring channels) is the most involved option, changes the room's ceiling height and the exposed-concrete look the project may want for other reasons, and is a genuine trade-off to weigh room by room rather than apply everywhere by default.
- Sound masking (an engineered, speech-frequency-shaped background sound, distinct from plain white noise, delivered through small ceiling or wall speakers) is a real, purchasable product category used in commercial open-plan spaces specifically to shrink the distance at which speech is intelligible; it is a supplement to absorption, not a replacement for it, and is a genuinely creative option for an open-plan concrete house with rooms that need speech privacy from each other.

**Sound Transmission Class (STC) and the project's own wall assemblies.** Poured concrete and CMU walls are reported to reach STC in the mid-40s to mid-50s range, driven primarily by mass per unit area (a heavier wall transmits less airborne sound); this is well above a typical wood-framed interior partition and is a genuine, real advantage of the material this project already uses structurally. NCMA/CMHA TEK 13-01D (a standards-body technical note, a real, citable source once purchased or accessed) documents a weight-based empirical formula for calculating CMU wall STC and a table of furring/drywall combinations that can either raise or lower the base STC depending on whether the cavity carries sound-absorbing insulation (fibrous insulation improves the added assembly; two layers of drywall with an unfilled furring cavity can create a resonance that lowers performance instead of raising it). The same source is explicit about flanking paths as the practical failure point regardless of the base wall's rating: unsealed through-wall penetrations, back-to-back electrical boxes, and unsealed control joints can each cut an assembly's real-world performance dramatically (one figure found: a 0.1 percent open area in an otherwise-sealed partition can drop transmission loss from 40 dB to 30 dB). This is a direct, practical coordination point between this chapter and the electrical and mechanical rough-in already planned elsewhere in the project.

**Impact Insulation Class (IIC) and footfall noise on and between concrete floors.** IIC measures how well a floor-ceiling assembly blocks structure-borne impact noise (footsteps, dropped objects, rolling chairs or carts), and is a different, separate rating from STC (which measures airborne noise). A bare, unfinished concrete slab is reported to test low on this scale (a 4-inch slab reported around IIC 25; a 200 mm slab reported around IIC 28), meaning footsteps are clearly audible below. The multifamily code minimum (not directly binding on a single-family house, but a useful reference point) is IIC 50 lab-tested or 45 field-tested; carpet with a 6 mm foam pad is reported to reach roughly IIC 50 to 54; an acoustic underlayment under hard flooring, especially combined with a resiliently mounted or furred ceiling below, is reported able to push IIC above 70; footstep "heel clicks" are reported to become inaudible only above roughly IIC 65 to 70. For a two-story (or partial daylight-basement) design in this project where one concrete floor sits above an occupied space below, this is a real, currently unaddressed design question: a bare structural slab used directly as the finish floor (the project's own stated preference for polished concrete) is the worst case for impact noise transmission to the room below, and the fix is either a different finish assembly at that specific floor, or an accepted trade-off.

**A specific buildable answer found: acoustic mat plus gypsum-concrete (gypcrete) topping.** A resilient sound-control mat laid on the structural slab, topped with roughly 3/4 to 1-1/2 inches of gypsum concrete (the same general topping material already used in the industry to encase radiant tubing, described under Flooring below), is reported as one of the more cost-effective ways to add real impact-noise isolation to a concrete floor system, and is reported to combine with radiant tubing in the same pour. This is a genuine candidate detail for any upper floor in this project that both wants radiant heat and wants better impact-noise performance than a bare structural slab, and needs verification against a real product's technical data (named lead found: Maxxon Acousti-Mat combined with a gypsum product such as Firm-Fill SCM-125) before it goes further than a flagged direction.

**Mechanical system noise into a hard structure.** A heat pump, HRV/ERV, radiant manifold, or well pump that is rigidly connected to a concrete or CMU structure can couple its vibration into the structure and travel further, and be perceived more clearly, than the same equipment bolted to a wood-framed wall. This is a coordination point between this chapter's finish and furnishing plan and the mechanical design covered elsewhere (vibration-isolating pads or hangers, flexible duct and pipe connectors at the point where equipment meets the structure).

### 3. Comfort And Warmth In A Mass House

**The real, statable problem.** A concrete or CMU interior left bare is harder, more acoustically live, and (absent the radiant floor and envelope design already specified elsewhere) can read and feel colder than a wood-framed, carpeted, drywalled house, independent of its actual measured temperature. The project's radiant floor and envelope chapters already address the physics of actual surface and air temperature; this section is about the furnishing, material, and finish layer that sits on top of a technically comfortable house and determines whether it also feels lived-in rather than bare.

**Textiles as the highest-value, lowest-cost, fully reversible lever.** Consistent across sources: heavy curtains or drapery at windows, area rugs (especially large, thick, and placed in seating and sleeping areas), throw blankets, and upholstered furniture with varied cushions and textures are the repeated, practical answer to both the "feels cold" complaint and (per the acoustics section above) the reverberation complaint, at the same time, with the same purchase. This project's own low-VOC materials-chemistry standard (already stated in the existing chapter) is a real constraint to carry into any textile and furnishing recommendation: natural fibers, low-emission foams and finishes, and the same off-gassing discipline already applied to paint and cabinetry.

**Wood at touch points, stated as a physical fact rather than a look.** Wood has a lower thermal conductivity than concrete, so it registers as warmer to the touch at the same air temperature; this is the specific, checkable mechanism (not a style preference) behind using wood at stair treads, window returns, bench seats, and other places a hand or bare foot actually contacts the material. Which species and finish, and the moisture and alkalinity concerns of direct wood-to-concrete contact, are Aesthetics' stated question (already flagged there); this file's angle is where in the house that contact-point strategy actually matters for daily comfort (a kitchen floor spot in front of the sink, a bedroom's first-step-out-of-bed zone, a bathroom bench).

**Worldwide precedent for softening thick-wall interiors, distinct from thermal-lag physics.** Across long masonry- and stone-building traditions, wall hangings, woven rugs and kilims, and layered textiles are the historical answer to a thick, thermally massive wall's hard interior surface, independent of the wall's own thermal performance (which vernacular building already manages through wall thickness and orientation, covered under Passive And Bioclimatic Design). This is a genuine, real, buildable design lineage this project can draw on directly: the same masonry-softening problem this project has was solved historically with the same category of textile answer this section already recommends, not a new idea, a documented and repeatable one.

**Tactile and psychological "coldness," an open research question.** Whether a room reads as cold because of actual surface temperature, or because of texture, hardness, and acoustic liveliness independent of temperature, or some combination, was not resolved in this pass and is flagged as a real research question: if texture and acoustics contribute independently of temperature, then a technically warm (radiant-heated, well-insulated) room furnished with only hard surfaces could still read as cold to occupants, which is a direct, practical argument for treating the furnishing and acoustic layer as part of the comfort design, not an afterthought to it.

**Color and light strategy on the mass surfaces themselves**, cross-referenced to Aesthetics (paint, limewash, and stain choice) and to the Lighting section below (warm-white color temperature, reported in the 2700 to 3000K range, as the specific, checkable mechanism behind "warm" light).

### 4. Universal Design, Aging In Place, And The Lifetime Home

The existing manuscript chapter already establishes the legal ground clearly and correctly: ADA, the Fair Housing Act's design-and-construction rules, and Section 504 do not reach a privately owned single-family house; ANSI A117.1 (2017 edition, the edition Oregon's current codes reference) is a voluntary technical standard here, not a requirement; and a stated low-regret feature list already exists (no-step entry, wider ground-floor doors, blocking for future grab bars, curbless showers, comfort-height toilets, reachable outlet heights, stacked closets for a future lift). This section's job is to go past that stated baseline into the dimensioned, sourced, and technology-integrated detail the existing text does not yet carry.

**ANSI A117.1 numbers found, to reconcile against the exact 2017 edition text before manuscript use (multiple secondary sources, some internally inconsistent and flagged):** clear floor space for a wheelchair, 30 inches by 48 inches minimum (one source states the 2017 edition increased the length dimension to 52 inches, which conflicts with the 48-inch figure other sources give and needs reconciling against the primary standard); a parallel-approach 30-by-48-inch clear space centered on a kitchen sink, range, or cooktop; a 60-inch-diameter circular turning space, or a T-shaped alternative in the full standard; a forward reach range (unobstructed) of 15 to 48 inches above the floor; and a water-closet seat height of 17 to 19 inches, which the existing chapter already states independently as its "comfort-height toilet" recommendation. These numbers are the technical layer beneath several already-adopted low-regret features and are worth stating explicitly, room by room, once this project's actual floor plan exists.

**Zero-threshold entry, engineered.** Real construction detail found for a slab-on-grade house: cast the entry slab and the adjacent exterior walking surface (a porch, patio, or walkway) at matching heights so the finish floor can run through the doorway without a step; manage the resulting water-intrusion risk with a gentle slope away from the house at the exterior surface (one source's specific figure: roughly a 1:50 pitch, about 2 percent), a trench drain where slope alone would conflict with a level threshold, continuous flashing, and a deep roof overhang or covered entry (a composition move Aesthetics already names for weather protection, doing double duty here for accessibility). For a wheelchair user specifically to operate the door itself, the recessed opening needs roughly 18 inches of clear space beyond the door's rough opening on the latch side for maneuvering, a specific, checkable dimension the existing chapter's "one no-step entry route" line does not yet carry. The same slab-recess planning question applies to a curbless shower (already covered for its waterproofing detail under Draft E's item 56); whether one pre-pour slab strategy can serve both the entry and a curbless shower efficiently is a real, project-specific design question.

**A second reference framework: the UK Lifetime Homes Standard.** A 16-point design-criteria checklist developed in the 1990s (Habinteg Housing Associations and the Joseph Rowntree Foundation are the named originators) for "inclusive design": features named include wider doorways, level thresholds, flexible room layouts, and walls reinforced in advance for future grab bars, the same underlying idea this project already applies as "blocking for future grab bars" but developed as a complete, named, non-US checklist. Comparing this project's already-adopted list against the full 16-point standard is a genuine way to find any low-regret feature the project has not yet considered, without adopting UK regulatory language as if it applied here.

**Worldwide precedent beyond the UK and US frameworks.** Japan is a documented leader in "barrier-free" (its own term, now shifting toward "universal design" in Japanese practice as well) housing, including a national housing-loan program that has offered preferential interest rates for barrier-free and energy-conscious design since the 1990s, and design guidance that specifically favors co-locating a bathroom near a bedroom for a shorter, safer nighttime path, a concrete layout idea this project's own floor plan can use directly regardless of any regulatory framework.

**Aging-in-place professional practice, useful even for new construction, not just remodeling.** NAHB's Certified Aging-in-Place Specialist (CAPS) credential is a real, named three-day training and certification program; hiring or consulting a CAPS-credentialed designer for a plan review is a real, purchasable service this owner-builder project could use before the design is finalized rather than after. Separately, an occupational therapist's home assessment (typically framed around an existing resident's current mobility and daily-task performance, reported at roughly $200 to $800 for a dedicated aging-in-place assessment, or $300 to $1,000 for a broader home-safety assessment, with hourly consulting around $75 to $200, all 2026, unverified, and Medicare Part B is reported to cover the OT's own assessment time with a physician referral, not the resulting modifications) is normally a reactive service performed after a health change; whether a similar assessment can be purchased proactively, during design, for a house that does not yet have an occupant with a known mobility need, is a real, open, and directly useful question for a "build it once, for decades" project.

**Elevator and lift provisioning, with real cost figures.** The existing chapter already recommends stacking closets at roughly 5 by 5 feet for a future lift or small elevator. Real 2026 figures found (unverified, needs a second source before manuscript use): a standard home elevator, roughly $35,000 to $80,000-plus depending on floors served and cab configuration; a LULA (Limited Use/Limited Application) unit, a barrier-free category between a full commercial elevator and a simple wheelchair lift, roughly $50,000 to $100,000-plus. Shaftless, machine-room-less models exist specifically for a house where a full masonry hoistway was not built in from the start, which is a relevant fallback fact for this project even if the primary plan is to provision the shaft during construction rather than retrofit later.

**Assistive and monitoring technology**, a fast-moving product category as of 2026: fall-detection systems (wearables, radar-based sensors, and AI-interpreted camera systems, distinguishing a real fall from an ordinary stumble and alerting a contact or emergency service), voice-activated assistants integrated with emergency calling and device control, motion-triggered pathway lighting specifically to reduce nighttime fall risk, and remote family monitoring of daily-activity patterns. Whether this is scoped in this chapter (the livability decision to want the capability) or in Smart Home, Network, Security, And Local Automation (the specific hardware, local-first architecture, and privacy design, matching that chapter's already-stated local-processing philosophy) is a boundary this file flags rather than resolves.

**Kitchen and bathroom universal-design specifics beyond the existing fixture-clearance Code block:** varied or adjustable-height counter sections, open knee space and a roll-under approach at a sink or cooktop, front- or side-mounted appliance controls reachable from a seated position, a roll-in shower's actual usable clear floor space measured with the door or curtain in its open position (not just the stall's stated interior dimension), a floor-mounted or wall-hung sink chosen specifically for wheelchair approach, and mirror and lighting height usable both standing and seated.

### 5. Built-Ins, Storage, And Kitchen/Bath Layout For Mass Walls

**Cast concrete niches and recessed shelving.** A recess formed directly into a concrete wall's formwork (rather than applied afterward) is a real, buildable technique: comfortable depths reported at roughly 4 to 5 inches for decorative objects and 6 to 8 inches for books or larger items; rounded interior corners and mesh reinforcement at the recess corners are both reported as real crack-prevention measures (a sharp re-entrant corner concentrates stress); a fully cast concrete shelf or ledge with embedded steel reinforcement is reported, in one weak, unverified source, at roughly $200 to $400 per linear foot, a figure that needs a real, current quote before any manuscript use but is a useful order-of-magnitude starting point. This is a genuine structural alternative to an applied, fastener-hung shelf everywhere the design is fixed early enough to form it into the pour.

**The CMU cavity as intentional storage.** A double-wythe cavity wall (this project's own owner's-path wall assembly, per Structural System And Concrete Design and the skeleton Wall System chapter) or an unfilled CMU cell in a single-wythe wall is, structurally, unused space; whether and where a cell can be left deliberately ungrouted and opened as a niche or display recess without compromising the reinforcement, grout, or seismic design already specified is a real coordination question between this chapter and the structural and masonry-craft chapters, not a decision this file can make alone.

**Utility chases planned into the coursing itself.** A doubled course, an offset unit, or a purpose-made chase block can carry a plumbing or electrical run inside the wall thickness while keeping it more serviceable than a fully embedded, inaccessible line; this is a laying-sequence decision that belongs with Masonry Craft's coursing and reinforcement content, cross-referenced here for the storage and fixture-layout consequence.

**Freestanding and leg-supported furniture as the lower-risk default.** Wherever a wall's fastening capacity at a specific spot is unproven, hollow, or simply not yet planned, furniture-style cabinetry and shelving that carries its own load to the floor (rather than hanging entirely from a wall anchor) sidesteps the whole masonry-fastening problem. This is a genuinely practical, creative option for a solo owner-builder: not every piece of built-in-looking storage needs to actually be built into the wall.

**Fixtures that need more than a one-time anchor.** A murphy bed, a fold-down desk, or any wall-mounted furniture rated for repeated opening and closing puts a cyclic, not just static, load on its wall fastener, which is a materially different and more demanding case than a single fixed shelf; this needs an anchor class and, likely, an embed rated for fatigue, not just peak static load, a distinction the general anchor-selection guidance above does not by itself resolve.

**Kitchen electrical and low-voltage coordination with a mass wall.** Which outlet boxes, switch boxes, and low-voltage pathways (for under-cabinet lighting, a range hood, a future accent light) are built into the wall before it is finished, versus realistically impossible to add later without core-drilling into finished CMU or concrete, is a direct, practical planning question this chapter shares with the electrical rough-in chapter and the pre-pour planning discipline the project already applies to plumbing.

### 6. Flooring Over A Slab: Extending Draft E And The Existing Chapter

The existing manuscript chapter already has a full, code-cited treatment of polished concrete, tile, and engineered wood over a radiant slab, including the moisture-testing Code block (ASTM F2170/F1869) and the finish-floor R-value limit. This section adds three real material classes and three real problem areas the existing text does not yet cover.

**Cork.** Compatible with radiant heat with real limits: surface temperature should stay under roughly 85°F (28°C), matching the tile and vinyl temperature caps the existing chapter already states for other materials; a floating installation is reported as the safer, more reliable method over radiant; a glue-down cork tile is workable over a hydronic system only with a real concrete overpour above the tubing (one source's figure: at least 1 inch), because cork adhesives are heat-sensitive and can soften if the concrete layer above the tubing is too thin; cork's own R-value needs to stay under the existing chapter's stated finish-floor limit, same as any other finish.

**Rubber and linoleum.** Both are reported as compatible with radiant heat by multiple flooring-system manufacturers; rubber-backed products are specifically noted for slip resistance in fall-prone areas, a direct link to the Universal Design section above.

**Acoustic underlayment under a floating floor, layered with the radiant system.** This is the direct link back to the Acoustics section above: an underlayment that measurably improves IIC also adds thermal resistance, which cuts into the same finish-floor R-value budget the existing chapter already caps; reconciling "quieter floor" against "full radiant output" at any specific upper-floor location is a real design trade-off, not a free addition.

**Threshold and transition details that do not damage embedded tubing.** Multiple sources warn against screwing a transition strip down directly into a radiant-heated subfloor; glue-down or snap-track transition systems are the reported safe alternative at any point where two flooring materials, or a heated and an unheated zone, meet.

**Real-world radiant-flooring failures beyond the moisture-emission failure already covered:** buckling from inadequate expansion gaps (reported ranges found: roughly 3/8 to 3/4 inch at the perimeter, depending on material and room size) and adhesive softening from sustained heat exposure at the high end of a material's rated range, both distinct failure modes from the vapor-emission failure the existing Code block already addresses.

**A livability-only question distinct from the finish-selection Code content already in place: the sound of daily use on a hard floor.** Footsteps, dropped items, and rolling chairs or carts directly on polished concrete or large-format tile are a localized, specific noise complaint distinct from whole-room reverberation (covered under Acoustics above); the fix in a specific problem spot (a rolling office chair, a kitchen prep zone) is more often a small area rug or furniture pad than a whole-room acoustic treatment, worth stating as its own, cheaper answer.

### 7. Lighting And Daylight Quality Indoors

**Layered lighting as a design method, not just a fixture list.** The standard professional model, and one the existing chapter already gestures toward with its three-layer mention, is ambient (overall room-filling light: recessed downlights, cove lighting, a ceiling fixture, sconces), task (focused, brighter light at a specific work surface: under-cabinet strips, a desk lamp, pendant light over an island, a mirror-side sconce), and accent (directional light on art, texture, or a feature: picture lights, adjustable track heads, wall washers). Each layer is reportedly best planned as its own dimmable circuit or zone, decided at rough-in, not added as an afterthought.

**Task lighting levels**, a real, missing number in the existing chapter: no specific lux or footcandle target was resolved in this pass and is flagged as an open research item, alongside the related question of how much more light an aging eye needs at the same task than a younger one (repeatedly asserted in aging-in-place sources as significant, without a specific multiplier found yet that would survive a manuscript citation check).

**Circadian and tunable-white lighting.** Fixtures with an adjustable color temperature (moving from a cooler, more energizing white during the day to a warmer white in the evening, intended to support a more natural daily light pattern) are a real, purchasable product category; whether this needs anything different in the rough-in (a different driver, a different control wiring approach) than the fixed-color-temperature LED fixtures the project would otherwise specify is an open, practical question that connects directly to the Smart Home chapter's local-first control philosophy.

**Concrete and CMU's own reflectance as a lighting-design input.** Exposed concrete and CMU are darker and less reflective than painted drywall, so the same light fixture and lamp output produce a dimmer-feeling room; Aesthetics already notes that an exposed concrete ceiling typically wants surface-mounted or suspended fixtures rather than recessed cans (no pocket to recess into); this file's angle is the resulting whole-room light-level and fixture-count consequence, a lighting-design question distinct from the fixture-placement-and-look question Aesthetics already owns.

**Daylight distribution once the glazing is fixed.** Passive And Bioclimatic Design already owns window sizing, clerestory placement, and light-shelf physics; a genuinely separate, interiors-side question is what happens to that daylight once it enters a finished room: light-colored ceiling and upper-wall finishes bounce daylight deeper into a room than a dark or textured finish does, which is a finish-selection decision, not a window-sizing one.

**Glare control at the finish level**, distinct from exterior shading (also Passive And Bioclimatic Design's territory): a matte or ground concrete floor finish versus a highly polished one, and interior window treatments (sheer versus blackout, and where each belongs) both change how much daylight glare a finished room actually delivers to an occupant, independent of how much daylight the window itself admits.
