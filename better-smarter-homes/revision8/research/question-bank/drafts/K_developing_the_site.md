# Part 1: Gathered Understanding

## 1. Site Development Overview And Phasing

- The general owner-builder pattern found repeatedly in forums and real-project writeups: build a shop or garage-with-utilities first, live in a finished room inside it (bath plus one conditioned room), and use the rest as tool/material storage and a covered work area while the house is built. Reported advantage: the owner stays on site, so evening and weekend hours turn into build hours instead of a commute. Reported failure mode: money and time sunk into the shop leaves the house underfunded, or the owner gets comfortable in temporary quarters and the house stalls.
- A real legal wrinkle surfaced in the search: some U.S. jurisdictions do not allow an accessory structure to be built, or occupied, before a primary dwelling exists on the parcel, and living in a shop before a certificate of occupancy is issued for it is a common but technically noncompliant practice. This needs a direct Oregon and county answer; it is not the same question as the ORSC accessory-structure permit-exemption size threshold (below), which is about *permits*, not about *sequencing relative to the house* or *occupancy legality*.
- Oregon accessory-structure permit exemption (ORSC, general provision, needs exact section verification): a non-habitable, detached, one-story accessory structure with a floor area of 200 square feet or less and a floor-to-average-roof-height of 15 feet or less needs no *building* permit. On a parcel of 2.0 acres or more, with the structure at least 20 feet from all property lines, the exempt floor area rises to 400 square feet. The structure still has to meet the code even without a permit, and electrical, mechanical, and plumbing trade permits still apply where the work triggers them. This is a real lever for sequencing: a first practice structure (a woodshed, a small generator shed, an unheated tool shed) sized under the threshold can go up fast, without plan review, before the house design is even finalized.
- Standard staging/laydown-area practice (general construction-management sources, not homestead-specific): a site utilization plan is a living document that gets redrawn per phase; each phase gets its own staging area, access route, and work zone; the laydown area is distinct from the working construction footprint so incoming material does not block the crew or the trucks.
- Utility-corridor logic: dig every buried utility (power, water, communications, and any future irrigation or shop supply) in coordinated trenches while they are open, even for runs that serve a future outbuilding, because a trench reopened later costs far more than a few extra feet of conduit or pipe run at the same time as the main trench. (This point overlaps Draft E's E62 and the utility-coordination section of Build Sequence and Site Coordination; the site-development-specific extension is planning the corridor routes to *outbuildings that do not exist yet* so their eventual trenches tie into the one already open.)
- Solar-array shading logic applies to the whole site plan, not just the roof: any building, and any tree, placed south, east, or west of a ground-mount or roof array can shade it as it and neighboring vegetation mature over decades. General guidance found: keep taller future structures and slow, tall-growing trees to the north of the array; model shade for the full mature height of anything planned nearby, not the as-built height; a solar pathfinder or 3D shade tool run at the site-planning stage catches conflicts before an outbuilding gets sited in the array's future shadow.
- Septic reserve area is already treated in the Land, Site, and Feasibility chapter as "protected infrastructure...not leftover land for outbuildings or landscaping." This draft's contribution is the reminder to draw the *entire* site plan (shop, block-yard, retaining walls, driveway, fencing, outbuildings) against the septic primary and reserve areas and the well's sanitary setback at the same time as the house, not after, since an owner-builder who places a shop or a block-yard first, ahead of a final septic/well layout, risks contaminating or losing the reserve area to a structure that is already built and hard to move.
- The general sequencing logic gathered from real projects: driveway and site access first (so trucks can reach everything else), then temporary utilities, then the shop/covered work area (a place to keep tools, mix concrete out of the rain, and stage material), then the block-yard and production run, then the foundation and house shell, with outbuildings and site walls filled in around the house schedule wherever labor and weather allow. Several real accounts described a small barn or garage built first and used explicitly as a "practice run" for techniques (forming, block-laying, roofing) before those same techniques were trusted on the house.

## 2. The Owner's Shop

- Typical recommended sizes for an owner-builder or homestead shop, gathered from multiple building-supplier and owner-forum sources: 30x40 feet (1,200 square feet) as a practical starting point with room for a vehicle bay plus a dedicated rear work zone; 30x50 (1,500 square feet) to physically separate a parking/equipment zone from a workshop zone; 40x60 (2,400 square feet) for a mid-scale operation handling farm-class equipment, several vehicles, and a full fabrication shop.
- A shop that also has to support block-making adds requirements a generic hobby shop does not carry: floor area or an adjacent slab flat and hard enough for a vibrating table or an egg-layer machine to walk on (see Draft B, section 7, for the pad spec itself); clearance and door height for a skid-steer, mixer, or delivered pallets of cement and aggregate; three-phase power or a generator/phase-converter plan if a press needs it (Draft B, section 4); and a dedicated bay for bagged cement and covered aggregate that stays dry through the wet season.
- Door sizing: sources recommend oversized and taller overhead doors (14x14 feet or larger for equipment-class use) because the marginal cost of a bigger door is small next to the daily friction of maneuvering equipment through an undersized one, especially on a wet or uneven rural site.
- Floor: gravel is acceptable and cheaper for pure equipment storage; concrete is recommended once the space is heated, finished, or expected to take heavy point loads (a vehicle lift, a stationary press, a vibrating table) reliably over years. A concrete floor in an unheated shop is prone to condensation when warm humid air meets the cold slab (worst in spring, when the slab is still cold but ambient air is warming and humid); mitigation found in owner forums: insulate the slab edge/perimeter, keep doors shut until inside and outside temperatures equalize rather than relying on ventilation alone, and treat an insulated door as a real comfort and condensation improvement, not just an energy one.
- Covered work area for wet-season block and form work does not have to be the same building as the tool/fabrication shop. A cheap detached option found in the research: a hoop house/high-tunnel structure (bolt-together steel arches, UV-treated poly covering) runs roughly $2.50 per square foot for the structure, can be erected by two people with hand tools in days rather than weeks, and gives a dry, tall, open-span covered area suitable for mixing, block-casting, and material staging during the rainy months. Trade-off: the plastic covering needs replacement roughly every 4-8 years, and it can be cut or lifted, so it gives no locked-door security for tools or material left inside overnight.
- A pole-barn kit (posts, trusses, steel roofing/siding, trim, fasteners) for a 30x40 shell runs roughly $12,000-$36,000 for materials only (2026), commonly cited around $12,000-$20,000 for a basic kit from a major supplier; add $6,000-$15,000 for a concrete slab, one overhead door, and basic electrical, bringing an owner-assembled shell to roughly $18,000-$35,000 plus the owner's own labor. Pole-barn kits are explicitly designed for owner erection; a two-person crew with a tractor and a boom or forklift can reportedly raise a 30x40 shell in a long weekend.
- A shipping-container shop is a distinct creative option: a fully insulated, wired 40-foot high-cube container with shelving, windows, and a shed-roof extension runs roughly $10,000-$25,000 for a basic DIY off-grid build, versus $30,000-$60,000 for a mid-range contractor build with plumbing and finishes. Reported advantages: fast delivery (weeks, not months), a genuinely secure and lockable shell from day one, easy to place before the driveway or utilities are finished. Reported drawbacks: limited width per container (roughly 8 feet clear, before any multi-container or cut-and-join work), and insulating/venting steel against condensation is its own design problem, closely related to the unheated-concrete-floor condensation issue above.
- Storage is the piece owner-builders report underestimating: main equipment gets planned for, but the accumulation of fasteners, consumables, spare parts, seasonal items, and overflow tools is what breaks a shop layout within the first year or two of use.

## 3. The Block-Yard And Production Pad At The Site Level

(Cross-reference: Draft B, section 7, "Production logistics for ~100/day," covers the pad's own construction spec, curing racks, crew workflow, and seasonal limits in detail. This section is the site-planning layer above that.)

- The production pad needs to sit somewhere on the site plan relative to: the shop (short material travel for cement/aggregate/water and for moving fresh block to protected curing), the driveway (delivery trucks for bulk cement/aggregate need to reach the mixer without crossing the finished house pad), and the house footprint itself (block gets carried or carted from curing to the wall being built, so the campaign that runs for weeks to months benefits from a short, flat haul route rather than a long one).
- General concrete-flatwork guidance (not block-specific, but the same principles apply to a curing yard pad): a 4-inch gravel base under the pad improves drainage, resists frost heave, and gives a stable working base; a slope of roughly 1 inch per 4 feet sheds water off the yard rather than letting it pond around curing block; a screeded sand layer on top of the gravel, checked with a straight edge, gives the flat, level surface an egg-layer or vibrating table needs.
- The pad's drainage is a whole-site question, not just a local one: where the yard's runoff goes (toward the house, toward the septic reserve area, toward a neighbor, into a swale that was designed for a different flow) has to be checked against the site's overall drainage and erosion-control plan, not solved in isolation, since the yard runs for months and adds a hard, sloped surface to a site that did not have one before.
- What happens to the pad afterward is a real, underexplored question. General sources on reusing old concrete pads say existing slabs rarely match the anchor pattern a purpose-built structure (a greenhouse, a garage) needs, so a pad built to a production spec is not automatically a free foundation for something else; it is, however, a plausible base for a patio, an equipment parking pad, an outdoor workspace, or a base course under a later greenhouse floor if the design accounts for it from the start rather than assuming reuse.
- Security and weather protection for a multi-month outdoor production campaign: general construction-site security sources note rural/remote sites carry higher theft risk because of low foot traffic and slow law-enforcement response, and recommend perimeter fencing, lockable material storage, and lighting; none of that is block-yard-specific, but a cement/rebar/mold inventory sitting in a rural yard for months is a real target, and the yard's fencing can double as the perimeter security discussed in section 7 below.

## 4. Outbuildings Built From Owner-Made Block

General pattern gathered across sources: building a small, low-consequence, largely non-structural or basic loadbearing structure (a garden wall, a coop, a small shed) before the house's real structural masonry is a repeatedly recommended way to learn mortar consistency, coursing, plumb/level control, and joint tooling at low risk. The outbuildings below are candidate practice projects in roughly ascending order of complexity and consequence if something goes wrong.

### 4.1 Barn/Garage

- A real, published owner build (Backwoods Home Magazine) rebuilt a pole garage and added an attached root cellar and bunkhouse; the garage/bunkhouse portion cost about $4,062 and the whole project about $5,233 in 2011/2012 dollars (unverified for inflation; treat as a historical cost anchor, not a current one). The build reused a pole structure (rows of 4x6 posts) and added homemade gambrel trusses sized for snow load.
- CMU wall openings (garage doors, large vehicle doors) need a lintel: either a steel angle-iron or fabricated beam, or a precast/CMU lintel block with rebar and grout, sized to the opening width. Wide openings (10-foot class, the size of a shop or barn door) need a substantially sized steel beam and grouted, reinforced CMU cores on both jambs to carry the bearing load; this is a genuine engineering question once the opening gets wide, not a prescriptive owner-judgment call.

### 4.2 Generator Shed

- Core safety fact repeated across every source: a fuel-fired generator produces lethal carbon monoxide and must never run in a fully enclosed, unventilated space; U.S. safety authorities are explicit that "shed" does not mean "safe" without real airflow.
- Working ventilation design uses the chimney effect: a low intake vent on one wall and a high exhaust point let hot air rise and pull fresh air in continuously, without needing power.
- Clearance: leave at least 3-4 feet of open space on all sides of the generator for airflow and engine cooling, not just for service access.
- Shed shape: a lean-to (single sloped roof against an existing wall) is cheap and effective at shedding water away from the unit; a standalone gable roof shed gives more interior room and better rain/snow shedding at higher cost.
- Fuel storage is kept separate in intent from the running generator: flammable fuel in approved, labeled containers, away from the exhaust and any ignition source, in the smallest practical on-hand quantity, per general fire-safety guidance (Oregon-specific fuel-storage code already partly covered in Build Sequence and Site Coordination's fuel-transport section; the shed-storage-quantity threshold itself is a distinct, unresearched question).
- Noise: a standard factory sound-attenuated enclosure cuts roughly 10 dB versus an open unit; a custom-built enclosure with acoustic lining, vibration-isolating mounts under the generator, a quality muffler, and baffled intake/exhaust ducting can do substantially more; some jurisdictions regulate a decibel limit at the property line, which is a real, unresearched Oregon/county question for a generator running during a multi-day outage near neighbors.

### 4.3 Battery Shed

- LiFePO4 (the common off-grid battery chemistry) has a fairly narrow happy operating band: sources converge on roughly 25°C (75-78°F) as ideal, with meaningful derating and risk above 30°C (86°F) and real danger above 50°C (122°F).
- The hard rule found repeatedly: LiFePO4 cells must not be *charged* below 0°C (32°F); they can discharge at lower temperatures, but charging a cold cell causes lithium plating, a form of permanent damage. Minimum safe storage (not charging or discharging) is cited around -4°F.
- Design response gathered from off-grid forums: insulate the shed even if active cooling is not planned initially, because a well-insulated shed mostly needs winter heat, which is cheap to add, rather than summer cooling, which is expensive; paint the roof and walls a heat-reflective light color; add temperature-triggered automation so the system heats in winter and vents or cools in summer without a person having to intervene; batteries generate some of their own heat during normal charge/discharge cycling, which helps once the enclosure is sealed and insulated.

### 4.4 Pump House / Well House

- Two distinct freeze-protection strategies found: (1) insulate the pipes and equipment directly (spray foam, pipe insulation, heat tape) inside a lightly built or even unheated shed, or (2) put the pressure tank, filters, and plumbing in a below-frost-line pit under a false floor, so the earth itself provides the frost protection and the shed above the pit can be a simple, uninsulated structure used for other purposes too.
- Ventilation still matters even in a below-grade design: any enclosed pump pit needs air exchange because gases can accumulate, and moisture control (a plastic sheet under the slab, for example) reduces the need for winter venting in the first place.
- Access: the top or a section of the pump house needs to come off or open fully, because a stuck pump or a failed pressure tank sometimes has to come out vertically, and a well-service truck with its own boom/derrick needs to position directly over the wellhead, which constrains where walls and roof framing can go.
- Prefab pump-house enclosures (steel, fiberglass-reinforced polymer, or precast concrete) are sold in standard sizes from about 4x6 feet up to 12x40 feet; this is a real buy-versus-build comparison point against an owner-poured or owner-block pump house, not yet priced in current dollars.

### 4.5 Greenhouse

- Passive-solar greenhouse design (found across multiple horticultural sources) uses the same thermal-mass logic as the house itself: a dark-colored concrete floor or dark stone/water storage absorbs solar gain during the day and releases it at night, smoothing the temperature swing; the design commonly pairs an insulated, mass-free north wall (reflective or well-insulated) with south-facing glazing, so gain concentrates on the mass instead of escaping.
- Concrete-block foundations for a greenhouse are a documented DIY path in their own right (a specific how-to source titled "How to Build a Concrete Block Greenhouse Foundation" turned up in the research; the content itself was not pulled, but the existence of a common, published owner method is a real lead).
- An attached greenhouse (sharing a wall with the shop or the house) can use the shared wall as thermal-mass storage in both directions; an oriented, detached greenhouse near the block-yard or the garden has to build its own mass into the floor and any water/rock storage instead.
- Ventilation is treated as a required active system in every source, not an afterthought: ridge vents, operable side vents or windows, and a circulating fan prevent overheating on a sunny day even in a cold month, because passive solar gain in an enclosed glazed space can run well past comfortable and into plant-damaging heat without it.

### 4.6 Woodshed

- Sizing: a cord of stacked firewood is 128 cubic feet (a 4x4x8-foot stack, or the equivalent); most households store 1-3 cords, so a roughly 10x10-foot shed suits light use and a 10x12-foot shed gives room for about four-plus cords with a two-zone (seasoning versus ready-to-burn) layout.
- Airflow is the entire design brief: air needs to reach the wood from at least two directions, so fully open-sided sheds work best and slatted/louvered walls work nearly as well while adding some weather protection; a 1-2 inch gap between wall boards, or dedicated vent openings, keeps air moving through a clad shed; 2-4 inches of space between stacked rows lets air reach every log's surface, not just the outer face of the pile.
- Structural basics found repeatedly: an elevated floor (roughly 6-8 inches off grade) keeps ground moisture out of the bottom layer of wood; a roof pitched at least 4:12 sheds rain and snow reliably.
- Moisture target: green, freshly split wood is commonly 40-50% moisture by weight; a wood stove needs it under roughly 20%, and reaching that from green requires 6-18 months of airflow-driven drying, which is the entire reason the shed's ventilation design matters more than its aesthetics.

## 5. Food And Cold Storage

### 5.1 Root/Cold Cellar

- Target environment, consistent across every source: 32-40°F and 85-95% relative humidity for root-crop storage.
- The controlling design principle for a concrete root cellar is earth-coupling: the below-grade concrete floor and walls are left *uninsulated on the room side* specifically so the room stays thermally locked to the stable temperature of the surrounding earth; insulating those surfaces would defeat the entire mechanism. Any insulation goes on the *outside* of the below-grade concrete (against the soil) or on above-grade components, never between the earth-facing concrete and the room.
- A published, detailed real build (Backwoods Home Magazine, "Build a Concrete Root Cellar") gives an unusually complete data set: 12x16-foot footprint, 8 feet deep, 8-inch-thick concrete floor, walls, and roof; walls poured in two 4-foot lifts (the practical maximum single-pour form height cited); #5 rebar on a 16-inch grid both directions; about 21 cubic yards of concrete total; fiberglass fiber added to the mix for crack control; exterior masonry waterproofing sealer plus 1-inch rigid foam over that; a 12-inch corrugated drain pipe down the lower wall and a 4-inch perforated French drain arced around the upper side, buried about 12 inches deep; a 12-inch roof-mounted wind turbine vent; interior finished in 1x12 pine boards over the concrete. Total project cost around $5,000 in the source year (unverified for inflation). The builder's specific problem-solution: 8-inch-thick forms are too narrow for a standard concrete chute, forcing a pump (added cost), and a retired water-tank builder's advice, laying 30-pound tarpaper along the wall-to-roof perimeter before the roof pour, kept the roof slab from bonding to and cracking the walls as the two cured at different rates.
- Real-world Pacific Northwest experience (a rainy-climate-specific forum thread) is blunt about waterproofing expectations in Oregon's climate: even well-built concrete basements in the region leak at a high rate by owner report, so the design principle that emerged from multiple builders is to plan for controlled drainage rather than assume a perfectly dry, sealed structure. Concrete practices reported as working: a french drain under a gravel base beneath the structure, a sloped floor directing any intrusion to a drain rather than letting it pool, siting into a north-facing slope so the entry does not bake in sun, and building to at least 8 feet of depth/earth cover for temperature stability. A specific material failure reported: buried cedar posts rotted out in under 10 years, so wood is not a below-grade structural material choice in this climate regardless of species.
- Ventilation is passive in almost every source: two ducts, one set low (intake) and one set high (exhaust), using the stack effect (warm air rising and pulling cooler air in behind it) to create gentle, continuous air exchange without power. A packed-earth or gravel floor holds humidity better than a sealed concrete floor, which is a real tension against the earth-coupling logic above (concrete floor for thermal stability versus earth/gravel floor for humidity); some designs split the difference with a concrete perimeter/structure and a gravel or earth floor section specifically for humidity.
- A root cellar can double as a storm/safe room, which several sources note may open access to disaster-preparedness or storm-shelter funding programs; this is a real, unresearched Oregon-specific lead (FEMA or state safe-room cost-share programs), not a verified fact.
- Above-ground and in-basement alternatives exist for a site where digging a detached cellar is not practical: an insulated corner of an existing below-grade space (a basement corner with two exterior, uninsulated walls) can be walled off and function as a smaller root cellar without a separate excavation, at some loss of capacity and control compared with a purpose-built detached structure.

### 5.2 Pantry And Canning Kitchen

- Rotation discipline (FIFO, first in first out) is the organizing principle in every food-storage source: older stock moves to the front or top, new purchases go to the back or bottom, and a short recurring habit (a five-minute reset when unloading groceries, or a quarterly deeper check) keeps the system honest without becoming a chore.
- Physical layout guidance: heavier canned goods belong on lower shelves both for lifting safety and to keep shelving from bowing over years of load; zoning the pantry into "weekly staples," "backstock," and "home-canned/specialty" areas keeps daily use separate from the deeper reserve and reduces both overbuying and forgotten stock.
- Mechanical rotation aids exist as a purchasable or a build-it-yourself option: gravity-fed slanted shelving or rotating drum racks physically force older cans forward as newer ones are loaded from behind, which removes the rotation discipline from the household's memory and makes it automatic.
- A dedicated canning kitchen or summer kitchen (a secondary cooking/processing space, sometimes outside the main house envelope) is a real vernacular pattern for households that can or preserve at volume, keeping the heat, steam, and mess of a canning run out of the finished kitchen; this project's shop or an outbuilding is a plausible location for that function, not yet researched in Oregon-specific or code terms.

### 5.3 Chest Freezers, Backup Power, And Disaster Stores

- Load figures: a chest freezer draws roughly 80-150 running watts, with a startup/surge draw of 600-1,200 watts when the compressor kicks on; a 1,000-2,000 watt generator or inverter is commonly cited as sufficient to run one.
- Outage survival: a full, unopened chest freezer holds a safe temperature for roughly 24-48 hours with no power at all; a common generator-conservation strategy during an extended outage is to run the generator about an hour out of every 8-12 hours rather than continuously, just enough to re-cool the freezer's mass before shutting down again.
- This is a direct, load-bearing overlap with the Solar, Battery, and Energy Independence chapter: the freezer (and any chest-freezer-based disaster food reserve) is exactly the kind of steady, predictable, deferrable load that a home battery and solar system is suited to carry through a grid outage without a generator at all, and the sizing math (freezer running watts times hours per day) is a real input to that chapter's load-calculation work, not a new fact to establish here.
- Disaster food-quantity planning: dry grains run roughly 1,500-1,700 kcal per pound, and adult daily needs run roughly 2,000-2,500 kcal, which is the arithmetic basis multiple food-storage calculators use to size a household's stored-grain weight for a given duration; commonly referenced planning frameworks include LDS/Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints home-storage guidance and FEMA emergency-preparedness guidance (both unverified leads here, not yet checked against primary sources).
- The root cellar, the pantry, and the chest freezers form one system for this house's stated disaster-independence goal: root-cellar storage covers what does not need power at all, chest freezers (fed by solar/battery) cover what needs steady cold, and shelf-stable pantry/canned goods cover the rest; sizing all three together, rather than each in isolation, is a real design question this project has not yet framed.

## 6. Retaining Walls And Site Walls

- Oregon-specific threshold, found directly and consistent with national segmental-retaining-wall industry guidance: a retaining wall over 4 feet tall, measured from the bottom of the footing to the top of the wall, needs a building permit and Oregon-registered-engineer-stamped drawings. A wall of any height that supports a surcharge (a slope, a driveway, a structure, or other load above the retained soil, not just plain backfill) can trigger the same engineering requirement even under 4 feet. This lines up with the general segmental-retaining-wall industry standard (CMHA) of a 3-4 foot practical ceiling for unengineered gravity walls.
- With engineering, segmental block walls scale far past the unreinforced ceiling: conventional gravity SRWs are commonly presented up to about 8 feet, and geogrid-soil-reinforced SRWs are commonly presented up to about 14 feet in standard guides, with no true theoretical maximum once full engineering and soil reinforcement are applied; some built examples exceed 50 feet.
- Drainage is the single most repeated failure-prevention theme across every retaining-wall source, independent of wall type: clean, angular gravel (roughly 3/4 to 1.5 inch), at least 12 inches thick, immediately behind the wall; weep holes spaced roughly every 3-7 feet through the wall face to relieve hydrostatic (water) pressure before it builds; geotextile fabric between the gravel zone and the native soil so fines do not migrate in and clog the drainage zone over time. Hydrostatic pressure from trapped water, not the retained soil's static weight alone, is described repeatedly as the actual mechanism behind most wall failures.
- Gabion walls (wire-mesh baskets filled with stone) are a distinct, low-skill, high-labor option well suited to an owner-builder who is already sourcing or crushing rock on site. Specification details found: double-twisted hexagonal woven wire mesh, a common mesh size cited as 8x10 with 2.7-3.0 mm wire diameter; stone fill sized roughly 4-8 inches on the longest dimension, clean and angular for good interlock; fill dropped from no more than about 24 inches to avoid damaging the basket as it is loaded; basket seams tied with wire looped through every other mesh opening, alternating single and double loops, at a minimum density of about 3 fasteners per linear foot. Cost found: roughly $10-$40 per square foot for a DIY-adjacent build, with professional installation averaging around $60 per square foot across a $30-$210 range depending on site access and wall height; gabion construction is repeatedly described as the cheapest wall type to own over a 30-50 year horizon even where its initial cost is not the lowest, because it needs little maintenance and does not require replacement the way some faced or mortared systems eventually do (this durability claim is a marketing-adjacent lead, not verified).
- Compressed/stabilized earth block (CEB) retaining walls are a real but narrower option: dry-stacked CEB (no mortar) is generally treated as suitable only for non-loadbearing applications, and plain, unstabilized earth block loses strength and can disintegrate under prolonged water contact, which a retaining wall's downhill/backfill side guarantees. Cement-, lime-, or fly-ash-stabilized CEB (commonly cited around 8% stabilizer by the earth mix) is the version treated as viable for wet or damp exposure, which directly connects to this project's own cement-stabilized-rammed-earth interest already flagged for the Owner-Made Blocks chapter; a stabilized-CEB or cement-stabilized-rammed-earth gravity retaining wall is a genuine "reuse the block-yard skill on a lower-stakes structure first" candidate.
- Owner-made standard CMU (the same block made for the house) is also a plain, direct retaining-wall option once the mix and press are proven: a straightforward gravity or reinforced/grouted CMU retaining wall built from the same units, mix, and crew skill as the house, at heights under the Oregon 4-foot unengineered threshold, is a low-risk way to spend early production block on something useful before the house walls themselves are trusted to owner-made units. This is a direct, unasked cross-reference into the Owner-Made Blocks chapter's own acceptance/testing content.

## 7. Gates, Fencing, Security, And Rural Access Control

- Livestock/perimeter fencing costs (2026, installed, wide range by source and terrain): woven-wire (field) fence roughly $5-$9 per linear foot installed, with bare materials for a standard 4-foot woven wire plus a barbed top strand running about $1-$2 per foot; a five-strand high-tensile electric fence roughly $0.89-$1.24 per foot installed, cheaper than woven wire but needs a working charger and periodic vegetation control to stay effective; per-acre framing runs roughly $630-$5,900 per acre depending on terrain and fence type, with straight runs on flat, accessible ground costing the least and hillside or wooded terrain costing meaningfully more.
- Deer/elk exclusion is a real, Oregon-specific concern (the Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife publishes its own deer/elk fence guidance; the PDF could not be parsed for exact figures here, so its specific numbers are a direct, flagged research gap). General cross-source figures: 8 feet or taller is the height repeatedly cited to reliably exclude deer, since an adult deer can clear 7 feet from a standing start and considerably more under pressure; orchard/vineyard-specific guidance cites 96 inches (8 feet) as a common target, with 75-90 inches accepted as a lighter-pressure compromise. Deer will go under a gap as small as 6 inches, so ground-contour-following, gap-free bottom detailing matters as much as height. A lower-cost partial measure: a baited two-strand electric fence at 2-3 feet off the ground, sized for light-to-moderate pressure rather than full exclusion.
- Gate types and widths: a standard farm/equipment gate is commonly 12 feet wide by 5 feet tall on 1.5-inch tubing; fire-code access-road gates are held to a stricter, separate standard, commonly 20 feet minimum clear width for a single gate on a fire apparatus access road generally, with a specific carve-out found for gates serving a one- or two-family dwelling driveway at a 12-foot minimum. Whether this project's driveway gate falls under the general 20-foot rule or the one/two-family 12-foot carve-out, and how that interacts with the existing citation to Oregon Fire Code 503 and Appendix D already in the Build Sequence chapter, is a direct, unresolved Oregon question.
- Cattle guards versus gates: a mid-grade steel cattle guard in common widths (14-16 feet) runs roughly $1,700-$3,500, with custom fabricated heavy-duty guards running $4,000-$10,000 or more; a well-built steel guard is cited as lasting 30-plus years with minimal maintenance, against a gate's ongoing wear (sagging hinges, failing openers) and the daily friction of manual operation. A cattle guard trades a one-time, higher install cost for zero operating friction and low maintenance, which is a real, explicit design tradeoff for a rural entrance this project has not yet framed.
- Automatic/solar gate operation is a well-supported off-grid product category specifically because trenching grid power to a distant gate is often the actual cost driver, not the gate or opener itself; a complete solar gate-opener kit (panel, battery, motor, control board) is a packaged, commodity product, and swing versus slide operator choice depends on the gate's own geometry and available side clearance.
- Rural security hardware for a construction site and later an occupied home: solar-powered cellular (4G/5G) security cameras and driveway motion/break-beam alarms are a mature, no-trench product category built for exactly this situation (long driveway, no nearby power, months of unattended material staging); construction-site theft-prevention guidance (general, not homestead-specific) recommends perimeter fencing with signage, lockable material storage, good lighting, and camera coverage aimed at tool and material storage specifically, since lumber and copper are the most commonly cited theft targets and rural/remote sites carry elevated risk from low visibility and slow law-enforcement response.

## 8. Driveway And Access, Beyond The Base Coverage

(Build Sequence and Site Coordination already cites Oregon Fire Code 503 and Appendix D for driveway fire-access dimensions, and Draft E's E61 already frames grade/culvert/drainage/base-rock/turnaround at a summary level. This section adds the engineering-level detail behind those summary points.)

- Base-course depth scales sharply with vehicle weight, which matters directly for a driveway that has to carry not just daily vehicles but loaded concrete trucks and delivery trucks during the build: a standard passenger-vehicle driveway (under about 6,000 pounds gross vehicle weight) is commonly built to an 8-12 inch total base depth, while a driveway expected to carry heavy vehicles (concrete trucks, delivery trucks, at 14,000 pounds and up) is commonly built to 14-18 inches of total base depth on good subgrade, with angular crushed stone/road base (interlocking under compaction) preferred over rounded gravel for load-bearing performance. Clay or otherwise weak subgrade adds further excavation depth and commonly needs a geotextile separation layer between the subgrade and the base rock.
- This is a direct, practical planning fact for an owner-builder who wants to save money by building a lighter driveway for daily use: a driveway under-built for daily traffic and then hit with concrete-truck and pump-truck loads during the pours is a real, reported failure mode (rutting, base failure) that costs more to repair than building the full heavy-vehicle base once, up front. It also directly supports the general finding that the durable driveway usually gets built first, before the house, not after, since it has to survive the entire construction traffic load either way.
- Culvert sizing found: a commonly cited practical minimum for a residential driveway culvert is an 18-inch diameter round pipe (or an equivalent cross-sectional area for a non-round shape), with residential driveway culverts commonly designed to a 10-year storm frequency (versus a higher standard for a real stream/fish-bearing crossing, which triggers separate fish-passage review already noted in the Build Sequence chapter). Design guidance: lay the culvert with a slight fall, roughly 0.5-2% grade, from inlet to outlet, and check that the design storm's headwater depth stays within roughly 1.5-2.0 times the pipe diameter so water does not back up over the driveway itself.
- Fire-apparatus turnaround geometry (national pattern; needs direct confirmation against the Oregon Fire Code Appendix D table already cited in Build Sequence and Site Coordination, since jurisdictions vary): a turnaround is commonly required once a dead-end access road or driveway exceeds 150 feet; a hammerhead (T-shaped) turnaround is commonly specified at roughly 60 feet deep by 20 feet wide per leg, and a cul-de-sac-style turnaround at roughly a 96-foot outside diameter (some jurisdictions specify up to 100 feet). Coordinating this turnaround geometry with the same pad used for a concrete-truck and pump-truck turnaround during the pours is a genuine two-birds design opportunity: one graded, based, all-weather turnaround area can plausibly satisfy both the fire-code requirement and the construction-traffic need, if it is sized and located for both from the start rather than solved separately.

## 9. Utility Corridors And Site Placement Planning

- The site plan has to resolve the relative placement of the house, the septic primary and reserve areas, the well and its sanitary setback, the solar array's shade-free zone (present and future), the driveway/turnaround, the shop, the block-yard, and every outbuilding, all at once, because each of these constrains where the others can go: the septic reserve area cannot be built on or paved over; the well's setback excludes certain structures and activities near it; the solar array needs a shade-free envelope that accounts for future tree and building growth, not just present conditions; the driveway and its turnaround need to reach the house, the shop, and (during the build) the block-yard without crossing the septic field.
- Utility corridors (power, water, communications, and any future irrigation or shop-specific supply) are cheapest to install once, together, while a trench is open; the Build Sequence chapter and Draft E's E62 already establish the one-trench-many-services principle for the house's own utilities. This draft's addition is planning corridor *routes* to future outbuilding locations at the same time, even if those buildings are years away, so a stub, a sleeve, or a spare conduit is already in the ground when the generator shed, the greenhouse, or the pump house finally gets built, instead of requiring a second excavation across a yard that by then has a driveway, fencing, or landscaping in the way.
