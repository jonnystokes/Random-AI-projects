## Part 1: Gathered Understanding

### 1. Cascadia Subduction-Zone Earthquake Resilience Beyond Minimum Code

**The hazard itself.** The Cascadia Subduction Zone can produce a magnitude 9+ earthquake. Western Oregon would experience 3-5 minutes of strong shaking near the coast, with duration and intensity decreasing inland (source: Oregon Department of Emergency Management, ORP background material). Long-duration shaking matters structurally in a way a short, sharp Californian-style event does not: connections experience many more load reversal cycles before the shaking stops, so a connection detail that survives one cycle can still fatigue and fail before minute four. The Oregon Resilience Plan (OSSPAC, delivered to the legislature February 2013) frames recovery on a 50-year timeframe and states that all five critical infrastructure sectors (transportation, energy, communication, water/wastewater, and the building stock itself) are highly vulnerable, with restoration times for basic services in many communities projected to exceed the roughly two-week to one-month threshold most businesses and households can absorb before displacement becomes forced. Willamette Valley soils include moderate-to-high liquefaction susceptibility concentrated along rivers and floodplains; Structural System And Concrete Design and Soil Ground Prep Drainage And Foundation Risk already state the SDC D0-D2 framework and the presumptive-bearing/liquefaction-screening material, so this file does not repeat site classification, only what a builder can do once the site is accepted.

**Redundancy and alternate load paths.** The general engineering principle (not Oregon-specific): a structure with multiple, parallel load paths can lose or degrade one element (a wall segment, a connection, a section of diaphragm) without progressive collapse, because adjacent elements pick up the redistributed load. The "redundancy factor" concept (ratio of reserve capacity to design capacity) is a way engineers quantify this. Practical translation for a concrete/masonry house: more shear-wall lines than the minimum, walls distributed on all four sides rather than concentrated on two, and diaphragm continuity (already partly stated in Structural System And Concrete Design's bond-beam and opening-trim material) are the mechanisms. Ductile materials (steel reinforcement with adequate elongation, A706 over A615 for the seismic-critical bars) let a structure redistribute force by yielding rather than fracturing, which is the material-level version of the same redundancy idea.

**Confined masonry as a real-world beyond-code data point.** Confined masonry (loadbearing masonry panels enclosed by reinforced-concrete tie-columns and tie-beams cast after the masonry, not the U.S. reinforced-CMU system where steel and grout go inside the block cores) has a century of Chilean field performance. After the 1939 Chillán earthquake (Modified Mercalli Intensity IX in places), more than half of inspected confined-masonry buildings had no damage at all. In the 2010 Chile earthquake, researchers found buildings with no damage standing next to structurally near-identical buildings with severe, brittle damage, and traced the difference to design and detailing choices, not the material itself. One quantified design lever: wall density (the ratio of shear-wall cross-sectional area to floor plan area) above roughly 1.15 percent correlated with fewer severe-damage outcomes than wall densities below that threshold; many 1990s Chilean buildings fell short of the 1.15 percent figure. This is a different structural system than this project's reinforced CMU, but the wall-density lesson and the "identical buildings, different outcomes traced to detailing" finding both translate directly.

**Full grouting versus partial grouting.** Most reinforced masonry outside the U.S. West Coast is partially grouted (grout only in the reinforced cells) for cost. Most West Coast seismic construction is fully grouted (every cell solid). Full grouting adds continuous confinement around every bar, not just the reinforced cells, and removes the void-and-consolidation-defect risk that partial grouting concentrates in fewer, more critical cells. Structural System And Concrete Design already specifies full grouting for SDC D0/D1 stem walls; the open question for the new chapter is what full-height, every-cell grouting throughout the above-grade walls (not just the code-required reinforced cells) would cost and buy in ductility margin, since the manuscript's default assumes minimum-cell grouting under prescriptive R606.

**Ductile detailing beyond the prescriptive minimum.** Ductile tie-column detailing in confined masonry research (closer stirrup spacing at tie-column ends, dowels at the masonry-to-tie-column interface, horizontal reinforcement in the masonry itself) improves post-cracking behavior without changing the basic system. The translatable question for reinforced CMU is whether closer-than-code vertical bar spacing, continuous (not lapped-and-staggered) vertical reinforcement, or horizontal joint reinforcement at every course instead of every other course meaningfully improves ductility margin for a cost the project can absorb once, at first construction, when it is far cheaper than any retrofit.

**Base isolation.** A base-isolated structure sits on bearings (elastomeric/rubber, or sliding, or a hybrid) between the foundation and the superstructure, so ground motion is decoupled from the building above; the building moves much less than the ground does. Historically reserved for hospitals, high-value commercial towers, and landmark buildings because of cost, but two research threads point toward residential feasibility: a Stanford-linked research estimate put a base-isolation retrofit system at roughly $15,000 added to an otherwise $400,000 house (a 2026 figure from a research-oriented secondary source, unverified), with a design goal of keeping the system under 5 percent of total construction cost; and a Japanese consumer product exists today. The homebuilder Ichijo, working with Bridgestone, developed a residential base-isolation system (Teflon-coated stainless sliding plates carrying the house's weight, paired with rubber bearings that recenter it after the shaking stops); Japan's construction ministry approved it in 2000 and extended the approval to soft-ground sites in 2002, and Ichijo reports more than 1,800 units sold. This is the deepest "beyond code" lever available and no existing chapter states it yet. Whether it is buildable, permittable, and cost-effective for a rural Oregon owner-builder on a concrete/masonry shell (rather than the lighter Japanese timber houses the Ichijo system targets) is an open, high-value question.

**Low-damage design (New Zealand).** Post-Christchurch New Zealand research and practice developed "low-damage" systems, most visibly precast post-tensioned rocking walls (PRESSS technology): a wall segment is allowed to rock at its base or at joints, opening and closing a gap under load, with the post-tensioning providing the restoring force and dissipaters (replaceable steel or friction elements) absorbing energy. Reported added cost for the technology in commercial buildings: just over 0.5 percent of total building cost (a single project-level figure, unverified, not necessarily representative). Residential adaptations are less mature and mostly studied in light-timber-frame New Zealand houses, not concrete/masonry, but the underlying idea, letting a wall move in a controlled, self-centering, repairable way rather than relying purely on strength and ductility, is a design philosophy not yet considered next to this project's purely prescriptive-versus-engineered framing.

**Connections and continuous load path.** Structural System And Concrete Design already specifies anchor bolts, bond-beam continuity, and diaphragm chord/collector reinforcement at the code-minimum level (ORSC R602.11, R606.3.5.1, TMS 402). The beyond-code question is where deliberately oversized or doubled connection hardware (anchor bolt spacing tighter than code minimum, hold-downs at every wall end rather than only where irregularity triggers them, positive mechanical connection of the roof/floor diaphragm to every wall segment rather than the minimum bond-beam anchorage) buys meaningful margin for a cost that is trivial at first construction and impossible to add later once the walls are grouted solid.

**Retrofit-grade robustness.** A useful framing: instead of designing new construction only to the code's life-safety minimum (the occupant survives; the building may be a total loss), design to the performance level normally reserved for judging whether an existing critical-facility retrofit is good enough. ASCE 41 (the standard governing seismic evaluation and retrofit of existing buildings, not new construction) defines four performance levels: Operational, Immediate Occupancy, Life Safety, and Collapse Prevention. Code-minimum new construction targets roughly Life-Safety-equivalent performance in the design-level event. Building new to an Immediate-Occupancy-equivalent target (the building is safe to re-enter and use with limited repair, not just safe to exit) is the "retrofit-grade" framing: what would it cost, in reinforcement, wall density, and detailing, to move a concrete/masonry house from Life-Safety-equivalent to Immediate-Occupancy-equivalent performance at first construction.

**Non-structural seismic measures beyond the code-required water heater strap.** A water heater is required by code to be strapped in SDC D0-D2 (upper and lower third, anchored to structural framing, not drywall) because a toppled 450-500 lb full water heater can tear open gas and water lines and start a fire or flood. Comprehensive non-structural bracing (tall furniture, bookshelves, the mechanical-room equipment stack, the pantry/food-storage shelving, the battery bank) is a much larger, unaddressed list: a house can survive structurally while its water heater, shelving, and stored supplies are thrown across the room, and an occupant in the room is injured regardless of the structure's own performance. Automatic seismic gas shutoff valves (triggered by ground motion equivalent to roughly Richter 5.4, per one manufacturer's stated threshold) are mandatory in parts of California and voluntary elsewhere; they close the supply line before a broken gas appliance or line can ignite.

**Detection and response.** ShakeAlert (USGS-managed, serving California, Oregon, and Washington) issues alerts seconds before strong shaking arrives at a given location, calculated from the earthquake's epicenter and the alert recipient's distance from it. It is designed to trigger automated actions (closing gas valves, opening firehouse doors, pausing surgery, slowing trains) at the infrastructure and institutional level; consumer/single-family-residential integration is not yet a mature, off-the-shelf product area, but the licensing and alert-delivery-partner structure exists and a smart-home or automation-layer integration (auto-closing the seismic gas valve, alerting occupants, pausing a task) is a plausible research target.

**Post-event assessment.** ATC-20 (Applied Technology Council, originally funded by California OES/FEMA) is the national standard procedure volunteer engineers and inspectors use to post-earthquake-damaged buildings green (inspected, safe), yellow (limited entry/restricted use), or red (unsafe, no entry). For an owner-builder who designed and understands their own house's structural system, the relevant question is what a homeowner can safely self-assess before a professional arrives, given that professional inspectors may be overwhelmed or unavailable for days after a Cascadia-scale event across the whole region simultaneously (unlike a localized California quake where inspectors can be imported from unaffected areas).

**Earthquake insurance context.** Roughly 20 percent of Oregonians carry earthquake coverage, which is always a separate policy from standard homeowners insurance (source: Oregon Division of Financial Regulation consumer guidance). Deductibles are stated as a percentage of dwelling coverage (commonly 10-25 percent) rather than a flat dollar figure, which changes the practical value proposition significantly compared to wind/fire insurance. One older (2009) Portland-market data point put annual premium for a $300,000 wood-frame dwelling at $200-$300/year, with masonry/brick homes costing more to insure (unverified, dated, and for wood-frame construction, so not directly transferable to this project's reinforced concrete/CMU shell). Insurers commonly place moratoriums on new earthquake policies for a period after a significant regional seismic event, which matters for a build timeline if a moderate Cascadia-adjacent event occurs mid-project.

### 2. Tsunami Inundation-Zone Siting And Vertical Evacuation

**Oregon's statutory framework.** Senate Bill 379 (1995), codified as ORS 455.446 and 455.447, restricts new construction of "essential facilities" and "special occupancy structures" (as those terms are statutorily defined; a single-family dwelling is likely outside both definitions, which is a research item, not an assumption) within DOGAMI-mapped tsunami inundation zones, unless a specific exemption applies or DOGAMI's governing board grants an exception after a public hearing and a finding that occupant safety is ensured to the maximum reasonable extent. DOGAMI's regulatory tsunami maps model inundation from a typical/most-likely Cascadia-sourced tsunami. This statutory restriction is distinct from, and layered on top of, any local zoning or building-siting question; Land Site And Feasibility already has a desk-research pass (DOGAMI HazVu, FEMA flood data) that should explicitly add a tsunami-zone check for any coastal or estuarine parcel under consideration.

**FEMA P-646 (Guidelines for Design of Structures for Vertical Evacuation from Tsunamis, 3rd edition, 2019).** This is FEMA's guidance for a structure built specifically to serve as a refuge above tsunami inundation depth when horizontal evacuation to high ground is not achievable in the available time. Its content includes hazard determination (tsunami depth and velocity), siting/spacing/sizing/elevation guidance for a refuge, tsunami and earthquake structural load determination (referencing ASCE 7-16 Chapter 6, the first national standard chapter to codify tsunami loads), and structural design concepts including using an existing structure as a refuge. This is community/institutional-scale guidance in its typical application (a school, a city hall, a parking structure), not single-family residential guidance, but its loading methodology (hydrodynamic force, debris impact force, buoyant uplift, breakaway-wall behavior below the design inundation depth) is the technical basis for any serious answer to "could this house's structure itself serve as a refuge."

**Local example: Cannon Beach.** Downtown Cannon Beach is cut off from high ground by Ecola Creek. DOGAMI modeling (Open-File Report O-24-12) found that a single vertical evacuation structure at one downtown site could raise survivability in the modeled tsunami-hazard zone from a baseline to as high as saving 100 percent of residents at some sites, and that seismically retrofitting the one bridge connecting downtown to the creek crossing lifted citywide survivability to roughly 97 percent in the model. This shows the vertical-evacuation-structure concept is live, funded research territory on the Oregon coast right now, not a hypothetical.

**Practical siting question for this project.** Because the project is explicitly framed as rural Oregon (not necessarily coastal), the primary near-term relevance is: (a) if a coastal or estuarine parcel is ever under consideration, add a DOGAMI tsunami-zone check to the site-feasibility desk pass, with the arrival-time and inundation-depth data for that specific stretch of coast; (b) independent of whether the specific parcel sits in a mapped zone, the structural concepts behind FEMA P-646 (breakaway construction below design flood/tsunami elevation, open ground-floor construction that lets water pass through rather than load the structure, elevation on a podium or piers) are directly relevant to any coastal Oregon build, and a dedicated comparison against this project's default concrete-shell approach is an open question.

### 3. Wildfire Hardening Beyond R327 And The Smoke Clean-Room (Draft D Section 9, Draft E Item E39)

Draft D already states the R327 element-by-element requirements (roof, vents, walls, overhangs, decks, glazing), Zone 0 defensible space, and the WUI/Chapter-7A-style material and cost tradeoffs. Draft E item E39 states the ventilation-system smoke clean-room strategy (MERV/HEPA on the intake, recirculation mode, envelope sealing during a smoke event). This section stays out of that ground and adds what neither includes.

**The science underneath the code: Jack Cohen's home ignition zone.** Retired USDA Forest Service researcher Jack Cohen's experimental work (starting in the late 1990s) established that most structures ignited in a wildfire are not ignited by the flame front itself but by wind-lofted embers landing on or against the structure, often minutes to hours after the fire front has passed nearby. His 1997 boreal-forest ignition experiments showed a structure 33 feet from an ignited forest block ignited on all exposed sides, while a structure 66 feet away was only charred on one side; this is the empirical basis for the roughly 100-foot "home ignition zone" concept and for the Firewise defensible-space program generally. The practical implication beyond the R327 element list: ember accumulation happens over hours, not seconds, so any deliberate design for ember removal (self-clearing gutters, non-flammable ground cover extending well past the mandatory 5-foot Zone 0, wind-direction-aware landscaping) matters more, over the multi-hour span during which embers accumulate, than the code's fixed element list credits it with.

**IBHS Wildfire Prepared Home standard (voluntary, insurance-linked, distinct from and generally stricter than R327).** The Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety runs a voluntary designation program, available in 14 states including Oregon, with two tiers: Essential (formerly "Base," focused on ember/wind-driven ignition) and Enhanced (formerly "Plus," adding radiant-heat and direct-flame-contact protection). It centers on the same three vulnerable zones as R327 (roof; vents and openings; defensible space) but layers an insurance-industry research basis, an annual maintenance self-review, and full recertification every three years. Because Oregon has no statewide wildfire-hardening mandate (per Envelope Waterproofing Thermal Performance And Radon's existing R327 section) and adoption is patchy by jurisdiction, a voluntary IBHS designation may be the more durable, insurance-relevant target for a parcel outside any adopting jurisdiction today that could be inside one after a future local vote, or that wants an insurance-market argument independent of local code adoption.

**Dedicated wildfire water: storage and delivery, beyond the interior/domestic system.** NFPA 1142 (Standard on Water Supplies for Suburban and Rural Firefighting) is the governing method for computing and confirming available firefighting water where no municipal hydrant system exists; it is distinct from NFPA 22 (tank construction). Reported reference points (unverified, multiple secondary sources, ranges vary by source): a residential-scale minimum around 2,000 gallons with no exposure hazards; a more commonly cited practical minimum of 2,500-5,000 gallons; NFPA 1142's own annex figure of roughly 1,000 gpm for a one- or two-family dwelling under 5,000 sq ft, with a 25 percent reduction allowed for an isolated home with no other structures within 30 feet, and a 50 percent reduction where an NFPA 13D interior sprinkler system is present. A dedicated exterior wildfire sprinkler/wetting kit needs its supply pump and tank sized with roughly 20 percent pressure margin above the kit's stated minimum so long hose runs to the far side of the roof still saturate. A pond, farm pond, or swimming pool can double as this reserve if a dry hydrant (a non-pressurized pipe intake a fire engine or a dedicated pump can draft from) is installed; reported reference figures for what a structure fire consumes (unverified): roughly 8,000+ gallons for a 2,000 sq ft home, 25,000+ gallons for a barn-scale structure. This overlaps the deep water-store discussion in section 6 below and should be sized as one combined reserve, not two separate tanks, where the site allows it.

**Structure wrap as a last-resort, short-duration measure.** Aluminized structure wrap (aluminum-faced fire-resistant fabric, reflecting more than 95 percent of radiant heat per manufacturer claims) is deployed ahead of an advancing fire front to block ember entry at vents/gutters/eaves and reflect radiant heat; documented residential survivals exist (a foil-wrapped Tahoe-area home reported to have survived the Caldor Fire while surrounding structures burned). Its protection window is short, reported at roughly 5-10 minutes of direct fire-front exposure, and it needs several people and several hours to install a typical structure, so it is a defend-in-place emergency measure, not a passive design feature; relevant to this project mainly as a stored-supply-and-drill question (does the household keep wrap, staples, and a trained procedure on hand) rather than a construction detail. For a concrete/masonry shell already largely ember- and flame-resistant at the wall, wrap effort concentrates on vents, eaves, window glazing, and any exposed wood trim or decking.

**Ember-resistant vent testing, one layer deeper than the code citation.** ASTM E2886/E2886M is the test method (an ember-intrusion test and a flame-intrusion/integrity test, with a maximum unexposed-side temperature limit reported around 662°F/350°C) that vent products are tested against to earn the "1/16 to 1/8 inch mesh or listed ember-resistant" compliance path already cited in Envelope Waterproofing Thermal Performance And Radon's R327 section. Product-level detail (which listed vent products exist, their price premium over ordinary vents, and their airflow performance versus a standard vent, relevant to the ventilation system's intake design) is the open, buildable-detail layer beyond the code citation.

**Interior fire sprinklers as a wildfire-adjacent, code-and-insurance-relevant layer.** NFPA 13D residential sprinkler systems are primarily a life-safety measure against interior fires (cooking, electrical, chimney), but as noted above they also reduce the NFPA 1142 exterior water-supply requirement by half where installed, and some jurisdictions or insurers treat them as a wildfire-adjacent mitigation credit. Cost, water-supply/pressure-tank interaction, and freeze-protection in an unheated space are the open questions.

### 4. Storm (Wind/Snow/Ice) And Multi-Week Grid-Down, Water-Out, Road-Out Survivability

**Passive survivability as a named design criterion.** The term describes a building's ability to maintain safe, livable interior conditions for occupants when external services (grid power, heating fuel, municipal water) are interrupted for an extended period, achieved through the building envelope and thermal mass rather than through backup equipment. Key levers reported across multiple sources: high insulation levels, high-performance low-air-leakage envelope construction, thermal mass that stores and slowly releases heat, and passive solar orientation. One reported municipal-level performance target (unverified, and likely aimed at much lighter, less thermally massive construction than this project's concrete/masonry shell): maintaining conditions under roughly 86°F for up to 72 hours of outage via dynamic thermal simulation. HVAC Ventilation Humidity And Air Quality's existing Outage Behavior And Energy Independence section already states the core mechanism for this specific house (concrete mass plus a tight envelope means indoor temperature decays over days, not hours, and the continuous mechanical loads that must be kept alive are small, on the order of a light fixture's draw); the open research space is the multi-week extension of that thinking layered with simultaneous water-out and road-out conditions, not just power-out.

**Rural outage-duration reality, not urban-outage duration.** Multiple sources converge on the same pattern: rural customers can wait five to fourteen times longer than urban customers for power restoration after a major event, because rural feeders serve fewer homes per mile of line, making each repair less cost-effective for a utility to prioritize first. Reported real-world examples: five-to-seven-day outages common in heavily wooded rural areas after major ice storms; rural Texas counties reporting six-day outages during a 2023 ice event; major hurricanes, ice storms, and derechos producing outages lasting one to four weeks across wide areas. A frequently cited practical account describes the qualitative texture of a long outage: by around day five, stored water runs low, generator noise creates conflict with neighbors, and cumulative exhaustion affects the whole household, independent of any single system failing outright. This "day five" framing is a useful design target: what does the house need to have already solved, passively or with minimal fuel/battery draw, by the time active-management fatigue sets in.

**Winter Storm Uri (Texas, February 2021) as a documented, extreme case study.** More than 4.5 million households lost power at the peak of the event; the reported death toll approaches 250 and economic damage estimates run toward $100 billion. The documented, transferable lesson is at the building-envelope level: the gap in "hours of safety" between an efficient, well-sealed building and an inefficient one was, in the analysis of multiple post-event reviews, the difference between survivable and fatal indoor conditions during the multi-day outage, independent of any single mechanical system. A second documented lesson: PEX piping's ability to expand under freeze pressure without bursting (unlike rigid copper) was cited as a specific material choice that prevented catastrophic water damage in some retrofitted homes; this project's plumbing chapters already lean toward PEX, and the disaster-specific angle (freeze-burst prevention during a multi-day power-and-heat-loss event) is a distinct benefit from routine freeze protection, not yet stated explicitly in that chapter.

**Backup heat, beyond "a wood stove" as currently stated.** HVAC Ventilation Humidity And Air Quality's combustion-appliance section already frames a certified wood stove as the non-electric outage heat source. Two additional non-electric or low-electric thermal-mass heating options exist and were not evaluated in that chapter: a masonry heater (a heavy masonry body, commonly 500+ kg, that is loaded and burned hot and fast once or twice a day, storing the heat and radiating it slowly over 12-24 hours; reported cost range $10,000-$30,000, a serious capital item) and a rocket mass heater (a high-efficiency small-diameter-wood burner that routes exhaust gas through a thermal mass, commonly a cob bench, before venting, reported cost range $500-$2,000, reported fuel-consumption reduction of 80-90 percent versus a conventional stove per anecdotal off-grid-community sources). Both pair naturally with this project's existing thermal-mass philosophy. A real comparison against the single wood-stove default, on capital cost, footprint, code/permitting status in Oregon (a masonry heater has its own chimney and clearance rules distinct from a factory-built stove), and multi-week fuel-supply logistics (small-diameter branch wood for a rocket mass heater is easier to self-supply on a rural lot than seasoned cordwood), is an open question for both.

**Generator interlock versus transfer switch, the code layer.** NEC Article 702 governs optional standby systems generally; 702.5 requires a transfer switch or equivalent means to prevent backfeed into the utility line. A listed interlock kit (a mechanical device preventing the main breaker and a generator backfeed breaker from being on simultaneously) is a permitted, lower-cost alternative to a dedicated automatic transfer switch, but must be listed for the exact panel model, and the generator breaker commonly must sit in a specific panel position for the interlock to function; some jurisdictions' AHJs require a dedicated transfer switch regardless of what the NEC permits. This is a code-and-cost layer missing from the current Solar Battery And Energy Independence skeleton chapter (it currently has no content); draft E's solar/battery section (E-8) is focused on sizing and architecture and does not state this code-compliance detail either.

**Long-duration fuel storage.** Gasoline degrades in roughly 3-6 months without treatment; a quality stabilizer (reported dosage around 1 oz per 2.5 gallons) is reported to extend usable shelf life to 12 months or more; diesel-specific stabilizers exist as a separate product class. This is a housekeeping/consumable-rotation question as much as a design question: does the mechanical-room or a dedicated fuel-storage structure provide safe, code-compliant, rotated storage for a multi-week reserve of generator fuel, distinct from and in addition to any propane tank already sized for cooking/backup heat.

**Road-out access.** A rural property commonly depends on a single driveway crossing a drainage ditch or seasonal watercourse via a culvert. Reported failure modes: undersized culverts clog and fail during heavy flow; erosion at the culvert inlet/outlet (scour) undermines the roadbed; a single washout event can block the only access route for vehicles, emergency services, and deliveries for days. This is a site/civil design question (culvert sizing, a second/alternate access easement or path, post-storm inspection routine) that belongs with Land Site And Feasibility and Soil Ground Prep Drainage And Foundation Risk, but the disaster-resilience framing (this is the access path emergency medical transport and a supply resupply must use after a regional event, not just a routine driveway) is new.

**Snow and wind design beyond code.** A continuous load path (roof-to-wall-to-foundation, already the subject of Structural System And Concrete Design's hold-point and connection material) is the general principle behind hurricane-tie/strap hardware; reported product-level uplift capacities exceed 1,500 lb per connector for some systems, well above minimum code engagement in most of Oregon's wind zones, which is itself a research question (does this project's wind exposure ever approach hardware-limited territory, or is the concrete/masonry wall system so far past minimum that only the roof-to-wall connection at a wood-truss roof, on the prescriptive path, is the governing wind-uplift risk).

### 5. Integrated Safe Room / Storm-Or-Seismic Shelter Cast Into The Concrete

**FEMA P-320 (Taking Shelter From The Storm, prescriptive residential safe room plans).** Provides pre-engineered concrete and other assembly options sized for a family, designed to ICC 500 criteria. Reported cost figures (2021 FEMA source, unverified/dated): an 8x8 ft safe room in new construction costs roughly $9,400-$13,100 including materials, labor, and contingency, but excluding the door; a 14x14 ft room costs roughly $18,900-$25,500. A separately sourced, lower figure describes a poured-concrete safe room built from the FEMA plans starting around $6,000-$10,000 installed for a family of four (source and year unclear, likely inconsistent methodology versus the FEMA figure, needs reconciliation). Building the same room into an existing (already-built) house costs roughly 20 percent more than building it during new construction, which is the strongest single argument for planning this into the design now rather than deferring the decision. A tested, labeled residential safe-room door assembly alone runs roughly $2,500-$3,200 (2021 figure). FEMA has periodically offered mitigation grants covering a significant share of safe-room construction cost in tornado-prone regions; whether any Oregon-relevant program exists (seismic-safe-room grants, as opposed to tornado-target programs) is an open question.

**FEMA P-361 and ICC 500.** ICC 500 is the enforceable consensus standard (referenced in the IBC/IRC since 2009) for storm shelter design and construction; FEMA P-361 is the non-mandatory best-practice guideline layered on top of it, intended to deliver "near-absolute protection" rather than the ICC 500 code-minimum. Both set requirements for wind load, windborne-debris impact (both standards reportedly test to withstand winds exceeding 250 mph and a wind-driven debris impact, commonly described as a 15 lb 2x4 missile at high speed), anchoring, ventilation, and communication access for occupants. Since this project's shell is already reinforced concrete/CMU, the marginal cost of building one room to full ICC 500/FEMA P-361 standard (thicker walls or added reinforcement in one room only, a rated door, and independent anchorage) should be far below the FEMA P-320 figures above, which assume a wood-framed host house needing an entirely separate structural system for the safe room.

**Siting rules, applicable to seismic use as well as storm use.** FEMA guidance recommends an interior room with no exterior walls (a windowless bathroom or closet is the commonly cited default), located away from masonry chimneys, large trees, and power poles, with a single door, positioned so the total travel distance from any point in the house to the safe room does not exceed roughly 150 feet. A garage-floor location is an accepted alternative to an interior room. A below-grade/basement location is acceptable only if it cannot flood and has no exterior doors or large windows (a walk-out basement disqualifies itself). For this project, a below-grade or ground-floor interior mechanical-adjacent room is the most natural fit, and it should be evaluated jointly against seismic performance (short travel distance during active shaking, not just storm/tornado warning lead time) as well as wind/tornado criteria, since Oregon's dominant hazard is seismic, not tornadic.

**Multi-use design.** A dedicated single-purpose safe room is a cost the household pays for a rare event. Combining the safe-room structural specification with a room that earns its keep daily (a windowless bathroom, a wine/root cellar, a home-office closet, the mechanical room itself if it can be cleared of hazards during an event) removes the "wasted space" objection and is explicitly suggested in FEMA's own guidance (bathrooms are called out because they already have water and a toilet). This project's cellar/pantry deep-store space (section 6 below) and its safe room are natural candidates to be the same room or adjacent rooms sharing one hardened wall set.

**Monolithic dome as a whole-house alternative achieving inherent shelter-grade performance.** A sprayed-concrete-and-rebar dome shell (Airform-and-shotcrete construction, a distinct system from this project's default reinforced CMU/cast-in-place approach) is marketed as meeting FEMA's "near-absolute protection" bar for the entire house, not just one room, because its shape sheds wind load (a hurricane is reported to barely register as a measurable pressure on a dome shape) and its monolithic shell has no discrete panel-to-panel connections to fail. Documented survivals include a Florida Keys dome home reported to have withstood two hurricanes (Irma 2017, Ian 2022) and another reported to have survived three major Gulf hurricanes. This is a different building system than this project's committed wall approach, but it is a legitimate, real-world worldwide data point for "what does a whole-house-as-shelter concrete building look like," and the dome's connection-free, continuous-shell logic is a design principle applicable even where the project keeps its rectilinear CMU/concrete wall system.

### 6. Deliberate Deep Stores

**Water.** Two industry-standard framings for sizing: routine daily-use planning (commonly cited at 50-100 gallons per person per day for drinking, cooking, cleaning, and bathing, roughly 200-400 gal/day for a family of four) versus emergency-conservation planning (FEMA's baseline is 1 gallon per person per day for drinking and sanitation; other conservation-mode figures run 20-30 gal/person/day for drinking, cooking, and minimal hygiene combined). A frequently repeated two-week emergency planning figure is roughly 28 gallons per person (2 gal/day x 14 days) for drinking and basic sanitation alone, well below what routine daily use requires, which is the gap a dedicated cistern needs to bridge if the household is to sustain routine consumption, not bare-survival rations, through a multi-week event. Roof-catchment math: roughly 620 gallons collected per 1,000 sq ft of roof per inch of rainfall; a modest Oregon roof in a wet region can theoretically harvest well into five figures of gallons annually, though usable capture depends on cistern size, first-flush diversion, and how much of that falls outside the dry summer months when demand peaks. Buried cisterns (500-2,000 gal common residential range cited, up to 10,000+ gal for farm/commercial scale) avoid freezing and hold a stable cool temperature that resists algae growth, which matters for a project already committed to burying significant infrastructure. One gap in current planning: a combined reserve, sized as one number for domestic autonomy, wildfire suppression (NFPA 1142, section 3 above), and multi-week grid-down/well-down resilience together, rather than three separate calculations that might undersize the true simultaneous-failure case (the well pump is down with the grid, and the property needs firefighting water, in the same event).

**Water treatment redundancy.** A gravity-fed filter (no electricity required; contaminated water poured into an upper chamber gravity-flows through a filter media into a lower clean chamber) is the standard off-grid/emergency answer for biological contamination, reported to meet EPA removal-rate standards for bacteria and viruses in product literature; activated carbon alone is explicitly reported as inadequate against microorganisms and needs pairing with a biological barrier. Shock chlorination (a concentrated bleach dose, reported at roughly 3 pints of unscented 5.25-8.25 percent sodium hypochlorite bleach per 100 gallons of well water, circulated through the whole plumbing system, held 12-24 hours, then flushed) is the standard well-disinfection response after any flood, surface-water intrusion, positive bacteria test, or well/pump service work; a well that was physically submerged needs the casing scrubbed above the waterline before chlorination and, per extension-service guidance, is often better handled by a licensed well driller with professional equipment than attempted solo. A follow-up bacteria test 10-14 days after flushing confirms success; shock chlorination does not address chemical, heavy-metal, or pesticide contamination, only biological.

**Power and fuel.** Draft E section E-8 already states off-grid-versus-hybrid sizing, battery chemistry/cold-weather derating, generator auto-start and fuel-type tradeoffs, and interconnection/permission-to-operate timing, in real depth; this file does not repeat that. The multi-week deep-store angle E-8 does not fully develop: propane tank sizing and NFPA 58 setback rules for a tank large enough to run a whole-house backup generator plus cooking plus backup water heating for weeks rather than days (reported standard setbacks: 10 ft from buildings/property lines for tanks up to 500 gal water capacity, 25 ft for 501-2,000 gal tanks; septic-system setbacks are a separate, county-health-department-enforced requirement, commonly 10-25 ft; NFPA 58 contains no such setback); stabilized long-duration storage of gasoline and diesel as a supplementary or bridge fuel; and the NEC 702 interlock/transfer-switch code layer (section 4 above) that governs how any of this stored fuel capacity gets connected to the house safely and legally.

**Food.** A root cellar integrated into a concrete/masonry foundation or basement is a natural fit for this project's already-committed thermal mass: the target condition is roughly 32-40°F and 85-95 percent relative humidity for most root crops, and a larger cellar volume provides more thermal buffering against both winter freeze and spring warming, which is a direct function of the concrete mass already being built for structural reasons. A packed-earth or gravel floor holds humidity better than a sealed concrete floor, which argues for a deliberately unsealed floor zone within an otherwise concrete structure, a detail not yet reconciled with this project's default all-concrete philosophy. Passive stack-effect ventilation (a low intake and a high exhaust duct to the exterior) regulates both temperature and humidity without power. Separately, long-term shelf-stable pantry storage (canning, dry goods, freeze-dried) is a quantifiable, calculator-driven planning exercise (multiple public calculators exist, commonly built on CDC and LDS/Church-published storage guidance, with per-person-per-time-period tables for grains, canned/dried protein, fats, legumes, dairy, sugar, produce, and water) that has an architectural consequence: a dedicated, climate-appropriate pantry room sized and shelved for a specific number of weeks or months of storage, not an afterthought closet.

**Medical.** Two distinct planning layers: an immediate trauma-response kit (bleeding control is the reported first priority over airway/wound-care in current "Stop the Bleed"-influenced kit design: at least one windlass tourniquet, hemostatic gauze for junctional wounds tourniquets cannot reach, pressure dressings, trauma shears, gloves), and a multi-week isolation-planning layer (prescription medication stockpiling realities and limits, over-the-counter supply depth, and basic diagnostic tools) that the general disaster-preparedness literature documents in far less depth than the trauma-kit layer. For a rural property distant from emergency response, the "you are the help until help arrives" framing used in Oregon's own 2 Weeks Ready program is directly applicable, and generic national kit-list content states it far less thoroughly than the trauma-kit material does.

**Oregon's own baseline: 2 Weeks Ready.** Oregon Department of Emergency Management's Be 2 Weeks Ready program is the state's own stated minimum target (an emergency plan plus supplies for every household member for at least two weeks), with a food plan, a water plan, and basic injury/illness self-treatment, built on the "you are the help until help arrives" premise. This is a useful baseline to explicitly exceed: the state's own floor is two weeks, and this project's stated goal (a multi-week, not multi-day, grid/water/road-out survivability) should be stated as a deliberate multiple of the state's own public target, with the reasoning stated (rural location, single road access, longer realistic restoration time than an urban address).

### 7. Concrete And Masonry's Disaster Advantages And Remaining Weak Links

**Advantages, gathered across hazard types.** Non-combustibility is the headline wildfire and general-fire advantage; concrete and masonry do not provide fuel to a fire the way wood-framed construction does, and post-fire the shell is often reparable rather than a total loss, unlike a wood structure that may need to be rebuilt from the foundation up. Documented hurricane performance: the "Sand Palace" of Mexico Beach, Florida, an ICF (insulated concrete form) home, survived Hurricane Michael (2018, a Category 5 storm at landfall) largely intact (reported damage limited to minor water intrusion and a cracked window) while neighboring conventional homes were leveled; ICF and concrete assemblies are reported in manufacturer and industry sources to test successfully against large wind-borne debris impact (a 2x4 shot at high speed shattering on impact rather than penetrating). Post-flood, concrete does not rot, does not feed mold the way wet wood framing and drywall do, and can be pressure-washed and disinfected rather than demolished, which is reported to shorten and reduce the cost of flood recovery relative to wood-framed construction (a claim found mainly in manufacturer and industry material; independent flood-recovery case studies would confirm or qualify it). Non-combustible, disaster-resistant construction is the basis for real insurance discounts in documented programs (FORTIFIED Home's wind/hail discounts, reported as high as 30-50 percent off the wind portion of premium in some states/insurers, and the separate Wildfire Prepared Home designation), which converts the disaster-resistance argument into an ongoing, quantifiable annual cost saving, not just a one-time avoided-loss argument.

**Weak links, gathered across hazard types.** Seismically, mass without ductile detailing is a liability, not an asset: the Chile evidence above shows structurally similar confined-masonry buildings suffering catastrophically different outcomes based on detailing choices, and unreinforced or poorly detailed masonry is generically one of the worst-performing building types worldwide in earthquakes because higher mass means higher inertial (F=ma) seismic demand on the structure, and masonry is inherently brittle (fails suddenly, without warning deformation) unless the reinforcement and confinement are specifically detailed to provide ductility. This is precisely why Structural System And Concrete Design already treats SDC D0-D2 above-grade concrete/masonry as an engineered-path near-default for western Oregon rather than leaving it prescriptive; the research angle for the new chapter is making that seismic-liability-without-detailing point explicit and connecting it forward to the beyond-code ductility material in section 1. In fire, concrete's own weak point is spalling: dense, low-permeability, high-strength concrete can fail explosively in the 250-400°C range because trapped pore pressure and moisture cannot escape fast enough as the surface heats, which can expose reinforcement and abruptly reduce a structural member's effective cross-section; this is a high-strength/high-performance-concrete-specific risk more than an ordinary residential-strength-concrete risk, but it is a real, counter-intuitive weak link in an otherwise fire-resistant material: concrete's general fire resistance does not mean concrete is immune to fire-induced structural failure. Cost premium, thermal bridging at any structural connection or tie (already stated in Structural System And Concrete Design and Envelope Waterproofing Thermal Performance And Radon), and the practical impossibility of adding a penetration, an embed, or a new opening once a wall is poured and cured (the flip side of the project's own future-proofing/embed-planning discipline) round out the list: concrete's disaster resilience is bought at the price of needing every structural and service decision correct before the pour, because correcting it after is demolition, not remodeling.

### 8. Cross-Cutting: Communications, Post-Disaster Assessment, And The Financial Layer

**Communications when cellular and internet are down.** Amateur (ham) radio, requiring an FCC license obtained by exam, can reach dozens of miles through local repeaters and, under the right conditions, far beyond; it is the traditional backbone of volunteer emergency communication during regional disasters when commercial networks fail. Satellite messengers and personal locator beacons (two-way text and SOS over satellite, no cellular signal needed) are a lower-training-threshold complementary layer. For a rural, single-road-access property, a communications plan is a distinct deliverable from the physical stores discussed in section 6, and it should be sized against the same "multi-week, simultaneous grid/water/road-out" scenario rather than a single-system outage.

**Post-disaster structural self-assessment.** Stated under section 1 (ATC-20) above; repeated here because it is also the natural bridge to Documentation, Maintenance, And Future Upgrades, which is where a homeowner's own pre-event structural documentation (as-built drawings, the engineer of record's design assumptions, photographs of reinforcement before concealment, already required by this project's existing hold-point and pre-cover records) becomes the resource a homeowner or a volunteer engineer needs to make a faster, better-informed green/yellow/red self-assessment after a Cascadia-scale event than a stranger walking in cold.

**The financial/insurance layer.** Earthquake insurance in Oregon is a separate, percentage-deductible policy most homeowners do not carry (roughly 20 percent uptake per the state's own consumer guidance); FORTIFIED Home and Wildfire Prepared Home are voluntary, third-party-verified construction standards tied to real, documented insurance premium discounts in the states where insurers offer them; and Oregon's Be 2 Weeks Ready program is the state's own explicit floor for household self-sufficiency. None of this is currently stated anywhere in the manuscript's existing chapters; Personal Project Finance (budget/financing/cash flow) and Legal Permits And Owner-Builder Rules are the two most likely homes for the insurance-market and disclosure-law material, with the physical construction and stores material landing in the new Resilience And Disaster Independence chapter.
