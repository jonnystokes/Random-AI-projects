## Part 1: Gathered Understanding

### 1. How ORSC R104.11 Actually Works: The Administrative Mechanics

ORSC R104.11 (Alternative materials, design and methods of construction and equipment) does not prevent installation of any material or method not specifically prescribed by the code, provided the alternative is approved. The approval standard: the building official finds the proposal satisfactory, complying with the code's intent, and at least the equivalent of what the code prescribes in quality, strength, effectiveness, fire resistance, durability, and safety (source: IRC/ORSC R104.11 text via codes.iccsafe.org and an Oregon-adopted chapter 1 PDF; this base text is already anchored in the existing owner-fabrication research for CMU, cross-reference the owner-made-blocks and testing chapters rather than re-deriving the testing science here).

R104.11.1 (testing authority): where evidence of compliance is insufficient, the building official may require tests at no expense to the municipality. Test methods are as specified in the code or by other recognized test standards. Where no recognized method exists, the building official approves the testing procedure. Tests must be performed by an approved agency.

Administrative facts not yet covered by the existing R104.11 research, which focused on the CMU and ASTM testing-science angle:

- The base IRC text (outside Oregon) is reported by a secondary source to require a written response with stated reasons when the official denies an alternative. Oregon's own adopted text needs a direct, primary check for whether this requirement carries over unchanged.
- The alternative-method approval is a building-official-level decision made during plan review. Nothing in R104.11 itself requires a public hearing or advance notice; it is an administrative determination between the applicant and the official.
- At least one Oregon county (Clackamas) maintains a standing printed application form for an "alternate means and methods" request, confirmed for at least the plumbing code (OPSC). Whether a parallel structural or building-code version exists there or elsewhere was not confirmed in this pass. This is a real, useful lead: an owner-builder can ask a department directly whether a written alternate-methods intake form already exists, rather than guessing at what a letter submission should contain.
- The escalation path when a local official will not move is not a single local appeal. Oregon layers three separate statewide tools on top of R104.11 (see section 3 below), a materially richer structure than a state with only a local appeals board as the fallback.

### 2. Approved Agencies, Testing Authority, And What Counts As Evidence

There is no public, statewide list of "approved agencies." Acceptance is the local official's judgment on a per-project basis. Accreditation bodies (A2LA, IAS) exist and make a lab easier to accept, but nothing in the ORSC makes accreditation legally mandatory for residential work. ORSC R104.4 lets the official accept written, certified reports of inspection from approved agencies or individuals. That is the legal hook for accepting a university lab, a community-college testing program, or a private materials lab's letter without the official personally witnessing production (already noted in the existing owner-fabrication research). Because "approved agency" status is granted informally per project rather than through a public registry, establishing a lab or a witnessing arrangement's credibility at the pre-application stage is the practical work of "getting approved," done through conversation and documentation rather than through a certificate the owner already holds.

### 3. Oregon's Three Statewide Escalation Tools (OAR 918-008)

Three distinct, separately defined tools exist under OAR 918-008, confirmed by direct rule-text fetch:

- **Site-Specific Interpretation** (OAR 918-008-0090, defined in -0080): a division-issued interpretation of a specialty code provision for use by a municipality, for a single project only. Confirmed by direct fetch: only a building official may request one. A property owner cannot petition for this directly; the owner would need to ask the official to request it.
- **Statewide Code Interpretation** (OAR 918-008-0085, defined in -0080): a division-issued binding interpretation of a specialty code provision that applies in every jurisdiction. The petitioner definition found in -0080 does not obviously restrict who may request one. Required content: a brief description of the facts and circumstances, and the specialty code section at issue. Quarterly, the division communicates its actions to the relevant advisory board.
- **Alternate Method Ruling** (OAR 918-008-0095, defined in -0080): "a request to rule on the acceptability of new materials, designs, or innovative methods of construction not covered by the state building code." The petitioner definition explicitly includes any person residing in, doing business in, or owning property in Oregon, plus building officials. Required content, confirmed by direct fetch: information on the material, design, or method; the relevant specialty code section; and a brief description of the technical and scientific facts and circumstances supporting the request. The relevant advisory board reviews and recommends; the division makes the final decision and distributes it consistent with ORS 455.060. A named real example surfaced in this pass: "Statewide Alternate Method No. 26-01" (continued reference to the 2022 OSSC for nonprescriptive design), confirming the numbering convention exists and rulings are publicly named, though the full public list or database was not successfully retrieved in this pass.
- **Reconsideration** (OAR 918-008-0115) is available when a site-specific or statewide interpretation request is denied.
- Submission channel found for the Alternate Method Ruling Application: mail to P.O. Box 14470, Salem, OR 97309-0404, or email to bcd.ptsptech@oregon.gov.

Practical read: the Alternate Method Ruling is the tool an owner-builder can petition directly and that becomes usable statewide, a real lever. Winning one county's approval of owner-made block and then converting it into a statewide ruling means every other Oregon jurisdiction has to honor it, without refighting the technical argument county by county. The site-specific interpretation is a tool the owner asks the official to invoke, not a direct owner petition. The statewide code interpretation fits a dispute about a provision's meaning more than a genuinely novel material.

### 4. Appeals: Local Board And State Specialty Code Chief

OAR 918-008-0120 (State Building Code Appeal Process), confirmed by direct fetch:

- A person aggrieved by the building official's decision chooses one of two routes at the outset: the local jurisdiction's appeals board, or the state specialty code chief. The rule states plainly that the choice cannot change once made.
- A state-level appeal requires: appellant, jurisdiction, and involved-party contact information; the specific code citations at issue; a written description of the appeal (diagrams allowed); copies of any prior written interpretations or decisions; the appellant's justification for reversal; the status and date of any stop-work order; and anything else the chief requests. A $20 filing fee applies.
- Timeline: file within 30 calendar days of the building official's decision. The chief targets 14 days for a decision, with a 30-day maximum, extendable by the BCD Administrator for complex issues.
- A decision from either route can be further appealed to the relevant advisory board within 30 days, with an additional $20 fee.

Local board example (Portland, the only jurisdiction examined in detail in this pass): the Building Code Board of Appeal has three members and three alternates, appointed by the mayor, qualified by experience and training, with at least one builder and one architect. It hears appeals beyond a first-tier "Administrative Appeals Board" specific to Portland, meaning at least one Oregon city runs two local tiers before any state-level appeal. The board can affirm, annul, or modify the official's action where substantially equivalent safety is shown, generally conforming to national fire-prevention, fire-safety, and construction-safety standards. It meets as appeals arrive, not on a fixed schedule.

One secondary source suggests ORSC R112.4 (Administration) may not be adopted by Oregon BCD as part of the state code; this needs direct primary-text confirmation rather than being carried into the manuscript as stated.

### 5. The Advisory Boards Behind The Rulings

The Building Codes Structures Board assists the DCBS director in administering the structural, prefabricated-structures, and certain energy programs, plus the accessibility program under ORS 447.210-447.280. Two different member counts turned up in this pass (nine in one source citing ORS 455.132/455.144, eleven in another citing the board's own page), a direct conflict flagged for the research phase to resolve against primary statute text. Members serve four-year terms, appointed by the governor with Senate confirmation, broadly representative of the industries and professions in building construction and energy conservation, with specific named seats including a general contractor specializing in buildings over three stories, a heavy-industry contractor, and a representative recommended by the Oregon Disabilities Commission. Which board specifically reviews an Alternate Method Ruling touching residential structural masonry or concrete was not confirmed directly in this pass; Oregon appears to run separate boards for different code areas (structures, and likely mechanical and electrical/plumbing), and the mapping from code area to board needs primary confirmation.

### 6. The Local Building Department Structure: Rural, State-Run, And Contracted

Oregon does not staff a single statewide inspector corps for every county. Jurisdictions run their own programs, or BCD administers directly in counties without one (an already-established fact from the existing inspection chapter, tied to ORSC R109.1 and ORS 455.148/455.150). BCD publishes a Local Building Department Directory and a jurisdictions/administration page, but the actual county-by-county list (which counties are BCD-direct, which are locally run, which are contracted to a private firm) could not be extracted through the fetch tool in this pass; this is a genuine, well-defined research gap for the next phase.

A real contracting pattern is confirmed: at least one small Oregon city, Aurora in Marion County, contracts its entire building, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical plan-review and inspection function to a private firm, Northwest Code Professionals (NWCP), which serves roughly 50 Oregon public jurisdictions plus multiple tribal nations. NWCP separately markets pre-design code-consulting services to architects and developers. This is a materially different AHJ relationship than an in-house county department: the reviewer and inspector work for a contractor, not a county civil-service department, and a firm that already markets itself as a code consultant may be a more natural first conversation partner for an unconventional design than a traditional department. SAFEbuilt is a national community-development-services contractor offering comparable services; no confirmed current Oregon client was found in this pass.

County reputation, gathered from owner-builder forum and community accounts, all unverified, dated, and needing reconfirmation before any of it is treated as durable fact:

- Klamath County: reported to have approved multiple straw-bale houses and at least one experimental cob house before 2009, requiring a licensed engineer's stamp for non-standard designs. Described as "less stringent," with structures under roughly 150 to 200 square feet reported permit-exempt.
- Douglas County: reported, as of roughly 2012, as declining to offer any permit path for cob at that time.
- Coos County: reported as open to alternative building, and home to the Cob Cottage Company in Coquille, a natural-building school active since the late 1980s, meaning unusual local familiarity with earthen and cob construction among area builders and possibly officials.
- Deschutes County: reported as expensive (land and permit costs) but already home to strawbale construction and at least one earthship; one strawbale project reportedly had to be built as a post-and-beam structure with straw infill rather than as a load-bearing bale wall.
- Clackamas County: publishes a standing alternate-means-and-methods application form, confirmed for at least the plumbing code.
- Lane County: administers the state code as adopted, sits in Seismic Design Category D1; no alternative-materials track record specific to Lane County surfaced in this pass.

### 7. Pre-Application And Early-Assistance Practice

The general pattern across Oregon jurisdictions: a pre-application or early-assistance meeting lets building, planning, engineering, fire, and utility staff review a proposal informally before a formal application, generating written comments the applicant folds into the eventual submittal. Portland's specific program includes free 15-minute question appointments and formal early-assistance meetings priced from a land-use fee schedule; one specific fee point found is $77.50 for a 30-minute "Time Certain Appointment," a narrow service, not necessarily the general pre-application fee (the general fee schedule itself needs a direct fetch in a later pass). The existing second-wave research already flags "the CMU owner-manufacture testing package (pre-application meeting)" as an unresolved AHJ item; this draft's job is to deepen how that meeting should be prepared for and used, not merely note that it exists.

Real advice surfaced from greenhomebuilding.com and permies.com: bring complete, professionally drawn plans; get a design professional's stamp before or immediately after the pre-application meeting if the material is genuinely novel; approach the official as a technical partner rather than an adversary; ask directly whether the jurisdiction has handled a comparable material before and whether a written alternate-methods form exists; and in at least one account, an in-person visit got a better reception than a phone call.

### 8. Phased Permits And Deferred Submittals

ORS 455.467 sets statutory plan-review timelines for simple low-rise residential dwellings, confirmed by direct fetch: 10 business days in jurisdictions under 300,000 population, 15 business days at or above that population, measured from a complete application. The clock does not apply where outside-agency approval is needed, the structure is complex enough to need added review, or the BCD Director authorizes an alternate schedule. A jurisdiction with a repeated pattern of missing the deadline is treated as violating ORS 455.160. Whether "complex enough to need added review" would routinely swallow an alternative-materials submission is an open, practical question.

The same statute's phased-permit language is written explicitly for commercial construction: municipalities must allow deferred plan submittal by phase, and issue a permit for each phase as it is approved, for a commercial construction project. No equivalent statewide statutory phased-permit right for single-family residential construction surfaced in this pass. Any "foundation-only" or "early start" residential permit is local policy, not a state-guaranteed right, and needs jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction confirmation.

Deferred submittal is a separate, better-documented mechanism. Portland's structural-engineering deferred-submittal guidelines describe the pattern: the main permit is approved first with specific items excluded by agreement (commonly trusses, specialty stairs, guardrail systems); the registered design professional in responsible charge lists the deferred items on the main permit set; the deferred item's own design professional seals its drawings and calculations; the architect or engineer of record reviews and stamps the submission as conforming; the item is submitted through an online portal; the jurisdiction reviews it for conformance with the already-approved permit; and construction of that specific element cannot proceed until its own approval issues. Wilsonville publishes a specific deferred-submittal agreement form, a real template worth examining directly. Every documented deferred-submittal example found in this pass (trusses, stairs, guardrails) already has an established prescriptive or standard design path; whether a jurisdiction would accept a genuinely novel owner-made-block wall system as a deferred item, rather than requiring it resolved before the main permit issues, is an open, county-specific question.

### 9. Special Inspection And Structural Observation: Mostly Absent From ORSC

Confirmed across two independent passes, including the existing owner-fabrication research: "special inspection" as a formal, defined program is an IBC Chapter 17 and Oregon Structural Specialty Code (OSSC) concept, not part of the ORSC (one- and two-family dwellings). Detached one- and two-family dwellings up to two stories are broadly exempted from IBC seismic special-inspection triggers even where OSSC might otherwise apply to a building type; this general finding needs a direct primary IBC 1704/1705 text check before it is stated as settled fact.

What the ORSC has instead, for an alternative-material or engineered-design residential project: R104.11.1's authority to require testing at the owner's expense, and R104.4's acceptance of certified third-party or approved-agency reports. A building official who wants ongoing quality assurance across a full production run of owner-made block has to attach that as a condition of the R104.11 approval itself (a required testing frequency, periodic lab reports, a site-visit schedule), because no named ORSC special-inspection chapter exists to invoke for this occupancy type.

Structural observation, a distinct concept (periodic visual site visits by the engineer of record confirming as-built work generally matches design intent, not a code-compliance verification), is likewise principally an IBC and commercial concept, triggered by high wind or seismic categories or at the building official's discretion under IBC 1704.6, and is not a standard ORSC requirement. Some individual jurisdictions add it by local amendment, or an engineer of record adds it voluntarily through their own contract terms regardless of any code trigger. For a genuinely novel structural system on a house, a paid, voluntary structural-observation arrangement with the engineer of record is a real, available risk-reduction tool even where the code does not require it, and does not appear anywhere in the existing chapters yet.

### 10. Recognized Standards And Precedent Codes For Earthen And Alternative Structural Materials

R104.11.1 lets the official rely on other recognized test standards, and R104.11 measures equivalence against quality, strength, effectiveness, fire resistance, durability, and safety. A submission for rammed earth or CSEB is strongest when it can point to an existing, named, recognized document rather than asking the official to evaluate the material from first principles. Real options surfaced in this pass:

- **ASTM E2392/E2392M, "Standard Guide for Design of Earthen Wall Building Systems."** Covers adobe, rammed earth, cob, and cast earth as structural and non-structural wall systems, and explicitly frames earthen construction as now open to rational structural design methods. A live ASTM standard (editions found referenced as E2392/E2392M-23 and -24). This is exactly the kind of recognized test standard R104.11.1 contemplates, and the existing owner-made-blocks research (draft A) does not appear to name it in its R104.11 section.
- **New Mexico Earthen Building Materials Code (14.7.4 NMAC).** Described by its own advocates as the first code-adopted rammed-earth code and the first code-adopted CEB code in the United States. Covers adobe, burned adobe, CEB, rammed earth, and terrón, and contains a wet-strength compression test protocol for rammed earth referencing ASTM D1633. A genuinely in-force state building code, not a guideline, which is stronger evidence than an industry guide when arguing equivalence, because it shows another US jurisdiction already treats the material as adequate for one- and two-family bearing walls under defined values.
- **New Zealand earth-building standards: NZS 4297 (engineering design), NZS 4298 (materials and construction), NZS 4299 (earth buildings not requiring specific engineering).** A three-part, seismically engineered earth-building code from a high-seismic country, covering adobe, pressed brick, rammed earth, and poured earth, with stated elastic seismic deflection limits and a height cap (one source states 6.5 meters from footing top to wall top). Because Oregon's own seismic design categories (D0 to D2 in western Oregon, per the existing code-map chapter) are the reason prescriptive concrete and masonry paths are unavailable, a genuinely seismic earth-building standard may be more persuasive than New Mexico's lower-hazard-region code for the structural argument, used alongside the New Mexico code's US regulatory precedent value. One source claimed free download availability; unconfirmed directly.
- **IRC/ORSC Appendix S (older editions) and Appendix AS (2023 ORSC), Strawbale Construction.** Oregon has already adopted a prescriptive strawbale appendix into its own residential code (reported adopted 2017, effective 2018, carried into the 2023 ORSC as Appendix AS). Two consequences worth surfacing: first, the appendix is optional, not mandatory unless specifically referenced in the adopting ordinance, so an owner must confirm with the specific county whether Appendix AS is locally adopted before assuming strawbale is prescriptively available at all, an exact parallel to the already-flagged local-adoption check for ORSC R327 (solar) in the existing research; second, Oregon's own code-development process has already shown willingness to formally adopt an alternative-material appendix through the standard ICC and state amendment cycle, a real precedent path, separate from and slower than R104.11, that natural-building advocates used to get strawbale and cob model-code appendices written and adopted nationally (named advocates surfaced: Martin Hammer, David Eisenberg of the Development Center for Appropriate Technology, John Fordice of the Cob Research Institute; the Northwest Eco Building Guild's "Code Innovations Database" is a named resource cataloging officially approved sustainable-building solutions).
- The structural masonry parallel already in the existing code-map chapter: ACI 332-20 and PCA 100 serve as the recognized-standard alternative to full ACI 318 engineering for prescriptive residential concrete walls in Oregon's higher seismic categories. No equivalent ACI or PCA document yet exists for owner-made or earthen block, exactly the gap ASTM E2392 and the New Mexico and New Zealand codes partially fill.

### 11. Real-World Oregon And Regional Precedent

- **Benton County, Oregon, rammed-earth house.** A named, real, completed owner-builder project (Dave Wills) reportedly required architectural drawings, engineer's drawings, and compression testing before construction, with PSE Consulting Engineers providing the structural engineering; reported as the first rammed-earth house in the Benton County area (Corvallis Advocate, 2017 story). The article itself could not be fetched in this pass (HTTP 403) and needs a re-fetch through a different route or a cached copy in a later research phase. This is a strong, specific, real lead worth a dedicated follow-up, including a possible direct contact with PSE Consulting Engineers.
- **Cob Cottage Company, Coquille, Coos County.** Founded by Ianto Evans and Linda Smiley, who moved into their own cob house in 1989 and formally founded the company in 1993 with Michael Smith. Developed the "Oregon Cob" method, emphasizing sand, clay, and straw ratios for thinner, stronger, more sculptable walls than the UK cob tradition. Runs apprenticeships that build a new structure most years, and is widely credited with a major share of cob's US revival. A genuine, physically located body of more than 35 years of Oregon-specific earthen-building experience, and a realistic direct-contact target for permitting history.
- **permies.com forum threads** (Cascadia and natural-building sub-forums) contain first-hand, dated, unverified accounts: a papercrete project reportedly was approved in one unnamed location after the official classified the material as adobe because of its clay content, a real reclassification tactic; one poster reported a successful approval attained simply by mentioning an intent to appeal if refused; a Washington State cob home reportedly got approved using a conventional wood-frame structure with cob as non-structural infill, a hybrid strategy (see section 13).
- General finding across every source in this pass: Oregon counties vary widely and unpredictably on alternative materials, no two accounts agree on which county is easiest, and every account is time-stamped, several a decade or more old. The only safe conclusion for the manuscript is to call and ask, in writing, for the specific parcel and specific material, not to publish a ranked list of friendly counties.
- **EarthDwell (Washington State) and Root Down Designs** are named companies doing rammed-earth work in the wet Pacific Northwest; neither was examined in this pass for permitting detail, only confirmed to exist and to be active in a climate comparable to western Oregon.

### 12. Insurance And Financing Reality

Multiple sources, most substantively greenhomebuilding.com, describe a real, recurring problem: homeowner-insurance underwriters default to conventional-material mental models, and naming an unfamiliar material can trigger an unwarranted risk reaction (strawbale read as a fire hazard is the most cited example) even when the actual assembly performs comparably to or better than conventional construction. Reported practical tactics: describe the assembly and its performance rather than leading with an unfamiliar material name; document safety features with photographs; note fire-department distance and hydrant proximity; obtain at least three quotes and negotiate with competing offers; expect a custom-drafted or manuscript policy rather than an off-the-shelf form; read policy language closely for exclusions. No specific Oregon-licensed carrier names willing to insure rammed-earth, CEB, or strawbale homes surfaced in this pass, a genuine gap for a later research phase.

Construction-lending questions (whether a lender treats an R104.11-approved but code-novel wall system differently at appraisal because of a thin comparable-sales set, or during draw-schedule inspections) were not explored in this pass at all. This overlaps the Personal Project Finance chapter and is flagged here rather than developed.

### 13. Creative-But-Real Approval Strategies Found In The Wild

Collected directly from the sources above, stated as strategies rather than guarantees:

- Build the relationship before the design is final. Attend a pre-application meeting specifically to ask whether the jurisdiction has handled a comparable alternative material, and whether a written alternate-materials application form already exists locally.
- Bring a licensed engineer's stamp proactively rather than waiting to be asked. Multiple independent sources across very different eras (the Klamath County straw-bale and cob accounts, the Benton County rammed-earth account, general natural-building advice) converge on the same finding: an engineer's stamp is the single most common condition officials attach, and obtaining one before the first meeting shortens the process more than any other step found.
- Cite an existing, named, recognized standard rather than asking the official to build an equivalence finding from nothing: ASTM E2392, the New Mexico Earthen Building Materials Code, or the New Zealand earth-building standards, layered under a local PE's stamped calculations that reference them.
- Consider a hybrid structural strategy, as a fallback or even as a first design: use conventional engineered concrete or reinforced masonry, already inside the prescriptive or engineered paths the code-map chapter documents, for the primary vertical load path and lateral system, and reserve owner-made block or rammed earth for non-structural or secondary applications (interior partitions, garden walls, low freestanding retaining walls below an engineering threshold, veneer). At least one real account, a Washington cob home using a stick-frame structure with cob infill, used exactly this pattern to get approved. This reduces the R104.11 burden to a non-structural-material submission, a materially lower bar under the same code section, while still using the owner's block-making skill and reducing material cost elsewhere.
- Stage the precedent. Build a small, genuinely permit-exempt structure first (Oregon's roughly 200-square-foot, 10-foot-wall non-habitable accessory-structure exemption, a real, low-stakes way to place a first batch of owner-made block into a real wall with no permit required at all) to work out the mix, the press, and the curing process, and to have a physical, inspectable example ready for the pre-application meeting on the house itself.
- Use the agricultural-building exemption (ORS 455.315) where the property carries farm or forest zoning. An agricultural building, such as a shop or equipment and crop storage structure, is exempt from the structural permit entirely, though not from electrical, mechanical, plumbing, or septic permits, and not from the local planning department's determination of whether the building qualifies as agricultural. Housing the block press, the mixer, and the curing yard inside a structurally exempt farm building is a real way to get the production operation running under a roof before the house's own structural approval is resolved, and the same building can later serve as the "here is what we already built and tested" exhibit at the house's pre-application meeting.
- Convert one county's win into a statewide asset. Petition for a Statewide Alternate Method Ruling once a local official has approved the material, rather than treating that approval as a one-off. A successful statewide ruling binds every other Oregon jurisdiction and removes the need to refight the same technical argument later, whether at a second permit or in a dispute years after occupancy.
- Treat an unfavorable answer as a menu, not a wall. The Oregon-specific escalation set found in this pass, a site-specific interpretation the official can request, a statewide code interpretation, an alternate method ruling, a local appeals board, a state specialty-code-chief appeal, and reconsideration after either interpretation route, is unusually rich compared to a single local-appeals-board path. A well-documented, well-tested material has several distinct doors, not one.
- Consider a university or community-college materials-testing partnership, an unconfirmed lead for any specific Oregon institution, as a way to obtain creditable, independently witnessed compression testing at lower cost than a commercial accredited lab, while building a paper trail of disinterested third-party involvement that strengthens the R104.4 approved-agency argument.
