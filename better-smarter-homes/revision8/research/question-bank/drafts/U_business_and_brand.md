### 2. The documented build as content and brand

**Audience-building reality.** Reported figures for the rural/homestead/off-grid content niche (from industry content-marketing sources, not creator-disclosed data, so treat as directional, not settled): one source reports the niche generates roughly $6.90 RPM (revenue per thousand views) with hardware, seed, off-grid-equipment, and sustainability advertisers paying $10-13 CPM, about 200,000 channels active in the niche, and audience loyalty around 94 percent, among the highest of any content category (outlierkit.com, 2026 listicle, unverified, needs a primary or higher-quality secondary source). A separate estimator lists one large homesteading channel (878K subscribers) at an estimated $741-$16,700/month, and a smaller channel at roughly $2,450/video and $7,360/month (speakrj.com, vidiq.com; both third-party estimators, not creator-disclosed, wide ranges). The earlier Wave 2 research already found that only about 3 percent of channels ever reach even the basic monetization threshold (recurpost.com), which stays the honest headline; the RPM/CPM figures above are new detail that should not be written as fact without a better source.

Diversification is the consistent finding across every source: successful niche creators layer ad revenue, sponsorship (reported 20-30% of income), affiliate (10-20%), and products or services (10-30%), rather than depending on one channel (multiple sources). Patreon-specific data: the Crafts/DIY category is under 0.3 percent of all Patreon creators, the smallest represented category, but individual creators with a devoted niche audience can still earn meaningfully (one cited example: a niche craft creator earning about $6,500/month from roughly 2,700 YouTube subscribers who convert to Patreon support, detailed.com). A generic small-creator Patreon benchmark: 200 patrons at an average $7/month grosses about $1,400, nets about $1,160 after platform and payment fees (influencermarketinghub.com). Coaching is flagged by newsletter-monetization sources as "the most underutilized" revenue line and the highest return per subscriber, because it does not require a large audience, only a clear result and a reader who wants it (thecreatorplaybook.substack.com).

**The book as a product.** Self-publishing economics (figures found; primary-source confirmation against kdp.amazon.com and ingramspark.com recommended before publication, since royalty terms change):
- Amazon KDP ebook: two royalty tiers, 35% and 70%; the 70% tier applies only to books priced $2.99-$9.99 and is reduced by a delivery fee of about $0.06/MB.
- Amazon KDP print: 60% of list price minus print cost for sales on Amazon; 40% minus print cost for "expanded distribution" (other retailers); a June 2025 change lowers the royalty rate further below certain regional list-price floors.
- IngramSpark: wholesale model, author receives list price minus a wholesale discount typically 40-55%, minus print cost; ebook royalties reported at 45-60% depending on retailer; IngramSpark raised its "market access fee" to 1.875% of list price effective February 1, 2026.
- A worked comparison found: the same book nets about $5.74/copy through KDP versus $3.75/copy through IngramSpark, because IngramSpark's wholesale structure gives retailers margin to resell the book, which is also what makes bookstore and library distribution possible (a channel KDP-only publishing does not reach).
- Crowdfunding a book before writing/printing it is a real, used mechanism: reported examples range from about $3,700 (70 backers, fiction) to about $15,000 (from a $3,000 goal, a technical/reference book) to five-figure repeat launches (a cookbook author) to $20,000+ (a niche nonfiction collection). One aggregator claims Kickstarter publishing projects with at least 25 backers succeed more than 80 percent of the time (multiple self-publishing blogs, anecdote-level evidence, not a controlled statistic).

**Plans and drawings.** The existing chapter already establishes the legal basis and the single-build-license/unstamped-reference disclaimer pattern. New pricing detail: stock house plans commonly run $500-$2,500 per set depending on square footage, format (PDF vs. CAD), and designer reputation, and most stock plans still need an estimated $1,500-$4,000 in modifications before permit submittal because they were not drawn for the specific site, soil, or local amendment set (homeguide.com and others, consistent across multiple stock-plan-industry sources, moderate confidence). This suggests two distinct product ideas for a documented build's plan library: (a) the whole-house plan set, priced like the stock-plan industry, explicitly unstamped and site-unverified; (b) narrower, cheaper add-on products, such as the block press jig, a specific detail assembly, or a documented mix design, sold on their own rather than only as part of a full house plan.

**Courses.** Online-course platforms (Teachable, Kajabi, Thinkific, Podia) charge monthly platform fees plus, on lower tiers, a per-transaction cut. Reported figures: Teachable removed its free tier in 2025 and its entry paid tier (about $29/month annual) still carries a 7.5% transaction fee, meaning on a $150 course sold 100 times, roughly $1,575 goes to transaction fees alone plus the annual subscription; Kajabi has no transaction fee but a different tier/contact-limit structure, and sources suggest it becomes the better value once monthly course revenue clears roughly $10,000 (creatorlms.net, ddiy.co; course-platform-comparison sites, figures change often and should be re-checked against the platforms' own pricing pages at write time).

In-person, hands-on workshops are a real, long-established parallel to online courses in the natural-building and owner-builder world, and may fit a solo concrete/block-making project better than a video course, since the skill is physical. Real tuition figures found: a 2-day natural-mineral-plaster workshop at $485; a basic cob workshop at $1,000 (with a $200 deposit and a $100 discount for two or more registrants, food and camping included); a 21-day natural-building certification at $1,975; a solo instructor's day rate of about $1,200 (plus GST) for a two-person teaching team, plus travel and lodging, when hired to teach elsewhere; one online workshop discounted from $250 to $50 as an introductory offer (cobworkshops.org, sustainablelifeschool.com, earthenhand.com, theyearofmud.com). These are direct provider-published prices, higher confidence than the aggregator-sourced YouTube/Patreon figures above. A rough per-participant/per-day tuition band across these examples is about $100-$250/day, before food and lodging.

**Consulting and coaching.** A close, real precedent exists: a company called Freestone Built sells "owner-build coaching" (their own description: "like personal training, but for building") as a recurring $799/month subscription (3-month minimum) with mobile-app-based communication, bid review, milestone checklists, and subcontractor-selection guidance, or a hybrid flat-fee model at $6-10/square foot with a $3,500 non-refundable deposit that includes plan review and budget preparation; their service area is limited to one metro region (freestonebuilt.com, company's own site, direct precedent, single example, not yet cross-checked against a second operator in this space). This is a real, working business model in the same category the owner is considering. General construction-consulting hourly rates run $67-150/hour on average, with about 39 percent of consultants in the $100-250/hour band and about 19 percent above $250/hour for specialty work; "owner's representative" work specifically is quoted at $75-150/hour standard, $150-250+/hour for senior or intensive coordination work (consultingsuccess.com, mastt.com).

**Build-log/channel format.** The existing chapter already frames the "owned audience" stack (website plus email newsletter as the canonical home, platforms as distribution) as protection against platform-revocable reach. New detail: Substack/newsletter creators earning meaningfully ($5,000-10,000+/month) consistently report stacking several revenue types at once (paid subscription tier, community access, workshops, consulting, digital products) rather than depending on subscriptions alone (multiple Substack-based creator sources, moderate confidence, self-reported). This reinforces the existing chapter's mechanism-table approach: list the mechanisms and how they combine, not a single projected number.

**Existing publishers as a parallel market.** A route that does not require building an audience from zero: pitching finished articles to already-established outlets in this exact niche. Reported freelance rates: Fine Homebuilding (Taunton Press) pays roughly $100-200/page for a feature article and $35-400 for shorter department pieces, depending on length and complexity (one source states a flat $100/article for a different article class, an inconsistency that should be resolved against Fine Homebuilding's current writer's guidelines directly); Mother Earth Living (a Mother Earth News sibling publication) is reported at $25-150/article (makealivingwriting.com, an aggregator of freelance markets, moderate confidence, rates likely dated and should be re-checked against the outlet's live writer's-guidelines page, which exists at motherearthnews.com/writers-guidelines). This is modest money per piece but real, fast, low-risk exposure and a credibility line (a bylined feature in an established trade or homesteading publication) that a new brand cannot buy.

### 3. The make-your-own-block capability as a possible micro-enterprise

This is the area with no coverage anywhere in the existing chapter and the least-settled real-world information, because it sits at the intersection of a hobbyist/prosumer operation and a regulated manufactured-product industry.

**Selling finished units: the scale problem.** Block-making machine costs found span three tiers: entry/manual ($800-$2,000), mid-range semi-automatic ($5,000-$15,000, matching the range already flagged in the shared research veins as the target commercial-machine-class), and one cited fully automatic unit at $10,000-$12,500 producing at least 6 blocks/minute (multiple block-machine manufacturer and marketplace sites, aectearthblock.com, block-machine.net, indexbox.io; these read as vendor/marketing content, not independent testing, and should be treated as a starting price range, not a specification guarantee). By contrast, an actual commercial CMU plant (the kind competing on price with a big-box retailer) is modeled at $905,000 to $1.2-2.5 million in startup capital, producing hundreds of thousands of units in year one (financialmodelslab.com and similar business-plan-template vendors; these sites sell financial-model templates, so the specific dollar figures read as illustrative/template output rather than an audited real case, but the order of magnitude gap between a prosumer press and a real plant is the durable finding). This gap is the central fact for this section: a single owner-operator with one press cannot compete with an established CMU plant on price or volume, and should not try to.

Concrete block is heavy and low value relative to its weight and bulk, so transport cost dominates the economics of selling finished units; the realistic market for a small producer is local (a short truck radius), not regional or national. No source directly stated a transport-cost-per-mile breakeven figure for this project; that is a real, missing number.

**The liability ladder: what class of product to make.** Concrete/masonry products split into distinct ASTM categories with different consequences if they fail: ASTM C90 covers load-bearing concrete masonry units (structural CMU, used in permitted load-bearing walls); ASTM C936 covers interlocking concrete pavers (non-structural, walking/driving surfaces); ASTM C1372 covers segmental retaining-wall units (gravity-held, mortarless, landscape use) (cmha.org/masonryandhardscapes.org, a standards-adjacent trade association source, higher confidence than the marketing sites above). A landscape/hardscape product (pavers, garden wall block, stepping units) that carries no structural load and is not built into a permitted structure is a fundamentally lower-liability product than a block sold and represented as load-bearing CMU for someone else's wall. Selling a structural product commercially would put the seller in the position of the "manufacturer" the ASTM C90 quality-control and batch-testing regime is written for, which is a different and heavier burden than the owner's own one-time alternative-materials test-to-prove path (ASTM C39/C140 lab compression testing under ORSC R104.11) for his own house. The alternative-materials approval on the owner's own permit is not, by itself, a transferable product certification another buyer or another jurisdiction can rely on; this distinction does not appear to be researched anywhere yet and is a genuine gap.

A code-recognized path exists for a manufacturer who wants a product code officials elsewhere can approve without a separate case-by-case alternative-materials review: an ICC-ES Evaluation Report (ESR), or the IAPMO equivalent, is a standard, real mechanism used across the building-products industry (icc-es.org, a primary source, describes the program clearly), but no cost figure for a small manufacturer was found in this research; ICC-ES's own materials direct inquiries to contact them directly for pricing. This should be flagged as an open, real question rather than an assumed cost.

**Selling the method instead of the object.** This may be the more realistic lane, and it has real precedent:
- Open Source Ecology's "Liberator" compressed-earth-block press is already free and open-source: downloadable build instructions, or a purchasable kit/finished machine from OSE directly, producing up to 10 bricks/minute at about 25 lb/block (opensourceecology.org, wiki.opensourceecology.org, primary source, the organization's own site). Any owner-built or owner-improved press competes against a free, published design, which means the sellable value is not the machine itself but a proven, tested, documented mix and process built on top of it.
- The Auroville Earth Institute (Auroville, India; founded by HUDCO/Government of India in 1989) is the clearest working example of a "sell the method" business built on compressed stabilized earth block: it runs paid training courses (on-campus and online), consultancy, architectural design and construction services, heritage conservation work, direct production of stabilized-earth material, and quality-control testing services, and sells its own manual press (the Auram press) in partnership with a manufacturing partner (Aureka); the institute reports having trained more than 10,000 people from 79 countries and worked in 36 countries (earth-auroville.com, auroville.org, primary institutional source, reasonably credible as self-description though not independently audited). The revenue mix, training plus consulting plus equipment plus QC testing plus a small amount of direct production, is a template the next research pass should examine in more depth for the manuscript.
- A mobile press-as-a-service model (the operator brings a press to the buyer's site and presses the buyer's own soil into block for the buyer's own project) was a hypothesis going into this research; no specific named US operator running this exact model was confirmed in the searches performed. This should be recorded as an unverified creative idea, not a confirmed precedent, and needs a dedicated search pass.
- Watershed Materials (founded 2011 by David Easton, an earth-building pioneer, an architecture professor, and a nano-aluminosilicate materials scientist) is real evidence that a low-cement/geopolymer masonry block business is fundable and technically real: the company received a $740,000 National Science Foundation grant, and reports its Watershed Block achieves up to 50% cement reduction and 65% embodied-energy reduction versus conventional CMU, with geopolymer test samples reaching about 7,000 psi (roughly double ordinary concrete strength) using no portland cement (watershedmaterials.com, a 2014-era NSF press release via prweb.com, and azobuild.com; the strength and reduction figures are company-published claims, not independently re-verified here). This case is useful mainly as a caution: reaching this stage took institutional grant funding, multiple technical co-founders, and years of formulation work, not a solo owner-builder's spare time. It sets realistic expectations for how far "selling the method" could scale versus how far it realistically will for one person.

**Regulatory touchpoints specific to a block-selling or block-teaching operation**, beyond the ASTM/ICC-ES material above:
- OSHA's respirable crystalline silica construction standard (29 CFR 1926.1153, osha.gov, primary federal source) applies to occupational exposure; it governs an employer's exposed employees. A solo owner has no OSHA employee-exposure duty toward himself, but hiring even one paid helper, or running a hands-on workshop where participants operate dust-generating equipment, may create exposure-control and, for employees specifically, a written exposure control plan obligation. Oregon OSHA (the state-plan equivalent) would be the applicable enforcement body in Oregon; not yet separately researched.
- Teaching a hands-on workshop (block pressing, masonry technique) is legally distinct from running a jobsite: general liability insurance does not cover claims arising from instruction/negligent teaching; that requires professional liability/errors-and-omissions coverage, and a liability waiver alone is not considered sufficient protection without both proper insurance and a lawyer-drafted release (craftcover.com, businessinsuranceusa.com, insurance-industry sources, consistent across multiple independent pages, moderate-good confidence). This is a real, specific insurance gap the existing chapter's media-liability/E&O framing does not yet cover, because it assumed content and consulting, not hands-on instruction with power equipment.
- Zoning: see section 7 below (cross-referenced to the land and site-development chapters); this may be the actual controlling constraint on whether any of this is legally possible on the specific parcel at all.

### 4. Productizing owner-builder knowledge: pricing, formats, market

**Format-by-format price anchors** (see section 2 above for the sourcing behind each): book ($2.99-9.99 ebook at the 70% KDP royalty band; print priced to cover 60/40% minus print cost); plan set ($500-2,500, single-build license); online course (platform fees of roughly $29-$100+/month plus, on some platforms, per-transaction fees, layered on top of whatever price the course itself is set at); in-person workshop (roughly $100-250/participant/day found across natural-building-school precedent, often bundling food/lodging); consulting/coaching ($67-250+/hour general consulting rates; a real owner-build-coaching precedent at $799/month subscription or $6-10/sqft hybrid).

**Market-size honesty.** No credible, specific figure for "how many people build their own home" or "the size of the owner-builder market" in the US was found in this research; general homebuilding industry statistics (a $169.8 billion 2026 US home-builder market per one industry data aggregator) describe the professional contractor industry, not owner-builders, and do not break out a self-built or owner-permitted share (statista.com, ibisworld.com). This is a real, honest gap: the manuscript should not manufacture a market-size number. It can honestly state that the audience is a niche within a niche (owner-builders, within concrete/masonry builders, within a further subset interested in a documented, code-anchored, long-life approach) without a specific total.

**Funding productization itself.** Crowdfunding a book, plan set, or course before it exists (pre-selling it to fund its own creation) is a real, established practice with real, if scattered, precedent figures (see section 2 above). This is a genuinely useful mechanism for a documented build: it tests whether the audience wants the specific product before the owner spends the time making it, and it front-loads some of the production cost.

### 5. Brand identity, naming, and positioning

This research does not invent or suggest anything about the project's own actual brand identity, name, or positioning; that is the owner's decision. What follows is a list of comparable, real projects and brands, useful only as reference points for how others in adjacent niches positioned themselves.

**Adjacent brands and projects** (for positioning research, not imitation):
- Faircompanies / Kirsten Dirksen: an independent media project (founded 2007 by Kirsten Dirksen and Nicolás Boullosa) documenting alternative housing and off-grid/tiny/experimental homes worldwide; one of the longest-running, largest video archives in this space; a 90-square-foot micro-studio video from early in the channel's history has over 25 million views; the channel still publishes roughly weekly after nearly two decades (faircompanies.com, YouTube channel, underthechokotree.com). ### 6. Intellectual property and rights beyond the existing chapter

**Trade secret versus patent for a proprietary mix or method.** This is a real, concrete choice the owner would face if he wanted to protect a specific block or concrete mix design (a novel dry-cast mix, a stabilization ratio, a curing schedule) as a business asset. General IP-law findings (govfacts.org, adventip.com, tangibly.com; standard, non-controversial IP-practice content, not construction-specific): a formula or process that a competitor could not easily reverse-engineer from the finished product is a strong trade-secret candidate; trade-secret protection has no filing cost and can in principle last indefinitely, but only if the owner takes active, consistent steps to keep it secret (limited access, confidentiality marking, non-disclosure agreements); a patent gives a stronger, publicly registered right but costs significantly more, requires public disclosure of exactly how the invention works (which a competitor can then read once granted or published), and expires after about 20 years. For a mix design that might stay competitively useful for decades, and where the owner has no patent budget, trade secret is the more realistic starting position, with the explicit tradeoff that it protects nothing if the mix can be reverse-engineered from a finished, sold block.

**AI training data and text-and-data-mining opt-out.** A live and evolving area as of 2026, relevant because a documented build generates a large volume of original photos, video, and technical writing that AI systems may scrape for training. Under the EU's framework (AI Act Article 53, extraterritorial for any general-purpose AI provider offering a model in EU markets), a rights holder must use a machine-readable opt-out signal, such as a robots.txt rule or a specific TDM-reservation metadata tag, to have scraping/training excluded; a plain-language statement on a website ("please do not train on my content") does not count as a valid opt-out under this framework (reedsmith.com, an international law firm's client-alert page, and promise.legal). The US position is reported as considerably less settled, with no equivalent formal opt-out regime and active litigation over whether AI training on copyrighted material is fair use (multiple sources, terms.law, aivortex.io). This should be flagged as fast-moving and in need of a currency check at the actual write date, not treated as settled.

**DMCA realism for a solo creator.** The DMCA created a notice-and-takedown safe harbor for platforms (they are not liable for user-uploaded infringing content if they remove it promptly once notified), which is the practical tool a solo creator without legal budget actually has against content theft (reposted videos, copied plan PDFs, scraped articles); it was not designed for, and does not cleanly address, AI-training use of content, which is a separate and less certain fight. This sets a realistic expectation: takedown requests to platforms are usable and free; pursuing an AI company for training on scraped content is a different, harder, and likely not solo-affordable fight.

**International sale of digital products.** The existing chapter's plan-library disclaimer material (unstamped, buyer confirms local code compliance) is US/Oregon-framed. A digital product (ebook, PDF plan set, online course) is trivially purchasable by a buyer anywhere in the world, and the standard industry practice found is the same disclaimer logic extended outward: plans are represented as designed against the code in force at the time and place they were drawn, and the buyer is responsible for a local professional review and for confirming local code compliance before building, wherever "local" is (multiple stock-plan-industry disclaimer pages, consistent with what the existing chapter already found domestically). Copyright itself is automatic in nearly all countries under the Berne Convention without any registration requirement, but US registration is still what is required to sue for infringement in US courts and to make statutory damages and attorney fees available domestically, which the existing chapter already covers; the new point is that this protection is not symmetric internationally and enforcement abroad is a separate, harder question that should be routed to an attorney rather than assumed.

### 7. Entity, tax, and insurance touchpoints for a side venture

This section deliberately does not duplicate the personal project finance chapter's construction-financing content (loans, draws, holding costs during the house build) or the existing business chapter's already-settled entity/tax framework. It targets what a SIDE VENTURE selling products, courses, or coaching adds on top of what a purely content/media brand already has to handle.

**Multi-state sales tax on digital and physical products.** New for this research: as of 2026, most US states (about 41 of 46 that publish a figure) use a $100,000 economic-nexus threshold for remote sellers, a few use $250,000 or $500,000, and states are actively dropping transaction-count thresholds (Illinois removed its 200-transaction count at the start of 2026, following Utah in 2025), which increases exposure for a seller with a small number of high-ticket sales (a plan set at $1,500-2,500, or a $799/month coaching subscription, could cross a state's dollar threshold with relatively few sales) even without high transaction volume (nexusbystate.com, numeral.com, avalara.com; avalara.com in particular is an established sales-tax-compliance company, higher confidence than a generic blog). Digital-goods taxability is inconsistent state to state and is reported as expanding (more states taxing streaming/digital subscriptions in 2026); most states treat a live, real-time course or consultation as a non-taxable service but treat pre-recorded, on-demand course access as a taxable digital product, which is a distinction directly relevant to choosing a course format. Where the seller uses a marketplace (Etsy, a course marketplace, Amazon), many states require the marketplace itself to collect and remit sales tax on the seller's behalf ("marketplace facilitator" laws), which can remove some of this burden depending on the sales channel chosen.

**Product liability insurance for a manufactured item.** The existing chapter's insurance framework (media liability/E&O, commercial general liability) was written for a content/consulting business. Selling a physical, structural or quasi-structural product (a block) to a buyer who will build with it is a different insurance category: manufacturer/product liability. General contractor-liability figures found ($61/month average for masonry contractors, $102/month for concrete contractors, or $300-500/year for a small concrete/masonry package with a $2 million limit; techinsurance.com, insurance-marketplace aggregators) describe CONTRACTOR liability (covering work performed as a contractor), not manufacturer liability for a unit sold to a stranger who then builds a wall with it; no source in this research gave a manufacturer-specific figure for masonry/concrete products, which is a genuine, flagged gap requiring a direct insurance-agent quote.

**Workshop/instruction liability**, covered in section 3 above: professional liability/E&O, not general liability, covers instructional negligence claims, and a signed waiver does not substitute for it.

**Home-based manufacturing and zoning.** This may be the actual controlling question for the whole block-micro-enterprise idea, and it belongs cross-referenced to the land/site-feasibility chapter and to the "Developing the Site" chapter's shop/block-yard material, not duplicated here. Findings: Oregon allows home occupations on Exclusive Farm Use (EFU) land under ORS 215.448, but the business must stay "clearly secondary" to the property's agricultural character and must not "unreasonably interfere" with other permitted uses in the zone; at least one Oregon county's planning guidance states that a "Type 1" home occupation (the more permissive category, allowed outright in most residential and resource zones) is a conditional use in EFU zones and is not allowed at all in Forest (F1/F2) zones, meaning a value-added manufacturing or product-sales activity on farm- or forest-zoned land likely needs a conditional-use permit at minimum, and may not be allowed at all depending on the specific zone and county (deschutes.org planning document; Oregon counties administer EFU zoning individually within the statewide framework, so this needs confirmation against the specific parcel's county and zone, not treated as a single statewide answer). This directly affects whether commercial block sales, a paid workshop hosting outside visitors, or any regular customer traffic is legally possible on the actual property, independent of every other question in this file.

**CPA/bookkeeping cost reality**, for grounding the "what to bring to a CPA" framing already used in the existing chapter and the finance-chapter skeleton: reported small-business/single-member-LLC ranges are $500-1,000/month for standard outsourced bookkeeping (or $200-500/month for budget/offshore options), $300-1,500 for annual tax preparation, $500-2,000/month for a fuller advisory/retainer arrangement, $150-450/hour for direct CPA time, and roughly $1,500-5,000/year all-in for a small business's typical accounting needs (catalyst-cpa.com, beancount.io, sdocpa.com; accounting-firm marketing content, wide ranges, consistent with the existing chapter's own "the CPA supplies the numbers" framing rather than a single hard figure).
