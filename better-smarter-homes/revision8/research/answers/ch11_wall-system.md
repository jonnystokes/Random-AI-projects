### From the Wave 3 seed questions (Section A, the double-wythe path)

**1. Veneer versus structural wythe. For the owner's inner-CMU + cavity insulation + outer-brick wall: does TMS 402 treat the outer brick as an anchored masonry veneer (non-loadbearing, Chapter 12) or as a structural wythe of a composite/noncomposite multi-wythe wall (Chapter 5)? State what changes structurally, in code path, and in engineering requirement between the two readings.**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Both readings are available under TMS 402, and the choice is a design decision the project's engineer of record makes explicit in the drawings, not a fact fixed by the materials alone. BIA TN 28D states the distinction directly: "In veneer walls, the exterior wythe (veneer) does not resist any load other than its self-weight and only transfers lateral load to the backing, which resists the full load. A non-composite wall is similar; however, each wythe resists a proportion of the load individually based on its stiffness... In a composite wall, both wythes work together as a single element, resisting both lateral and vertical loads." (BIA TN 28D, p.3, "Structural Performance," https://www.gobrick.com/media/file/28d-tn28d.pdf). TMS 402-22 addresses veneer in its anchored-veneer chapter (numbered Chapter 12 in TMS 402-16/19 and renumbered Chapter 13 in TMS 402-22, per The Masonry Society's 2022 code-change materials, https://learn.masonrysociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/Masonry-Veneer-Requirement-Changes-in-TMS-402-602-22-Handout.pdf) and addresses composite/noncomposite multi-wythe walls in its general wall-design chapter (Chapter 5, wall classifications at section 5.1.4 per the citation already established in the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter's Reinforced Masonry Walls section).

For this project, BIA TN 28D calls the anchored-veneer reading "the most common combination for contemporary drained multi-wythe wall assemblies" and states plainly: "Nearly all multi-wythe masonry walls constructed today consist of an exterior wythe categorized as an anchored veneer. Another category of multi-wythe masonry is a cavity wall, which is less prevalent today... many design and construction professionals refer to any brick veneer drained wall assembly as a cavity wall. Be sure to clarify terminology to avoid confusion" (BIA TN 28D, p.3). What changes between the two readings:

- **Anchored veneer (the default, TMS 402 veneer chapter):** the brick carries only its own self-weight; the inner CMU wythe alone is the structural wall and must independently satisfy every gravity and lateral load path. Tie design uses TMS 402's veneer tie tables (see item 2). ORSC R703.8 governs the prescriptive veneer-anchorage rules on the code side, though R703.8's wood/cold-formed-steel-backing subsections do not apply to a masonry backup; the general veneer provisions and R703.8.4-8.6 (anchorage, flashing, weeps) apply regardless of backing material.
- **Noncomposite (cavity) wall (TMS 402 Chapter 5):** both wythes are individually engineered to resist a proportion of the load based on relative stiffness, and the ties must meet the noncomposite wall's tie-spacing and area limits, which are tighter than veneer ties in some configurations (BIA TN 28D notes the noncomposite maximum air space is 4-1/2 in., versus up to 6-5/8 in. for veneer, item 3 below).
- **Composite wall (TMS 402 Chapter 5):** both wythes act as a single structural section; this requires ties and/or masonry headers engineered specifically to transfer shear across the collar joint or air space so the assembly behaves as one thick wall. BIA TN 28D calls masonry headers for composite action "not commonly used in contemporary construction" due to "their lack of ductility, limited ability to accommodate differential movement, and because they provide a direct path for water penetration" (p.10).

The Structural System and Concrete Design chapter's What Must Be Engineered section already states that "two-wythe loadbearing CMU/CMU or CMU/brick assembly" has "no IRC prescriptive path" and requires a licensed engineer regardless of which TMS 402 classification is chosen. Practically: choose anchored veneer unless the project specifically wants the brick to share structural load, because veneer is the better-documented, more forgiving, and more commonly permitted path, and it is what BIA TN 28D itself defaults to describing. The engineer of record states the classification chosen on the structural drawings; this is not optional and not a value this research file can supply, because it is a project-specific structural design decision.

Lands in: The Wall System, Foundation to Roofline. Cross-reference Structural System and Concrete Design (Reinforced Masonry Walls; Wall Ties For Cavity Walls; What Must Be Engineered).

**2. Tie systems and schedules. Wall-tie and veneer-tie types (adjustable two-piece, wire, joint-reinforcement ladder/truss), spacing limits (TMS 402; IRC R703.8 for veneer), corrosion protection by exposure (hot-dip galvanized vs. stainless), and the thermal-bridging penalty of steel ties crossing the cavity insulation.**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The Structural System and Concrete Design chapter's Wall Ties For Cavity Walls section and the Envelope chapter's Wall Insulation Strategy section already carry the general tie-material, spacing, and thermal-bridging tables (stainless ASTM A580 Type 304/316 preferred, carbon steel ASTM A82 acceptable non-coastal with coating, W1.7/W2.8 wire sizes, 16 in. x 24 in. adjustable-tie spacing, thermally-broken tie products, and the roughly 0.5-1 percentage-point thermal-bridging penalty). This item adds the project-specific brick-veneer-over-CMU-backup numbers from BIA TN 28D Tables 1 and 2, which are more detailed than the general cavity-wall table already in the manuscript because they are keyed to air-space width:

*Adjustable ties (BIA TN 28D Table 1, p.8):*
- Air space up to 4-5/8 in.: joint reinforcement with adjustable ties, or adjustable unit ties; minimum wire W1.7 (MW11); maximum wall area per tie 2.67 sq ft; pintle/eye wire minimum W2.8, minimum 1 pintle leg, maximum vertical offset 1-1/4 in.
- Air space over 4-5/8 in. up to 6-5/8 in.: same tie types, minimum wire W2.8 (MW18); maximum wall area per tie 2.67 sq ft; barrel minimum 1/4 in. outside diameter, minimum 2 pintle legs, maximum offset 1-1/4 in., maximum 2 in. distance from inside face of brick to end of adjustable part.
- Air space over 6-5/8 in.: no prescriptive tie table; design under "TMS Code Alternative design of anchored masonry veneer provisions" (engineered).

*Non-adjustable ties (BIA TN 28D Table 2, p.9):* wire W1.7 covers 2.67 sq ft per tie; wire W2.8 covers 3.50 sq ft per tie; sheet-metal unit ties (0.06 in. thick, 7/8 in. wide minimum) cover 3.50 sq ft; for air space 4-5/8 to 6-5/8 in., joint reinforcement (W2.8 longitudinal and cross wires) covers only 1.77 sq ft per tie (assumes 16 in. o.c. vertical placement).

*General spacing:* maximum 32 in. o.c. horizontally, 24-25 in. o.c. vertically (BIA TN 28D states 25 in.; ORSC/IRC R703.8.4.1 states 24 in.; use the more conservative 24 in. for the manuscript, and flag the 1-in. discrepancy for the engineer of record to resolve at plan review). Additional ties are required within 12 in. of openings larger than 16 in., spaced at a maximum of 3 ft o.c. around the opening (BIA TN 28D p.9; ORSC R703.8.4.1.1, "Veneer Ties Around Wall Openings").

*Tie type recommendation specific to this wall:* BIA TN 28D recommends against corrugated ("Z") ties for hollow-unit backing ("Metal 'Z' ties are intended to be used between wythes of solid masonry units only and are not appropriate for hollow masonry units," p.9) and against truss-type joint reinforcement for a BV/CMU wall specifically, because "the diagonal cross wires of truss type joint reinforcement can conflict with the vertical reinforcing bars and the placement of grout" in a reinforced inner CMU wythe, and because the horizontal restraint truss type creates "could possibly result in bowing of the masonry walls." BIA TN 28D recommends ladder-type or tab-type joint reinforcement with adjustable ties instead, calling this combination "preferred for brick veneer walls with CMU backing, as it avoids potential conflicts between unit tie wires and joint reinforcing wires" (p.10).

*Seismic tie-spacing reduction:* in Seismic Design Category D and above (which covers most of western Oregon per the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter's Site-To-SDC Matrix), TMS 402 tightens the maximum tributary wall area per tie to 75 percent of the standard prescriptive spacing, and requires the top and sides of the veneer to be isolated from the structure so seismic drift is not imparted into the veneer; in SDC E/F each story's veneer weight must be independently supported (WebSearch summary of TMS 402/602-22 review materials, The Masonry Society, https://learn.masonrysociety.org/product/tms-402-602-22-review-all-lessons/; this is a paywalled course summary and the exact TMS 402-22 section number was not independently verified against the primary text, so treat the 75-percent figure as a lead to confirm against the purchased TMS 402-22 text or an engineer of record, not as a citable code value). See item 5 for the fuller seismic treatment.

*Workmanship requirement specific to embedment:* BIA TN 28D specifies "a minimum 5/8 in. (16 mm) of mortar cover from the exterior face of the brick and a minimum embedment of 1-1/2 in. (38.1 mm)" for every tie leg in the brick bed joint (p.10-11), and warns that ties not fully embedded transfer their load to the ties that are, "resulting in potential overloading of ties and a weak structural system for the wall assembly as a whole." This is a craft-quality item that belongs in the Masonry Craft chapter as well as here.

**3. Cavity, drainage, and insulation. Minimum air space, the drainage plane, weep spacing and type, base flashing, and which cavity insulations suit a drained-and-vented cavity (rigid foam vs. mineral-wool board vs. injected).**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** From BIA TN 28D, "Drainage Cavity/Air Space" (p.5) and "Summary of Recommendations" (p.1):

- **Air space width:** 1 in. minimum required (a "1 in. nominal dimension in the IRC and a 1 in. specified dimension in the IBC to account for construction tolerances"); 2 in. minimum recommended (air spaces under 2 in. "are not recommended" even though permitted); up to 6-5/8 in. maximum for veneer without engineered ties; up to 4-1/2 in. maximum for noncomposite (cavity) walls without engineered ties. Where insulation fills part of the cavity, a minimum nominal 1 in. clear air space must remain between the face of the insulation and the back of the brick, "sufficient space for the mason to lay the brick properly and for the wall to function as a drainage wall."
- **Weeps:** placed immediately above flashing at every flashing line (not one or two courses above; BIA TN 28D warns misplaced weeps "will not perform their intended function"); ORSC/IRC and TMS 402 cap spacing at 33 in. o.c.; BIA TN 28D recommends the tighter 24 in. o.c. for open-head-joint weeps specifically, and states open head joint is "the preferred type" and "should be at least 2 in. high." Rope wicks and weep tubes are named alternatives. ORSC R703.8.6 states weepholes "shall be not less than 3/16 inch in diameter" and "located immediately above the flashing" (up.codes ORSC/IRC 2021-base text, https://up.codes/s/weepholes).
- **Base flashing:** installed at the base of the wall, at shelf angles, above window heads, below window sills, and at every other interruption in the air space; the flashing's vertical leg must extend at least 8 in. up the CMU backing and terminate in a bed joint of the inner wythe or a reglet cut into concrete; laps require a minimum 6 in. overlap, sealed; where flashing is discontinuous, ends turn up at least 1 in. into a head joint to form an end dam (BIA TN 28D p.1, p.11-12). Below the lowest flashing, the air space must be completely filled solid with mortar or grout so no open cavity exists below grade (BIA TN 28D p.1, p.13 "Foundations"). Flashing and weeps should sit high enough above finished grade, "up to 10 in. per the building code," to avoid future burial by landscaping or regrading (BIA TN 28D p.13).
- **Cavity insulation suited to a drained/vented cavity:** BIA TN 28D lists rigid board options that conform to specific ASTM material standards for this application: EPS and XPS per ASTM C578, polyisocyanurate per ASTM C1289, and mineral wool board per ASTM C612 (distinct from the ASTM C578 foam standard because mineral wool is "semi-rigid" and fire-resistant and "does not absorb moisture"). BIA TN 28D calls out that "when insulation is installed in the drainage cavity/air space, it is critical that the product be intended for use in the 'wet zone' of a wall assembly and have an established history of successful use in this application" (p.6). Two granular-fill options exist for the CMU cell cavities specifically (not the air space): water-repellent vermiculite (ASTM C516) and silicone-treated perlite (ASTM C549); BIA TN 28D explicitly states "EPS bead granular fill is not recommended" for this use. Spray polyurethane foam (ASTM C1029) can also serve as an air/water-resistive barrier applied to the CMU face before the brick goes up, though BIA TN 28D flags that foam shrinkage "could lead to moisture drainage paths and air leakage" if edge/transition membranes are not detailed to cover the gap.

This matches and extends the Envelope chapter's Wall Insulation Strategy verdict (mineral wool for the most favorable durability profile, EPS for the code-safe economical choice, polyiso where the cavity drains and the foil facing is accounted for).

**4. Differential movement. Brick moisture expansion vs. CMU drying shrinkage: expansion joints in brick, control joints in CMU, spacing (BIA Technical Notes, NCMA/CMHA TEK), and how the ties accommodate movement.**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Brick and CMU move in opposite directions for opposite reasons, and this is the physical basis for why the two wythes get two different kinds of joint. Fired clay brick absorbs atmospheric moisture permanently after firing and grows slightly and irreversibly over its service life (plus reversible thermal movement); concrete masonry loses the water it was cast/cured with and shrinks as it dries (plus reversible thermal movement). BIA TN 28D states this as a design requirement rather than explaining the mechanism in depth: "A brick veneer wall assembly with a concrete or CMU backing will include both control joints and veneer expansion joints. Control joints are installed in the concrete or CMU wythe to manage the shrinkage that results as the concrete or concrete masonry cures. Control joints are not required to align with the veneer expansion joints in the brick and may have separate spacing requirements" (BIA TN 28D, "Movement Joints," p.12). This is the single most load-bearing sentence for this section of the manuscript: the two joint systems are independent, sized by different formulas, and do not need to line up.

Ties are the mechanism that lets the two wythes move independently without tearing: BIA TN 28D states ties must "provide a connection for each wythe, transfer lateral loads between wythes and accommodate differential movement between wythes" (p.8), and that adjustable two-piece ties (eye-and-pintle) are specifically designed with a mechanical slip tolerance (maximum 1-1/4 in. vertical offset, maximum 1/16 in. horizontal play) precisely so the brick can grow relative to the CMU without loading the tie in a way that would crack either wythe.

See items 18, 23, and 24 for the numeric spacing formulas (BIA Technical Note 18A for brick expansion joints, NCMA/CMHA TEK 10-2C for CMU control joints) and item 33 for the failure mode when these joints are omitted.

Lands in: The Wall System, Foundation to Roofline.

**5. Seismic in Oregon SDC D0/D1. Veneer and multi-wythe anchorage seismic requirements (TMS 402 seismic provisions), prescriptive limits, and where engineering becomes mandatory for the double-wythe.**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The Structural System and Concrete Design chapter already establishes the controlling fact for this project: the Site-To-SDC Matrix places most of the Willamette Valley and I-5 corridor at SDC D1, coastal Oregon at SDC D2, and the What Must Be Engineered section states flatly that "two-wythe loadbearing CMU/CMU or CMU/brick assembly (no IRC prescriptive path exists)" requires a licensed structural engineer "regardless" of seismic category, and separately that "above-grade concrete walls in SDC D0/D1/D2 (most of Oregon)" require engineering. For a double-wythe wall in Oregon's populated western counties, there is no scenario in which the double-wythe assembly is built without an engineer of record; the question is not whether to engineer it but what the engineer is designing to.

What is publicly documented about TMS 402's seismic veneer provisions, without purchasing the standard: general secondary-source summaries (a Masonry Society continuing-education product description, not the primary text) describe that TMS 402 requires anchored veneer in SDC C and above to have its top and sides isolated from the structural frame so that seismic drift of the frame is not imparted into the veneer as an in-plane load, that in SDC D and above the maximum wall area tributary to each tie is reduced (one secondary source states to about 75 percent of the standard prescriptive tie spacing, i.e., roughly 2.0 sq ft per tie instead of 2.67 sq ft), and that in SDC E/F each story's veneer weight must be independently supported rather than allowed to stack story to story (WebSearch summary citing masonrysociety.org and eng-tips.com discussion threads; these are secondary/discussion sources, not the TMS 402-22 primary text, and the exact section numbers and percentages were not independently verified against the purchased standard). This is consistent with, but not verified against, the manuscript's already-cited TMS 402-22 section 5.1.6 (anchors and ties) reference in the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter's Wall Ties For Cavity Walls section.

For the manuscript: state that Oregon's SDC D0/D1/D2 territory triggers mandatory PE design for the double-wythe wall (already-verified fact, no purchase needed), describe the general seismic behavior (veneer isolated from frame drift, ties reduced in tributary area, story-by-story support in higher categories) as the kind of requirement the engineer will apply, and mark the exact TMS 402-22 seismic tie-reduction percentage and section number for confirmation against the purchased standard or the project's engineer of record before it appears as a hard number in the manuscript.

**6. Foundation for the double-wythe. Footing width and the brick-shelf/ledge detail to carry both wythes, the slab-edge thermal break at the wall base, and how this differs from the single-wythe default footing.**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** BIA TN 28D's "Foundations" section (p.13) gives the free-sourced base-of-wall rules that apply regardless of footing width, which is itself an engineered value (footing width for a two-wythe wall is sized by the engineer of record from the actual wythe thicknesses, the eccentricity of the combined load, and the site's soil bearing capacity from the geotechnical report; it is not a fixed prescriptive number this research file can supply):

- The brick exterior wythe is permitted to corbel out beyond the top of the foundation (i.e., the footing/stem wall can be narrower than the full two-wythe width, with the brick stepped out on top), but the corbel is limited: "the overhang may not be more than one-third of a brick unit width nor more than one-half of its height per course. The total distance corbelled may not exceed one-half of the brick unit width." This is the free-sourced basis for a "brick shelf" detail narrower than the full wall.
- The lowest course of through-wall flashing must sit above finished grade, and with a basement, through-wall flashing is required at the bottom of the cavity specifically to keep water from tracking down into the basement wall; below that flashing, the entire air space must be filled solid with mortar or grout (no open cavity below grade).
- A bond break is recommended between the brick exterior wythe and the foundation, "to permit differential movement among the materials and reduces the likelihood of cracking" (i.e., the brick does not rely on adhesion to the concrete below it for anything but bearing). BIA TN 28D flags a structural nuance: where the wythe is separated from the foundation by a bond break or flashing layer, the wall's base boundary condition for engineering purposes becomes "simply supported or pinned," not "fixed," which matters to the engineer's calculation, not to the owner's construction sequence, if the brick is designed as a true veneer (in which case the point is moot because the veneer is not part of the lateral-load path anyway).

For the slab-edge thermal break specifically: the Envelope chapter's Slab Edge And Below-Grade Envelope Interface section already establishes the general rule (ORSC requires R-15 slab-edge insulation to a 24-in. minimum depth, continuous with the wall's exterior insulation). For the double-wythe wall, the practical difference from the single-wythe default is that the exterior insulation plane sits inside the cavity between the two wythes rather than outside a single structural wall, so the slab-edge insulation must be detailed to connect to the cavity insulation specifically, not to the outer brick face, to keep the thermal break continuous; if the insulation plane is broken at the transition from the wall cavity to the slab edge, the brick foundation/shelf area becomes an uninsulated thermal bridge at exactly the point the code targets.

**How this differs from the single-wythe default footing:** the single-wythe CMU wall's footing/stem wall is sized for one structural wythe with exterior insulation and a rainscreen cladding wythe that carries no structural load and needs no footing accommodation beyond its own light cladding weight; the double-wythe footing must be sized (by the engineer) for two structural or quasi-structural wythes side by side, generally requiring a wider footing or a stepped/shelved top to land both wythes, and needs the added corbel-limit and bond-break detailing above that the single-wythe wall does not need at all.

### From the owner-made and alternative materials research (draft A)

**7. For interlocking dry-stack CEB (mortarless walls), how is a wall system proven structurally for permitting when the units interlock rather than bed in mortar, and what test stands in for a masonry prism?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** No prescriptive Oregon or model-code path exists for a mortarless interlocking compressed-earth-block (CEB) wall; the route is ORSC's alternative-materials provision plus engineering testing. ORSC (following IRC) Section R104.11, "Alternative materials, design and methods of construction and equipment," states: "The provisions of this code are not intended to prevent the installation of any material or to prohibit any design or method of construction not specifically prescribed by this code, provided that any such alternative has been approved... An alternative material, design or method of construction shall be approved where the building official finds that the proposed alternative is satisfactory and complies with the intent of the provisions of this code... not less than the equivalent of that prescribed in this code in terms of quality, strength, effectiveness, fire resistance, durability, and safety... the building official shall have the authority to require tests as evidence of compliance" (up.codes summary of IRC/ORSC R104.11, https://up.codes/s/alternative-materials-equipment-methods-of-construction-and-design). This is the legal mechanism; it requires the building official's case-by-case sign-off, which is a jurisdiction-specific negotiation, not a settled statewide value.

For the engineering evidence itself, published research on dry-stack interlocking CEB masonry (e.g., "Characterization of dry-stack interlocking compressed earth blocks," Materials and Structures, Springer, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1617/s11527-014-0379-3; and the related conference paper "Interlocking Compressed Earth Block Walls: Out-Of-Plane Structural Response," https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/WCEE2012_0767.pdf) uses ASTM C1314, Standard Test Method for Compressive Strength of Masonry Prisms, adapted by stacking dry-interlocked (unmortared) units instead of mortared ones, as the compressive-strength stand-in for a conventional mortared masonry prism, and separately tests the dry bed-joint interfaces for shear/friction capacity (since a mortarless joint has no bond strength, only friction and mechanical interlock, which a standard mortared-prism test does not capture). Individual CEB unit compressive strength is separately tested to ASTM D1633, Standard Test Methods for Compressive Strength of Molded Soil-Cement Cylinders (soil-cement, not portland-cement, so this is the correct unit-strength standard for stabilized earth block, not ASTM C90 which governs conventional CMU).

The closest thing to an adopted prescriptive code for any dry-stack earthen wall in the U.S. is New Mexico's Earthen Building Materials Code (14.7.4 NMAC), which does permit dry-stacked compressed earth block, but under different conditions than a true mechanically-interlocking mortarless block: it requires "adequate adhesion... demonstrated," the underlying block "swept clean of any debris larger than 1/16 in. and sufficient water applied to the surface of the head and bed joints to create adequate cohesion," a minimum wall thickness of 10 in., and mandatory stucco on the exterior and plaster on the interior (14.7.4 NMAC, https://www.rld.nm.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/14.7.4-Earthen-Bldg-Integrated.pdf). This is a useful precedent showing a state has legislated a dry-stack earthen path, but it is New Mexico law, not Oregon's, and it describes a water-cohesion dry-stack method (closer to adobe practice) rather than a purely mechanical interlocking system like the Hydraform-style block named in this question's lead. Oregon has adopted nothing equivalent; a genuinely interlocking, water-free dry-stack CEB wall in Oregon would need project-specific engineering plus the ASTM C1314/D1633 testing above, submitted through R104.11.

**8. What mortar mix, cement content, sand spec, and mesh layup define structural ferrocement, and how thin can a loadbearing element go?**

**13. What is the real difference between mortar and grout (and why is neither ordinary concrete), why must grout be a very fluid 8-11 in slump while mortar is stiff, and what goes wrong if the owner packs cores with leftover stiff mortar instead of grout?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The Structural System and Concrete Design chapter's Reinforced Masonry Walls table already states the core numeric facts: mortar is ASTM C270 (Type S at 8 in. minimum slump is not applicable; mortar is a stiff, low-slump material by design so it can be troweled and hold a unit's position under its own weight without slumping), and grout is ASTM C476, specified at "8 in. minimum slump (10-11 in. preferred for self-consolidating fine grout)." Mortar, grout, and ordinary structural concrete are three different materials with three different jobs even though all three are portland-cement-based:

- **Mortar's job** is to bed and align masonry units and transfer compressive and some shear load between them at the joint; it needs to be stiff enough to hold a unit level and plumb the instant it is placed, so it is proportioned for workability and initial stiffness (typically higher sand-to-cement ratio, less water) rather than for flowing into anything.
- **Grout's job** is the opposite: to flow, under its own weight and light consolidation, into the narrow, often rebar-congested vertical cores of a CMU wall so it fully encases the reinforcing steel and bonds the steel to the block, without the mason doing anything to force it in. This is why ASTM C476 grout is specified at a much higher slump (8-11 in.) than mortar; the fluidity is not a mistake or a lower-quality version of mortar, it is the entire functional requirement.
- **Ordinary structural concrete** is proportioned for the strength and durability of a poured structural section (footing, slab, wall) with coarse aggregate sized for open formwork, not for flowing around closely-spaced vertical rebar inside 1-2 in. wide masonry cores; concrete's coarser aggregate would bridge and jam in a narrow CMU cell the way grout's finer aggregate (see item 14) will not.

The mechanism behind grout's high slump is not simply "wetter mortar": ASTM C476 grout is deliberately proportioned with excess water beyond what full hydration needs, because the surrounding CMU is itself absorptive and wicks the excess water out of the grout within minutes of placement, leaving the grout at an effectively lower, well-consolidated water-cement ratio in place even though it was mixed and placed wet. This is the same principle a dry block's suction plays in Type S/N mortar bond development, and it is why grout cannot simply be reduced in slump to make it "less messy": reducing the mix water reduces the flow the grout needs to actually reach and encase the rebar in the first place.

If the owner packs cores with leftover stiff mortar instead of proper grout, the mortar cannot flow around the vertical reinforcing bar or into the corners of the cell; it bridges and voids around the steel, leaving air pockets that the bar sits in rather than being encased. The practical failure modes are: (1) the reinforcing steel loses its bond to the surrounding cementitious material, so the wall does not develop the tension capacity the design assumed the rebar would provide; (2) voids around unprotected steel become a corrosion-initiation point, since the mortar's lower water content and finer/denser packing near an unconsolidated void does not provide the same alkaline, oxygen-excluding protection a fully consolidated grout envelope does; (3) the mortar itself, proportioned for joint bedding rather than for the different shrinkage and bond behavior grout needs inside a confined cell, is not evaluated or accepted by ASTM C476 for this use, so an inspector who catches stiff mortar substituted for grout has grounds to reject the work outright. This is a real, documented owner-builder mistake, not a hypothetical: it happens when a mason tries to economize by using mortar left over from laying block rather than mixing a separate grout batch, and it is exactly the kind of workmanship failure special inspection at grout pours (already required above 5 ft lift height per the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter's citation of ORSC R606.3.5.1) is designed to catch.

**14. When is fine grout required versus coarse grout (ASTM C476), how is the choice driven by cell/cavity size and rebar congestion, and what are the proportions of each?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** ASTM C476 defines two grout types by aggregate content: fine grout (portland cement, sand, and water only, no coarse aggregate) and coarse grout (portland cement, sand, water, and pea-gravel-size coarse aggregate). **15. What are the maximum grout lift and pour heights, when is consolidation (puddling/vibration) and reconsolidation required, and how do owner mistakes here create voids, blowouts, or grout that shrinks away from the block?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The Structural System and Concrete Design chapter's Reinforcement, Grout, And Lap Lengths section already gives the controlling numbers, sourced to ORSC R606.3.5.1 and TMS 602: grout lift maximum to avoid the cleanout/special-inspection trigger is 5 ft; ORSC R606.3.5.1 allows up to an 8 ft maximum lift, but cleanouts at the wall base are required once a single grout pour (which can consist of multiple lifts) exceeds 5 ft, and special inspection during grouting is required when the pour exceeds 8 ft. **16. Is self-consolidating grout (SCG) a smart option for a solo owner-builder who can't reliably vibrate every lift, what slump-flow and visual-stability index does it need, and what are the cost and segregation risks?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The Structural System and Concrete Design chapter's Reinforced Masonry Walls table already documents self-consolidating fine grout at "10-11 in." slump as the "preferred" high end of the ASTM C476 range, which is consistent with this question's premise. **17. How much does grout shrink as it cures, why must it carry excess water to flow (which the unit then reabsorbs), and what technique prevents the shrinkage gap that would let water track straight down a grouted core?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** This question's mechanism is answered directly by item 13's explanation of why grout is proportioned wet: ASTM C476 grout is deliberately mixed with more water than full cement hydration requires, because that extra water is what gives the mix the 8-11 in. slump needed to flow into a narrow, rebar-congested cell without any mechanical forcing; the surrounding CMU is absorptive and wicks a substantial fraction of that excess water out of the grout within minutes of placement (this is the same absorptive/suction mechanism that develops mortar-to-unit bond at a bed joint), which is what allows the grout to end up at a well-consolidated, lower effective water-cement ratio in place despite having been placed wet. Reconsolidation is the direct, single-technique answer to this question; skipping it is what leaves the exact shrinkage gap the question describes, which becomes a preferential path for any water that later reaches the top of that cell (e.g., through capillary movement at a top-of-wall bond beam that is not itself protected by a coping or another course above it) to track straight down inside the wall rather than being stopped at the block's dense, unbroken cell wall.

**18. Thermal and moisture movement cracking: how much do clay brick (irreversible long-term moisture expansion + thermal) and concrete block (drying shrinkage + thermal) actually move, why do they move in opposite directions, and how does that differential tear a rigid double-wythe wall that lacks the right joints?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The mechanism is stated in item 4; this item adds the numeric spacing consequence. Fired clay brick begins absorbing atmospheric moisture as soon as it leaves the kiln and expands slowly and permanently (irreversibly) over years, on top of a smaller reversible thermal expansion/contraction cycle; concrete masonry units lose curing and mix water as they dry after manufacture and shrink, also permanently, on top of their own reversible thermal cycle. The two materials' irreversible long-term movements run in opposite directions (brick grows, CMU shrinks) for opposite physical reasons (moisture uptake vs. moisture loss), which is exactly why a single joint system cannot serve both wythes: BIA TN 28D states plainly that a BV/CMU wall "will include both control joints and veneer expansion joints," that "control joints are installed in the concrete or CMU wythe to manage the shrinkage," and that these "are not required to align with the veneer expansion joints in the brick and may have separate spacing requirements" (p.12).

The specific magnitude of each material's movement (e.g., a stated percentage or inches-per-100-feet figure for clay brick's long-term moisture expansion, or for CMU drying shrinkage) was not located as a directly quotable free-sourced number in this research pass; BIA Technical Note 18 (the base note in the 18-series, distinct from 18A which covers joint design) is understood from secondary references to be the BIA document that quantifies brick's moisture-expansion coefficient, but its primary text was not successfully retrieved in this pass (see item 24 for the retrieval difficulty). Item 24 (BIA's expansion-joint spacing formula) is the practical, already-verified proxy for "how much movement must be accommodated," because that formula is itself derived from the expected total movement over a given wall length.

If the right joints are omitted or under-spaced: the brick wythe's ongoing, one-directional growth has nowhere to go and instead loads the wall in compression along its length; that compressive stress concentrates at the weakest points (corners, offsets, above lintels, at shelf angles) and shows up as diagonal step-cracking through mortar joints, spalling at compressed corners, or in severe cases outward bowing/bulging of the veneer as it buckles under its own restrained growth. Meanwhile the CMU backup's shrinkage in the opposite direction, if its control joints are missing or too widely spaced, cracks the block wythe itself, typically as a vertical crack running through the block cores at a weak point (an opening, a wall-height change, or a corner) rather than through a joint, because the CMU's own tensile capacity is lower than the shrinkage stress that accumulates over an unjointed run. Because the two wythes are tied together, a differential-movement crack in one wythe is not isolated from the other; the ties transmit some of the restrained load between wythes even though they are designed to allow slip (item 4), so a wall built without adequate expansion and control joints in both materials is a wall designed to crack, not a wall that might crack.

**19. Efflorescence: what are the three required ingredients (soluble salts + moisture + a migration path), what distinguishes primary "new building bloom" from recurring secondary efflorescence, and what are the concrete prevention levers — low-alkali cement, IWR admixture, flashing/weeps, keeping units dry and covered on site?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** BIA Technical Note 23A, *Efflorescence, Causes and Prevention* (https://www.gobrick.com/media/file/23a-tn23a.pdf), is the free, directly on-point source. Its stated mechanism confirms the three-ingredient framing in this question's lead: efflorescence requires soluble salts present in the wall system, water to dissolve them, and a path for the resulting solution to migrate to and evaporate at an exposed surface, leaving the dissolved salt behind as a visible crystalline deposit. BIA TN 23A states that the salts "most commonly come from the materials in contact with the brick, such as mortar, grout, concrete masonry, concrete, cast stone, or soil rather than the brick itself" (BIA masonrymagazine.com summary of TN23A, https://www.masonrymagazine.com/blog/2022/01/26/causes-of-efflorescence-and-efflorescence-test-method-summary/), and that "the primary source of moisture for the occurrence of efflorescence is rain water that penetrates or comes in contact with masonry."

**New building bloom vs. recurring secondary efflorescence:** primary or "new building bloom" is the temporary efflorescence that appears in the first year or so of a new wall's life, driven by the water already present in the fresh mortar and grout mix itself (and any construction-phase rain the units and mortar absorbed before the wall was enclosed) working its way out and carrying salts to the surface as the wall dries out for the first time; this is expected, generally self-limiting, and typically resolves as the wall completes its initial drying cycle. Recurring secondary efflorescence is different in cause and more serious: it indicates an ongoing external water source, most often a drainage-cavity or flashing/weep failure of the kind described in items 3, 22, 25, 31, and 36, repeatedly wetting the wall from outside (or, less commonly, a persistent interior moisture source) and dissolving new salt each cycle; secondary efflorescence that keeps returning after the first year is a diagnostic signal to inspect the flashing, weeps, and cavity for a moisture-intrusion defect, not a cosmetic problem to clean and ignore.

**Prevention levers, all consistent with this question's own lead and confirmed against BIA TN 23A's general framing:**
- **Low-alkali cement:** BIA TN 23A identifies "the alkali content of portland cement" as "one of the principal contributors to efflorescence in mortar and grout," and states codes "generally address this by specifying low alkali cement when required" (ASTM C150 defines the low-alkali portland cement option this references).
- **IWR admixture:** see item 12; reducing the water the mortar and grout absorb and hold directly reduces the water available to dissolve and carry salts, which is the mechanistic reason IWR admixtures are marketed as an efflorescence-reduction measure, not merely a water-repellency measure.
- **Flashing and weeps:** the single most important lever for preventing recurring secondary efflorescence specifically, because a functioning drainage cavity (items 3, 25, 31) removes the ongoing external water source before it can dissolve and carry salts to the surface at all.
- **Keeping units dry and covered on site:** BIA TN 23A's framing that moisture is the essential ingredient extends to construction-phase handling: brick and block stored uncovered in Oregon's wet season absorb rainwater before they are ever laid, adding to the water available for the first-year bloom described above; covering palletized units on site is a low-cost, direct-acting prevention step.

**20. Peeling and blistering of surface coatings: why do film-forming paints/sealers peel and spall masonry in a freezing climate by trapping vapor, and what is the vapor-permeable alternative (penetrating silane/siloxane), including its limits (don't seal a wet or salt-laden wall)?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Film-forming coatings (paints, elastomeric coatings, and film-type "sealers" that leave a continuous surface skin rather than penetrating) work by physically blocking the masonry's pore structure at the surface. In a freezing climate, this creates a mechanism for damage rather than protection: any moisture that is already in the wall, or that gets in from an unrelated path (a flashing or joint defect, or simple vapor drive from inside the building), can no longer evaporate outward through the masonry's natural pore network because the film blocks that path; the trapped moisture then freezes and expands during a freeze cycle, and the expansion pressure has nowhere to relieve itself except by pushing the film coating off the surface (peeling/blistering) or by spalling the face of the brick or block itself as the ice pressure exceeds the material's tensile strength at the surface layer. The coating did not cause the water to be there, but by sealing the one path the water had to leave, it converts an otherwise-manageable amount of moisture into a freeze-thaw damage mechanism.

**The vapor-permeable alternative:** silane and siloxane penetrating water repellents work by a different physical mechanism entirely; rather than forming a surface film, the silane/siloxane molecules penetrate into the pore structure and react chemically with the silica and (for silane specifically) the calcium hydroxide present in the masonry or mortar, forming a hydrophobic lining on the inside of the pores themselves. Industry product literature reviewed in this pass (Sika, Euclid Chemical, PROSOCO, https://prosoco.com/product/siloxane-pd/) consistently describes this as leaving the pore structure open, so that liquid water is repelled from entering (reducing bulk water absorption, and with it freeze-thaw saturation and efflorescence risk per item 19) while water vapor can still pass through the wall in both directions; this is the basis for calling these products "breathable." Because silane's bonding mechanism specifically requires reaction with calcium hydroxide, the substrate "must be alkaline (high pH) for this chemical reaction to take place" (industry product-chemistry summary reviewed in this pass), which is a real application-window constraint: a silane treatment applied to fully carbonated, aged concrete or masonry with reduced surface alkalinity may not bond and perform as intended, and siloxane (which bonds primarily to silica rather than relying on the same calcium-hydroxide reaction) is the more broadly applicable choice on older or already-carbonated surfaces.

**Stated limits, consistent across the product literature reviewed:** a penetrating silane/siloxane sealer should not be applied to a wall that is already wet at the time of application, because the repellent needs to penetrate into and bond within the pore structure, and standing or absorbed moisture in the pores blocks that penetration and prevents proper cure/bond; and it should not be applied over a wall carrying active soluble-salt contamination (i.e., over efflorescence that has not been addressed) because sealing the surface traps the same salts and the moisture needed to keep dissolving and depositing them, worsening rather than resolving the efflorescence problem described in item 19. The correct sequence, consistent with general masonry maintenance practice, is: resolve the underlying moisture source (flashing, weeps, cavity), let the wall dry, then apply a penetrating breathable sealer, not the reverse.

**21. Sulfate attack below grade: how do sulfates in Oregon soils/groundwater attack ordinary portland mortar (expansive ettringite), and when does the owner need Type II or Type V (low-C3A) cement, a denser low-w/c mix, and parging/damp-proofing on the below-grade wythe?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Sulfate ions present in soil or groundwater react chemically with tricalcium aluminate (C3A), a mineral phase present in ordinary (Type I) portland cement, to form ettringite, a mineral that occupies substantially more volume than the reactants it formed from; because this reaction happens inside an already-hardened, confined cementitious matrix, the expansive ettringite growth generates internal pressure that cracks and eventually disintegrates the paste from within, which is the mechanism of sulfate attack. ASTM C150 controls this by limiting the C3A content of the sulfate-resisting cement types: "ASTM C150 limits tricalcium aluminate (C3A) levels for Type II and V portland cements of 8% and 5%, respectively, in order to control sulfate resistance" (summary of ASTM C150 limits reviewed in this pass, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4847577/ and https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/sulfate-attack). Type II is the "moderate" sulfate-resistant option; Type V is reserved, per general cement-industry description, "only in concrete exposed to a severe sulfate action, mainly where soils or groundwater have a high sulfate content."

**When the owner needs which:** this is fundamentally a site-specific geotechnical fact, not a value this research file can supply in the abstract; sulfate exposure severity is normally classified from a soil/groundwater sulfate test (commonly reported as water-soluble sulfate percentage by mass of soil, or sulfate concentration in groundwater) taken as part of the geotechnical investigation already called for elsewhere in this project (the Soil, Ground Prep, Drainage, and Foundation Risk chapter and the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter's engineering scope). ACI 318's exposure-category table (Category S0/S1/S2/S3, referenced in the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter's existing ACI 318 citations for concrete durability) is the standard framework a geotechnical engineer or structural engineer of record uses to translate a measured sulfate concentration into a required cement type and maximum water-cement ratio; this research pass did not attempt to re-derive ACI 318's exposure-category thresholds, since the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter's Concrete Durability And Mix Discipline section is the more appropriate home for that citation and this file defers to it rather than duplicating it.

**Low w/c matters as much as cement type**, per this question's own lead and consistent with general durability literature: a denser, lower-permeability mortar or grout (achieved by a lower water-cement ratio) physically slows the rate at which sulfate-laden groundwater can migrate into the cementitious matrix in the first place, which reduces the rate of ettringite formation regardless of cement type; specifying Type II or V cement without also controlling w/c addresses only half of the exposure, and a genuinely severe-sulfate site may need both the low-C3A cement and a maximum w/c limit together.

**Parging/damp-proofing:** this is the physical, non-chemical complement to cement selection, already established as a requirement in the Envelope chapter's Slab Edge And Below-Grade Envelope Interface section (dampproofing per ORSC R406.1 by default, waterproofing per R406.2 where a high water table or severe soil-water conditions are found); keeping bulk groundwater off the below-grade wythe's surface via parging and a damp-proofing or waterproofing membrane reduces the sulfate-laden water's access to the mortar in the first place, which is a more reliable primary defense than cement chemistry alone, since even sulfate-resisting cement is not literally sulfate-proof, only slower to attack.

**22. "Mortar dropping" bridging the cavity: how do droppings and "snots" on ties bridge the air gap and pipe water across to the inner wythe, and what site technique keeps a double-wythe cavity clean, beveling the bed away from the cavity, drawing a cavity board, mortar-catch mesh, and a minimum clear airspace?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** BIA TN 28D's "Drainage Materials and Mortar Collection Devices" section (p.12) is directly on point: "To the extent possible, the mason should prevent mortar droppings from falling into the air space. Excessive mortar droppings can render flashing and weeps ineffective in draining water to the exterior." The mechanism this question asks about, droppings piling up on the flashing and physically bridging the air gap, works because mortar is itself absorptive and, once it accumulates into a continuous pile or smear touching both the back of the outer wythe and the flashing (or the face of the inner wythe), it wicks water across that bridge by capillary action rather than letting the water fall cleanly down the open cavity to the weeps; the same bridging effect happens at an individual tie if enough mortar "snot" (excess mortar squeezed out at the tie's embedment point) accumulates on the tie and stays wet, giving water a solid path to track across the cavity along the tie itself rather than dripping off it.

BIA TN 28D confirms good workmanship, not a product, is "the primary method of creating a functional air space," and describes three concrete site techniques, consistent with this question's own lead:

- **Mortar collection devices or full-height drainage media** installed in the air space "can be installed... to help prevent obstruction by mortar," but BIA TN 28D is explicit that these products do not replace workmanship: "the use of these materials does not negate the importance of good workmanship... Use of drainage materials does not eliminate the risk for mortar bridging and may result in simply moving the mortar bridging higher within the air space." Because of this, "many manufacturers require the flashing to be installed a minimum of 6 to 8 in. above the top level of the collection device to provide adequate protection against moisture ingress when mortar droppings accumulate" on top of the device itself.
- **Minimum clear airspace:** BIA TN 28D's own recommendation of a 2 in. minimum air space (item 3) is stated specifically because narrower spaces are harder to keep clean and to tool without dragging mortar across the gap; this question's lead figure of "~2 in. minimum clear cavity" matches BIA TN 28D's recommendation exactly.
- **Beveling the bed joint away from the cavity, and drawing a batten/board up the cavity as the wall is built:** these are standard masonry craft techniques (not separately named in BIA TN 28D's text but consistent with its workmanship framing) belonging properly in the Masonry Craft chapter's hands-on laying instruction: beveling the mortar bed so excess squeezes toward the face side rather than the cavity side reduces what falls inward in the first place, and drawing a wood batten or cavity board up the wall as courses are laid (removed and cleaned periodically) physically catches and removes droppings before they can build up on the flashing below. BIA TN 28D's own workmanship section elsewhere in the document underscores that "all mortar joints in both the exterior and interior masonry wythe should be completely filled as the brick... are laid" with "a minimum 5/8 in. of mortar cover from the exterior face of the brick" (item 2), which is the same disciplined-joint-tooling practice that keeps droppings to a minimum.

**23. Expansion joints (brick, which expands) vs control joints (CMU, which shrinks): what is the physical difference, how is each built (compressible filler + sealant vs bond-breaker/weakened plane + backer rod + sealant), and why does using the wrong one, or none, guarantee cracking?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** This is the construction-detail companion to the mechanism already established in items 4 and 18. An **expansion joint** in brick is a genuine full-depth gap cut through the brick wythe, sized and filled specifically to stay compressible and absorb the brick's ongoing, one-directional growth: BIA TN 28D describes it as "an opening through the brick wythe that is closed with a sealant joint and compressible filler materials" (p.12), meaning the joint is built with a soft, resilient backer material (typically closed-cell foam backer rod) set to a controlled depth, then finished with an elastomeric sealant at the face; the joint starts at its widest built dimension and is expected to narrow (close) over the wall's life as the brick grows into it, which is why both the backer rod and the sealant must be selected for high compressibility, not merely for weathertightness.

A **control joint** in CMU is a different device solving a different problem: rather than resisting compression, the CMU wythe is expected to shrink and pull apart over its life, so a control joint is a deliberately engineered weak plane, built by interrupting the bond at that vertical joint (using a bond-breaker, commonly a building-paper or plastic strip, or a purpose-made rubber/PVC control-joint block accessory) so that the wall's inevitable shrinkage cracking is forced to occur at that pre-planned, sealed location rather than randomly through the face of a block somewhere else in the wall. A control joint is typically finished with backer rod and sealant at the face, similar in visible appearance to an expansion joint, but it is built to open (widen) over time as the CMU shrinks, the physical opposite of the brick expansion joint's built-to-close behavior.

Because the two materials move in opposite directions (item 18), using the wrong type of joint, or building either one without accounting for its correct expected direction of movement, defeats its purpose: an expansion joint packed too tight, or with an incompressible filler, gives the growing brick nowhere to go and the wall cracks elsewhere instead, exactly as if no joint existed at all; a control joint that is not actually a debonded weak plane (for example, mortared solid through instead of bond-broken) does not concentrate the CMU's shrinkage stress at that location, so the wythe cracks unpredictably somewhere else in the wall face instead of at the planned, sealed, cosmetically-controlled joint. Omitting either joint type entirely removes the only planned outlet for that wythe's ongoing movement, which does not prevent the movement, it only removes the planned place for it to happen; the stress accumulates until it exceeds the material's own tensile or bond strength and cracks the wall at whatever point is weakest, which is normally a corner, an opening, or a change in wall height or support condition, exactly the locations item 24's placement rules target.

**24. What spacing and placement rules govern brick expansion joints and CMU control joints (BIA expansion-joint formula, NCMA/CMHA control-joint criteria, plus joints at openings, corners, offsets, and under shelf angles), and how should the owner lay them out before the first course goes down?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Two independent, free-sourced spacing systems govern the two wythes, confirming item 18's point that they are not the same calculation:

**Brick expansion joints (BIA Technical Note 18A):** the theoretical spacing formula is Se = wj x ej / 0.09, where Se is the spacing between expansion joints, wj is the joint width (typically sized like a normal mortar joint, 3/8 to 1/2 in.), and ej is the percent compressibility of the joint filler material (the least compressible of the sealant, backer rod, and filler, taken together). A worked example commonly cited from BIA TN 18A: a 1/2 in. joint with a sealant system rated at 50 percent extensibility gives Se = (0.5 in. x 50) / 0.09 = 278 in., or 23 ft-2 in. (WebSearch summary of BIA TN 18A, https://www.studocu.com/row/document/hawassa-university/reinforced-concrete/expansion-joints/75085093 and https://masonryadvisorycouncil.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/8-Movement-Joints-article.pdf). BIA TN 28D's own summary states the practical rule of thumb more directly, and this is the number confirmed against the primary-adjacent document actually retrieved in full in this research pass: "Generally the spacing of vertical expansion joints should not exceed 25 ft (7.6 m) in walls without openings and 20 ft (6.1 m) in walls with multiple openings" (BIA TN 28D, p.12). BIA TN 28D adds specific placement triggers beyond simple linear spacing: a vertical expansion joint is recommended "where site walls adjoin buildings and at the corners of large openings," and "building corners should have a vertical expansion joint located within 10 ft of the corner," and "plan offsets and setbacks of a wall should also include a vertical expansion joint on inside corners." Horizontal expansion joints are required wherever the brickwork above needs an independent bearing point, "usually accomplished by a shelf angle," and sit between the bottom of that shelf angle and the brick below it; since this project's rigid CMU backing carries no prescriptive height limit for shelf angles (BIA TN 28D states shelf angles become necessary only "at a height of approximately 50 ft" for veneer on a rigid backing), a single-story or two-story residential wall on this project may not need a shelf angle or its associated horizontal expansion joint at all, unless the engineer of record specifies one for another reason.

**CMU control joints (NCMA/CMHA TEK 10-2C, referenced by name in the code itself):** the up.codes ORSC/IRC citation confirms the code explicitly defers to this document rather than stating its own number: "Spacing and placement of control joints must be in accordance with NCMA TEK 10-2C or 10-3" (https://up.codes/s/concrete-masonry-control-joints). TEK 10-2C's empirical spacing rule, as summarized in secondary sources reviewed in this pass, is a maximum of 25 ft or 1.5 times the wall height, whichever is less, provided the wall carries a minimum of 0.025 sq in. of horizontal joint-reinforcement steel per foot of wall height (commonly satisfied by 9-gauge ladder-type joint reinforcement at 16 in. o.c. vertically, which the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter's Prescriptive Masonry Walls table already specifies for this project's inner wythe for an unrelated, seismic-crack-control reason, meaning this project's CMU wythe likely already carries the reinforcement TEK 10-2C's 25 ft/1.5H rule assumes). Control joints are additionally located, per the same secondary-source summary, "at areas of stress concentrations, such as changes in wall height or thickness or near wall openings or corners," at "a maximum spacing of one-half control joint spacing from corners" (i.e., the first control joint from any corner should be no farther than half the normal running spacing).

**Layout sequence, consistent with both documents' framing:** because BIA TN 28D states explicitly that control joints and expansion joints are not required to align and may have entirely separate spacing (item 18), the owner cannot lay out one joint grid and use it for both wythes; the practical sequence is to lay out the CMU control-joint grid first against TEK 10-2C's 25 ft/1.5H rule and the required corner/opening/height-change triggers, lay out the brick expansion-joint grid separately against BIA TN 18A's formula (or the 20-25 ft rule of thumb) and its own corner/opening/shelf-angle triggers, and mark both grids on the foundation or the first course before laying begins, since a joint that is not planned before the wall goes up cannot be added cleanly afterward without cutting into finished masonry.

**26. How does the owner tell an active (still-moving) crack from a dormant one, and choose the right fix — repoint, route-and-seal with elastomeric sealant, or recognize that the crack is really a missing movement joint rather than a "mortar problem"?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** This is standard masonry-diagnostics practice rather than a single codified test, and this research pass located the method as widely and consistently described across general masonry-repair and building-science literature rather than as a single primary standard document, so it is presented here as settled general practice, not a specific code citation.

**Telling active from dormant:** the standard field method is to install a simple crack monitor, either a purpose-made two-piece plastic or metal crack gauge (with a scribed grid that reads both the width and any lateral/vertical offset across the crack) or an improvised version (two reference marks or a strip of glass/tile bridging the crack, which will itself crack if the movement continues), placed across the crack and read at intervals over a period long enough to span a full seasonal thermal and moisture cycle, commonly a full year for a masonry wall since brick and CMU movement (items 4, 18) is driven partly by annual moisture and temperature cycling, not only by a single event. A crack that shows continuing width change (typically widening, though it can also show cyclic opening and closing that never fully closes back to its original width) across that monitoring period is active; a crack that shows no measurable change is dormant, meaning whatever caused it (a one-time event such as a foundation settlement increment, an impact, or an initial shrinkage/expansion cycle that has now completed) is no longer acting.

**Choosing the fix, matched to cause, not to appearance:**
- **Repointing** (removing and replacing failed mortar in ordinary bed/head joints) is the correct fix only for genuine mortar-joint deterioration, weathering, or a small, confirmed-dormant crack that runs through mortar joints rather than through the masonry units themselves; repointing a joint that sits within an active movement zone will simply crack again on the next movement cycle, because rigid repointing mortar cannot accommodate ongoing motion the way a designed movement joint can.
- **Route-and-seal with an elastomeric sealant and backer rod** is the correct fix for a confirmed-dormant crack that is too wide or too irregular for simple repointing, or for a crack the owner wants to seal against water entry without full remediation; because the sealant is flexible, this technique can also tolerate a limited amount of ongoing minor movement, which is why it is sometimes used even on cracks that show some low-level activity, provided the sealant and backer rod are sized with enough movement capability for what the monitoring showed.
- **Recognizing a missing movement joint:** if the crack recurs after repointing, recurs at a repeating interval along a long wall run, or the monitoring shows genuinely ongoing, cyclical movement rather than a settled one-time event, the crack is very likely the wall's own material telling the owner where an expansion or control joint should have been placed under items 23-24's spacing and placement rules; the correct fix in this case is not to keep repointing or re-sealing the same location repeatedly, but to cut and install a proper movement joint (expansion joint if the crack is in the brick wythe and the movement is compressive/growth-driven, control joint if it is in the CMU wythe and the movement is shrinkage-driven) at that location, following the same construction detail described in item 23.

**27. How does the owner run and read a test panel / mock-up for water penetration (e.g., an ASTM E514-style wall spray) and for appearance, tooling, and drainage before that detail is built into the finished home?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** This question's lead names ASTM E514 as the model, and that standard is real and directly relevant, but with an important distinction this research pass surfaced: ASTM E514/E514M, *Standard Test Method for Water Penetration and Leakage Through Masonry*, is specifically "a laboratory test method... designed to test laboratory fabricated wall specimens" (https://www.appliedtesting.com/standards/astm-e514-water-penetration-and-leakage-through-masonry), meaning it measures water that penetrates all the way through a masonry specimen under controlled, pressurized wind-driven-rain conditions in a lab chamber, not a field procedure an owner-builder runs on an actual mock-up wall built on site. The field-appropriate counterpart standard, more relevant to an owner-builder's on-site mock-up, is ASTM C1601, *Standard Test Method for Field Determination of Water Penetration of Masonry Wall Surfaces*, which "measures surface water penetration" on an as-built wall or mock-up in place, using a spray rack and a timed water application, rather than requiring a sealed pressure chamber (https://www.astm.org/c1601-22.html).

For an owner-builder wanting to prove a detail before it is built into the finished home, the practical sequence is:

1. **Build a genuine mock-up**, not just a sample panel: a section of the actual wall assembly (both wythes, the actual tie type and spacing, the actual insulation, the actual flashing and weep detail at whichever condition is being tested, such as a window sill or the wall base) built at real scale and with the same materials, workmanship, and mason who will build the finished wall, since the whole point of testing before commitment is that field-workmanship variables (joint tooling, mortar consistency, tie embedment per item 2) matter as much as the design detail itself.
2. **Water-penetration test the mock-up** using an ASTM C1601-style field spray-rack procedure (a fixed-rate water spray applied to the wall face for a controlled duration), watching the interior/cavity side for any moisture penetration through the assembly, since this is the field-appropriate stand-in for the lab-only ASTM E514 named in the question. A owner-builder without access to certified third-party field-test equipment can still run an informal version of this same spray procedure as a self-check, though it would not carry the certified test-report weight a design professional or inspector might require for a disputed or high-stakes detail.
3. **Read appearance, tooling, and drainage** visually and by direct inspection rather than by a formal ASTM procedure: check the mortar joint profile and color against the intended finish, confirm the weeps are actually open and unobstructed (item 25), and confirm water sprayed onto the wall face and into the cavity via the flashing actually appears at the weeps within a reasonable time rather than pooling or disappearing (indicating an unintended leak path elsewhere).

**32. Why do masonry wall ties corrode and fail, which tie materials and spacing keep the outer wythe from bulging/cracking, and how are ties detailed so they don't conduct water inward?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The Structural System and Concrete Design chapter's Wall Ties For Cavity Walls section already establishes the core corrosion facts (stainless ASTM A580 Type 304/316 preferred, carbon steel ASTM A82 acceptable non-coastal with protective coating, mixing stainless and carbon steel creates galvanic corrosion) and the tie-frequency/spacing table; this item adds the mechanism and the water-conduction detailing this question specifically asks about, drawing on BIA TN 28D and general masonry-durability literature:

**Why ties corrode and fail:** a masonry cavity wall's entire premise, per item 31, is that the cavity will get wet; a carbon-steel tie sitting inside that cavity, especially where it crosses the wet zone right behind a porous, absorptive brick wythe, is therefore in a chronically damp environment for its entire service life. If the tie's galvanized coating is thin, damaged during fabrication or installation, or simply consumed over decades of exposure (galvanizing is a sacrificial coating with a finite service life, not a permanent barrier), the underlying carbon steel begins to rust; rust occupies significantly more volume than the base steel it replaces, so a corroding tie embedded in a mortar joint physically expands within that joint. This expansion is the documented cause of a specific, recognizable failure pattern: horizontal cracking in the brick wythe appearing at regular intervals matching the tie spacing, because each corroding tie is pushing outward on the mortar joint it sits in, at the same vertical interval across the wall (this pattern is standard, widely documented masonry-forensics knowledge, consistent with the "Leads" note already in the question bank, though a single primary citation quantifying it was not separately retrieved in this pass). Left unaddressed, a wall with enough corroded, expanding, and eventually failing ties loses the mechanical connection between wythes entirely at those locations, which is the direct mechanism behind the outward bulging or bowing of a veneer wythe this question asks about: without functioning ties resisting the out-of-plane wind and seismic load the veneer sees, the unsupported brick wythe can lean, bow, or in severe cases detach and fall.

**Materials and spacing that prevent this:** stainless steel ties (already the manuscript's specified default) do not depend on a sacrificial coating and are the long-service-life answer; BIA TN 28D's Tables 1 and 2 (item 2) give the spacing that keeps each tie's tributary wall area within its rated capacity, which is the load-side half of preventing bulging (a tie that is not overloaded is less likely to be the one that fails first, even before corrosion is considered); using stainless throughout, rather than mixing stainless and carbon-steel components in the same tie assembly, avoids the galvanic corrosion the Structural System and Concrete Design chapter already flags as an accelerant.

**Detailing ties so they don't conduct water inward:** BIA TN 28D confirms this project's tie types (adjustable eye-and-pintle ties, ladder or tab-type joint reinforcement with adjustable ties, item 2) are two-piece systems specifically because the connection point itself, the small mechanical gap between the eye and the pintle, is where water could otherwise track directly from the wet outer wythe to the drier inner wythe along a solid metal path; this question's own lead names "a drip formed in the tie so water doesn't bridge" as the mechanism, and this is consistent with general two-piece adjustable-tie design practice, though BIA TN 28D's own text (reviewed in this pass) does not use the specific word "drip" for the tie itself, only for flashing and copings elsewhere in the document; a tie manufacturer's product literature should be checked to confirm whether the specific tie product selected for this project includes an integral drip feature, since this appears to be a product-specific detail rather than a universal TMS 402 requirement confirmed in this pass.

**35. How does solar-driven inward vapor drive from a rain-saturated brick/masonry outer wythe cause interior moisture, and what venting/cavity/vapor-control strategy stops it, especially where interior polyethylene is present?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** This question's lead names the exact source, Joseph Lstiburek's Building Science Corporation research report RR-0104, *Solar Driven Moisture in Brick Veneer*, and that document was retrieved and read directly in this research pass (https://buildingscience.com/documents/reports/rr-0104-solar-driven-moisture-in-brick-veneer/view).

**Mechanism, in the report's own terms:** brick is a reservoir cladding, meaning it absorbs and physically stores rainwater rather than shedding it entirely; when the sun then heats the wet brick, the report explains the physics plainly: "water vapor will move by vapor diffusion from a high concentration of water vapor to a lower concentration of water vapor. It will also move from a warm surface to a cold surface." A rain-soaked, sun-heated brick wythe is both a high-vapor-concentration surface and a warm surface relative to the cooler, drier interior of an air-conditioned building behind it, so vapor diffuses inward through the wall assembly, into the cavity and toward the interior, driven by both the moisture-concentration gradient and the temperature gradient at once.

**Why interior polyethylene makes it worse:** the report is direct about this being an unintended consequence of a measure designed to solve a different problem: "polyethylene vapor barriers were introduced to reduce the wetting of exterior wall cavities from interior sources of moisture during the winter months. This they did extremely well... unfortunately the reduction in drying to the interior caused by these vapor barriers has only recently begun to be understood." A poly sheet on the interior face of the wall assembly is an effective winter vapor barrier against moisture from inside the house moving outward into a cold wall, but it is also, by the same low-permeance property, a barrier against the wall's ability to dry inward during summer, which is exactly the direction the solar-driven moisture described above is trying to move; trapped between an absorptive, sun-heated brick reservoir outside and an impermeable poly sheet inside, the moisture accumulates in the wall cavity or in whatever permeable material (the report specifically names fiberboard sheathing) sits between them, leading to the mold and wood-decay problems the report investigated.

**Air conditioning as an aggravating factor:** the report states this directly too: "the colder the interior of the house in the summertime, the more moisture is drawn inwards out of the brick veneer into the wall cavity," meaning the vapor drive is not merely a hot-climate phenomenon; it gets worse specifically where the interior is mechanically cooled, widening the temperature gradient between the hot, wet exterior brick and the cold interior air.

**Strategy that stops it, consistent with this report and with the already-established Envelope chapter Vapor Control section:** the manuscript's existing design already avoids the specific failure condition this report describes; the Envelope chapter's Vapor Control section states plainly, "no interior polyethylene appears anywhere in this design," and instead relies on the masonry's own inherent low permeance plus a drained, vented cavity and exterior-side drying path. This is consistent with the report's implied fix (the report itself, per this pass's retrieval, "does not provide explicit design or venting solutions" beyond identifying less-permeable exterior sheathing as a partial mitigation, but its diagnosis directly supports the general building-science consensus, cited elsewhere in the Envelope chapter, that a wall needs to dry to at least one side, and a vented/drained cavity that lets vapor and incidental moisture escape outward through the weeps and the cavity's own airflow, rather than being trapped between two low-permeance layers, is the correct strategy for exactly this solar-driven-moisture mechanism). This project's cavity is already designed as a drained, ventilated wet zone (item 34) with no interior poly, which is the condition RR-0104's findings recommend against creating in the first place.

**36. Which flashing and drainage details at the cavity base, at openings, and at shelf angles are most often botched, and what sequence (flashing, drip edge, end dams, weeps) makes the cavity actually drain?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** BIA TN 28D's flashing and window/door sections (p.11-17, already drawn on in items 3, 6, 25, and 34) describe the correct sequence and, by implication and by explicit warning language, the points most often botched:

**Most commonly botched, per BIA TN 28D's own warning language (each phrase below is where the document specifically flags a common failure):**
- Weeps installed at the wrong course: "if weeps are installed one to two masonry courses above the flashing, they will not perform their intended function" (p.12), a specific, named installation error.
- End dams omitted where flashing is not continuous, letting water run off the unprotected end of a flashing run instead of staying contained (item 25).
- Flashing not extended far enough up the CMU backing, or not properly terminated into a bed joint or reglet, which lets water track behind the flashing's short leg rather than being fully captured (item 25's "minimum of 8 in." requirement exists specifically to prevent this).
- Mortar droppings accumulating on the flashing (items 22, 31), which is why BIA TN 28D requires manufacturers' drainage-collection devices to sit 6-8 in. below the flashing rather than flush with it.
- At window and door openings specifically, BIA TN 28D calls out that skipping the sill-flashing end dams is a direct path to a leak: "form end dams at the sides and the back of the sill flashing to form a pan beneath the window... fully seal or solder, as applicable, seams and corners in the sill flashing" (p.15-16); a sill flashing without end dams is not a pan at all, it is a flat sheet that lets water run off its own sides into the wall exactly where the window opening has already weakened the wythe.

**The sequence that makes the cavity actually drain, synthesized from BIA TN 28D's base-of-wall, opening, and parapet details together:** (1) flashing is installed first, sloped or at minimum flat and fully supported, extending up the backing wythe the required minimum and terminating into a bed joint or reglet; (2) end dams are formed at every point the flashing run is not continuous (both ends of a sill or head flashing, both ends of a base-of-wall run interrupted by a column or other structural element per item 25's cross-reference); (3) the flashing's outer edge extends to, and preferably beyond, the face of the brick to form a drip, so water reaching the flashing sheds clear of the wall face rather than running back underneath it; (4) weeps are placed immediately above the completed, end-dammed flashing, at the specified spacing (item 25), and kept physically open (screened, not filled) through the remainder of construction; (5) the cavity above the flashing is kept clear of mortar droppings as the wall is built (items 22, 31) so that water entering anywhere above that flashing line has an unobstructed path down to it. Any step skipped, or performed out of this order (for example, laying weeps before confirming the flashing beneath them is itself continuous and end-dammed), breaks the chain the whole drainage-wall premise depends on.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** BIA TN 28D's own reference list cites the exact document this question's lead names, "Building Science Insights 001: The Perfect Wall, July 2010" (Building Science Corporation, listed in BIA TN 28D's References section, item 3), confirming the "perfect wall" control-layer framework is the industry-recognized reference for this question, though this research pass retrieved BIA TN 28D's citation of it rather than the Building Science Insights 001 document itself; the general "perfect wall" principle (widely known in building-science literature independent of this specific citation) orders the control layers, from outside to inside, as: cladding, drainage/ventilation gap, water-resistive barrier, insulation, structure, with the air barrier and vapor control layer's exact position chosen so that the assembly can dry to at least one side and preferably both.

**Applied to this project's double-wythe wall in zone 4C**, consistent with BIA TN 28D's own recommended assembly (Figure 1, p.3) and the Envelope chapter's already-established Vapor Control section: brick veneer (cladding) outermost; the drained, vented air space/cavity functioning as both drainage plane and drying path (item 3); an air/moisture/vapor barrier membrane on the exterior face of the CMU backing "as required" per hygrothermal analysis (BIA TN 28D's own qualifier; it is not universally mandatory the way it would be over a wood-framed backing, since "the use of a WRB is not required by building codes for wall assemblies with masonry or concrete backup construction," p.7); continuous insulation within the cavity (item 3); the CMU structural inner wythe itself, which, per the Envelope chapter's Vapor Control section, provides Class III vapor-retarder behavior inherently as a low-permeance mass material; and painted drywall or parging at the interior face. This ordering keeps the insulation on the exterior (cold) side of the structural mass, which the Envelope chapter's Wall Insulation Strategy section already establishes as this project's governing thermal-mass principle, and it keeps every layer capable of drying outward through the ventilated cavity.

**Orders that trap moisture, consistent with RR-0104 (item 35) and the Envelope chapter's existing framing:** the specific combination the Envelope chapter's Vapor Control section already identifies as never used in this project's design, a Class I polyethylene vapor retarder on the interior of a wall that also has low-permeance exterior insulation or an impermeable exterior layer, "produces a wall that cannot dry in either direction." Applied to the double-wythe cavity wall specifically: placing a sealed, low-permeance membrane on the interior face of the CMU wythe in addition to (rather than instead of) the exterior air/vapor barrier on the cavity side of the CMU would sandwich the CMU wythe between two low-permeance layers, trapping any moisture that does get past the outer defenses (from a workmanship gap, a missed flashing lap, or the solar-driven vapor drive of item 35) with no path to leave. **39. How do builders diagnose and repair a double-wythe wall that is already leaking or has failing ties without demolishing the outer wythe (helical retrofit ties, repointing, cavity clearing, retrofit weeps/flashing)?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** This is a repair/retrofit scenario for an existing wall rather than new construction, and while the primary manuscript content for this project is new construction, the question is legitimately relevant as owner-maintenance knowledge (item 26's crack-diagnosis discussion is the adjacent piece), so it is answered here from product and industry sources.

**Diagnosis:** the standard non-destructive survey method for a suspected failing-tie or leaking cavity wall, consistent with general masonry-forensics practice, is a borescope inspection: drilling a small access hole through a mortar joint (not through a unit) at representative locations and inserting a borescope camera to visually inspect the cavity's condition (mortar droppings, standing water, visible tie corrosion, insulation condition) without opening the wall; this is named directly in this question's own lead ("borescope cavity survey") and is consistent with standard building-envelope-consultant practice for this kind of investigation.

**Repair without demolishing the outer wythe:** Helifix (a manufacturer whose product literature was reviewed directly in this pass, https://www.helifix.com/applications) makes a family of helical remedial tie products specifically for this scenario, installed from the exterior face through small drilled pilot holes rather than by removing and rebuilding wythes:
- **DryFix:** "a versatile dry mechanical pinning and remedial tying system for tying masonry and replacing wall ties without resin or grout," installed by "simple power-driving of the precision engineered, slim profile remedial ties into position via a small pilot hole," with the tie end left "recessed below the outer face to allow an 'invisible' finish" (Helifix product literature). This is the direct retrofit answer to a wythe that has lost its original tie connection: a new mechanical tie is driven in to re-establish the connection without touching the brick face beyond the small pilot hole.
- **TorkFix:** described as "a well proven method of stabilizing masonry veneer walls which have become detached from the inner supporting structure due to wall tie failure or omission at the time of construction, suitable for tying masonry veneers to inner wythes of brick, concrete block, wood or steel stud" (Helifix product literature), i.e., a mechanical repair anchor purpose-built for exactly the bulging/detached-veneer failure mode described in item 32.
- **CemTie:** "a versatile, fully grouted, remedial helical tie used to stabilize all forms of solid masonry, secure failed lintels and brick rings, reconnecting separated internal and external building walls" (Helifix product literature), a grouted (rather than dry mechanical) helical tie option for solid masonry conditions.

**Repointing and cavity clearing:** repointing failed or cracked mortar joints follows item 26's cause-matched-fix logic (repoint only genuinely dormant, joint-confined deterioration; route-and-seal or install a proper movement joint where the underlying cause is differential movement, not simple weathering). Cavity clearing (removing accumulated mortar droppings from an existing wall's cavity) is a genuinely difficult retrofit compared to keeping the cavity clean during original construction (item 22), since the cavity is not accessible without either removing units or working through small access openings; this research pass did not locate a standard named technique or tool for retrofit cavity clearing beyond the general borescope-guided, small-access-point approach implied by the diagnosis method above.

**Retrofit weeps/flashing:** where an existing wall's flashing and weeps are found deficient (missing, blocked, or wrongly placed per item 36's failure list), the general retrofit approach documented in the building-envelope-repair industry is to cut new weep openings (removing head joint mortar at the correct spacing and location immediately above the existing or a newly-inserted flashing) rather than rebuilding the wythe; this was not confirmed against a specific named product or standard in this research pass and should be treated as general industry practice rather than a citable standard.

**41. How is coursing actually planned so that window heads, sills, foundation transitions, and roofline transitions all land on full courses (no sliver courses), across both the CMU inner wythe and the brick outer wythe simultaneously, given that block and brick modules do not automatically align to the same course heights?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** This is a real, documented coordination problem with a standard, well-sourced solution, not an open design question: modular concrete masonry units are manufactured on an 8 in. vertical module (a nominal 8 in. high CMU unit plus its mortar joint repeats every 8 in.), while common modular brick, laid with its typical bed-joint thickness, comes to a 2-2/3 in. constructed height per course, so three courses of brick plus their mortar joints equal exactly one 8 in. module. This is stated directly in the industry sources reviewed in this pass: "the course elevations of the CMU must be coordinated with those of the brick, both systems using an 8 in. vertical module... three courses of brick (including the mortar joints) equals one 8-inch high module, thereby maintaining the modular coordination of the project," commonly expressed as a "3 to 8" ratio, or "6 courses of brick match two courses of block laid... 6 to 16" (summary drawing on CMHA's Modular Layout of Concrete Masonry TEK 05-12, https://www.cmha.org/resource/tek-05-12/, and general masonry-coordination references reviewed in this pass, e.g., https://www.globalspec.com/reference/78625/203279/13-5-brick-veneer-walls-with-cmu-backup).

**Practically, this means:** the two wythes are not coursed independently and then hoped to align; the mason (or the drawings, for a wall with any complexity) sets a single governing vertical grid at 8 in. increments, and every feature that must "land on a full course," a window head or sill, the top of the foundation, the underside of a bond beam, the eave or parapet line, is dimensioned to that same 8 in. grid, which then automatically works for both materials because 3 brick courses and 1 CMU course both resolve to the same 8 in. unit exactly. This is also the structural reason ties must be spaced correctly (item 2): BIA TN 28D notes "the International Building Code limits the vertical deviation between two parts of the tie to 1-1/4 in., making it essential that the coursing of the CMU backup wall should match the coursing of the brick veneer" (industry summary reviewed in this pass); if the two wythes' courses drift out of alignment over a wall's height, the tie's eye-and-pintle offset (item 2's 1-1/4 in. maximum) can be exceeded, which is a structural tie-detailing failure, not merely a cosmetic one. The other detail confirmed in this research: "nonstandard mortar joint thicknesses becomes... necessary in multiwythe veneer construction, where discrete bed joints of adjacent wythes must align to accommodate the anchors tying the walls together," meaning in practice the mason sometimes fine-tunes brick or block joint thickness slightly (within the material's allowable joint-thickness tolerance) at specific courses to bring the two wythes back into exact tie-height alignment, rather than only ever hitting it by pure 8 in./2-2/3 in. arithmetic.

**Applied to this project's specific transitions:** the foundation top, every window head and sill, and the roofline/eave or parapet line should each be set as a dimension that is a whole multiple of 8 in. above the established datum (commonly the top of the finished floor slab), so that both the CMU course count and the brick course count resolve to a whole number of courses at every one of those lines simultaneously; this is a drafting exercise the project's architect performs once the actual finish-floor elevation, window rough-opening heights, and roof/parapet height are fixed, not a value this research file can supply in the abstract, but the 8 in. module and the "3 brick courses = 1 CMU course" relationship are the settled, free-sourced tools that make it possible.

**49. What real corner-window or ribbon-window detail is buildable in a loadbearing double-wythe masonry wall without an engineered lintel becoming a visually dominant element, and how does that lintel then get expressed or concealed?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** BIA TN 28D's Shelf Angles/Lintels section (item 6, p.11) gives the real, buildable lintel constraints this question needs, though it does not address corner or ribbon windows as a named special case; those are genuinely open architectural design problems, not settled facts, and are answered here with the structural constraints that bound what is buildable, leaving the actual expression choice to design.

**The structural facts that bound the problem:** a lintel over any opening in this wall type must be sized to bear a minimum 4 in. on the brick at each side of the opening, and its horizontal leg (if a steel angle, the most common lintel type named by BIA TN 28D, ASTM A36/A36M steel, 1/4 in. minimum thickness) must be sized to a dimension of at least two-thirds the brick veneer's thickness; the maximum allowable deflection is L/600, and standard steel angle spans documented in the ORSC/IRC allowable-span table (found in this research pass's earlier R703.8.3.1 review) run from 6 ft (for a 3x3x1/4 in. angle) up to 14 ft (for a 6x3-1/2x5/16 in. angle) depending on angle size and stories above; longer spans require an engineered lintel design specific to the opening, not a prescriptive table value. A corner window (an opening that turns a building corner, with no masonry pier between two adjacent openings at the corner) or a ribbon window (a long, continuous horizontal opening, effectively a very long single lintel span) both push toward, or past, the edge of what a prescriptive angle lintel can span, meaning both details realistically require an engineered lintel, sized specifically for the actual span and load by the structural engineer of record, not a standard prescriptive angle.

**How the lintel gets expressed or concealed, once sized:** this is a genuine, undecided design choice with two documented directions in general masonry-architecture practice (not sourced to a single citation in this pass, since it is a design-expression question rather than a code fact): a lintel can be expressed as a visible structural or decorative band (a contrasting material, a projecting soldier course above it that reads as a deliberate header line, or an exposed steel or precast element left visible as an honest structural statement), or it can be concealed by returning the same brick coursing across its face with the lintel set back behind the brick soldier or rowlock coursing that continues the wall's normal pattern above the opening (a "brick-faced lintel" or steel angle with a brick soldier course applied to its face, a standard masonry-veneer technique). For a corner or ribbon window specifically, where the lintel span is longer and the structural element correspondingly deeper or heavier, concealment becomes harder to achieve convincingly the longer the span gets, which is a real reason a design team might choose to express rather than hide a long-span lintel at a ribbon window, but this is a design decision for the Aesthetics and Architectural Expression chapter to make once the actual opening is drawn, not a fact this research file can resolve in the abstract.

**52. What connection detail actually attaches a steel or wood element to a cured concrete or grouted CMU wall (cast-in embed, post-installed anchor, ledger) without compromising the wall's waterproofing or structural cover to reinforcement, and is this detail already covered in the structural chapters or does it need its own treatment here?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** BIA TN 28D's Structural Bearing section (item 40's cross-reference; p.17-18) gives complete, free-sourced, project-matched detail for exactly this question, covering steel joists, precast concrete planks, and wood framing bearing on or attached to a BV/CMU wall's inner wythe:

- **Cast-in embed / wedge insert:** "for concrete structures, shelf angles are typically fastened using bolts corresponding to a matching wedge insert cast into the concrete. Alternatively, cast-in embed plates may be present on concrete structures, which permit welding as an attachment method." This is the connection method planned for during the original concrete pour, before the element it will later receive is even on site, and it is the generally preferred method for a planned, structural connection because it avoids drilling into cured, reinforced concrete at all.
- **Post-installed anchor:** "post-installed anchors designed for structural anchorage into cured concrete, grouted masonry and hollow masonry can also be used to connect shelf angles to the structure," used where the connection location was not planned for at pour time, or for retrofit work (item 39's helical tie products are a specialized version of this same general category); BIA TN 28D is explicit that "the structural engineer for the project must design these fabrications and connections," meaning the anchor type, embedment depth, and edge-distance spacing (needed to avoid compromising the wall's structural cover to reinforcement, exactly as this question asks) is a project-specific engineering calculation, not a prescriptive table value this file can state generically.
- **Ledger:** for wood floor framing specifically, "alternate framing support options include the use of standard metal joist hangers attached to a ledger bolted to the inner wythe of CMU," and BIA TN 28D adds the reinforcement/grout coordination this question specifically asks about: "if the inner wythe consists of hollow masonry, then cells where anchors will be installed, such as those supporting the ledger board or individual hangers, must be grouted solid," meaning a ledger connection is not simply bolted into an ungrouted hollow CMU cell; the specific cells receiving the anchor bolts must be identified and grouted, which is a construction-sequencing item (grout those specific cells solid before or as the ledger is installed) as much as a design item.

**53. What drip-edge, reveal, or sill detail at every horizontal projection on this project's exterior (window sills, the water table, the roofline) actually controls where rain-streak staining concentrates, and can those details be planned from the start to make streaking either invisible or a deliberate part of the wall's read?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The physical mechanism is well established in general masonry-weathering knowledge and is consistent with, though not separately re-derived from, the specific drip and slope details BIA TN 28D already documents for this wall type in items 40 and 45: rain-streak staining on a masonry wall concentrates below any horizontal projection or discontinuity that water sheds from, a sill, a water table, a coping, or an unprotected horizontal joint, because water running down the wall face collects at that projection and, if the projection lacks a drip edge (an intentional groove or overhang that breaks the water's surface-tension path and forces it to fall clear of the wall rather than creeping back underneath and down the face below), the water instead wraps back onto the wall face directly below the projection and runs down it repeatedly over years, concentrating dirt, efflorescence salts (item 19), and biological growth into a visible streak pattern directly beneath that point.

BIA TN 28D's own sill detail already specifies the drip-forming geometry this question asks about: "sill extension, slope to drain, min. 15 degrees recommended... overhang, min. 1-1/2 in. recommended... weep" (Figure 17, p.16, item 40), and its coping guidance states copings "must provide a drip on at least one side of the wall and slope toward the drip" (item 45); both of these are, by their stated purpose, streak-control details as much as they are water-penetration-control details, since a sill or coping that slopes to drain and projects far enough to form a true drip is simultaneously keeping water out of the wall and keeping the streak line clean and off the wall face immediately below the projection.

**Can these be planned to make streaking invisible or deliberate?** Yes, both directions are real, achievable design choices once the actual sill, water-table, and roofline details are drawn (which, per this batch's opening note, has not happened yet in the unwritten chapter): a genuinely adequate drip edge and slope at every horizontal projection (following BIA TN 28D's own minimum dimensions above) is the direct, physical way to make streaking minimal/invisible, because it is preventing the water-wrap mechanism at its source rather than trying to hide staining after it occurs; alternatively, a design could deliberately under-detail or omit the drip at a chosen projection, accepting and even designing for the resulting streak pattern as a weathering feature (a documented approach in some masonry architecture that treats patina and streak patterns as an intentional part of a building's aging character), though this is a design philosophy choice with a real durability tradeoff, since the same water-wrap mechanism that creates a deliberate streak pattern is also the mechanism items 19-20 already identify as a driver of efflorescence and coating failure, so a "deliberate streaking" choice should be made knowingly, not as a shortcut around proper detailing.
