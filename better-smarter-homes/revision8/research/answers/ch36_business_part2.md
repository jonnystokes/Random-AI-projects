24. What is the realistic transport radius within which selling finished concrete or CEB block is economically viable, given the material's weight-to-value ratio?
Lands in: Business and Brand Framework; cross-reference Owner-Made Blocks and Alternative Concretes. [COST] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]

**Answered (2026-07-15):** No source gives a single named "economical radius" figure for concrete or CEB block specifically, so this answer is built from the underlying delivery-cost structure rather than a single quoted number. Standard 8x8x16 CMU delivered cost runs roughly $1.60-$2.98 per block in 2026 (varying by source and region), and most concrete/masonry suppliers price delivery on a two-part structure: a flat or low per-mile rate within a base service radius, then a materially higher marginal rate beyond it. The base radius reported across ready-mix and block-delivery sources clusters at roughly 15-25 miles from the plant at standard rates (some sources cite up to 30 miles), with mileage surcharges of $0.50-$3.00 per mile, or $10-$25 per mile, once a load moves outside that radius; total delivery charges commonly range $100-$300 within the base radius and $150-$500+ per truckload once distance and specialized unloading (a piggyback forklift) are added. The mechanism driving this: concrete block has a low value-to-weight ratio (a full 8x8x16 CMU weighs 38-43 lb and sells delivered for roughly $2-$3), so freight cost as a percentage of the delivered price climbs fast with distance, in sharp contrast to a low-weight, high-value product where freight is a rounding error. Rural areas compound this because they combine longer average hauls with less price competition among haulers. **Applying this to an owner-made block micro-enterprise:** the same physics that makes commercial CMU delivery uneconomical beyond roughly 20-30 miles applies at least as strongly to a small owner-run operation, which lacks a commercial plant's volume discounts and dedicated delivery fleet — a realistic sale radius is the distance a buyer can reasonably self-haul with a pickup and trailer (commonly cited informally in masonry-industry discussion as "local pickup only" for small producers) or a radius small enough that the owner's own vehicle and fuel cost do not erase the margin on a low-unit-price product. No source specific to an owner-made-block micro-enterprise was found quantifying this exact threshold; the honest manuscript statement is that the realistic radius is set by the same delivery-cost math commercial suppliers already price (roughly 20-30 miles before marginal cost climbs sharply), adjusted down for a small producer's lack of scale, and that any specific target market beyond that radius needs its own delivered-cost calculation before being assumed viable.

26. Does an alternative-materials approval obtained under ORSC R104.11 for the owner's own house create any transferable value for a buyer elsewhere seeking their own local alternative-materials approval, even without a formal ICC-ES/IAPMO listing?
Lands in: Business and Brand Framework; cross-reference Owner-Made Blocks and Alternative Concretes. [CODE] [TEST-TO-PROVE] [CREATIVE-IDEA]

**Answered (2026-07-15):** No formal transferable right exists, and this project's own earlier code-map research already establishes the precise mechanism that explains why. A R104.11 alternative-materials approval is a project-specific, jurisdiction-specific decision made by one building official for one permit; it carries no binding force on any other official or jurisdiction. This stands in direct, documented contrast to a **Statewide Alternate Method** ruling issued by the Building Codes Division, which by rule (OAR 918-008-0110: "All jurisdictions administering and enforcing the state building code must enforce statewide code interpretations and allow the use of alternate method rulings consistent with the original scope of the ruling") binds every Oregon building official automatically (per `ch02_oregon-code-and-compliance-map.md`, items 3-4, already researched). A single owner's R104.11 approval is not a Statewide Alternate Method and does not acquire that status merely by having been granted once; converting a single-project approval into a binding statewide ruling would require a separate application to BCD through the alternate-method-process the code-map research already located (oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/pages/alternate-method-process.aspx). What the owner's approval does create, short of formal transferability: a documented, real-world evidence package (the test reports, the official's written approval conditions, any inspection or third-party-observation records) that a second builder in a different jurisdiction could present to their own local official as persuasive (not binding) precedent under that jurisdiction's own R104.4 report-acceptance authority — this project's earlier research (`ch12_blocks_part2.md`) already documents that R104.4 lets an official accept "written, certified reports of inspection by approved agencies or individuals," which could in principle include a prior owner's documented test package, but whether any specific official chooses to weigh that package is entirely discretionary and unconfirmed by any documented case in this research. The honest manuscript statement: the R104.11 approval has real, usable evidentiary value the owner can hand to a future builder, but it has no binding, transferable legal status, and every subsequent buyer or builder starts their own approval process from the beginning with their own official.

27. What ASTM C90 batch-testing and quality-control regime would apply if the owner sold block represented as structural/load-bearing to a third party, and how does its cost and frequency compare to a one-time ASTM C39/C140 lab test run for the owner's own house?
Lands in: Business and Brand Framework; cross-reference Owner-Made Blocks and Alternative Concretes. [CODE] [TEST-TO-PROVE] [COST]

**Answered (2026-07-15):** ASTM C140 (Standard Test Methods for Sampling and Testing Concrete Masonry Units) sets the sampling mechanism ASTM C90 relies on for compliance determination: a **lot** of units is defined by the manufacturer's own production/shipment grouping, and from each lot, a sample set is drawn and tested — a commonly cited minimum is six units per sample (three units tested for dimensions, weight, density, absorption, and percent solids; three units tested for compressive strength), per widely-cited summaries of C140's sampling structure (this project's own earlier research in `ch12_blocks_part1.md` and `ch12_blocks_part2.md` already establishes the C90/C140/C39 framework and its 1,900-2,000 net-psi minimum at 28 days). **The critical structural difference between a one-time owner test and an ongoing sold-product regime is not the test method itself (both use C39/C140) but the frequency and the legal posture:** a one-time lab test run, of the kind this project's own house-build R104.11 approval already contemplates, demonstrates that one production run of block meets a strength target on the date tested, submitted once to satisfy one building official for one project. Selling block represented as structural/load-bearing to third parties converts this into an ongoing manufacturing quality-control obligation: because C90 compliance is a per-lot claim, not a one-time claim, a producer selling continuously would need to re-sample and re-test at a frequency tied to production lots (commonly interpreted in commercial CMU manufacturing as testing representative samples from each distinct production run or a defined shipment quantity, not a single test that certifies all future production indefinitely), each round of testing carrying its own lab cost (a C39/C140 lab test run costs in the hundreds of dollars per sample set, based on standard concrete-testing-lab rate ranges, though this pass did not find a single current, citable price specific to CMU compression-and-absorption testing and this figure should be obtained directly from a candidate lab before being used as a firm number). Beyond the recurring testing cost itself, a producer holding out block as structural to buyers who are not present to observe the owner's own production process also takes on a materially different liability posture than a one-time owner-tested batch built and used entirely on the owner's own supervised project (see item 58 in `ch36_business.md`'s continuation, and the disclaimer/documentation question at item 69 in the master question file, both cross-referenced rather than answered here). The honest manuscript statement: the test methods do not change, but the obligation shifts from "prove it once, to one official, for one job" to "prove every lot, indefinitely, to every buyer's satisfaction," which is a fundamentally larger and more open-ended commitment than the one-time house-build test program already planned.

29. Does Oregon's contractor-licensing framework treat selling a manufactured building material differently from selling construction labor, and does manufacturing/selling raw block on the owner's own property trigger any additional state registration beyond ordinary business registration?
Lands in: Business and Brand Framework. [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-15):** Yes, confirmed directly from the statute. ORS 701.010(3) exempts "a person who furnishes materials, supplies, equipment or finished product and does not fabricate them into, or consume them, in the performance of the work of a contractor" from CCB licensure — selling a finished material (block) without also performing the installation/construction work is categorically outside the CCB licensing trigger, which under ORS 701.021(1) (already quoted in `ch36_business.md`, item 21) attaches to "undertak[ing], offer[ing] to undertake, or bid[ding] to do work as a contractor for compensation," i.e., performing, offering, or bidding actual construction labor, not manufacturing or selling a product. This means manufacturing and selling raw block from the owner's own property does not, by itself, trigger CCB contractor licensing, regardless of volume, as long as the owner is only selling the material and not also contracting to install it. **Beyond CCB, "additional state registration"** is a separate and real question: any business selling a product for profit in Oregon needs ordinary Secretary of State business registration (an assumed business name filing or an LLC/corporation formation, depending on structure — already covered at the framework level in the existing manuscript chapter's section 21.5) and, because the sale is a retail transaction, needs to address Oregon's Corporate Activity Tax and any local business-license requirements at the county/city level, which are jurisdiction-specific and not uniform statewide. A block-making operation does not trigger any specialty state manufacturing license or permit distinct from ordinary business registration under the sources reviewed; the two things that could add a further layer are (a) any air-quality/dust-emissions permit a county or DEQ might require for a stationary concrete-mixing/aggregate-handling operation above a certain scale (a jurisdiction-specific fact not resolved in this pass), and (b) the EFU/home-occupation zoning cap already established in the Land, Site, and Feasibility research (`ch03_land_part3.md`: a block-yard is not "farm use" under ORS 215.203(2)(a) and must fit ORS 215.448's home-occupation limits if the parcel is EFU-zoned), which is a land-use constraint, not a business-licensing one, but functions as a real gate on the same activity.

33. What does OSHA's (and Oregon OSHA's) respirable crystalline silica standard require in practice for a solo owner who occasionally hosts one paid helper or workshop participants operating a block press or saw, short of the full written-exposure-control-plan obligation that applies to employers with regularly exposed employees?
Lands in: Business and Brand Framework; cross-reference The Owner-Builder's Body and Mind for the self-exposure angle. [CODE] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]

**Answered (2026-07-15):** The question's own premise needs a correction before it can be answered honestly: 29 CFR 1926.1153 does not have a documented reduced-obligation tier for an "occasional" or small-scale employer. The federal standard's actual small-business accommodation is different from what the question assumes: **every** employer, regardless of size or frequency of exposure, may choose between (a) performing exposure assessment and then engineering/work-practice controls tailored to the measured exposure, or (b) simply following **Table 1** of the standard, which specifies a pre-approved engineering control (commonly water suppression or a shroud with a dust-collection system, for saw-cutting and other listed masonry tasks) for a listed set of equipment and tasks; using Table 1 correctly satisfies the standard without needing to conduct exposure monitoring at all, which OSHA describes as the option "especially useful for small employers" — but this flexibility applies to any employer using Table 1 controls, not specifically to an occasional or single-employee one, and the written exposure control plan requirement is not waived merely because exposure is infrequent. **The more consequential and directly useful distinction this research found, not stated in the question:** OSHA's jurisdiction is built entirely on the employer-employee relationship. 36. Does selling a documented, tested mix design and process (without selling any physical block) as a licensed "recipe" avoid most of the manufacturer-liability and ASTM-compliance burden discussed above?
Lands in: Business and Brand Framework. [CREATIVE-IDEA] [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-15):** Largely yes, for the specific manufacturer-liability theory that attaches to strict products liability, and this has real, on-point legal precedent, though the fit is not perfect and one distinct liability theory survives. **The on-point precedent:** *Winter v. G.P. Putnam's Sons*, 938 F.2d 1033 (9th Cir. 1991) — a case in which readers of a published mushroom-identification guide were severely poisoned after relying on the book's (erroneous) species descriptions and sued the publisher under strict products liability. The Ninth Circuit held that **the informational content of a book is not a "product" subject to strict products liability**; the court's own framing (a chart used to navigate is a physical "product," but a "how-to" book teaching a skill or method is "pure thought and expression") drew the line the question is asking about directly: liability attaches to the tangible manufactured thing (there, the mushrooms actually picked and eaten; here, the physical block actually produced and built with), not to the documented instructions that led to it. Applied to a licensed mix-design-and-process "recipe": if the owner sells only documented information (a mix ratio, a press-setup specification, a curing protocol) and never touches, manufactures, or sells the resulting physical block, the buyer who acts on that information becomes the manufacturer of their own units, and the strict-products-liability chain (which runs to whoever actually made and sold the physical product) does not reach back to the recipe's author under the *Winter* reasoning. **What does not go away:** *Winter* itself notes publishers are not immune from all liability, only from strict-products-liability treatment of the ideas themselves; a negligent-misrepresentation or ordinary-negligence claim (the recipe was carelessly wrong, not merely that harm resulted from following it) remains a live theory in principle, and *Winter*'s holding is itself a single circuit's reasoning, not a nationwide rule binding every state court a future buyer might sue in. This means selling a documented mix design as a licensed "recipe" meaningfully reduces (rather than fully eliminates) the manufacturer-liability exposure discussed in items 27-28 above, and shifts the ASTM-compliance burden entirely onto the buyer who actually produces and represents their own block (the buyer, not the recipe-seller, would need to run their own C90/C140 testing program if they in turn sell structural block). This is a real, citable legal mechanism, not a loophole invented for this research, but its application to a specific future licensing contract (what the license agreement says about liability, indemnification, and disclaimers) is a drafting question for an attorney, not something this research can finalize.
Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/938/1033/294363/ (Winter v. G.P.
