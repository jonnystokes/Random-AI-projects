### Section G: Intellectual Property Beyond the Existing Chapter

**49. For a specific proprietary mix design or press method the owner might develop, does trade-secret protection or patent protection better fit the realistic threat model?**
Lands in: Business and Brand Framework; cross-reference Owner-Made Blocks and Alternative Concretes. [CODE] [CREATIVE-IDEA]

**Answered (2026-07-15):** Neither fits cleanly, and the mismatch is structural, not a close call between two viable options. Trade-secret protection requires the information to actually stay secret and be subject to the owner's own reasonable efforts to keep it that way (USPTO Trade Secret Policy, https://www.uspto.gov/ip-policy/trade-secret-policy; USPTO Trade Secrets IP Toolkit, https://www.uspto.gov/sites/default/files/documents/tradesecretsiptoolkit.pdf) — but this brand's business model, as scoped elsewhere in this chapter (item 36: selling a documented, tested mix design and process as a licensed "recipe"), is to publish and sell the exact formula and method. The moment the mix design and press method appear in the book, the course, or a licensed product, trade-secret protection over that specific formula is gone permanently; it cannot be "a little bit secret." Trade secrecy remains available only for whatever the owner deliberately keeps out of the published material (an unpublished further refinement, never printed or taught), and only if actively guarded (NDAs, marked-confidential documents) — a genuinely separate practice from the "published curriculum" part of the business, not a description of the published mix itself.

Patent requires the inverse trade: public disclosure to the USPTO in exchange for a time-limited (20 years from filing) right to exclude others, including independent inventors, from making, using, or selling the claimed invention (same USPTO sourcing; general comparison analysis, https://www.perspireip.com/blog/trade-secret-protection-beats-patent/). A patent could in principle apply to a genuinely novel press mechanism or an unusual stabilizer chemistry, but three practical barriers apply directly here: (1) **novelty** — the compressed-earth-block and dry-cast-concrete-block fields already carry real prior art, including specific stabilizer-dosage and press-mechanism patents already surfaced in this manuscript's own block-chapter research (a fly-ash/sodium-silicate dosage patent, https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8519016; dry-cast block-machine patents at https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8844228 and https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9636842), meaning any specific mix ratio or press modification would need to clear a real novelty/non-obviousness bar, not just be new to the owner; (2) **cost and time** — the patent process runs roughly 2-3 years and requires attorney drafting and prosecution costs, a material expense for a solo side venture; (3) **enforcement** — a patent's practical value depends on the holder's ability to detect infringement and afford litigation, which a solo operator is poorly positioned to do.

The protection layer that actually is available, and is distinct from both trade secret and patent, is copyright over the specific written description, diagrams, and photographs of the mix design and press method — an idea, process, or method of operation is not itself copyrightable (the same expression-versus-idea boundary already established for the plan library in `done_researching/research_questions_ch21_ch24.md`, bullet 11), paired with the same single-build/single-use contractual license terms already established there. Plainly: nothing stops a reader who owns the book or course from using the published proportions; what copyright and the license protect against is someone re-publishing or re-selling the text, photos, or video itself.

**What to bring to a patent/IP attorney:** a prior-art search on the specific formula or press modification before spending money pursuing a patent, and a written NDA for any pre-publication period the owner wants to keep genuinely secret before the book or course goes to market.
SETTLED for the general trade-secret-vs-patent framework and its structural incompatibility with a publish-and-sell business model; NOT-RESEARCHABLE-PRO for whether any specific mix or press modification the owner actually develops is novel/patentable, which requires a patent attorney's prior-art search.

**50. What are the actual, current (2026) mechanics of a text-and-data-mining opt-out for a US-based creator's website and video content, given that the clearest legal framework found is extraterritorial (EU AI Act Article 53) but the US framework is unsettled?**
Lands in: Business and Brand Framework. [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-15):** Confirmed: two different regimes, one binding on certain AI developers, one not yet law at all in the US. Under the EU AI Act, Article 53(1)(c) requires providers of general-purpose AI (GPAI) models to "put in place a policy to comply with Union law on copyright and related rights, and in particular to identify and comply with...a reservation of rights expressed pursuant to Article 4(3) of Directive (EU) 2019/790" (Article 53 text, https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/53/) — meaning GPAI providers training for the EU market must recognize and honor a rightsholder's machine-readable text-and-data-mining (TDM) opt-out signal. Enforcement teeth (fines up to €15M or 3% of global turnover) phase in from August 2, 2026 (EU AI Act GPAI timeline, https://www.legiscope.com/blog/eu-ai-act-timeline-deadlines.html). This gives a US creator real, if indirect, leverage: it binds the GPAI providers' compliance policies for the EU market, and several major AI developers have said their policies apply globally to their infrastructure, though no US-specific enforcement mechanism compels this.

By contrast, the United States has no equivalent statutory TDM opt-out regime as of this research date: no federal law requires an AI developer to honor a robots.txt disallow or any other opt-out signal, and whether AI training on copyrighted text is fair use at all is being fought case by case in ongoing author/publisher litigation against AI developers, not settled by a dedicated opt-out statute. The practical mechanics available to a US creator today are therefore voluntary and infrastructure-level, not a legal opt-out right: (1) robots.txt disallow directives naming known AI-crawler user agents (GPTBot, Google-Extended, CCBot, ClaudeBot, Bytespider, and others), honored only by crawlers that choose to comply; (2) the W3C TDMRep protocol, a machine-readable rights-reservation record (detailed at item 51 below) that creates a documented legal record under the EU framework even though it does not itself block a non-compliant crawler; and (3) infrastructure-level enforcement such as Cloudflare's AI Crawl Control (item 51), which actually blocks non-compliant bots at the network level.
SETTLED (the extraterritorial EU mechanism and the absence of a settled binding US mechanism are both confirmed); this is an actively evolving area of law and should be rechecked before publication.

**51. What machine-readable opt-out signal should the manuscript actually recommend implementing on the brand's own website and video platforms, in concrete, actionable terms?**
Lands in: Business and Brand Framework. [METHOD] [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-15):** A three-layer stack, recommended together rather than any one alone, in order of how much real protection each adds:

1. **robots.txt with explicit AI-crawler disallow lines**, naming known AI training crawlers (GPTBot, Google-Extended, CCBot, ClaudeBot, Bytespider, Applebot-Extended, and others as new ones are published) — free, universal, the baseline every site should carry, but voluntary and unenforced against any crawler that chooses to ignore it.
2. **TDMRep** (the Text and Data Mining Reservation Protocol, a W3C-recognized standard), implemented as a `/.well-known/tdmrep.json` file (or equivalently a `TDM-Reservation`/`TDM-Policy` HTTP response header, or a `<meta>` tag in each page's `<head>`) declaring TDM rights reserved (implementation guide, https://www.openshadow.io/guides/tdmrep-ai-training). This is the specific machine-readable form the EU AI Act's Article 53(1)(c) references via Directive (EU) 2019/790 Article 4(3); its value is evidentiary — it creates a documented, dated record that rights were reserved, useful if enforcement or a complaint to an AI provider is ever needed, even though it does not itself block a crawler that ignores it.
3. **If the site sits behind Cloudflare** (a realistic, low-cost, common choice for an independent creator's own website): Cloudflare's Content Signals / managed-robots.txt and AI Crawl Control features, rolled out to all customers including the free tier from July 1, 2026, actually enforce a block against named AI-training crawlers at the network level rather than merely requesting compliance (https://blog.cloudflare.com/control-content-use-for-ai-training/; https://developers.cloudflare.com/ai-crawl-control/features/manage-ai-crawlers/). Enabled via Security Settings > Configure AI bot policies, this distinguishes "search," "ai-input," and "ai-train" crawler categories, so the site can keep allowing search indexing while blocking training-specific scraping.

For video specifically (YouTube), no equivalent creator-facing opt-out signal was confirmed in this pass; the only lever is the platform's own terms of service and whatever AI-training setting, if any, YouTube Studio currently offers channel owners — this should be checked directly in YouTube Studio's own current settings at the time of writing, since no source in this pass confirms what YouTube currently offers.
SETTLED for the three-layer stack (robots.txt, TDMRep, Cloudflare AI Crawl Control), each independently sourced and immediately actionable; the YouTube-specific setting is an open item to check directly in-platform rather than a researched fact, since no source in this pass addresses it.

**52. What is the realistic, current (2026) DMCA takedown process a solo creator can actually execute without an attorney, for reposted video or copied plan-PDF content specifically?**
Lands in: Business and Brand Framework. [METHOD] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]

**Answered (2026-07-15):** Confirmed directly from the US Copyright Office's own Section 512 material (https://www.copyright.gov/512/; https://www.copyright.gov/dmca/), corroborated by practitioner guides (https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/responding-dmca-takedown-notice-targeting-your-content). A valid takedown notice must substantially contain: (1) a physical or electronic signature of the copyright owner or authorized agent; (2) identification of the copyrighted work claimed to be infringed; (3) identification of the infringing material and information sufficient to let the service provider locate it (a direct URL); (4) the sender's contact information; (5) a statement of good-faith belief the use is unauthorized; and (6) a statement, under penalty of perjury, that the information is accurate and the sender is authorized to act. No attorney is required — this language is fixed, short, and copyable directly from the Copyright Office's or a platform's own template.

For a **reposted video**: use YouTube's own copyright-complaint webform (reached through the video's report flow), which requires exactly these six elements and generally acts quickly because the platform has its own strong incentive to comply, given its own Section 512 safe-harbor obligations. For a **copied plan-PDF or content reposted off-platform** (a scraped or re-hosted copy on another site): send the same six-element notice to that host's designated DMCA agent, searchable via the Copyright Office's own agent directory (https://www.copyright.gov/dmca-directory/); if the host can't be identified or won't respond, file a URL-removal request directly with Google Search, which removes the infringing page from search results independent of whether the host itself acts.

The **counter-notification** process (relevant if the creator's own content is wrongly taken down, or a bad-faith notice is filed against the creator) requires a signature, identification of the removed material and its former location, a good-faith statement under penalty of perjury that the removal was a mistake, and consent to federal-court jurisdiction; after a compliant counter-notice, the host must restore the material in not less than 10 and not more than 14 business days unless the original complainant files suit in that window. The one real risk to flag for the manuscript: Section 512(f) creates liability for knowingly filing a false takedown or counter-notice, so any notice the creator sends should rest on a genuine, good-faith belief the material is actually infringing, not merely disliked or competitive.
SETTLED (directly sourced from the US Copyright Office's own Section 512 material, corroborated by secondary DMCA practice guides).

**53. Does the Berne Convention's automatic international copyright protection meaningfully help a solo creator enforce rights against infringement in a country outside the US, or is enforcement abroad realistically unavailable without a local attorney?**
Lands in: Business and Brand Framework. [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-15):** Confirmed, and the honest answer separates two things the question itself already suspects are different: automatic protection is real; automatic enforcement is not. The Berne Convention (administered by WIPO, 181 member countries) requires member states to extend automatic copyright protection to works from other member countries the moment of fixation, with no registration or other formality required, under the "national treatment" principle — the work is treated the same as that country's own citizens' works (Cornell Legal Information Institute, https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/berne_convention; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berne_Convention). This means a US creator's book, plans, photographs, or video are automatically protected under the domestic copyright law of every other Berne member country from the moment of creation, with no foreign registration step needed.

But Berne sets minimum protection standards; it creates no international court and no supranational enforcement mechanism. When infringement happens in another Berne country, the rights-holder must use that country's own courts, procedures, and remedies — which in practice means retaining a locally licensed attorney in that specific country, because a US copyright registration or US attorney has no standing to litigate in a foreign court (https://patentlawip.com/blog/berne-convention-copyright-united-states/). The honest, publishable guidance: Berne gives the creator a real, existing worldwide legal right the moment content is published, without needing to register anywhere else — but stopping a specific instance of foreign infringement (a plan set resold overseas, a video re-uploaded on a foreign platform) realistically requires hiring local counsel in that country, an expense and effort threshold that will exceed what most small individual infringements are worth pursuing. The realistic first-line tools for foreign infringement remain platform-level takedowns (item 52) and any US-reachable enforcement point, not foreign litigation.
SETTLED.

Lands in: Business and Brand Framework. [METHOD]

**Answered (2026-07-15):** Beyond "get written releases," the concrete components a documented-build/workshop-hosting creator's release should contain, per widely used release templates and photography/production-industry practice (https://www.legalzoom.com/templates/t/model-release-form-for-minors; https://www.wrapbook.com/blog/free-model-release-form-templates-every-producer-needs; https://www.jotform.com/form-templates/parental-consent-and-release-form): the full legal name of the subject and, separately, of the person signing; an explicit grant of rights stating exactly what will be done with the image or footage (video, photos, which specific platforms, whether the use is commercial/monetized — not a vague "may be used"); a stated duration (an indefinite release versus one limited to a specific event or period is a real, negotiable term, not a fixed default); and a signature block.

**55. Does selling a plan set or course to a buyer in a different country create any US tax, VAT/GST collection, or export-control exposure the existing chapter's Oregon/US framework does not address?**
Lands in: Business and Brand Framework. [CODE] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]

**Answered (2026-07-15):** Yes, and the VAT exposure is more immediate than most US-based creators assume — a real, previously unaddressed gap in the existing Oregon/US-only framework. The EU's VAT e-commerce rules apply to a US business selling digital content (a plan-set PDF, a pre-recorded course, an electronically delivered coaching call) to EU consumers **from the very first sale** — there is no minimum-sales threshold for a non-EU seller. The €10,000 EU-wide threshold that does exist applies only to EU-established microbusinesses selling cross-border within the EU, not to non-EU sellers (confirmed across current VAT-compliance sourcing, e.g., https://www.avalara.com/us/en/vatlive/global-vat-gst-on-e-services.html; Non-Union OSS mechanics at https://www.getmyvat.com/en/resources/oss-registration-eu-non-eu-sellers). A US seller with even one EU customer for a digital product is technically required to register for the EU's Non-Union OSS (One-Stop-Shop) scheme (registering in any single EU member state covers all 27) and charge/remit the buyer's-country VAT rate on that sale. The UK (above £85,000 UK turnover) and Australia (above A$75,000) run their own separate, threshold-based digital-services VAT/GST regimes entirely outside the EU's OSS system — meaning a US creator selling digital products internationally can face three or more separate, non-harmonized tax regimes, not one global rule.

The widely used practical workaround, confirmed as the standard pattern in cross-border digital-goods commerce (though not independently re-verified against every specific platform this project might use): sell through a platform or payment processor acting as "merchant of record," so the platform — not the individual creator — bears the VAT registration and remittance burden. This is a concrete question to ask any course/plan-library platform under consideration (item 16's territory): does it act as merchant of record for international VAT/GST, or does that burden pass through to the seller.

On **export control**: residential building plans and a concrete/masonry mix-design "recipe" are ordinary published civilian construction information, not military or dual-use technology; Export Administration Regulations (EAR) guidance confirms published, publicly available civilian technical information of this kind falls outside controlled "technology"/"technical data" categories (https://www.bis.gov/regulations/ear; https://www.research.ucsb.edu/export-control/export-control-faqs). No source in this pass suggests residential construction plans or mix-design documentation would trigger EAR licensing. The one general compliance point that applies regardless of product type: any US business selling internationally, digital or physical, is subject to standard OFAC sanctions-list screening (not selling to comprehensively sanctioned countries or specifically designated persons) — a general US business-law baseline, not something construction-specific, and not independently re-verified against the current OFAC list in this pass.
SETTLED for the VAT/GST mechanism and thresholds (a materially significant, previously unaddressed finding) and for the export-control non-applicability finding; NOT-RESEARCHABLE-PRO for which specific platform, if any, acts as merchant of record and absorbs the VAT burden — a platform-selection and CPA/tax-preparer question to confirm directly before international sales begin.

**56. What are the current (2026), state-by-state economic-nexus thresholds and digital-goods taxability rules most relevant to a small seller of plan-set PDFs, a pre-recorded course, and live coaching calls, distilled into a decision structure the owner can actually use?**
Lands in: Business and Brand Framework; cross-reference Personal Project Finance without duplicating construction-financing content. [CODE] [COST]

**Answered (2026-07-15):** Current (2026), post-*Wayfair* economic nexus has largely converged on a single, transaction-count-free revenue threshold: **41 states use $100,000 in sales**, 2 states use $250,000, and 3 use $500,000, and states that formerly paired the revenue threshold with a 200-transaction alternative have been actively dropping the transaction count (Illinois removed its 200-transaction threshold effective January 1, 2026; Utah did the same effective July 1, 2025) — confirmed across current nexus-tracking sources (https://www.avalara.com/us/en/learn/guides/state-by-state-guide-economic-nexus-laws.html; https://www.salestaxinstitute.com/resources/economic-nexus-state-guide; https://taxcloud.com/blog/sales-tax-nexus-by-state/). Stacked on top of nexus is **digital-goods taxability**, which is genuinely non-uniform: more than 30 states now tax digital/streaming products and more than 20 tax SaaS, and the list keeps expanding — Maine added digital audiovisual and audio works (including subscription-type products) to its taxable-services definition effective January 1, 2026, as one recent example (same 2026 sourcing above). Some states tax digital books/courses as tangible-personal-property equivalents, some exempt educational content specifically, and some have no digital-goods sales-tax category at all — no single list substitutes for a live, state-specific check.

Translated into what a physical-product sale (a mix-design "recipe," a specific batch of block, or licensed press-method documentation) needs beyond the existing paper-plan disclaimer language: (1) the specification and instructions must be specific and complete enough that a deviation from them is provable — vague process documentation weakens this defense before any dispute arises, meaning the same rigor this book already applies to its own construction documentation needs to extend to whatever is sold; (2) the documentation must name foreseeable deviations explicitly and warn against them (wrong stabilizer ratio, wrong compaction pressure, wrong cure time, substituted materials), since a warned-against foreseeable misuse is a stronger defense than an undocumented one; (3) for anything sold as tested or batch-verified (connecting to this chapter's own ASTM-testing-cost research elsewhere in this section), the specific tested batch/lot needs its own retained records, because a failure claim needs the seller to show what was actually tested versus what the buyer may have altered; and (4) the existing plan-library disclaimer's core pattern needs a physical-product-specific companion clause stating explicitly that any deviation from the documented mix ratio, press pressure, cure schedule, or process voids any representation about the product's tested performance — making the disclaimer track the same "documented instructions plus explicit foreseeable-misuse warning" structure the misuse defense actually rewards.
