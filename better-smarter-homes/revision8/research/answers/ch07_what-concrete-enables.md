1. Which of the three earth-sheltering forms (elevational/single-slope, penetrational/full berm, atrium/courtyard) fits an Oregon hillside parcel with a south-facing slope best, and what does each cost in added excavation and retaining structure compared with the daylight-basement baseline already assumed in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement chapter? [CREATIVE-IDEA] [METHOD]
   Leads: Malcolm Wells's three-form classification (elevational, penetrational, atrium); UNVERIFIED which Oregon jurisdictions have built precedent for any of the three.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The three-form classification is documented (attributed generally to earth-sheltered-design literature descending from Malcolm Wells's work) in CMHA TEK 06-07A: **Elevational** ("one whole building face exposed, while the other sides, and sometimes the roof, are covered with earth"), described as "the easiest type of earth sheltered building to construct, and therefore the most economical"; **Penetrational** ("built above grade, with earth bermed around and on top of it," entire building covered except at windows and doors); and **Atrium/courtyard** ("built completely below grade, typically on a flat site," rooms arranged around a central open courtyard, typically under 3 ft of earth cover). For a south-facing hillside parcel specifically, the elevational form is the one that exploits a single-aspect slope directly: the exposed glazed face opens downhill to the south, and the remaining walls and roof are cut into or bermed against the uphill soil, which is also the geometry CMHA identifies as lowest-cost to construct. The penetrational (full-berm) form does not need a slope at all (it can sit on flat ground with soil pushed up around it), so a sloped site is not the form's natural fit, and it costs more because the entire envelope, not just three sides, needs full waterproofing, drainage, and structural soil-load design. The atrium form is the poorest fit for a sloped parcel: CMHA's description assumes "typically a flat site," because a sunken courtyard needs a level excavation pad, and building it into a hillside would require extensive cut-and-retain work around the courtyard perimeter on the uphill side, adding retaining-wall cost the other two forms do not carry. No comparative dollar-per-square-foot or cubic-yard excavation figures broken out by the three forms specifically were found in this pass; CMHA and the general earth-sheltered literature describe the relative construction difficulty and cost ranking (elevational cheapest and easiest, penetrational and atrium progressively more excavation- and waterproofing-intensive) without a quantified table. No source in this pass identified specific built Oregon precedent for any of the three forms on a parcel comparable to this project's; that remains unverified. Because the actual choice depends on this project's specific unchosen parcel (slope direction, slope grade, soil type, and which face gets solar access), this is fundamentally the owner's site-fit decision to make once a parcel exists, informed by the general form/cost ranking above.
   Source: CMHA TEK 06-07A, https://www.cmha.org/resource/tek-06-07a/ (checked 2026-07-12).
   Lands in: What Concrete Uniquely Enables, earth-sheltering forms subsection.
OWNER-DESIGN-CHOICE (the general form-versus-cost ranking is documented above; the specific form-to-parcel fit is unanswerable until an actual hillside parcel with a known slope, orientation, and soil type is chosen, at which point a civil/geotechnical engineer should confirm excavation quantities for each candidate form).

2. What is the actual design dead load, in psf per inch of soil cover, for saturated winter soil on a bermed wall or an earth-covered roof, and how does it stack against the roof and wall design loads already tabulated in the Structural System And Concrete Design chapter? [CODE] [MATERIALS/INGREDIENTS]
   Leads: one builder's account cites roughly 10 psf per inch of saturated soil and a combined design load near 185 psf for a specific earth-covered structure (Earthwood); UNVERIFIED, needs an engineering source rather than a builder's own account.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The roughly 10 psf-per-inch figure for saturated soil is corroborated beyond the single Earthwood builder account: Mother Earth News's general earth-sheltered design guidance states the same figure independently ("the weight of the saturated earth, about 10 PSF for every inch"), and green-roof design guidance (a growing-medium-specific but structurally analogous case) states comparable numbers from the design-load side: growing medium at an as-installed density of roughly 5.5-6.0 psf per inch dry and 7.5-8.0 psf per inch saturated, with code guidance (echoed at up.codes' green-roof summary) that "the design dead load of growth media should be based on a saturated density of not less than 100 lb/ft3" and that the weight of landscaping/soil materials "shall be considered as dead load and shall be computed on the basis of saturation of the soil as determined in accordance with ASTM E2397" (Standard Test Method for Determination of Dynamic Weight of Saturated Green Roof Systems). Ordinary earth soil (not engineered green-roof growth medium) commonly runs somewhat denser when saturated, which is consistent with the roughly 10 psf/inch figure repeated in the earth-sheltered literature versus the roughly 7.5-8.0 psf/inch figure for lighter engineered growth medium. None of these sources is a structural-engineering textbook or an ASCE 7 provision stating a single universal psf/inch value for native soil; the code-mandated route (ASTM E2397, referenced in green-roof code sections) is to test the project's actual soil at its actual saturated density rather than use a generic rule of thumb for the final structural design. For early planning against this project's Structural System And Concrete Design chapter's roof and wall load tables, 10 psf per inch of saturated soil cover is a reasonable, multiply-corroborated planning figure (e.g., 24 inches of cover implies roughly 240 psf of soil dead load alone, before structure dead load and snow/live load are added), but the number that goes into the stamped structural design must come from the project's own geotechnical report and the structural engineer of record's calculation, not from this planning figure.

How does Mononobe-Okabe seismic earth-pressure analysis change the reinforcement and thickness of a bermed exterior wall in Oregon Seismic Design Category D0/D1, compared with the ordinary basement-wall seismic provisions already summarized in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement chapter? [CODE] [METHOD]
   Leads: Mononobe-Okabe pseudo-static analysis is the commonly cited seismic lateral-earth-pressure method, with safety factors against sliding and overturning reportedly dropping from about 1.5 static to about 1.1 seismic; UNVERIFIED how ORSC/ASCE 7 requires it applied to a habitable bermed wall specifically.
   
**Answered (2026-07-12):** The trigger and the safety-factor numbers are both confirmed, though from separate documents rather than one source. ASCE 7 Section 11.8.3 (cited at 2010/2016 editions) requires "inclusion of dynamic seismic lateral earth pressures on basement and retaining walls" for structures assigned to Seismic Design Category D, E, or F; Oregon's SDC D0/D1 falls inside that trigger, so a habitable bermed wall in that category is not covered by the "ordinary" static-only basement-wall design used for SDC A-C. ASCE 7 itself does not name a specific calculation method; per the same source, the dynamic seismic lateral earth pressure is required to be "provided by the registered design professional preparing the geotechnical investigation report," with the 2009 NEHRP Provisions Part 3, Resource Paper 12 cited as the standard reference guidance document for that calculation, a document whose contents (and its endorsement of Mononobe-Okabe pseudo-static analysis specifically) were not independently verified in this pass beyond secondary citation. Separately, the general IRC/model-code retaining-wall provision (echoed in residential code manuals) states that where a retaining wall's design load combination includes an earthquake load, "the minimum safety factor for retaining wall sliding and overturning shall be 1.1," against 1.5 for the static-only case, which matches the "leads" figures in this question. Practically, for an Oregon SDC D0/D1 bermed exterior wall, this means: (a) the wall cannot be sized using the plain-static-earth-pressure method used for a normal daylight-basement wall elsewhere in this project; (b) a geotechnical engineer must supply the dynamic seismic earth-pressure input for the specific site soil; (c) the structural engineer of record must combine that seismic earth-pressure load with gravity, soil, and other loads per ASCE 7 load combinations and design to at least a 1.1 safety factor against sliding and overturning under the seismic case (versus 1.5 static); and (d) the result is very likely a thicker wall and/or heavier reinforcement than the ordinary basement-wall tables in the Foundation chapter provide, since those tables were not derived for combined static-plus-seismic bermed loading. No source in this pass gave a specific reinforcement or thickness delta (for example, "add one size of bar" or "add 2 inches") for this exact case; that number is project- and site-specific and falls to the engineer of record.
   Source: ASCE 7 Section 11.8.3 requirement and SDC D/E/F trigger, https://www.skghoshassociates.com/blog/seismic-lateral-earth-pressure-on-basement-and-retaining-walls/ (checked 2026-07-12); seismic safety-factor figures (1.5 static / 1.1 seismic) and the >48-inch-unbalanced-fill / >24-inch retaining-wall design trigger, general IRC-derived residential code manual language as summarized at https://up.codes/viewer/oregon/irc-2021/chapter/4/foundations and corroborating retaining-wall guidance found via web search 2026-07-12 (exact ORSC section number for the seismic-earth-pressure trigger itself was not independently confirmed in the Oregon-adopted chapter text in this pass and should be re-verified directly against the ORSC/OSSC text before citing a section number in manuscript prose).

Which below-grade waterproofing systems (hot rubberized asphalt, bentonite/HDPE composite panel, sheet membrane, fluid-applied) hold up best under permanent earth or vegetated cover, where the membrane can never be inspected or replaced without full excavation, and what documented multi-decade field-performance data exists for each? [MATERIALS/INGREDIENTS] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]
   Leads: hot-applied rubberized asphalt and bentonite/HDPE composite panels are the systems most cited for permanent burial; UNVERIFIED long-term (30-plus year) field data.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Manufacturer and trade-publication sources confirm hot-applied (fluid) rubberized asphalt and bentonite/HDPE composite sheet as the two systems most commonly specified for permanent, never-to-be-re-exposed below-grade burial. Hot rubberized asphalt is applied at roughly 350-400°F and cures to a thick, fully monolithic, seamless membrane; the absence of seams and laps is repeatedly cited as its key advantage for permanent burial, since seams and laps are the most common failure point in sheet-applied systems. Bentonite/HDPE composite panels (a 20-mil virgin HDPE layer bonded to a layer of sodium bentonite clay, faced with a protective fabric) work by a different mechanism: the bentonite swells on contact with water and self-seals around fasteners, minor tears, and penetrations for as long as it stays confined under load between the wall and the soil; manufacturer literature markets "decades of proven success," but this pass found no independently monitored, non-manufacturer field study documenting 30-plus year performance for either system specifically under permanent earth or vegetated cover (as opposed to normal, eventually-re-exposable foundation burial). Sheet membranes (self-adhered rubberized-asphalt or thermoplastic sheet) carry seam risk that hot-applied and bentonite systems avoid, making them the weaker choice specifically for the never-re-exposed case, though they remain standard for ordinary, shallower foundation waterproofing. Fluid-applied membranes (spray or trowel-applied polyurethane or similar) share hot rubberized asphalt's seamless advantage but were not found in this pass with permanent-burial-specific field data either. In short: the two best-supported choices for permanent burial are hot rubberized asphalt (for seamless monolithic coverage) and bentonite/HDPE composite (for self-healing redundancy against minor future movement or puncture), often specified together (a bentonite panel over a hot-rubberized-asphalt or fluid-applied primary membrane) precisely because neither system alone has independently documented 30-plus-year field data and belt-and-suspenders redundancy is the practical answer to that gap; this is a documented industry practice, not a verified multi-decade performance guarantee.

Meadows, "Bentonite Waterproofing Membrane," https://www.wrmeadows.com/clay-tite-bentonite-waterproofing-membrane/; "Below Grade Waterproofing 101," WATERPROOF! Magazine, https://www.waterproofmag.com/2019/06/below-grade-waterproofing-101/ (all checked 2026-07-12).

5. Where a bermed wall's waterproofing fails years after backfill, what detection methods (embedded moisture sensors, electronic leak-detection grids, dye testing) can locate the failure without full excavation, and is any of them practical to install at construction time for a wall that will never be re-exposed? [PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [TOOLS]
   Leads: electronic leak-detection grids, used on commercial roofing and below-grade work, embedded at membrane installation; UNVERIFIED cost and residential-scale availability.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Electronic Leak Detection (ELD) is a documented, commercially available technology specifically for this problem. ELD works by exploiting the fact that an intact waterproofing membrane is electrically insulating; where the membrane is intact it blocks current, and where there is a breach, current passes through to a conductive layer beneath it, letting a technician (post-installation) or a permanently embedded sensor grid (for future monitoring) locate the breach's exact position without excavation. Two forms exist: a post-construction survey method (a technician walks the surface with test equipment against a conductive substrate, such as the structural concrete deck itself, immediately after the membrane is installed and before backfill, to confirm zero breaches before the wall is ever covered) and a permanently embedded conductive grid or sensor-tape system installed at the time of membrane application, wired to a monitoring point that stays accessible after backfill, so a future breach can be triangulated and located from outside without excavating the whole wall. For a wall that will never be re-exposed, the embedded-grid version is the only one of the three methods in the question that is genuinely installable at construction time and useful after backfill: dye testing requires access to apply and observe the dye, which a permanently buried wall does not allow after the fact, and embedded moisture sensors only tell you that moisture is present near the sensor, not where the actual membrane breach is. No residential-project cost figures or residential-scale product availability were found in this pass; the ELD systems documented (Carlisle SynTec, Detec Systems' IntegriScan, and similar) are marketed and case-studied primarily for commercial roofing, plaza decks, and large below-grade commercial structures, not single-family residential below-grade walls, so cost and vendor willingness to serve a small owner-builder project remain unverified and should be confirmed directly with a regional ELD vendor before counting on it as a design feature.
   Source: Intertek, "Protecting Your Investment: How ELD Technology Helps Safeguard Buildings from Costly Leaks," https://www.intertek.com/blog/2023/2023-12-20-electronic-leak-detection/; Carlisle SynTec, "SpecTopics: Electronic Leak Detection (ELD) Systems," https://www.carlislesyntec.com/en/Resources/Media/Blog-Landing-Page/SpecTopics/2023/04/06/Electronic-Leak-Detection-Systems; Detec Systems, "IntegriScan Electronic Leak Detection (ELD) Scanning Equipment," https://detecsystems.com/waterproof-membrane-integrity/ (all checked 2026-07-12).
   Lands in: What Concrete Uniquely Enables, earth-sheltering waterproofing subsection.
NOT-RESEARCHABLE-PRO (the technology and its mechanism are documented above; residential-scale cost and vendor availability for this project's actual location must be confirmed directly with a regional ELD/waterproofing-testing contractor before the design relies on it).

6. What drainage design (perimeter drain depth, daylight outlet grade, or sump capacity) does a fully bermed wall carrying two or more feet of soil need, beyond the ordinary-basement perimeter-drain specification already stated in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement chapter? [METHOD] [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** General earth-sheltered design guidance describes a two-tier drainage system for a fully bermed wall or earth-covered roof, beyond the single perimeter footing drain used for an ordinary daylight basement: (1) an interceptor drain installed at the crest of the berm, at the top of the earth-covered roof or wall's high point, to catch surface and near-surface water before it can travel down through the full soil profile against the wall; and (2) the ordinary perimeter footing drain (French drain) at the base of the wall, as already specified for a basement, but sized and detailed to handle the added infiltration volume from two-plus feet of overlying soil rather than from a shallow foundation perimeter alone. Directly against the waterproofing membrane, a drainage plane (a gravel layer or a prefabricated dimple-board/geocomposite drainage mat) is specified to let water move down to the footing drain quickly rather than build up hydrostatic pressure against the membrane; this drainage plane, not just the perimeter pipe, is the detail that most distinguishes bermed-wall drainage from an ordinary basement's, because a basement wall backfilled without deep soil cover does not carry the same sustained hydrostatic head risk that two-plus feet of saturated soil creates against a permanently buried wall. Where the site allows gravity discharge, solid (unperforated, once past the perforated collection section) drain pipe should run to daylight at a slope of 1/16 to 1/8 inch per foot; where the site does not allow gravity daylight discharge, the perimeter drain must discharge to a sump with pump capacity sized for the same increased infiltration volume, though no specific sump-capacity number for a two-plus-foot bermed condition was found in this pass. Geotextile fabric wrapping the drain trench and aggregate is standard to prevent fine soil particles from clogging the pipe over the wall's un-re-enterable lifespan. No ORSC/IRC section number specific to bermed-wall (as distinct from ordinary basement) drainage design was found in this pass; the ordinary basement perimeter-drain code provision (R405) applies as a floor, with the interceptor drain and drainage-plane details added as documented good practice rather than a separate code mandate.
   Source: general earth-sheltered drainage design description via https://engineerfix.com/what-is-a-berm-home-the-basics-of-earth-sheltered-living/, https://earthcontactproducts.com/water-drainage/, and https://www.greenhomebuilding.com/QandA/earthshelter/waterproofing.htm (checked 2026-07-12).
   Lands in: What Concrete Uniquely Enables, earth-sheltering drainage subsection; cross-reference to Foundation, Basement, Slabs, and Concrete Placement perimeter-drain specification.
SETTLED (as documented good-practice guidance; no separate ORSC code section specific to bermed-wall drainage beyond ordinary basement perimeter drains was found, and sump capacity for a specific bermed condition is a project-specific civil/drainage engineering calculation).

7. How does a bedroom satisfy its required emergency escape and rescue opening (5.7 sq ft net clear, per the dimensions already stated in the Envelope and Interiors chapters) on a bermed wall, and what window-well or elevational-design solution avoids the conflict? [CODE] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]
   Leads: window wells sized per ORSC R310 already covered for ordinary basements; UNVERIFIED whether an Oregon building official treats a bermed above-grade wall the same as a basement wall, or effectively requires sleeping rooms on the exposed face only.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** IRC/ORSC R310.1 requires every sleeping room (and every basement) to have at least one operable emergency escape and rescue opening with a net clear opening of not less than 5.7 sq ft, net clear height not less than 24 inches, and net clear width not less than 20 inches, opening directly to a public street, alley, yard, or court. Where the opening's sill sits below adjacent grade, R310.2.3 requires a window well with a horizontal area of not less than 9 sq ft and a horizontal projection and width of not less than 36 inches, connected to the building's foundation drainage system (or an approved alternative), and fitted with a permanent ladder or steps where the well is deeper than 44 inches. This code text does not distinguish a "bermed" wall from an ordinary basement wall structurally: any below-grade sill, whether it belongs to a conventional basement or to a fully bermed above-grade wall, triggers the same window-well requirement, because R310 is keyed to the opening's sill height relative to grade, not to whether the surrounding wall is classified as a basement wall. This means a penetrational (full-berm) design, where every face is covered except at doors and windows, must cut a window well of at least the same 9 sq ft dimensions at every sleeping-room opening on a bermed face, which functionally punches a hole in the earth cover at that location and concentrates a waterproofing and drainage detail there. The design solution documented in the earth-sheltered literature (and implicit in the CMHA elevational-form description) is to route all sleeping rooms to the single exposed face in an elevational design, so R310 openings occur only on the already fully daylighted wall and no window well is needed at all; this is the main practical reason the elevational form is described as easiest and most economical to build. No source in this pass found a specific statement from an Oregon building official (or an ORSC provision) explicitly confirming that a bermed above-grade wall is treated identically to a basement wall for R310 purposes; the code text itself makes no distinction, so the answer follows directly from reading the sill-height trigger, but a specific building official's application of it (for example, whether they would accept a below-grade-sill bedroom on any bermed face at all, versus steering the design toward the exposed face) should be confirmed at a pre-application meeting.
   Source: IRC/ORSC R310.1 and R310.2.3, as summarized at https://www.iccsafe.org/wp-content/uploads/bcac/IRC-R3114.pdf and https://codes.iccsafe.org/s/IRC2021P2/chapter-3-building-planning/IRC2021P2-Pt03-Ch03-SecR310.1 (checked 2026-07-12); earth-sheltered form descriptions, CMHA TEK 06-07A, https://www.cmha.org/resource/tek-06-07a/ (checked 2026-07-12).
   Lands in: What Concrete Uniquely Enables, earth-sheltering egress subsection; cross-reference to Envelope and Interiors chapters' R310 dimensions.

8. What insulation placement (a horizontal "umbrella" extending outward from the wall at shallow depth, versus conventional vertical perimeter insulation) suits a bermed or earth-coupled wall aiming for an annualized geo-solar thermal-storage effect, and how does it interact with the frost-protection and slab-edge insulation details already specified in the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement chapter? [METHOD] [CREATIVE-IDEA]
   Leads: Hait's PAHS design uses horizontal umbrella insulation extending roughly 20 feet from the structure; UNVERIFIED performance and constructability on a high-rainfall Oregon site.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** John Hait's Passive Annual Heat Storage (PAHS) design is confirmed as documented: an insulated, waterproof horizontal "umbrella" extends outward from the structure at shallow depth in all directions, reportedly roughly 20 feet, keeping the earth mass beneath and beside the structure warm and dry relative to the surrounding, seasonally-cycling soil outside the umbrella's edge, using roughly a six-month thermal time lag so summer heat stored in the soil surfaces in winter and vice versa. Proponent literature claims a 30-foot-diameter home with a 20-foot umbrella engages roughly 45,000 cubic feet (about 1,800 tons) of soil mass and can float indoor temperature between roughly 70°F winter and 76°F summer without added heating or cooling. This is structurally and thermally the opposite strategy from conventional vertical perimeter/slab-edge insulation, which insulates the wall or slab edge itself and lets the ground beneath stay at a stable deep-earth temperature; the umbrella instead insulates a large horizontal plane of soil around the building so that soil itself, not the wall, becomes the thermal-storage and insulating element, meaning the umbrella must be detailed as a continuous, waterproof, horizontal membrane-and-insulation assembly running out from the foundation, not merely a vertical board at the slab edge. No source in this pass found an engineering or constructability evaluation of this detail specifically for a high-rainfall Oregon site (where a 20-foot horizontal buried waterproof umbrella has to shed the same wet-winter water load the rest of the building's drainage system already has to manage); this remains genuinely unverified and is flagged as an open engineering question rather than a settled design detail. See item 16 below for the independent building-science critique of the broader PAHS claims.

9. What real-world problems have owner-builders and small contractors documented with earth-sheltered and bermed homes in wet, temperate climates (condensation, indoor humidity, persistent mustiness, mold), and what design or mechanical fix (dehumidification, HRV sizing, vapor-open interior finishes) resolves each? [PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [REAL-WORLD-EXPERIENCE]
   Leads: several owner accounts describe chronic condensation and mustiness in older earth-sheltered homes; UNVERIFIED whether the vapor-open interior assemblies and mechanical dehumidification already specified generally in the HVAC chapter resolve this for a bermed design specifically.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Documented owner experience (permies.com earth-berm forum threads and Wikipedia's earth-shelter overview, which summarizes multiple secondary accounts) confirms the pattern: condensation, high indoor humidity, and persistent mustiness are the most commonly reported problems in earth-sheltered homes in humid climates, arising when warm, moisture-laden interior air contacts cool below-grade surfaces (walls, or ductwork/earth-tube surfaces where used), and if the resulting moisture is not removed it creates conditions for mold and mildew growth along with a musty smell. One documented North Carolina owner account states the fix in practice: keep indoor relative humidity below roughly 48 percent using a humidity monitor and a dedicated dehumidification unit (the owner's climate did not need supplemental heating or air conditioning otherwise, only humidity control), and a second owner account adds a materials-selection fix: "build with mold resistant materials (NO DRYWALL)" and ensure every construction layer is fully dry before it gets enclosed by the next layer, since trapped construction moisture behind an earth-coupled wall has no ordinary drying path back out. Both accounts frame the underlying problem as the wall's earth contact removing the normal drying-to-outside path that an above-grade wall has, which is consistent with why this project's own vapor-open interior finish strategy (already specified generally in the Envelope and HVAC chapters) is relevant here but needs bermed-wall-specific confirmation: a vapor-open interior finish gives moisture a path to dry toward the conditioned interior air, where mechanical dehumidification or HRV/ERV ventilation can then remove it, rather than trapping it against the cold earth-coupled wall. No source in this pass gave a specific HRV or dehumidifier sizing number for a bermed Oregon design; that remains a mechanical-design calculation tied to the specific building's below-grade wall and roof area, moisture load, and occupancy.

[PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [TEST-TO-PROVE]
   Leads: "Interpretation of passive solar field data with EnergyPlus models: Un-conventional wisdom from four sunspaces in Eugene, Oregon" (ScienceDirect); UNVERIFIED beyond an abstract-level description found in this pass.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The study is confirmed and its finding is directly on point. Rempel and colleagues instrumented four residential sunspaces in Eugene, Oregon from January through June 2011, recording air, mass, globe, and soil temperatures, relative humidity, and incident solar radiation at 10-minute intervals, then validated EnergyPlus finite-difference heat-flow models against the field data (the models predicted 84-93 percent of measured temperature variability); the paper, published in Building and Environment (vol. 60, pp. 158-172, 2013), is explicitly framed against the problem that "passive solar design in the Pacific Northwest relies greatly on traditions established elsewhere, resisting adoption of distinct regional practices despite growing evidence of their value." Applied to this question: this is independently monitored, university-conducted, published evidence that Oregon's cloudy, wet winter climate does undercut standard passive-solar mass-sizing assumptions, specifically toward smaller mass and better moisture isolation, not toward the very large, deeply soil-coupled thermal mass a PAHS or annualized-geo-solar (AGS) design deliberately maximizes. This does not prove a PAHS/AGS design cannot work in the Willamette Valley or on the coast, but it is independently monitored Oregon-specific evidence that the two pillars the design depends on (solar charging and reliable soil dryness) are both harder to achieve here than in the sunnier, drier climates where PAHS's own claims and most passive-solar mass-sizing guides originated.
