103. How does a buyer search DLCD's Measure 49 claim database or analyzer tool to confirm whether a specific candidate parcel carries a valid, transferable, unexpired home-site authorization, and what does the resulting document actually authorize (one dwelling, a specific number of home sites, specific conditions)? [METHOD] [TOOLS]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The tool is the Measure 49 Analyzer, a web mapping application hosted on the Oregon Explorer Natural Resources Digital Library and funded by DLCD. For counties that have shared tax-lot data with DLCD, a buyer can search for a Measure 49 claim property by election number, claim name, or interactively by clicking the parcel on the map; the tool then displays the authorized claim property boundary and the specific numbers of new dwellings, new parcels, and total home sites authorized by that claim's final order, and lets the user find and download the actual "Final Order and Home Site Authorization" document for that claim. The tool also shows simplified maps of the high-value farmland, high-value forestland, and groundwater-restricted areas that Measure 49 itself defines (since those designations limit what a claim could be approved for in the first place), and can calculate and generate a downloadable report of what percentage of a claim property falls in each category. Where a buyer does not already know the claim's election number, DLCD separately maintains registries of Measure 37 claims filed with the state, Measure 49 Election Notice Mailings, and Measure 49 Election Returns, each searchable to help identify the applicable election number for a specific property. The final order document itself is the controlling authorization: it states, claim by claim, exactly how many dwellings, home sites, or lots the claimant was approved for and any conditions attached, so a buyer must read that specific order rather than assume a generic "one dwelling" outcome. This item connects directly to item 102 (already answered in this chapter's earlier part), which establishes that a subsequent, non-original-claimant owner has a fixed number of years from a triggering date to complete the authorized development; the Analyzer and the final order together are how a buyer confirms both that the authorization exists and transfers, and what its clock and conditions actually are.

104. What exact year did Oregon voters approve Measure 56 (the notice-of-rezoning-impact statute), and what specific notice content and timeline does it require a local government to send a landowner? [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Oregon voters approved Ballot Measure 56 in the November **1998** general election, not 1996; the question's premise date should be corrected in the manuscript. Measure 56 requires cities and counties to mail individual written notice to an affected property owner when the local government proposes a legislative zoning or comprehensive-plan change that would rezone the owner's property or that would limit or prohibit a use of the property that was previously allowed; it also requires DLCD to notify cities and counties when the legislature or the department itself adopts a new law or rule that limits or prohibits an otherwise-permissible land use, so the local government can pass that notice along. The implementing statute for counties, read directly from the primary text, is ORS 215.503 ("Legislative act by ordinance; mailed notice to individual property owners required by county for land use actions"): it requires that, in addition to any notice already required by ORS 215.060 or 215.223(1), the county governing body mail a written individual "notice of land use change" to each affected owner **at least 20 days but not more than 40 days** before the first hearing on an ordinance that would rezone the owner's property or amend the comprehensive plan in a way that would require rezoning it. The statute further specifies the notice's required content: it must be approved by the governing body, must describe in detail how the proposed ordinance would affect the use of the property, and must carry specific boldfaced warning language across the top of the page (the statute gives the required language verbatim). The equivalent statute for cities is ORS 227.186, and the state-to-local-government notice requirement is ORS 197.047; neither was independently fetched and quoted in this pass, and should be verified directly the same way if the manuscript needs their exact text.
   Source: ORS 215.503, local file `revision6_code_library/ors/ors215.html` (quoted directly, fetched 2026-07-12); Ballotpedia, "Oregon Measure 56, Notices to Landowners about Land Use Law Changes Measure (1998)," ballotpedia.org/Oregon_Measure_56,_Notices_to_Landowners_about_Land_Use_Law_Changes_Measure_(1998); Oregon DLCD, "Landowner Notification," oregon.gov/lcd/nn/pages/landowner-notification.aspx; both checked 2026-07-12.

105. Has a Measure 49 home-site authorization ever been successfully challenged or invalidated after a subsequent owner purchased the property relying on it, and what was the fact pattern? [PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [REAL-WORLD-EXPERIENCE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** No documented case was found in this pass of a Measure 49 home-site authorization being successfully challenged or invalidated specifically after a subsequent (non-original-claimant) owner purchased the property in reliance on it. What is confirmed instead is the general legal structure that makes such a challenge unlikely absent a separate defect: unlike Measure 37 relief, Measure 49 home-site authorizations were designed to be transferable, and a subsequent owner has a fixed number of years (10 years from the triggering date, per item 102 answered earlier in this chapter) to complete the authorized development; the authorization itself runs with the land rather than the original claimant. Search results did surface a related but distinct body of Measure 49 litigation: LUBA's jurisdiction over Measure 49 determinations is itself limited (Measure 49, section 16, provides that most claim determinations are not "land use decisions" and so fall outside LUBA's normal jurisdiction, narrowing the avenues available to challenge a claim determination in the first place), and separate case law (for example, the discussion of Ballot Measure 37 subdivision remedies being overridden by Measure 49) concerns disputes over which remedy an original claimant was entitled to, not a later challenge to a subsequent owner's reliance. A buyer should treat the absence of a found case as limited confidence rather than proof of complete safety, and should independently confirm through a title company and, if the claim is unusual or old, an attorney experienced in Measure 49 claims, that the specific final order was properly recorded and that no pending appeal or DLCD compliance action affects it.

106. What is the exact statutory rollback-tax lookback period for farm-use special assessment disqualification under ORS 308A.703, and does it differ for EFU-zoned farmland versus farmland outside an EFU zone? [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Confirmed by direct statute text. ORS 308A.703 governs the "additional tax" (the rollback tax) imposed when land is disqualified from farm-use special assessment; subsection (3) sets the lookback period, and it does differ by zone: **ten years** for "exclusive farm use zone farmland," but only if the land, immediately following disqualification, remains outside an urban growth boundary; **five years** for nonexclusive farm use zone farmland, for Western Oregon designated forestland, for Eastern Oregon designated forestland, and for exclusive farm use zone farmland that does not meet the outside-UGB condition (i.e., EFU farmland that ends up inside a UGB after disqualification). The additional tax itself equals, for each year in the applicable lookback window, the difference between the tax actually assessed on the land under special assessment and the tax that would have been assessed had the land not been specially assessed; the number of years used is the lesser of the statutory maximum (10 or 5, as applicable) or the actual number of consecutive years the land had qualified for the program. So the EFU/non-EFU distinction the question asks about is real and doubles the exposure (10 years versus 5) for farmland that stays in the countryside, which is the ordinary case for this project's parcel search.
   Source: ORS 308A.703, oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_308a.703, fetched and quoted 2026-07-12.

107. What is the exact statutory rollback-tax lookback period for forestland-deferral disqualification under ORS 308A.724 (or the correct current citation), and does it differ between the regular forestland program and the Small Tract Forestland option? [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** ORS 308A.724 itself is a procedural statute (application for a change of special assessment following disqualification) and does not state a lookback period; the correct citation for the lookback period is ORS 308A.703 for the regular forestland program (as in item 106 above: Western Oregon and Eastern Oregon designated forestland both fall in the **five-year** bucket, the same bucket as non-EFU farmland) and ORS 308A.707 for the Small Tract Forestland (STF) option, which is a different, more favorable program with its own additional-tax rule. Under ORS 308A.707(2)(b), where STF land is disqualified but remains in another qualifying special-assessment program, the additional tax is calculated for the lesser of **10 years** or the number of consecutive years the land was assessed as small tract forestland; under 308A.707(3)(a)(B), where the land is disqualified and does not remain in any special-assessment program, the additional tax reverts to the lesser of the number of consecutive qualifying years or **5 years**. So the answer to the "does it differ" half of the question is yes, in two ways: STF land that stays in some other qualifying program can face up to double the lookback exposure of regular forestland (10 years versus 5), while STF land that fully exits special assessment lands in the same 5-year bucket as regular forestland. This is a more granular and more favorable-in-the-good-case structure than the flat 5-year regular-forestland rule, and a buyer or owner considering the STF election specifically (rather than the standard forestland-deferral program) should have this distinction confirmed by the specific county assessor, since STF election eligibility itself (generally parcels under a certain acreage threshold) is a separate qualifying question not addressed here.
   Source: ORS 308A.703, oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_308a.703; ORS 308A.707, oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_308a.707; ORS 308A.724, oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_308a.724; all fetched 2026-07-12.

108. Exactly which acts trigger disqualification (recording a partition plat, ceasing qualifying use, building a non-farm/non-forest dwelling, an ownership transfer to a non-qualifying entity) and which specific target-county assessor offices have published a plain-language disqualification guide? [CODE] [METHOD]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The Oregon Department of Revenue's own farm-use manual and its "Assessment of Farmland" guidance sheets (in an EFU zone and outside an EFU zone) list the standard disqualification triggers in plain language, and multiple county assessor pages restate the same list: land loses farm-use special assessment if the owner sells or transfers it and the new use no longer meets farm use, if the land ceases to be used for qualifying farm use (including failing the required income test where one applies), if it is platted for subdivision (recording a partition or subdivision plat is itself treated as ending the qualifying use for the platted portion), or if the owner voluntarily requests disqualification. Building a non-farm or non-forest dwelling on the parcel disqualifies at minimum the homesite acreage from special assessment (see item 110 below on the mechanics of homesite carve-outs), and can trigger a broader disqualification if the remainder no longer independently qualifies. An ownership transfer to a non-qualifying entity, by itself, does not automatically trigger disqualification under Oregon's farm/forest special-assessment scheme; what matters is whether qualifying use continues after the transfer, since the special assessment (and the "potential additional tax" that follows it) attaches to the land, not to a specific owner, and survives a change of ownership as long as qualifying use continues. Two Oregon county assessor offices with clear plain-language disqualification guidance found in this pass, useful models regardless of which specific county this project's parcel search targets, are: Marion County ("Farm/Forest Programs," including a Frequently Asked Questions PDF) and Clatsop County ("Farm and Forestland Special Assessment"); Linn County also publishes a "Special Assessment" page covering the same ground. Whether the specific target county (not yet chosen) has published its own guide, and any county-specific administrative practice beyond the state-law minimum, is a confirm-directly item.

109. Can a purchase contract legally and cleanly assign the rollback-tax liability to the seller (since the seller enjoyed the tax benefit during their ownership) even though the tax technically becomes due upon a disqualifying act the buyer initiates after closing? [METHOD] [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Partially, with an important structural limit that the manuscript should state plainly. The additional (rollback) tax is a lien-like obligation that attaches to the land itself, not to whichever person happened to own it during the qualifying years; Oregon law does not require the tax to be paid at the time of a sale, and a parcel can be sold and resold for years while carrying a "potential additional tax" notation on the assessment and tax rolls, becoming actually due only when a disqualifying act occurs, whoever owns the land at that moment. Because the obligation runs with the land rather than with a specific past owner, a purchase contract cannot make the county collect from the seller once the buyer, as the then-current owner, triggers disqualification after closing; the county's claim is against the land and its owner of record at the time of the triggering act. What a purchase contract *can* do, and what Oregon real estate practice already has a standard mechanism for, is have the parties privately negotiate and contractually allocate that risk between themselves: the standard Oregon Real Estate Forms system includes a Special Tax Assessment Addendum (referenced in practice as Form 2.22) for disclosing that a property carries one of several specific special tax assessments, and buyer and seller can use that disclosure as the basis for a separate indemnification or price-adjustment clause obligating the seller to reimburse the buyer if a rollback tax is later assessed because of the buyer's own post-closing change of use. That is a private contractual promise enforceable between the two parties (subject to ordinary contract remedies if the seller later refuses or cannot pay), not a change to who the county can collect from; the manuscript should not present it as eliminating the buyer's exposure, only as a way to shift the economic burden back to the seller by agreement.
   Source: Oregon REALTORS, "Special Tax Assessments," oregonrealtors.org/news/tip-of-the-week/special-tax-assessments; Yamhill County, "How do I keep my property in farm deferral?" FAQ, yamhillcounty.gov/FAQ.aspx?QID=265; general mechanism cross-checked against ORS 308A.703, oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_308a.703; checked 2026-07-12.

110. Is there a way to site the house and disturbed building envelope on a farm- or forest-deferred parcel so as to keep the remainder of the parcel qualified and deferred, minimizing the rollback-tax base, and has any owner-builder done this successfully? [CREATIVE-IDEA] [REAL-WORLD-EXPERIENCE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Yes in mechanism, though this pass could not confirm a specific documented owner-builder account of doing it. Oregon's assessment statutes contain a dedicated "homesite" carve-out mechanism rather than an all-or-nothing rule: ORS 308A.253 ("Qualification of homesites") allows the specific homesite acreage under and around an approved dwelling on forestland, EFU-zoned farmland, or non-EFU farmland (where farm income exceeds half the owner's income) to be separately classified and specially valued, and general Oregon Department of Revenue and county-assessor guidance confirms that when a dwelling is approved and a homesite is carved out, it is possible for only that homesite portion to be disqualified from the broader farm- or forest-use special assessment while the remainder of the parcel continues to qualify and remain specially assessed, provided the remainder still independently meets the program's qualifying-use requirements (continued farm use or forest use on the rest of the acreage). This means the practical lever for minimizing the rollback-tax base is the same lever that already matters for the dwelling-siting decision on other grounds (item 27, the site-plan sequencing item answered earlier in this chapter): siting the house, driveway, septic, and other disturbed building envelope as compactly as possible, on the smallest area that still satisfies the county's minimum-homesite and siting-standard rules, so the smallest possible acreage is pulled out of special assessment and the largest possible remainder keeps its qualifying, lower-taxed status. This pass did not find a specific documented case of an owner-builder narrating this strategy successfully; it is offered as a reasoned application of the statutory mechanism, not a proven field account, and the exact minimum homesite size and any county-specific siting requirement should be confirmed with the specific county assessor and planning department before finalizing a site plan.

111. What specific clauses does a real, working Oregon tenancy-in-common co-ownership agreement for raw land contain (buyout formula, expense-sharing, default remedy, first-refusal rights, exit timeline), and is a template available or does this always require custom attorney drafting? [METHOD] [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Oregon law's starting point is that a conveyance of land to two or more unmarried persons creates a tenancy in common by default (ORS 93.180(1)(a)); Oregon has generally abolished joint tenancy with survivorship unless the deed clearly states an intent to create survivorship, so co-owners who want anything other than plain tenancy-in-common shares need to say so explicitly in the deed. A working tenancy-in-common (TIC) agreement, per general secondary guidance and Oregon-focused legal commentary, typically covers at minimum: a buyout mechanism (commonly an appraisal-based valuation combined with a right of first refusal, so a departing co-owner's interest is valued and the remaining owners get the first chance to buy it before it can go to an outside party); an expense-sharing formula (property tax, insurance, and any carrying costs, typically pro rata to ownership share); a default remedy for a co-owner who stops paying their share (commonly a right for the paying co-owners to advance the missed payment and add it, often with interest, to what the defaulting owner owes at buyout or sale); and an exit timeline or process (how much notice a co-owner must give before forcing a sale or exercising a buyout, since absent an agreement any co-tenant can otherwise file a judicial partition action under ORS 105.205 at essentially any time, per item 112 below). Templates exist (generic co-ownership and tenants-in-common agreement templates are sold online), but the consistent professional guidance found is that a template is a starting outline at best; because the agreement has to interact correctly with Oregon-specific default rules (the ORS 93.180 tenancy-in-common default, the ORS 105.205 partition-action right that exists absent a contrary agreement, and how the buyout/first-refusal terms would actually be enforced), custom attorney drafting is the realistic path for an agreement meant to hold up under real disagreement, not just a template filled in without review. One source estimated an attorney-drafted co-ownership agreement plus a recorded memorandum of agreement at roughly $10,000-$15,000 all-in for drafting, negotiation, and execution; this figure is from a general (non-Oregon-specific) source and should be treated as a rough planning number, not a quote.

112. What Oregon-specific partition-action cost and timeline data exists (the general figures gathered here are not Oregon-specific), and how does Oregon's partition statute (likely ORS chapter 105) differ from the general pattern described? [CODE] [COST]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The lead is confirmed: ORS chapter 105, sections 105.205 through 105.405, is the correct citation, and this is a distinct thing from the land-use "partition" (dividing a parcel into new legal lots, governed by county planning law and ORS chapter 92) covered elsewhere in this chapter's earlier items; ORS 105.205-105.405 instead governs a **judicial partition suit**, the lawsuit a co-tenant files to force a division or sale of jointly held real property when co-owners cannot agree. Read directly: ORS 105.205 ("Who may maintain partition") states that when several persons hold real property as tenants in common (with certain qualifying estates), "any one or more of them may maintain a suit for the partition of the real property according to the respective rights of the persons interested therein, and for a sale of all or a part of the property if it appears that a partition cannot be had without great prejudice to the owner." The statute then sets out a full judicial process: a detailed complaint naming every known and unknown interest (105.215), service and answer procedures (105.225-105.235), the court's power to order either a physical partition or a sale with proceeds divided by ownership share (105.240-105.245), a mechanism allowing one owner to borrow money and buy out another owner's fixed, court-determined interest rather than force a sale, where partition or sale would cause "great prejudice" to an owner (105.210), and a full set of provisions governing referee-conducted sales, distribution of proceeds, and costs (105.250-105.405, including 105.405 "Costs and expenses of partition"). This differs from the general national pattern in that it is a formal judicial lawsuit with court-appointed referees available to physically divide or sell the property, not an administrative or purely private buyout process; no Oregon-specific aggregate cost or timeline data (average attorney fees, average months to judgment) was found in this pass, and litigation cost and duration vary case by case with the number of parties, whether the case is contested, and the local court's docket, which makes an aggregate figure the kind of thing only a real estate litigation attorney handling current cases in the relevant county could give with any confidence.
   Source: ORS 105.205-105.405, local file `revision6_code_library/ors/ors105.html`, fetched and quoted directly 2026-07-12.

113. What are the gift-tax reporting requirements and cost-basis consequences when a family member transfers land below market value or as an outright gift, at a level of detail useful for a buyer to bring an informed question to a CPA, without this book giving tax advice directly? [METHOD]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** This is a federal (IRS) tax question, not an Oregon-specific one, and the manuscript should present it as background for a CPA conversation rather than as advice. The framework, current as of the 2026 tax year: a donor who gives any one recipient more than the annual gift-tax exclusion in a calendar year must file IRS Form 709 (United States Gift and Generation-Skipping Transfer Tax Return); the 2026 annual exclusion is $19,000 per recipient per donor (so a married couple can jointly give $38,000 to one recipient, such as the buyer, without needing to file, using a gift-splitting election). Filing Form 709 does not necessarily mean tax is owed: amounts above the annual exclusion are applied against the donor's lifetime gift/estate tax exemption, which for 2026 is $15,000,000 per person; actual gift tax is only owed once a donor's lifetime gifts exceed that very large lifetime figure, which is far beyond the scale of a typical land gift or below-market family land sale. Separately from the gift-tax filing question, the cost-basis consequence matters more for a buyer's future tax exposure: under the federal carryover-basis rule, a person who receives gifted property generally takes the donor's original adjusted cost basis (not the property's fair market value at the time of the gift), so if the land has appreciated significantly since the donor acquired it, that built-in gain is preserved and will be taxed as a capital gain when the recipient eventually sells, unlike inherited property, which instead usually gets a stepped-up basis to fair-market value at the donor's death. Where the transfer is a below-market sale rather than an outright gift, the portion of the discount below fair market value is itself treated as a gift for gift-tax purposes, subject to the same annual-exclusion and lifetime-exemption framework above, while the buyer's cost basis is generally what was actually paid, not the full market value. A buyer facing this situation should bring the donor's original purchase price and date, any prior gift-tax filings, and the property's current appraised value to a CPA, and ask specifically about the gift-tax filing threshold, the carryover-basis exposure on a future sale, and whether a formal appraisal is needed to document the transaction regardless of whether tax is currently due.
   Source: IRS, "Frequently asked questions on gift taxes," irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/frequently-asked-questions-on-gift-taxes; IRS, "About Form 709," irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-709; IRS, "Instructions for Form 709 (2025)," irs.gov/instructions/i709; IRS, "Property (Basis, Sale of Home, etc.)," irs.gov/faqs/capital-gains-losses-and-sale-of-home/property-basis-sale-of-home-etc/property-basis-sale-of-home-etc; 2026 annual exclusion and lifetime exemption figures cross-checked across multiple current tax-guide summaries (taxspecialty.com, countrytaxcalc.com); all checked 2026-07-12.

114. How does a life-estate/remainder arrangement (an older family member retains use for life, the builder holds the remainder) actually work for construction purposes; can the remainder-interest holder get a building permit and start construction before the life tenant's interest ends, and does this affect the owner-builder exemption's occupancy-intent test? [CODE] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The general property-law structure is well established: a life estate splits ownership into a present possessory interest (the life tenant, who has the legal right to possess and use the property for their lifetime but cannot sell, mortgage, or otherwise encumber the whole property alone) and a future interest (the remainderman, who will own the property outright once the life estate ends, but who generally cannot force the life tenant out or unilaterally sell or substantially alter the property during the life tenancy without the life tenant's agreement, since the life tenant also has a duty not to let the property's value be reduced through waste). Applying this cleanly to construction is where this pass could not find a direct, citable answer, and the manuscript should flag it rather than assert a clean resolution. Two separate legal questions are tangled together here. First, the building-permit question: Oregon's Construction Contractors Board owner-builder exemption, read directly from ORS 701.010(7), exempts "a person performing work on a property that person owns... or a person performing work on that person's residence, whether or not that person owns the residence" from contractor licensure, but the statute does not define "owns" in a way that resolves whether a remainder interest (a real, recorded property interest, but not present possessory ownership) counts as "owning" the property for this purpose, nor does it address a remainderman building a *future* residence they do not yet occupy. Second, the practical question of whether construction can even proceed on land another person (the life tenant) presently has the exclusive legal right to possess: as a matter of general life-estate law, the remainderman does not have the legal right to enter and build on the property during the life tenancy without the life tenant's consent, since that possessory right belongs to the life tenant alone; in practice this arrangement almost always requires the life tenant's explicit written agreement to the construction (effectively acting like a landowner's consent) regardless of what the CCB exemption statute says about "ownership." 137. What Oregon county-specific home-occupation and conditional-use permit process, timeline, and cost applies to hosting paying workshop visitors or selling a manufactured product from a rural residential, EFU, or forest-zoned parcel, for the specific county the parcel sits in? [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The state-law framework that any specific county's process sits on top of is ORS 215.448 (quoted and analyzed in depth in item 138 below): in an EFU, forest, or mixed farm-and-forest zone, a home occupation must be operated by a resident or an employee of a resident, employ no more than five full-time or part-time people on site, be operated substantially in the dwelling or in buildings normally associated with uses permitted in the zone, and not unreasonably interfere with other permitted uses in the zone; the county governing body can attach additional reasonable conditions of approval beyond these statutory minimums. Clackamas County, 81 Or LUBA 664 (2020)**, which concerned a conditional use permit for a home occupation hosting paying events (weddings) on EFU-zoned land, and worked through exactly the kind of questions this project's block-making workshop-hosting idea would raise: whether the applicant genuinely "operates" the home occupation under ORS 215.448(1)(a) even without full-time employees; how the five-employee cap under 215.448(1)(b) is counted (event staff count toward the cap regardless of who hires them, a rule separately confirmed in Green v. Douglas County, 63 Or LUBA 200 (2011)); and, critically, that a **new, purpose-built structure** for the home occupation (in that case, a free-standing restroom building sized for 300 guests) was found not to qualify as "accessory to or customarily associated with an existing dwelling," meaning ORS 215.448(3)'s bar on authorizing new construction "not otherwise allowed in the zone" is a real, binding limit on what can physically be built to support a paying-visitor operation. 138. Does ORS 215.448's "clearly secondary to the agricultural character of the property" test have any published interpretation, precedent, or size/revenue guidance (a percentage-of-income test, a square-footage cap, a customer-visit-frequency limit) that would help the owner judge whether a block-making or workshop-hosting operation could qualify, if the parcel is EFU-zoned? [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The premise needs correcting first: the phrase "clearly secondary to the agricultural character of the property" is **not** actual text of ORS 215.448, confirmed by reading the statute's full current text directly (quoted below), and it does not appear in the LCDC administrative rule that separately implements home-occupation policy for EFU land, OAR 660-033-0130(14), also read directly and quoted: "Home occupations and the parking of vehicles may be authorized. Home occupations shall be operated substantially in the dwelling or other buildings normally associated with uses permitted in the zone in which the property is located. A home occupation shall be operated by a resident or employee of a resident of the property on which the business is located, and shall employ on the site no more than five full-time or part-time persons." Neither source contains "clearly secondary" or an agricultural-character test. What does exist, and is the likely source of the confusion, is a genuinely close cousin found directly in LUBA case law: in **Jacobs v. Clackamas County, 73 Or LUBA 262 (2016)**, LUBA upheld a hearings officer's requirement that the applicant demonstrate the home occupation was **"clearly subordinate"** to residential use of the property, where the *local Clackamas County code* (not the state statute) defined "home occupation" to include only commercial uses "clearly subordinate to residential use of the property," with that local definition functioning to disqualify home occupations that fail it; LUBA held that determining whether a use is "clearly subordinate" requires considering all associated activities, including vehicle parking and use, not just the square footage occupied. A similar county-adopted phrase, "secondary to residential use," appears in a separate case, Stevens v. City of Island City, 68 Or LUBA 112 (2013), again as **local code language**, not state statute text. So the correct, precise answer for the manuscript: there is no state-level "clearly secondary to the agricultural character" test in ORS 215.448 itself; "clearly subordinate"/"secondary" language, where it exists, is **specific local-jurisdiction zoning-code language** (confirmed for Clackamas County and the City of Island City) that a hearings officer or LUBA then interprets case by case, and it may or may not exist in the specific target county's own ordinance. On the size/revenue guidance the question also asks about: no percentage-of-income test for home occupations was found anywhere in ORS 215.448, OAR 660-033-0130, or the LUBA case law reviewed (unlike the farm-dwelling income test under ORS 215.283, which is a different, unrelated statute already answered in item 88); square-footage caps do exist but are, again, local-code creations, not a statewide figure: Clackamas County's own ordinance was found capping a home occupation at 1,000 square feet of an accessory building (Weuster v. Clackamas County, 25 Or LUBA 425 (1993)), while the City of Island City's ordinance capped one at 600 square feet (the Stevens v. City of Island City line of cases, 2013-2015), two different local numbers, confirming there is no single statewide square-footage standard. What the state statute does give, confirmed directly, is the "operated substantially in" test (ORS 215.448(1)(c)): LUBA has repeatedly interpreted "substantially" to mean the home occupation must be conducted in the dwelling or associated buildings "to a large degree," "in the main," or as the "main part," compared to the portion conducted outside (Green v. Douglas County, 63 Or LUBA 200 (2011)), which is the actual statutory qualitative test a block-making or workshop-hosting operation on this project's own EFU parcel would need to satisfy, alongside the five-employee cap and the bar on new construction "not otherwise allowed in the zone" discussed in item 137 above.
