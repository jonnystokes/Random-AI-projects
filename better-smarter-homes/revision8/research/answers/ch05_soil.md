## Item 1

**1. What goes wrong when an owner-builder backfills against a foundation/stem wall too early or with the wrong material, and what backfill-and-compaction sequence prevents wall cracking and inward rotation?** [PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [METHOD]
Leads: heavy equipment compacting against an unbraced wall cracks it; backfill only after the slab/floor braces the top; free-draining granular backfill vs native clay against the wall.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Two related failure modes recur in practitioner accounts. First, backfill placed against a foundation or stem wall before the concrete has developed adequate strength, or before the wall is laterally braced at the top, lets the soil load push the wall inward or rotate it about its footing; standard poured-wall guidance states the requirement plainly: backfill is not placed until the wall has sufficient strength and has been anchored to the floor above, or has been sufficiently braced to prevent damage from the backfill load (concretemetric.com/guide/backfilling-around-concrete-foundations-guide, armadapouredwalls.com/foundation-backfilling-guide). Concrete reaches roughly 75 percent of design strength in 5-7 days and is considered fully cured at 28 days; where the floor diaphragm that would normally brace the top of the wall is not yet framed, temporary diagonal bracing (heavy timbers at about two-thirds of the wall height, spaced roughly 12 feet apart for an 8-foot wall) substitutes until the permanent floor connection exists (armadapouredwalls.com/foundation-backfilling-guide, builderonline.com/building/how-to-backfill-a-foundation_o).

Second, heavy compaction or excavation equipment working directly against an unbraced or green wall transmits a sudden, concentrated lateral load the wall was not designed to resist at that stage; the corrective practice is to restrict powered vibratory equipment to an outer zone away from the wall and use hand-guided plate compactors or jumping jacks in the zone immediately against the wall, compacting both sides of a wall simultaneously and in even lifts where the wall could otherwise be pushed off-center (machinesl.com/structural-backfill, checklistguro.com/blog/backfilling-and-compaction-checklist-around-foundation).

Material and compaction sequence found consistently across contractor and materials-testing sources: backfill in lifts no thicker than 6-12 inches loose, compacting each lift before placing the next; target roughly 90 percent of Standard Proctor maximum dry density (ASTM D698) in the zone against the wall using light equipment, and 93-95 percent in the outer general-backfill zone (machinesl.com/structural-backfill, thesuperiorhydrovac.com/blog/backfill-material-options). Free-draining granular material (clean sand, crushed gravel or crushed stone) is preferred against the wall over native clay or silt: granular material compacts predictably and does not retain water, while cohesive native soil swells when wet and shrinks when dry, producing a cyclic lateral load that works the wall connection over time; where native soil with moderate clay content is used it should be tested first, and high-plasticity clay should be rejected or blended with granular material rather than placed directly against the wall (concretemetric.com/guide/backfill-materials-for-retaining-walls-guide).

This bracing-and-sequencing framework is general good-practice guidance, not a single ORSC section, but it sits directly behind the Oregon code trigger already documented for Chapter 7 research: ORSC R404.1.1 requires an engineered wall design wherever a concrete or masonry foundation wall supports more than 48 inches of unbalanced backfill without permanent lateral support at both top and bottom (up.codes/viewer/oregon/irc-2021/chapter/4/foundations). A wall backfilled before it is braced is, for the duration it stands unbraced, effectively an unsupported-at-the-top wall; the height and soil condition at which that becomes unsafe is a structural question for the wall's designer, not a fixed number this research can generalize.

## Item 2

**2. On a sloping rural Oregon site with clay soils and a seasonal high water table, which perimeter and sub-slab drainage designs (footing drains, curtain drains, daylight outfall vs sump) actually keep the structure dry, and where do they clog or fail?** [PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [METHOD]
Leads: footing drain must sit at/below the footing wrapped in filter fabric + clean gravel; silt clogging of pipe/fabric; daylighted outfall preferred over sump where grade allows.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The controlling Oregon code minimum is ORSC R405.1: drains are required around concrete or masonry foundations that retain earth and enclose a habitable or usable space below grade. Drainage tile, a gravel-or-crushed-stone drain, perforated pipe, or another approved system must be placed at or below the top of the footing or below the bottom of the slab, and must discharge by gravity or mechanical means to an approved drainage system. A gravel-or-crushed-stone drain must extend at least 1 foot beyond the outside edge of the footing and at least 6 inches above the top of the footing, and must be covered with an approved filter membrane. A perforated pipe must sit on at least 2 inches of washed gravel or crushed rock (at least one sieve size larger than the pipe's joint opening or perforation), be covered with at least 6 inches of the same material, and be wrapped in or overlain by an approved filter membrane (confirmed against 2021/2024 IRC R405.1 text, which the 2023 ORSC carries forward unmodified for this section: up.codes/s/foundation-drainage search synthesis, codes.iccsafe.org/s/IRC2024P1). Where gravity discharge to daylight is not feasible, R405.2 requires a sump pit; Chapter 7 research already on file documents the sump specification (tight-fitting cover, Appendix F radon-county requirement) and that gravity daylight is the code's stated preference over mechanical lift (`done_researching/research_questions_ch05_ch08.md` lines 1026-1044).

A curtain drain is a distinct, shallower installation (commonly 12-24 inches deep) placed upslope of the house to intercept surface and shallow lateral groundwater flow before it reaches the foundation; it supplements but does not substitute for the R405.1 footing drain on a habitable below-grade space, which must sit at or below the footing itself (frontierbasementsystems.com/about-us/blogs/31016-curtain-drain-vs-french-drain, wmbconstruction.com/blog/bid/66939/what-is-a-footing-and-curtain-drain). On a sloping clay site, the practical design is both: a curtain drain on the uphill side to cut off surface and interflow before it saturates the working area, and the code-required footing drain at the foundation itself.

Daylight discharge is preferred over a sump wherever the site's grade provides enough fall to reach an approved outfall by gravity: it removes the power-dependency and pump-failure risk that a sump introduces, which matters on a rural property where a power outage during the wet season is the same event that raises the water table (jlconline.com/how-to/foundations/pumping-to-daylight-foundation-drainage-for-a-low-lying-site). On a genuinely sloping parcel this is usually achievable for at least part of the perimeter; the low corner of the foundation is the one most likely to need a sump as backup or primary discharge.

Failure mode: clay and silt fines migrating into the drain gravel and pipe perforations, which clogs the system slowly rather than all at once. Filter fabric (geotextile) reduces this but does not eliminate it in fine-grained soils; several materials sources note the fabric itself can clog within roughly a year in poor conditions if the geotextile is not matched to the soil's grain size, while a correctly specified non-woven geotextile, sometimes combined with a sand buffer layer between native clay and the drain stone, extends functional life toward 30-50 years (geosyntheticssociety library proceedings on geotextile clogging; ecogeomat.com/filtration-geotextile-drainage-systems; greenbuildingadvisor.com/question/sand-and-filter-fabric-over-foundation-drains). The specific geotextile selection standard (apparent opening size matched to soil gradation, governed by ASTM D4751 and related ASTM geotextile standards) sits behind an ASTM paywall; ORSC R405.1 itself only requires "an approved filter membrane material" without specifying the standard, leaving the exact product spec to the manufacturer's listing and the local inspector's approval.

Sources: https://up.codes/viewer/oregon/irc-2021/chapter/4/foundations ; https://codes.iccsafe.org/s/IRC2024P1/chapter-4-foundations/IRC2024P1-Pt03-Ch04-SecR405.1 ; https://www.frontierbasementsystems.com/about-us/blogs/31016-curtain-drain-vs-french-drain.html ; https://www.wmbconstruction.com/blog/bid/66939/what-is-a-curtain-drain ; https://www.jlconline.com/how-to/foundations/pumping-to-daylight-foundation-drainage-for-a-low-lying-site_o ; https://library.geosyntheticssociety.org/wp-content/uploads/resources/proceedings/122021/PPT-eposter-trab-aceito-0501-1.pdf ; https://ecogeomat.com/filtration-geotextile-drainage-systems/ ; https://www.greenbuildingadvisor.com/question/sand-and-filter-fabric-over-foundation-drains.

## Item 3

**3. How do experienced builders handle expansive/high-plasticity clay and soft/organic soils, what site investigation, over-excavation, engineered structural fill, or pier options prevent differential settlement?** [PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [COST]
Leads: geotech survey treated as non-negotiable; compacted structural fill in controlled lifts; drilled piers/shafts on expansive clay; clay swells 10-15% wet; trees within 15-20 ft cause localized shrinkage/settlement.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** This item overlaps heavily with research already on file for the foundation chapter (`done_researching/research_questions_ch05_ch08.md` lines 184-263), which this answer summarizes and extends rather than re-derives.

**Classification and thresholds.** Oregon publishes no state-specific expansive-soil threshold; the controlling frameworks are the Holtz & Gibbs (1956) plasticity-index bands (PI 0-15 low swell potential, 10-35 medium, 20-55 high, over 35 very high) and IBC §1803.5.3, which defines a soil as expansive when plasticity index is at least 15, at least 10 percent passes the #200 sieve, at least 10 percent is finer than 5 microns, and the Expansion Index (ASTM D4829) exceeds 20. The on-file research recommends PI 15 as the design-discussion trigger and PI 20 as the engineered-foundation trigger for a durability-focused Oregon build, more conservative than the bare code minimum because a long design life amplifies cyclic shrink-swell damage.

**Site investigation.** ORSC R401.4 requires a soil test when expansive, compressible, or shifting soils are present, with the finding recorded per ORS 455.440. Practitioner and code-official practice observed across Oregon jurisdictions treats a geotechnical consult as effectively mandatory once any field screening (ribbon test, pocket penetrometer) suggests moderate-to-high clay content, because the owner-builder cannot self-certify engineered fill; density verification has to come from a registered design professional or an AASHTO-certified soils technician, not from the owner's own compaction records. A one-day residential geotech consult typically runs $800-2,500 and produces the fill specification (density targets, lift thickness, moisture-conditioning protocol, testing frequency) the building official will accept.

**Over-excavation and structural fill.** ORSC R401.4.2 requires compressible or shifting topsoil or subsoil to be removed to a depth and width sufficient to reach stable moisture content in the active zone; that removed material may not be reused as fill or chemically/mechanically stabilized within the active zone. Observed jurisdictional practice: fills under 12 inches beneath a footing are typically accepted on visual observation alone; 12-24-inch fills typically require Standard or Modified Proctor testing (ASTM D698 / D1557) with density verification at 1-foot lift intervals; larger or critical fills require a PE-stamped fill specification, daily inspection logs, and a final PE letter of compliance.

**Pier and void-form options.** Where over-excavation and replacement is not economical, drilled piers bypass the active (shrink-swell) zone entirely, transferring the structural load to a deeper, stable bearing stratum; typical residential pier depths run roughly 8-30 feet depending on the depth of the active zone and soil conditions (voidform.com/knowledge-base/how-to-build-on-expansive-soil-foundation-solutions-that-work). Degradable void forms are placed beneath grade beams and slabs bearing on piers so that soil heave has a space to expand into without loading the structural element; the void depth is sized from the geotechnical report's Potential Vertical Rise (PVR) value, not a generic number. Lime or cement stabilization can reduce a soil's plasticity and swell potential directly, but is typically paired with a pier-and-void-form or similar secondary system rather than relied on alone (bigapplegroupny.com/how-to-avoid-the-effects-of-expansive-soil-on-buildings, discountpdh.com Foundation Design Options for Residential and Other Low-Rise Buildings on Expansive Soils course PDF).

**Trees.** Multiple sources converge on keeping trees roughly 20-30 feet from the foundation on expansive or shrink-swell-prone soil, with one peer-reviewed parametric study noting significant shrinkage settlement can occur at a distance equal to about half the tree's mature height, and most desiccating root activity concentrated in the top 3 meters (about 10 feet) of soil (Springer, "A Parametric Study of the Effect of Trees on Residential Footing Design on Expansive Soils," 2023; British Geological Survey, bgs.ac.uk/geology-projects/shallow-geohazards/clay-shrink-swell). This research could not independently verify the question bank's "15-20 ft" figure or the "clay swells 10-15% wet" figure as a fixed rule of thumb; volumetric swell is mineralogy-dependent and is properly measured by a lab free-swell test (ASTM D4546) rather than generalized to a single percentage, and the tree-distance guidance found in the literature runs somewhat wider (20-30 ft, or half the mature tree height) than the question's lead. The manuscript should not state either of those two numbers as settled without a lab test or a source this research did not locate.

Sources: https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/geo-2022-0664/html ; https://up.codes/s/geotechnical-evaluation ; https://voidform.com/knowledge-base/how-to-build-on-expansive-soil-foundation-solutions-that-work/ ; https://bigapplegroupny.com/how-to-avoid-the-effects-of-expansive-soil-on-buildings/ ; https://www.discountpdh.com/wp-content/themes/discountpdh/pdf-course/foundation-design-options-residential-other-low-rise-buildings-expansive-soils.pdf ; https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10706-023-02400-0 ; https://www.bgs.ac.uk/geology-projects/shallow-geohazards/clay-shrink-swell/ ; plus the on-file ORSC R401.4/R401.4.2 and PI-threshold sourcing in `done_researching/research_questions_ch05_ch08.md`.

## Item 4

**4. Which surface-water and grading mistakes (negative slope to the house, downspouts dumping at the foundation, missing swales) cause the majority of chronic foundation moisture, and what grading/gutter details prevent them?** [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]
Leads: constant soil moisture matters most on expansive soils; positive slope away, extended downspout discharge, functional gutters.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The Oregon code minimum is ORSC R401.3: lots must be graded to drain surface water away from foundation walls, with the grade falling not less than 6 inches within the first 10 feet from the building; where a lot line, wall, slope, or other physical barrier prevents that 6-inch fall, the code requires an alternate means (a drain, a swale, or another approved method) instead. Separately, R401.3 requires any impervious surface within 10 feet of the foundation to be sloped not less than 2 percent away from the building. Surface drainage generally must be diverted to a storm sewer or another approved point of collection that does not create a hazard (verified text: up.codes/viewer/oregon/irc-2021/chapter/4/foundations, IRC 2021 base carried into the 2023 ORSC).

Gutters and downspouts are separately required (ORSC R903, per the on-file Land/Site-preparation research at `done_researching/research_questions_ch17_ch20.md` line 363) wherever roof drainage would otherwise discharge within 5 feet of the building or cause erosion; downspout sizing is commonly figured at roughly 1 square inch of downspout area per 100 square feet of roof-drainage area in Oregon's higher-rainfall zones, with the discharge point routed to a splash block, an extension, a dispersal trench, a drywell, or a rain garden per the local jurisdiction's stormwater code.

On the failure-mode side, the practitioner and inspection literature converges on the same three items the question's leads name: a flat or negative (toward-the-house) grade, downspouts that discharge directly at the foundation instead of being extended away from it, and gutters that are missing, undersized, or clogged and therefore sheet water down the wall instead of collecting it. These are described consistently as the most common chronic-moisture causes at a foundation, and their consequence is described as most serious on expansive or shrink-swell soils specifically, because it is the soil moisture *variation* (wet-dry cycling near the foundation) rather than moisture alone that drives differential heave and settlement; a foundation on expansive clay generally needs the moisture content near it kept as constant as reasonably achievable, which grading and downspout control are the first line of defense for (geoinstitute.org "Living With Shrink/Swell Soils," a homeowner's guide; dalinghausconstruction.com/blog/what-is-expansive-soil-how-does-it-cause-foundation-settlement; allcountywaterproofing.com/2025/07/15/what-expansive-clay-soil-is-doing-to-your-foundation).

Sources: https://up.codes/viewer/oregon/irc-2021/chapter/4/foundations (ORSC R401.3) ; `done_researching/research_questions_ch17_ch20.md` line 363 (ORSC R903 downspout sizing) ; https://www.geoinstitute.org/sites/default/files/inline-files/LivingWithShrinkSwellSoils_updated.pdf ; https://www.dalinghausconstruction.com/blog/what-is-expansive-soil-how-does-it-cause-foundation-settlement/ ; https://allcountywaterproofing.com/2025/07/15/what-expansive-clay-soil-is-doing-to-your-foundation/.

## Item 5

**5. What creative site-water strategies can an owner-builder combine on a rural lot with no municipal storm system (French drains, dry wells, rain gardens, swales, capillary breaks/mud-mats under footings), and how are they sized?** [CREATIVE-IDEA] [METHOD]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** These are widely published rule-of-thumb sizing methods, not Oregon code requirements; on a rural parcel with no municipal storm system, whether any of them is formally required depends on the county's own grading/stormwater ordinance and (above roughly 1 acre of disturbance with potential discharge to surface waters) the DEQ 1200-C construction stormwater permit covered in item 11. Absent a specific local mandate, the following are the sizing conventions found in practitioner and extension-service sources:

**Dry well.** The common convention is the "one-inch rule": size the dry well to store at least the first inch of rainfall from its contributing area. One inch of rain over 1 square foot is about 0.623 gallons, so a 500-square-foot contributing roof area needs roughly 311 gallons of storage (500 x 0.623). A typical residential dry well is about 4 feet by 4 feet by 3 feet, sized for a medium roof section or patio (hellogravel.com/project/dry-well).

**Rain garden.** Two overlapping conventions appear: size the garden to 10-20 percent of its contributing drainage area, or divide the contributing area by 6 (clay soils) to 8 (sandy soils) to hold about 1 inch of runoff in a garden roughly 6 inches deep; a 1,200-square-foot roof area would need roughly 150-200 square feet of rain garden. A rain garden should sit at least 10 feet from the foundation and typically uses 6-12 inches of amended soil media (myrainplan.com/blog/rain-garden-design; nemo.uconn.edu/raingardens/sizing; frederickcountymd.gov Rain Garden Sizing Hand-Out).

**Swale.** A vegetated open channel is sized to its contributing drainage area's peak flow rather than a fixed dimension; the Rational Method (Q = C x i x A, where Q is peak discharge in cubic feet per second, C is a runoff coefficient for the surface type, i is rainfall intensity for the chosen design storm, and A is the contributing area) is the standard hand-calculation method for a driveway-scale or house-scale drainage area, and is the same method county road departments use for culvert sizing (see item 14). The design storm to use (commonly a 2-year or 10-year event for ordinary conveyance) is set by the local jurisdiction's drainage or road standard and should be confirmed with the county rather than assumed.

**Capillary break / mud mat under footings.** This is a compacted crushed-rock layer, or in wet-season conditions a thin unreinforced "lean concrete" mud mat, placed under a footing to break capillary rise from a wet or soft subgrade and to give a clean, stable working surface for reinforcement and formwork. It is not a stormwater-volume sizing question; thickness is a geotechnical/inspector judgment call for the specific subgrade condition, commonly a few inches of clean aggregate or 2-3 inches of lean concrete, and is covered further under item 6.

**Dependency to confirm:** none of the above is a substitute for the mandatory ORSC R401.3 grading and R405 footing-drain requirements (items 2 and 4); they are supplemental site-water management for the areas of the lot the building code does not reach (yard, block-yard pad, driveway shoulders). Confirm with the county planning or public-works department whether any local ordinance sets a minimum design storm, a specific BMP list, or a stormwater plan requirement for a rural residential lot below the 1-acre DEQ threshold, since some counties do regulate at a smaller scale than the state permit.

## Item 6

**6. How do builders protect an open excavation through Oregon's wet season, sidewall collapse, softened bearing soil, frost in the trench, and keep a subgrade from turning to mud before the pour?** [PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [METHOD]
Leads: never pour on frozen or softened subgrade; lean-concrete mud-mat; benching/sloping or shoring for stability.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Sidewall stability during excavation is governed by federal OSHA excavation standard 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P, which Oregon OSHA adopted into state rule as Division 3, Subdivision P by administrative order effective September 1, 1990, and which remains the controlling excavation-safety standard in Oregon today (osha.oregon.gov/osharules/div3/div3p.pdf; osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.652). A competent person must classify each soil or rock deposit as Stable Rock, Type A, Type B, or Type C per Appendix A before work proceeds. For an excavation less than 20 feet deep, Appendix B sets the maximum allowable slope for each classification: Stable Rock, vertical (90 degrees); Type A, 3/4 horizontal to 1 vertical (53 degrees); Type B, 1:1 (45 degrees); Type C, 1.5:1 (34 degrees). Sloping/benching to these angles is one compliant protective system; shoring (timber per Appendix C, or manufacturer-tabulated hydraulic/aluminum shoring) and shielding (trench boxes) are the alternatives under 1926.652(b) (osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926SubpartPAppB; osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926SubpartPAppA). Wet-season water intrusion is exactly the condition that degrades a soil's actual classification below what a dry-season visual assessment would suggest (a soil the competent person would call Type A dry can behave as Type B or C when saturated), which pushes the required slope flatter or forces a shift to shoring; the standard separately restricts working in an excavation with accumulated water unless special precautions (support/shield systems, active water removal, or other protection) are in place (29 CFR 1926.651(h)).

Subgrade softening and frost at the bearing surface are addressed by the concrete-placement provisions rather than the excavation-safety standard: loose soil, mud, water, debris, and ice must be removed from the space concrete will occupy before placement, per ORSC R403.1 and ACI 318 §26.5.2 (already documented for the foundation-inspection chapter in `done_researching/research_questions_ch05_ch08.md` line 810). In practice this means a pour is not made onto a frozen or a rain-softened subgrade; a footing inspection that finds either condition fails, and the contractor either waits for conditions to improve, over-excavates the affected material and replaces it with compacted structural fill or clean aggregate, or places a lean-concrete mud mat, a thin (commonly 2-3 inch) unreinforced concrete leveling course poured directly on the disturbed subgrade, to seal off further softening and provide a stable, clean surface for reinforcement placement before the structural footing is poured. The mud mat is standard wet-weather practitioner practice; it is not itself an ORSC-mandated element, and its use and thickness are a means-and-methods decision the contractor and inspector make in the field for the specific subgrade condition encountered.

Sources: https://osha.oregon.gov/osharules/div3/div3p.pdf ; https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.652 ; https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926SubpartPAppA ; https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926SubpartPAppB ; https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1926/subpart-P (1926.651(h), accumulated water) ; ORSC R403.1 / ACI 318 §26.5.2 as documented in `done_researching/research_questions_ch05_ch08.md`.

## Item 7

**7. How do builders decide slab vs crawlspace vs basement on a wet Oregon site, and what practices manage a high water table / hydrostatic pressure (under-slab drainage, sump, head-rated waterproofing, flotation/uplift)?** [PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [METHOD]
Leads: waterproofing must be rated for the actual head; drainage to relieve pressure vs sealing against it; empty tanks/slabs can float.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The three options carry increasing groundwater exposure in this order: slab-on-grade, crawlspace, basement. A slab-on-grade avoids below-grade hydrostatic exposure almost entirely; on a wet site the practice is to raise the pad with well-compacted fill if needed and place at least 4-6 inches of free-draining aggregate under the slab with gravity drainage to daylight where the site's grade allows (ecohome.net/en/guides/2370/slab-on-grade-or-foundation-and-basement; buildingadvisor.com/materials/foundations-sitework/foundation-drainage-subsurface-water). A crawlspace reduces hydrostatic exposure relative to a basement by the height of the crawlspace clearance, but still needs the same capillary break and drainage detailing as a slab, plus (per Oregon energy and moisture practice already on file) a sealed ground vapor barrier. A basement carries the most exposure and is the option most likely to need active water management (sump) rather than passive drainage alone if the seasonal high water table reaches or exceeds the basement floor elevation.

The Oregon code trigger that forces this decision out of the prescriptive path is ORSC R404.1.1: a concrete or masonry foundation wall must be engineered per R301.1.3 wherever it is subject to hydrostatic pressure from groundwater, or wherever it supports more than 48 inches of unbalanced backfill without permanent lateral support top and bottom (up.codes/viewer/oregon/irc-2021/chapter/4/foundations, corroborated in items 1 and 12). A basement on a site with real seasonal hydrostatic pressure is therefore engineered construction by definition, not a prescriptive-path option.

Waterproofing must be selected for the actual pressure it will see. ORSC R406.2 (below-grade waterproofing, as opposed to R406.1 dampproofing) is triggered where seasonal high groundwater is within 24 inches of the footing, or where the site's soils evaluation designates it; the on-file research already specifies the practical detail: a full waterproofing membrane system (for example, a 60-mil peel-and-stick polymer-modified asphalt membrane) is used where R406.2 applies, because a dampproofing coating is not rated to resist standing hydrostatic head, only to resist moisture vapor and incidental wetting (`done_researching/research_questions_ch05_ch08.md` lines 1026-1055).

Drainage philosophy: the ORSC R405 footing-drain system (item 2) is a pressure-relief strategy, lowering the water table locally around the foundation so the wall and slab never have to resist full hydrostatic head, rather than a sealing strategy that lets pressure build and relies on the waterproofing membrane alone to hold it back. Relief is the preferred approach wherever gravity or a reliable sump can carry the water away; sealing against full head (without relief) requires the structure itself, its waterproofing, and often its dead weight to be designed for that pressure, which is a materially more expensive and more failure-sensitive approach.

Flotation and uplift: an empty or under-loaded below-grade structure (a swimming pool, an empty cistern or tank, or in an extreme case a basement shell before it carries its full building dead load) can be pushed upward by buoyant force if the water table rises above its base while it is not carrying enough dead weight to resist that force. The documented remedies are added dead weight (a thicker base slab or ballast), tension piles or ground anchors, or a hydrostatic relief system (an under-slab drainage layer connected to a sump that actively relieves pressure rather than requiring the structure to resist full head) (search synthesis of academia.edu/researchgate papers on hydrostatic uplift prevention beneath basement slabs; sciencedirect.com "Methods of resisting hydrostatic uplift in substructures"). This is most relevant to an empty or partially built structure during construction and to any dedicated underground water-storage vault (see item 10), and less to a normally loaded, occupied basement, whose building dead load is usually sufficient once construction is complete.

## Item 10

**10. If a cistern is integrated into the foundation footprint (a dedicated basement room or a below-slab vault used purely for water storage), what does that do to the foundation's structural design, waterproofing detailing, and the foundation drainage hierarchy already specified in the soil chapter, and does it change the excavation and formwork sequence in the build-sequence chapter?** [METHOD] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** A water-storage vault built into the foundation is, by definition, a wall or slab intentionally holding a standing head of water against or beneath it, which puts it squarely inside two Oregon code triggers already documented elsewhere in this chapter: ORSC R404.1.1 requires an engineered design (per R301.1.3) for any concrete or masonry foundation wall subject to hydrostatic pressure from groundwater, and the same logic applies with more force to a wall deliberately holding water on purpose; and ORSC R406.2 (below-grade waterproofing, not the lesser R406.1 dampproofing) applies wherever the design must resist a real hydrostatic head. A basement-room or below-slab cistern is therefore outside the prescriptive foundation path regardless of what the surrounding house foundation would otherwise qualify for.

**Structural design standard.** Ordinary residential foundation walls are designed to ACI 332 (residential concrete) and ACI 318 (general structural concrete); a structure specifically built to hold liquid falls under ACI 350, "Code Requirements for Environmental Engineering Concrete Structures," which governs tanks, reservoirs, and similar water-retaining or wastewater-retaining concrete elements. ACI 350 imposes stricter serviceability requirements than ordinary structural concrete because the structure must be liquid-tight (not merely load-bearing): it specifies a higher load factor for lateral liquid pressure (1.7, versus 1.4 in ordinary design) and tighter crack-width and reinforcement-detailing limits to keep the structure "dense, durable, nearly impermeable" over its service life (concrete.org store listing for ACI 350-20; globalspec.com/std/14479550/350). A cistern integrated into the foundation should be designed to ACI 350, not simply detailed as an extension of the surrounding ACI 332 foundation wall.

**Waterproofing and the drainage hierarchy.** The chapter's existing foundation-drainage philosophy (item 7) is pressure relief: the ORSC R405 footing drain is meant to lower the water table around the foundation so the wall does not have to resist full hydrostatic head. That logic runs backward for an intentional water-storage vault, where the water inside the vault is deliberate, not a condition to relieve. The cistern shell needs its own dedicated positive-side waterproofing and, where used, a crystalline waterproofing admixture designed to self-seal minor cracking under sustained water contact, detailed and inspected separately from the surrounding building's R405 perimeter footing drain, so that the building's own foundation-drain system is not inadvertently draining water out of (or, on the outside face, adding hydrostatic load against) the cistern wall. Any shared wall between the cistern and an adjacent occupied basement space needs waterstops at every construction and control joint, and the cistern's own floor slab is typically thicker and separately reinforced from the surrounding building footings for exactly this reason (search synthesis on underground concrete cistern/tank design: watercache.com/portfolio/underground-concrete-tanks; isomat.eu/solution/waterproofing-of-basements-against-water-under-pressure).

**Flotation.** An empty cistern below the seasonal water table is subject to the same buoyant-uplift risk described in item 7; because a cistern is by design meant to be empty at times (before first fill, after a draw-down, or during inspection/maintenance), its structural design has to account for the empty-tank uplift case explicitly, not just the full-tank case, using added dead weight, tie-down anchors, or a dedicated hydrostatic relief detail sized for the empty condition.

**Excavation and formwork sequencing.** A below-slab or basement-room cistern requires excavation to a greater depth than the surrounding footings before any foundation work begins, because the tank's base slab sits below the building's ordinary footing elevation; the tank's base slab and walls are typically poured as a separate, earlier structural placement (with waterstops cast into every joint) before the surrounding foundation walls and footings are formed and poured, which changes the pour sequence documented in the Build Sequence chapter from a single foundation pour into at minimum two coordinated placements. This is a real change to the whole-project sequence and should be flagged for the Build Sequence chapter's research rather than assumed away.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The Oregon threshold is already documented and corroborated by two independent sources: ORSC R404.1.1 requires engineered design "in accordance with accepted engineering practice" for a retaining wall not laterally supported at the top that retains more than 48 inches (4 feet) of unbalanced fill, or for any retaining wall over 24 inches that resists lateral loads in addition to soil, with a minimum 1.5 factor of safety against sliding and overturning; the Clackamas County ORSC summary independently states the same 4-foot/engineered-design trigger for retaining walls (dochub.clackamas.us document, already cited in `done_researching/research_questions_ch05_ch08.md` line 598, and re-confirmed via up.codes R404.1.1 text in this research pass).

Two clauses matter for material choice, not one: height (over 48 inches of unbalanced fill without top restraint) is one trigger; carrying an additional load beyond soil (over 24 inches and resisting a surcharge such as a driveway or a structure) is a second, independent trigger. A wall under 4 feet with no additional surcharge is exempt from mandatory engineering regardless of material; a wall of any height that also carries a surcharge load is not exempt.

**Wall-type fit by height, from published comparison sources (concretemetric.com/guide/reinforced-vs-gravity-retaining-walls-guide; rocscience.com/learning/retaining-wall-types-from-gravity-to-gabion; infraconcrete.co/compare-retaining-walls):**
- **Gravity segmental retaining wall (SRW), unreinforced:** practical up to roughly 4-6 feet, resisting lateral pressure through its own mass alone; a natural fit for staying under the Oregon 4-foot threshold on a single tier.
- **Gabion (stone-filled wire basket):** also a gravity system, practical up to roughly 6 feet; useful where locally sourced stone is available and reduces material-hauling cost, at $15-30 per square foot per one cost source.
- **Geogrid-reinforced SRW / MSE wall:** the reinforcement (geogrid layers extending into the retained soil) carries lateral pressure as tension, letting the reinforced-soil mass act as a coherent gravity block; this is what makes taller walls (practical range roughly 6-50+ feet) economical, and cost sources describe reinforced/MSE systems as 25-50 percent cheaper than a cantilever concrete wall once height exceeds roughly 10 feet. A reinforced wall over 4 feet is, by the same R404.1.1 logic, already in engineered-design territory (a geogrid-reinforced wall's design is inherently an engineering calculation, not a prescriptive assembly), so choosing this type above 4 feet does not avoid the PE step; it is simply the more economical engineered option at greater height.
- **Cast-in-place concrete cantilever wall:** requires engineering design and quality-controlled construction at essentially any real retained height, and, per R404.1.1's second clause, requires it below 4 feet too if it carries a surcharge in addition to soil.
- **Owner-made CMU:** a gravity (unreinforced, or lightly reinforced but not soil-anchored) CMU wall is code-legal without a stamped design under the same conditions as any other gravity wall, that is, under 48 inches of unbalanced fill and with no additional surcharge; once either condition is exceeded, an owner-built CMU wall needs the same engineered design step (and, functionally, the same TMS 402 reinforced-masonry design discipline used elsewhere in the manuscript for above-grade block walls) as any other retaining wall type at that height or load.

**Net effect of the 4-foot threshold on the owner-build decision:** below 4 feet of unbalanced fill and with no surcharge, gravity SRW, gabion, owner-made CMU, and a modest cast concrete gravity wall are all legally buildable without a stamped engineered design, making material choice a cost/labor/appearance/available-skill decision rather than a code-forced one. Above 4 feet, or wherever a surcharge applies at any height, every material option in this list requires the same engineering step; at that point the deciding factor shifts from "which material avoids engineering" (none does) to "which engineered system is most cost-effective at the required height," where the published cost data favors geogrid-reinforced SRW/MSE construction over cast concrete cantilever once the wall is taller than about 10 feet.

**13. The published rule of thumb for horizontal offset between tiers, sourced from segmental-retaining-wall industry technical guidance, is that the setback between an upper and lower wall must be at least twice the height of the lower wall (D > 2 x H-lower) for the two walls to be designed and to behave as independent structures; this is described as the "zone of influence," extending horizontally about two times the wall height behind the lower wall. Any upper wall located within that zone of influence applies a surcharge load to the lower wall, and where the offset does not meet the 2:1 ratio, the design has to model the combination as a single taller wall for the purpose of the lower wall's stability analysis (allanblock.com/literature/PDF/ts1297.pdf; masonryandhardscapes.org/resource/srw-faq-001; allanblock.com/resources/articles/terraced-retaining-walls). **Sizing method.** The standard hand-calculation for a small private-road or driveway culvert crossing is the Rational Method, Q = C x i x A, where Q is peak discharge in cubic feet per second, C is a runoff coefficient for the contributing land cover, i is rainfall intensity for the chosen design storm and the site's specific rainfall data, and A is the contributing drainage area upstream of the crossing; this is the same method referenced for swale sizing in item 5 (pacificwatershed.com Appendix A, "Culvert Sizing procedures for the 100-Year Peak Flow"; stormwater.com "Ten Rules of Thumb for Culvert Crossings"). **Minimum-diameter and design-storm conventions actually observed in Oregon county road standards** (which set a floor even where the Rational Method result would call for something smaller): Klamath County requires driveway culverts sized to the 25-year, 24-hour storm event with a minimum culvert diameter of 12 inches (klamathcounty.org driveway permit documents); other sources describe 18 inches as the more common practical minimum where a defined channel exists, both because larger culverts handle debris better and because commonly available 18-inch corrugated metal pipe is, pound for pound, one of the strongest standard sizes to install (search synthesis, stormwater.com and related driveway-culvert guides).
