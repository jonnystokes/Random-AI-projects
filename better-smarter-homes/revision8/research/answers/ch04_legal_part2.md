[PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [REAL-WORLD-EXPERIENCE]

What can be stated with confidence, by distinguishing the two roles, is why this is a genuinely separate exposure from the code-inspection process already covered elsewhere in this chapter: a construction lender's draw inspector (sometimes called a fee inspector or construction-progress inspector) is typically a third party hired by the lender to verify percentage-of-completion against a line-item budget and draw schedule, for the lender's own risk-management purposes — this is a different function, a different inspector, and a different standard (physical progress against dollars released) than the local building official's code-compliance inspection. 48. If conventional construction lending proves difficult for a code-novel structural material, what real alternative financing paths, such as a portfolio lender who inspects in person and lends on the engineer's stamp rather than a standard appraisal, an owner-builder-specific loan product, or cash-and-draw self-financing, have other owner-builders of unconventional homes used? [CREATIVE-IDEA] [REAL-WORLD-EXPERIENCE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** No documented account of a specific Oregon or alternative-materials owner-builder using one of these paths was found in this pass, but each path itself is a confirmed, real category of Oregon-available financing, not a hypothetical: (1) Portfolio lenders — local banks and credit unions that hold loans on their own books rather than selling them to the secondary market (Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac), and therefore have more underwriting flexibility than a conventional conforming lender, are a documented, named category of construction lender ([nav.com/blog/self-build-construction-loans](https://www.nav.com/blog/self-build-construction-loans-1367911/)); this flexibility is exactly what would let a portfolio lender weigh an engineer's stamp and in-person inspection more heavily than a standard appraisal comparable-sales approach, though no source confirms a specific Oregon portfolio lender doing this for an alternative-materials home specifically. (2) Owner-builder-specific loan products exist as a distinct, named lending category, but carry materially harder terms than standard construction lending — a 25-35 percent down payment is commonly cited, versus 10-20 percent for a conventional construction loan, reflecting lenders' view that an owner-builder (without a licensed GC's track record) is a higher-risk borrower ([clearhouselending.com/blog/can-you-get-a-construction-loan-if-youre-building-it-yourself](https://www.clearhouselending.com/blog/can-you-get-a-construction-loan-if-youre-building-it-yourself); [creditsuite.com/blog/self-build-construction-loans](https://www.creditsuite.com/blog/self-build-construction-loans/)). (3) Cash-and-draw self-financing (paying as construction proceeds, without a lender at all) is not a named product with its own literature; it is simply the default fallback when the first two paths do not work, and it removes the appraisal/comparable-sales and draw-inspection problems in items 46-47 entirely, at the cost of requiring the owner to have or raise the full construction budget without borrowing against future value.
**What to confirm and with whom:** Oregon credit unions and community banks active in rural construction lending (a portfolio-lender inquiry is a phone-call-level question, not a researchable fact) — ask specifically whether they hold construction loans in portfolio and whether they have ever underwritten a non-conventional structural system.

49. Does the R104.11 approval letter or the eventual certificate of occupancy for an alternative-materials house carry any different weight for a county tax assessor's valuation than a conventional home of the same size, and has that produced any documented dispute or surprise? [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** No documented Oregon dispute or surprise involving an alternative-materials home's assessed value was found in this pass. What is settled is how Oregon county assessors value residential property generally, which frames why this is a real, if undocumented, exposure: Oregon's Real Market Value (RMV) standard (ORS 308.205) is "the estimated price an informed buyer would reasonably pay an informed seller in an arm's-length transaction as of the assessment date," and county assessors do not individually appraise each parcel — they statistically model groups of similar properties, and the RMV is more likely to be accurate where the property is "similar to others in its property class" with recent comparable sales activity ([oregon.gov/dor/forms/formspubs/real-property-assessment_303-670.pdf](https://www.oregon.gov/dor/forms/formspubs/real-property-assessment_303-670.pdf); ORS 308.205, [oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_308.205](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_308.205)). A structurally unusual home, by definition, does not fit a standard property-class model the same way a conventional home does, which plausibly produces either an under-valuation (the mass-appraisal model has nothing comparable to anchor to and defaults toward conventional-construction assumptions) or an over-valuation dispute (the model treats a genuinely lower-cost owner-built structure as equivalent to a conventionally-built home of the same square footage) — but this is reasoning about the mechanism, not a documented outcome. The R104.11 letter and certificate of occupancy themselves are code-compliance documents, not valuation documents; nothing found in this pass suggests the assessor's office gives either one any special valuation weight one way or the other.
**What to confirm and with whom:** the specific county assessor's office, once the home is built and assessed — if the assessed value seems wrong, Oregon law provides a formal appeal route through the county Board of Property Tax Appeals (referenced generally at [oregon.gov/dor/programs/property](https://www.oregon.gov/dor/programs/property/pages/personal-property.aspx)); this is the confirmed remedy, though no documented case of it being used for this specific scenario was found.
**Lands in:** Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules, closing/resale subsection.

50. What does a lender or title company require to see, at closing on a resale, regarding an owner-built home's structural-exemption recording under ORS 455.320, already a required county-clerk recording, versus its R104.11 alternative-materials approval, and can the absence of a clean paper trail on the latter delay or kill a sale even years after a passed final inspection? [PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [REAL-WORLD-EXPERIENCE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The premise needs a precise correction before this can be answered well, and the correct scope is itself the useful finding: ORS 455.320's county-clerk recording is not a universal requirement for every owner-built home — it is conditional. The statute exempts an owner-built dwelling only from structural-code requirements for "ceiling heights, room sizes and the maintenance of specific temperature levels" (ORS 455.320(2)), and the recording obligation in subsection (3) applies only "if the dwelling or other structure does not comply with these requirements" — i.e., only where the owner actually used the ceiling-height/room-size/temperature exemption and built something that would not otherwise meet code on those specific points. A dwelling that meets ordinary ceiling-height, room-size, and temperature-maintenance requirements (which a concrete or masonry home built to normal residential proportions almost certainly would) triggers no ORS 455.320 recording obligation at all. Critically, ORS 455.320(4) provides directly that "[n]oncompliance with subsection (3) of this section shall not affect, in any manner, any conveyance of interest in property subject to this section" ([ors455.html](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors455.html), text confirmed against the local statute file) — meaning even where the recording was legally required and was never made, the statute itself says that does not cloud title or block a sale. This is the settled legal answer for the ORS 455.320 half of the question. The R104.11 alternative-materials approval letter is a wholly separate document with no parallel statutory recording requirement at all — it is not filed with the county clerk by law, and no source found in this pass or in Wave 2/3 research to date identifies any lender or title company practice, statutory or customary, requiring it to be produced at a resale closing. No documented Oregon resale account (consistent with the gap already flagged at item 45 in Part 1 of this chapter) confirms or denies whether its absence has ever actually delayed or killed a sale; the closeout-binder practice already recommended in the manuscript (retaining the approval letter permanently as part of the property's own records, independent of any recording requirement) remains the only concrete mitigation identified.
**What to confirm and with whom:** a title company and the county clerk's office at the time of any resale — confirm whether anything was in fact recorded under ORS 455.320(3) for the specific property, and separately, ask the buyer's lender directly whether they require the R104.11 letter as underwriting documentation (this is lender-specific, not a matter of law).
**Lands in:** Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules, section 4.11 (closeout binder) and resale-timing section; this also refines the existing manuscript's ORS 455.320 framing (already correctly scoped at manuscript line 155's table, "Not a resale waiting period") by confirming the recording is conditional on using the exemption, not automatic for every owner-built home.

51. If a shop or block-production building qualifies for Oregon's agricultural-building structural exemption under ORS 455.315 because the land carries farm or forest zoning, exactly what has to be true about the building's use, not just its size, to qualify, who at the county makes that determination, and does producing blocks for sale or for the owner's own house construction, rather than for direct agricultural use, put the building outside the exemption's intended use? [CODE] (controlling agency: the local planning department determines qualification; ORS 455.315 sets the exemption) (attorney-grade: using an agricultural exemption for a use that could be read as non-agricultural manufacturing carries real risk and deserves written confirmation from the county before relying on it)

**Answered (2026-07-12):** ORS 455.315's text, read directly from the local statute file, answers the use-test half precisely. An "agricultural building" is defined by a closed list of qualifying uses: storage, maintenance, or repair of farm/forestry machinery and equipment; raising, harvesting, and selling crops or forest products; feeding, breeding, management, and sale of livestock, poultry, fur-bearing animals, or honeybees; dairying; or "any other agricultural, forestry or horticultural use or animal husbandry... including the preparation and storage of the produce raised on the farm for human use and animal use" (ORS 455.315(2)(a)(A)). Subsection (4) allows additional uses beyond that list only if the additional use is "(a) incidental and accessory to" a listed use, "(b) personal to the farm owner and the farm owner's immediate family or household," and "(c) does not pose a greater hazard to persons or property than" the listed uses. A block-production operation making concrete masonry units does not appear on the qualifying-use list under any plain reading, and manufacturing building material — even for the owner's own house — is not "preparation and storage of produce raised on the farm," which is the closest listed category. Whether it could be shoehorned in as an "incidental and accessory" personal use under subsection (4) is genuinely uncertain and turns on how a specific county reads "incidental and accessory" against a shop that is producing structural building material rather than processing farm produce; producing blocks for sale to third parties would almost certainly fail the "personal to the farm owner" test in (4)(b) regardless. The statute names no state-level qualification process — determination is a local planning department function, confirmed by item 65 below (the BCD field-office exemption form is itself submitted for planning-department or BCD review of farm/forest zoning and use, county by county). This is exactly the kind of use-classification judgment call the item's own attorney-grade flag identifies: relying on the agricultural exemption for a block-production shop, rather than an ordinary permitted accessory structure, is a use claim a county could later dispute, and the statute's own "incidental and accessory" and "personal... household" tests are not self-evidently met by a structural-block manufacturing operation.
**What to bring to that professional/what to confirm and with whom:** a written pre-application question to the specific target county's planning department (not a verbal one — see item 24 in Part 1 of this chapter for why), describing the block-production use precisely (self-use only, for-sale, or both) and asking directly whether it qualifies under ORS 455.315(2) or (4); if the answer is uncertain or the county is unwilling to commit in writing, this is a genuine candidate for a real-estate/land-use attorney review before relying on the exemption, given the retroactive-compliance risk the item itself flags.
Source: [ORS 455.315, Exemption of agricultural buildings](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors455.html) (text confirmed against local file); cross-reference item 65 below.
**Lands in:** Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules, agricultural-exemption subsection; Owner-Made Blocks and Alternative Concretes (production-shop siting).

56. Does ORS 701.305, or a related section, set a specific dollar threshold above which a contract between a homeowner and a hired contractor must be written, what is the exact current figure, and does that threshold apply to the subcontractor agreements the existing legal chapter already recommends documenting? [CODE] (controlling agency: Construction Contractors Board, under ORS chapter 701)

**Answered (2026-07-12):** ORS 701.305(1), read directly from the local statute file, sets the threshold at **$2,000**, not the roughly $2,500 figure flagged as an unverified lead in the question: "A contractor may not perform work to construct, improve or repair a residential structure or zero-lot-line dwelling for a property owner without a written contract if the aggregate contract price exceeds $2,000. If the price of a contract was initially less than $2,000, but during the course of performance the contract exceeds that amount, the contractor shall mail or otherwise deliver a written contract to the property owner not later than five days after the contractor knows or should reasonably know that the contract price will exceed $2,000. Failure to have a written contract will not void the contract." (ORS 701.305(1), [ors701.html](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors701.html), text confirmed directly against the local statute file.) Subsection (2) requires the Construction Contractors Board to adopt standard contractual terms by rule for any contract this section requires to be written. On the second half of the question: ORS 701.305 by its own text applies to "a contractor" performing work "for a property owner" — i.e., it governs the contract between the hired contractor and the homeowner directly. It does not, on its face, govern a subcontractor's agreement with the general contractor (a different contractual relationship, between two businesses, not between a contractor and a property owner), so the existing manuscript's recommendation to document subcontractor agreements in writing is good practice but rests on general contract-law prudence and the owner-builder's own liability-management interest (documenting scope, price, and licensing for CCB-compliance purposes per ORS 701.305's spirit), not on ORS 701.305's specific $2,000 trigger, which does not directly reach subcontractor-to-general-contractor agreements. Note also ORS 701.310, immediately following: a property owner has a statutory right to cancel a written contract by written notice any time before midnight of the next business day after signing, with limited exceptions (both parties agree work starts sooner; the contractor has substantially begun work; or the contract is a post-cancellation-period modification) (ORS 701.310, same source).

57. What is the complete, real shape of a Construction Contractors Board complaint and investigation against an owner-builder, as opposed to against a licensed contractor: what triggers CCB attention to an owner-builder project specifically, what does the investigation look like, and what is the realistic exposure if a paid but unlicensed helper is found on site? [CODE] [REAL-WORLD-EXPERIENCE] (controlling agency: Construction Contractors Board)

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The CCB's investigatory authority and process are set out in ORS 701.225, read directly from the local statute file, and this is the same statutory process regardless of whether the target is a licensed contractor or an owner-builder using unlicensed paid help — the statute investigates *activities*, not a pre-sorted category of person: "The Construction Contractors Board may investigate the activities of any person engaged in the building and construction industry to determine compliance with this chapter" (ORS 701.225(1)). An authorized inspector can require any person engaged in a regulated activity to demonstrate license compliance; if a person contracting directly with a property owner cannot demonstrate compliance, the inspector must issue a written notice of noncompliance, served on the person and on the property owner (or mailed to mortgagees/trust-deed beneficiaries of record), giving 10 days to demonstrate compliance before the inspector may order the work stopped (ORS 701.225(3)). If the person was never licensed at all when work began (as opposed to having let a prior license lapse), the board may order work stopped immediately, without the 10-day notice period (ORS 701.225(6)(a)). The board also holds subpoena and hearing power (ORS 701.225(4)-(5)). This confirms the investigation is not a criminal-style raid — it proceeds through a formal written-notice-and-cure structure with the property owner directly copied on the notice, which for an owner-builder means the owner is very likely to receive direct, formal notice rather than learning about an investigation secondhand. On the "what triggers it" question specifically, no comprehensive documented list of common triggers for owner-builder projects specifically (as opposed to licensed-contractor complaints) was found in this pass — see item 68 below for what could be assembled on that narrower question. 80. What specific contingency clause language (beyond the general "sole and absolute discretion" pattern found) has actually held up in an Oregon land-purchase dispute, as opposed to boilerplate that looks protective but has not been tested? [CODE] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** A real, tested, and directly on-point Oregon Court of Appeals case was located and its holding verified against the primary case text: **Zygar v. Johnson, 169 Or App 638, 10 P3d 326 (2000)** (Lane County Circuit Court appeal, decided September 13, 2000). The dispute arose from a November 1997 earnest money agreement in which the buyer's realtor had handwritten a condition onto the printed contract form: "SUBJECT TO PURCHASER'S APPROVAL OF A PEST & DRYROT REPORT." The inspection found dry rot from an unknown water-leak source; the buyer, whose fiancée had separately told him she hated the house, disapproved the report and terminated. The seller sued for breach. The Court of Appeals held for the buyer, and the key legal principle — verified directly against the case text — is that Oregon law applies a **subjective good-faith standard**, not an objective-reasonableness standard, to a satisfaction-based contingency: "buyer had to be genuinely dissatisfied with the inspection report. Once he was, however, he had the unqualified right under the contract to cancel the transaction, even if he had other reasons for exercising that right," and further, "a party may be dissatisfied with a number of aspects of a bargain," and if "one of the sources of dissatisfaction gives him a right under the contract to repudiate, the fact that there are other sources of dissatisfaction is immaterial" ([caselaw.findlaw.com/court/or-court-of-appeals/1486251.html](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/or-court-of-appeals/1486251.html); citation confirmed via [law.justia.com/cases/oregon/court-of-appeals/2000/a105018.html](https://law.justia.com/cases/oregon/court-of-appeals/2000/a105018.html)). The court also applied Oregon's rule that where a contract mixes handwritten and printed terms, the handwritten language controls over conflicting printed boilerplate (ORS 42.270) — which is itself a directly usable drafting lesson: a specific, handwritten or individually-negotiated condition tied to a named, objective document (here, "the pest & dry rot report") outperformed the printed form's general language in this case. The tested pattern this case supports, distinct from a vague "sole and absolute discretion" clause: tie the contingency explicitly to a specific, named report or event (not just "buyer's satisfaction" at large), and understand that Oregon courts will enforce a genuine subjective-dissatisfaction standard for such a clause without requiring the buyer to justify the dissatisfaction as objectively reasonable — which is more buyer-protective than the "reasonable basis" standard an earlier, unverified secondary-source summary had suggested, and this correction (verified against the actual case holding rather than an AI-summarized secondary source) is itself a finding worth flagging.
Source: Zygar v. Johnson, 169 Or App 638, 10 P3d 326 (2000), [caselaw.findlaw.com/court/or-court-of-appeals/1486251.html](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/or-court-of-appeals/1486251.html); [law.justia.com/cases/oregon/court-of-appeals/2000/a105018.html](https://law.justia.com/cases/oregon/court-of-appeals/2000/a105018.html).

What happens under Oregon contract law if a septic or well test result comes back marginal rather than clearly pass or fail, inside a contingency period, and the buyer wants to renegotiate price instead of walking away entirely; is a price-renegotiation right something that has to be written into the contingency clause explicitly, or is it available by default? [CODE] [METHOD]

Two confirmed sources establish this. First, Zygar v. Johnson (item 80 above) establishes that a standard satisfaction-based contingency's mechanism is binary by nature — the buyer is either satisfied (proceeds) or genuinely dissatisfied (may terminate); the case does not recognize, and by its own logic does not need to recognize, any third option of unilaterally demanding a price adjustment while keeping the contract alive, because the contractual right described is specifically "the unqualified right... to cancel the transaction," not a right to compel renegotiation. 86. What Oregon-specific partition-action cost and timeline data exists (the general figures gathered here are not Oregon-specific), and how does Oregon's partition statute (likely ORS chapter 105) differ from the general pattern described? [CODE] [COST]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Confirmed: Oregon's judicial partition action is governed by ORS 105.205 through 105.290, read directly from the local statute file in this pass. The process is a circuit court lawsuit, not an administrative or county process: "[w]hen several persons hold real property as tenants in common... any one or more of them may maintain a suit for the partition of the real property according to the respective rights of the persons interested therein, and for a sale of all or a part of the property if it appears that a partition cannot be had without great prejudice to the owner" (ORS 105.205).
