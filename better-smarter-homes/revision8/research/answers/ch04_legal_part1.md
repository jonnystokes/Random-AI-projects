1. Does ORS 455.446/455.447's "essential facility" and "special occupancy structure" definition ever reach a single-family owner-built dwelling, under any circumstance (home occupation, accessory structure use, future use change), or is a single-family home categorically outside the statute's restricted-construction list? [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The premise needs correcting first: the exact phrases "essential facility" and "special occupancy structure" do not appear anywhere in ORS chapter 455 (checked against the full local text of `ors455.html`, all sections, both terms). ORS 455.446 only authorizes DOGAMI to map tsunami inundation zones. ORS 455.447 is the operative section, and it uses different terms: it applies to a "major structure" (defined at 455.447(1)(c) as a building over six stories with 60,000+ sq ft aggregate floor area, any building over 10 stories, or parking structures as BCD defines by rule) and to buildings designated "Tsunami Risk Category III or IV for design" under Table 1604.5 of the Oregon Structural Specialty Code (OSSC) — the commercial code, not the ORSC. Table 1604.5's Risk Category IV is the IBC/ASCE-7 category commonly called "essential facilities" (hospitals, fire/police stations, emergency operations centers — buildings intended to remain operational after extreme events); Risk Category III covers buildings representing a substantial hazard to human life if they fail (large-occupancy assembly, etc.). A single-family dwelling is categorically Risk Category II under both the IRC/ORSC and the IBC/OSSC framework, and it does not meet the 455.447(1)(c) "major structure" size/height test under any of the circumstances the question raises (home occupation, accessory use, future use change) — none of those change a single dwelling's story count or floor area into "major structure" territory, and none reclassify it to Risk Category III or IV, which are occupancy-based classifications tied to public/emergency function, not to how one family uses its own house. So: a single-family owner-built dwelling is outside ORS 455.447's restricted-construction list on the facts as given, and it is very hard to construct a realistic path (short of building a genuine fire station or hospital) that would bring it inside. Text and definitions confirmed directly from the statute at [oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_455.447](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_455.447) (matches the local `ors455.html` text) and IBC Table 1604.5's Risk Category IV description at [up.codes/s/risk-category](https://up.codes/s/risk-category).
**Lands in:** Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules, section on hazard-zone/statute-of-restricted-construction scope (a short note that the tsunami/earthquake "major structure" statute does not reach the house is enough; no new subsection needed).

2. Where an NFPA 13D interior residential sprinkler system is installed for general fire life-safety, does Oregon or the local jurisdiction recognize the NFPA 1142 50 percent exterior-water-supply reduction it enables, and does that change the sizing conclusion in Q23? [CODE] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Note: "Q23" in this question refers to a cross-reference in the original Wave-3 seed material (a fire-suppression water-storage sizing question), not to a numbered item in this chapter's own list; that cross-referenced item is outside this agent's assignment (chapter/list boundary) and was not re-verified here — flag it for whoever finalizes the Water Independence chapter's sizing numbers.

On the sprinkler/water-supply question itself: Oregon has adopted NFPA 13D as the "Oregon One- and Two-Family Dwelling Fire Sprinkler Installation Code," 2022 edition ([up.codes/viewer/oregon/nfpa-13d-2022](https://up.codes/viewer/oregon/nfpa-13d-2022)), and BCD's own interpretation memo confirms it applies to voluntarily installed systems in one- and two-family dwellings ([oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/Documents/interp-01-01-resfiresprinklers.pdf](https://www.oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/Documents/interp-01-01-resfiresprinklers.pdf)). NFPA 1142 (2022 edition), the separate standard governing suburban/rural water-supply sizing, permits — it does not mandate — the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) to reduce the minimum required water supply when the structure is protected throughout by an automatic sprinkler system meeting NFPA 13, 13D, or 13R. A commonly cited figure is a 50 percent reduction (for example, a base 1,000 gpm/1-hour fire-flow figure for a one- and two-family dwelling under 5,000 sq ft dropping to roughly 500 gpm with an approved sprinkler system throughout) ([nfpa.org, "How Sprinklers Can Reduce the Required Water Supply for Rural Firefighting"](https://www.nfpa.org/news-blogs-and-articles/blogs/2024/02/27/using-sprinklers-to-reduce-minimum-water-supplies-for-suburban-and-rural-firefighting)). The key word is AHJ discretion: NFPA 1142 does not compel any specific fire district or rural fire protection district to grant the reduction, and Oregon has no statewide statute or rule found in this pass that forces adoption of the reduction. Whether a specific rural Oregon fire district or fire marshal will actually credit the 50 percent reduction for a specific project is a local, fact-specific determination.
**What to confirm and with whom:** the rural fire protection district or fire marshal with jurisdiction over the parcel — ask directly whether they apply NFPA 1142's sprinkler credit, and by how much, before relying on a reduced cistern or hydrant-flow figure in any water-supply sizing calculation.
**Lands in:** Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules (fire-code cross-reference) and Water Independence (the sizing conclusion itself, which needs the AHJ's actual answer, not an assumed 50 percent).

3. What insurance disclosure or hazard-zone notification obligations does Oregon law place on an owner-builder at permit application or at eventual resale for a parcel in a mapped seismic, tsunami, landslide, or wildfire hazard zone, and does meeting or exceeding IBHS/FORTIFIED-class voluntary standards change any disclosure obligation or liability exposure? [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Two separate points, and no obligation was found at permit application itself.

**At permit application:** no Oregon statute or rule was found in this pass that imposes a hazard-zone insurance-disclosure duty on the applicant at the time of pulling a building permit. Permitting is a code-compliance transaction, not a real-estate transaction, and the disclosure regime (below) is triggered by a sale, not a permit.

**At resale:** ORS 105.462-105.490 requires a seller of residential property to complete and deliver the statutory Seller's Property Disclosure Statement to every buyer who makes a written offer. The form covers, among other things, whether the property is in a designated floodplain and whether it is in "a designated slide or other geologic hazard zone"; wildfire-risk-zone status is also commonly included on the current OREF-020 version of the form used statewide. The seller's duty is limited to actual knowledge at the time of disclosure (ORS 105.464) — the seller is not required to commission a hazard-zone study, only to disclose what they actually know. A tsunami-inundation designation specifically is not a separately enumerated line item in the statute's own text as found in this pass; whether it is captured under the "geologic hazard zone" language or omitted is a fact worth confirming directly against the current OREF-020 form text rather than inferring. Sources: [ORS 105.464, oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_105.464](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_105.464); [Oregon Association of Realtors Property Seller Advisory](https://oregonrealtors.org/sites/default/files/Oregon%20Property%20Seller%20Advisory_0418_1.pdf).

**IBHS/FORTIFIED and liability/disclosure:** no Oregon statute was found that changes the seller's disclosure obligation, or an owner-builder's liability exposure, based on voluntarily meeting or exceeding an IBHS FORTIFIED-class standard. The practical effect of a FORTIFIED designation in the market is insurance-pricing (some carriers offer a premium credit for a certified FORTIFIED home), not a change in the statutory disclosure duty. Whether meeting FORTIFIED changes an owner-builder's exposure in a negligence or product-liability claim after a loss is a genuine legal question that depends on the specific facts of a specific claim; it is not answerable as a general research fact.
**What to confirm and with whom:** an Oregon real-estate attorney for the disclosure-liability interaction, and the specific insurer being underwritten for whether the project's design documentation would qualify for a FORTIFIED-adjacent credit.
**Lands in:** Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules (resale-disclosure note, adjacent to the existing 4.3 resale-timing section).

4. Does Oregon or the relevant rural county allow an accessory structure (a shop or garage) to be built and occupied before a primary dwelling exists on the parcel, and does living in it before its own certificate of occupancy create a code or insurance problem? [CODE] [REAL-WORLD-EXPERIENCE] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** No statewide ORSC or ORS provision found in this pass either grants or forbids "build the shop first, live in it later" as a general matter — this is genuinely a zoning-and-permitting question that sits with the county, not with the residential specialty code. Two things are certain from code text already established and reconfirmed in this pass:

1. Occupancy of any structure as a dwelling is separately gated by ORSC R110.1: "A building or structure shall not be used or occupied, in whole or in part, until the building official has issued a certificate of occupancy" (2023 edition; already cited in the existing manuscript chapter's section 4.8). This applies to the shop/garage itself if it is being used as a residence — a CO (or TCO) is required for that structure's residential use regardless of whether a "primary" dwelling exists elsewhere on the parcel.
2. A detached accessory structure permitted and built as non-habitable (storage, shop, garage) is not built to residential (R-3) standards for egress, light/ventilation, insulation, and sanitation. Converting it to occupancy without a permit revision and inspection to bring it up to residential requirements would itself be the code violation, separate from the "which comes first" question.

Beyond that, whether a specific rural county's land-use/zoning code allows any dwelling use (temporary or otherwise) of an accessory structure before or absent a permitted primary residence — and whether it allows a temporary manufactured-home or RV occupancy permit during construction, which several Oregon counties do offer as a documented, named permit type — is a county-by-county zoning-code fact, not a statewide one.
**What to confirm and with whom:** the specific county's planning/zoning department (not the building department) — ask by name whether they issue a temporary occupancy permit for a shop, RV, or manufactured home during construction of the primary residence, and what conditions attach.
**Lands in:** Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules, adjacent to 4.8 (Certificate Of Occupancy).

5. What is the actual ORSC section and current text for the non-habitable detached accessory-structure permit exemption (200 sq ft general, 400 sq ft on a 2-plus-acre parcel with a 20-foot setback), and does it change under the anticipated 2026 ORSC adoption? [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Confirmed as ORSC R105.2, "Work exempt from permit," item 1 under Building, 2023 edition (current as of this check). Exact text of the operative provisions:

> "Nonhabitable one-story detached accessory structures, provided that the building area does not exceed 200 square feet (18.58 m²) and does not exceed a height of 15 feet (4572 mm) measured from grade plane to the average height of the highest roof surface."
>
> **Exception:** "Where the structure is located on a parcel of 2.0 acres or greater in area, and the structure is located a minimum of 20 feet (6096 mm) from all property lines and regulated structures, the building area may be increased to 400 square feet (37.16 m²)."

Note the exception's setback is measured from *all property lines and regulated structures* (not just property lines) — the 20 feet has to clear both. This is the Oregon-specific amended text (the base IRC does not carry the 2.0-acre/400-sq-ft exception; it is an Oregon addition). Source: [up.codes/viewer/oregon/irc-2021/chapter/1/scope-and-administration_orsc](https://up.codes/viewer/oregon/irc-2021/chapter/1/scope-and-administration_orsc), R105.2, checked 2026-07-12.

Note also that the exemption is from the *building* permit only — separate electrical, plumbing, and mechanical permits are still required for any regulated work inside the structure, and the structure still has to meet the ORSC's structural, material, and safety requirements even without a permit (permit-exempt is not code-exempt).

**2026 edition:** the code library and this pass both confirm the 2026 ORSC (2024 IRC base) is anticipated to adopt near October 1, 2026, per the project's own edition-currency trigger. No confirmation either way was found in this pass that R105.2's specific figures will change in the 2026 cycle — BCD's 2026 ORSC matrix document exists ([oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/Documents/26orsc-matrix.pdf](https://www.oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/Documents/26orsc-matrix.pdf)) but was not opened in this pass to check for an R105.2 amendment line item. Recheck this specific section against that matrix once the 2026 edition is closer to formal adoption.

6. Is an alternative-materials request under R104.11 handled inside ordinary plan review, on the standard timeline, or as a separate process with its own clock, and does the ORS 455.467 plan-review timeline (10 or 15 business days) still apply to an application that includes an alternative-materials request? [CODE] (controlling agency: local building department; the statutory timeline is set by the Oregon Legislature and administered by BCD)

**Answered (2026-07-12):** These are two different processes on two different clocks, confirmed from primary text.

ORS 455.467(1) sets the plan-review clock: for "simple low-rise residential dwellings," 10 business days in a jurisdiction under 300,000 population, 15 business days at 300,000 or more, after a complete application. But 455.467(2)(b) explicitly exempts "a complex structure that requires additional review as determined by the department or municipality" from that clock. A submission that includes an R104.11 alternative-materials request is a strong practical fit for that exception — it plausibly is not a routine plan review — but the statute does not name R104.11 requests specifically, so this is an inference from the "complex structure" language, not an explicit carve-out; flag it that way rather than as settled fact.

Separately, and more concretely: the *state-level* alternate-method process itself (Statewide Code Interpretation under OAR 918-008-0085, or an Alternate Method Ruling under OAR 918-008-0095) runs through "the division" (BCD, in Salem) — a track that is administratively distinct from the local building department's plan review of a specific permit application. Full text of both rules was fetched directly ([oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0085](https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0085); [oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0095](https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0095)). Neither rule states a statutory decision deadline (unlike the appeal process at OAR 918-008-0120, which does set explicit 14/30-day decision windows — see item 12 below). So an R104.11/AMR request, when it is run as a state-level petition rather than folded into local plan review, is not bound by the ORS 455.467 10/15-day clock at all; it is bound by nothing but BCD's own processing pace.

Practical reading: if the alternative-materials question is submitted *as part of* the local building-permit application, the local reviewer very plausibly treats it as the 455.467(2)(b) "complex structure" exception and the 10/15-day clock does not apply. If it is instead run as a separate state-level petition (which the rules allow — see item 9, an AMR does not need a site address), it was never on the 455.467 clock in the first place.
**Lands in:** Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules, new subsection on the alternate-method process (this cluster of items, 6-21, could support one).

7. Once an alternative material is approved for one house, does the approval letter become a reusable reference for future permits on the same property, such as an addition or a second structure, or does every new permit require a fresh R104.11 request even for an already-approved material? [CODE] (controlling agency: local building department)

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The rule text supports reusability, by contrast with how the rules define the one process that is explicitly *not* reusable. OAR 918-008-0080's definitions (fetched directly, [oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0080](https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0080)) define a Site-Specific Interpretation as applying "only to a single project" — that limitation is written into the definition. Neither the Statewide Code Interpretation definition nor the Alternate Method Ruling definition carries that "single project" limitation; an Alternate Method Ruling is defined simply as "a request to rule on the acceptability of new materials, designs, or innovative methods of construction not covered by the state building code," and the division distributes its decision under ORS 455.060 (the general rule-distribution statute), consistent with a determination meant to stand generally rather than attach to one address.

That structure — one process explicitly scoped to a single project, two processes not so scoped — is the strongest evidence available that an approved Alternate Method Ruling is a standing, statewide determination about the material or method itself, not a permit-specific approval that expires with the first house. A second house on the same property, or anywhere else in Oregon, using the same material within the ruling's stated scope and conditions would not need a fresh AMR request on the material question itself. The important caveat, consistent with how the related Oregon Code and Compliance Map chapter's research treats this (a different chapter's question bank, not reproduced here, but the logic is shared): a local building official retains ordinary plan-review authority over the *specific project* — foundation design, connections, fire separation, and so on — even where the material itself is no longer in dispute. So "reusable" means the material/method question does not need to be relitigated; it does not mean the second permit skips plan review altogether.
**Lands in:** Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules, alternate-method subsection.

9. Can an R104.11 alternative-materials request be submitted and decided before the land is purchased or the full building permit is filed, the way a pre-application meeting can happen early, so the owner knows the material is viable before committing to a site? [METHOD] [CREATIVE-IDEA]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Yes, on the state-level track, and this follows directly from the required-fields text of the rule itself. OAR 918-008-0095(1) lists exactly three required fields for an Alternate Method Ruling request: (a) information on the material, design, or method; (b) the specialty code section at issue; (c) a brief description of the technical and scientific facts giving rise to the need for a ruling. No parcel address or site identification is required. Contrast this directly with OAR 918-008-0090(1)(c), the Site-Specific Interpretation rule, which explicitly requires "the physical address of the building site" as a required field — and which, separately, only a building official (not a property owner) may request in the first place (see item 10). Because the AMR fields do not require a site, and because ORS 455.060 governs the division's decision distribution generally rather than tying it to a specific permit, there is nothing in the rule structure that requires land ownership, or even a specific target parcel, before filing an AMR request. The material or method itself is what is being ruled on. Sources: [oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0090](https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0090); [oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0095](https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0095).

Practical note: this makes the AMR route materially more useful than a local pre-application meeting for exactly the purpose the question raises (de-risking the material question before committing to a site), since a local pre-app meeting is inherently tied to a specific jurisdiction and, often, a specific parcel.
**Lands in:** Legal, Permits, and Owner-Builder Rules, alternate-method subsection; cross-reference from Land, Site, and Feasibility as a sequencing tool.

10. Exactly which of Oregon's three statewide tools, the site-specific interpretation, the statewide code interpretation, and the alternate method ruling, can a property owner petition directly, and which can only be requested by a building official, and does that distinction change the practical strategy for an owner facing a denial? [CODE] (controlling agency: Oregon Building Codes Division, under OAR 918-008)

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Confirmed directly from OAR 918-008-0080, -0085, -0090, -0095 (all fetched in full):

| Tool | Who may request | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Site-Specific Interpretation | A building official only. "A building official may request a site-specific interpretation..." | OAR 918-008-0090(1) |
| Statewide Code Interpretation | A "petitioner" — defined broadly (see below) | OAR 918-008-0085(1) |
| Alternate Method Ruling | A "petitioner" — same broad definition | OAR 918-008-0095(1) |

OAR 918-008-0080 defines "petitioner" as: "Any person residing, currently doing business, wishing to do business, or owning property in the State of Oregon," or a building official authorized under the relevant ORS sections. So a property owner can petition directly for a Statewide Code Interpretation and for an Alternate Method Ruling — the two tools most relevant to a novel wall system — but cannot request a Site-Specific Interpretation directly; that one has to come from the building official.

**Practical strategy implication, confirmed by this structure:** if a local building official denies or is reluctant to approve an alternative material, the owner is not dependent on that same official's willingness to escalate — the owner can independently petition BCD directly for a Statewide Code Interpretation or an Alternate Method Ruling on the material itself, bypassing the need for the local official's cooperation to get a state-level review started. The Site-Specific Interpretation tool (official-only) is the one the owner cannot use as a self-help escalation path; it is a tool the official might use to get a single-project answer from the state, not a tool available to route around an unwilling official. Sources: [oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0080](https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0080); [-0085](https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0085); [-0090](https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0090); [-0095](https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_918-008-0095).

13. How does a petitioner engage with the relevant advisory board during an Alternate Method Ruling: is there a hearing the petitioner can attend and present at, or is the board's review conducted entirely on paper? [CODE] (controlling agency: Oregon Building Codes Division advisory boards)

What does it mean, day to day, to have a building department run by a contracted private firm, such as Northwest Code Professionals, rather than county staff: the same fee schedule, the same appeal rights, the same inspector continuity from project to project, or does contracting change any of those? [REAL-WORLD-EXPERIENCE] [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** There is a real, statutory, functional difference, and it is more specific than a generic "private firm vs. government staff" distinction — Oregon law imposes an extra oversight layer specifically on *discretionary decisions*, and an alternative-materials approval is expressly defined as one. Read directly from ORS 455.202-455.208 (local `ors455.html` text; this is squarely on point for a contracted-department scenario):

- ORS 455.202(1)(c) defines "discretionary decision" as: "(A) Waiving a plan review, an inspection or a provision of the state building code; or (B) Allowing an alternative material, design or method of construction." An R104.11-style alternative-materials approval made by a contract building official is, by this statute's own definition, a "discretionary decision."
- ORS 455.202(1)(d) and (3): the municipality must designate a "qualified employee" — its own staff, trained to the director's standard — who must "review and ratify or disapprove a contract building official's discretionary decision within 30 days after receiving notice of the decision." The contract official must notify both the qualified employee and the permit applicant of each discretionary decision, with a description of hearing and appeal options.
- ORS 455.202(4): the municipality "must establish a local board to which a permit applicant may appeal a contract building official's discretionary decisions" (with narrow exceptions where a neighboring county's board already covers it) — a board layer that exists specifically because the building official is a contractor, not municipal staff.
- ORS 455.202(5)(a): this local-board appeal right is explicitly "in addition to and not in lieu of any other rights of appeal the permit applicant may have" — i.e., the standard ORS 455.475/OAR 918-008-0120 appeal path still exists on top of this one.
- ORS 455.202(6): the municipality must have an independent auditor examine the building-inspection-program finances at least every two years where a contract building official is used.

So the honest day-to-day answer: appeal rights are *not* diminished by contracting — they are added to. A property owner disputing a contract official's alternative-materials call has both the standard state appeal route and this contract-specific local-board route, plus a mandatory 30-day municipal ratification check on the decision itself before it is even final. What is *not* addressed by this statute, and was not found elsewhere in this pass, is fee-schedule parity or inspector continuity — those are contract-specific business terms between the municipality and the firm, not something ORS 455.202-455.208 standardizes. Source: [oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_455.202](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_455.202) (matches local `ors455.html`); Northwest Code Professionals' own description of itself as providing "inspection, digital and paper plan review, and code consulting" for contracted jurisdictions confirmed at [nwcodepros.com/about-northwest-code-pros](https://nwcodepros.com/about-northwest-code-pros/), checked 2026-07-13.
