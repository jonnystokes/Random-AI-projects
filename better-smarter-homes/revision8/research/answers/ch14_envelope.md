**1. How does radon enter through the slab/foundation, and what construction sequence builds an effective passive system that can later be upgraded to active?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Two parts.

*Entry mechanism.* EPA's *A Citizen's Guide to Radon* (EPA402/K-12/002) states that radon enters through cracks in solid floors, construction joints, cracks in walls, gaps in suspended floors, and gaps around service pipes, and that a house with indoor air pressure lower than the surrounding soil (the typical case) acts as a mild vacuum on the soil beneath it. https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-12/documents/2016_a_citizens_guide_to_radon.pdf The driving force is stack effect: warm interior air rising and exiting high in the building depressurizes the lower level, and that depressurized substructure pulls soil gas in through every available opening in the slab and foundation wall. This is the same physical mechanism the envelope chapter's air-sealing section (8.3) already describes for uncontrolled air leakage generally; radon entry is a special case of the same pressure-driven flow, moving soil gas instead of exterior air. EPA, "How to Address Radon When Building a New Home": https://www.epa.gov/radon/how-address-radon-when-building-new-home

*Construction sequence.* Already fully specified in the envelope chapter section 8.11 (Radon Control), which states the ORSC Appendix AF passive-system components in built order: gas-permeable layer (4-inch clean stone) under the slab, 6-mil poly or 15-mil ASTM E1745 Class A soil-gas retarder over it, a 3-inch vent pipe embedded vertically in the aggregate before the slab pour with a T-fitting, every slab joint and penetration sealed, gasketed sump covers, a solid/grouted course in any hollow-block foundation wall at or above grade, and the vent pipe carried up through the roof with attic access and a roughed-in electrical circuit reserved for a future fan. That last element (AF103.8 attic access, AF103.12 the future-fan circuit) is precisely what makes the system upgradable: the system operates passively on stack effect at occupancy, and if post-occupancy testing (also specified in 8.11) shows elevated radon, an in-line fan is added at the already-accessible attic location and wired to the already-roughed-in circuit, converting the system to active subslab depressurization without demolition. No additional research is needed on this item; it restates and slightly extends what the manuscript already carries at full citation depth (ORSC Appendix AF; ORS 455.365; ANSI/AARST RRNC-2020 and CCAH-2020; EPA).

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Already answered in the envelope chapter at full depth. Section 8.1 ("The continuity check") lists exactly this discontinuity set: slab-to-wall transition, wall-to-roof transition at eave or parapet, window/door rough openings (head, jambs, sill), penetrations (pipes, conduits, vents, exhaust fans, hose bibs, condensate drains, structural ties), the below-grade-to-above-grade transition, and mechanical/electrical service entries. **3. How do interior humidity plus air leakage produce hidden condensation and mold inside walls and roofs, and what mix of mechanical ventilation (HRV/ERV, spot exhaust), air-sealing, and vapor control keeps assemblies safe?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Building Science Corporation's BSD-012 ("Moisture Control for New Residential Buildings") identifies air leakage condensation as one of the two major sources of moisture in the above-grade enclosure: wintertime condensation of water vapor carried in exfiltrating warm interior air (or, in summer, infiltrating humid exterior air) inside a wall or roof cavity, occurring wherever that air reaches a surface below the dew point. Walls with only cavity insulation and no dedicated air-control layer are named as particularly susceptible, because air movement through the cavity, not vapor diffusion through the materials, carries the bulk of the moisture. https://buildingscience.com/documents/digests/bsd-012-moisture-control-for-new-residential-buildings BSD-014 ("Air Flow Control in Buildings") states the corollary: in cold and mixed climates, enclosures are built airtight specifically so that ventilation can be controlled and deliberate rather than accidental, because uncontrolled infiltration cannot be relied on to dilute interior moisture and pollutants at a known rate. https://buildingscience.com/documents/digests/bsd-014-air-flow-control-in-buildings

This is the same working order the envelope chapter already states in section 8.14 (Ventilation Coordination): "the envelope eliminates uncontrolled air exchange; the mechanical system supplies controlled fresh air at the designed rate," ventilation sized to ASHRAE 62.2 through ORSC M1507, with combustion equipment sealed throughout because a tight envelope does not supply reliable infiltration air for atmospheric venting. The three-part mix the question asks for is therefore: (1) air-sealing to the Measure 7 threshold (2.75 ACH50, section 8.3) so leakage-driven condensation has nowhere to happen, (2) mechanical ventilation at ASHRAE 62.2 rates through the HRV plus spot exhaust at baths/kitchen/dryer (section 8.14, and the HVAC chapter for equipment sizing), and (3) vapor control per section 8.10, which for this project's concrete and masonry walls is inherent (low-permeance mass, exterior insulation and rainscreen drying outward, Class III painted drywall inward, no interior polyethylene anywhere in the design).

On mold specifically: Building Science Corporation's guidance for cold-climate assemblies is to hold interior relative humidity in the 25 to 30 percent range at 70 °F during the heating season, which keeps the relative humidity at interior wall surfaces below the roughly 70 percent threshold where surface mold growth becomes active. https://buildingscience.com/documents/digests/bsd-012-moisture-control-for-new-residential-buildings That is a cold-climate design target for surface condensation risk, not a universal number; the HVAC chapter's 40-60 percent whole-house RH design range (already cited in envelope-chapter section 8.10) governs comfort and occupant health, and the two numbers are not in conflict because the 25-30 percent figure describes the microclimate right at a cold single-pane-era wall surface, which this project's continuous-exterior-insulation wall does not reproduce (the structural mass stays near room temperature, so interior-surface RH tracks close to the whole-house RH). Any cold-deck or non-mass assembly in the design (the wood-framed truss roof option, section 8.7.3) should still be checked against ASHRAE 160 per the existing section 8.10 requirement.

**4. Which insulation-installation defects quietly waste the biggest share of performance (compressed/voided batts, gaps, wind-washing, thermal bypasses, missing continuous insulation), and how do pros install to Grade-I quality?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The controlling standard is ANSI/RESNET/ICC 301, Normative Appendix A, which defines three installation grades by measured defect area per cavity:

- **Grade I:** complete fill, uniform thickness, no gaps, no compression, no voids around obstructions, material in contact with all six sides of the cavity.
- **Grade II:** 0.5 to 2 percent missing-insulation area (roughly 7 to 27 square inches per a typical stud bay) and up to 10 percent compressed area (roughly 133 square inches per stud bay).
- **Grade III:** more than 2 percent missing area or more than 10 percent compressed area.

https://www.resnet.us/wp-content/uploads/ANSIRESNETICC301-2022_resnetpblshd.pdf ; grade thresholds also summarized at https://www.energyvanguard.com/blog/how-to-grade-the-installation-quality-of-insulation/

The performance cost of missing Grade I is large and well quantified: a Grade III installation performs roughly 30 percent or more worse than the same nominal R-value installed at Grade I, so a wall specified at R-19 but installed at Grade III behaves like R-13 or worse in service. https://www.energyvanguard.com/blog/how-to-grade-the-installation-quality-of-insulation/ Wind-washing (attic or wall-cavity air movement scouring the outer face of low-density insulation, most often at eaves and soffits) is disproportionately damaging: research reported by the Building Performance Association found that as little as one percent of an attic left uninsulated because of wind-washing can cut the average whole-attic R-value by 27 percent, because the uninsulated fraction acts as a near-total thermal short circuit. https://building-performance.org/bpa-journal/wind-washing/ Framing itself is a thermal bypass even where insulation is installed correctly: standard wood framing reduces a wall's effective R-value 14 to 18 percent below the cavity-insulation's nominal rating (a 2x6 wall with R-23 batts and standard framing performs at roughly whole-wall R-18), which is why continuous exterior insulation, this project's specified strategy, is valuable independent of installation quality: it covers the framing member and eliminates that bypass path entirely. https://sinoinsulation.com/is-the-compression-of-fiberglass-insulation-a-problem/ Compression has its own separate effect: compressing a batt rated for a given cavity depth into a shallower cavity raises R-value per inch slightly but lowers total R-value, because thickness is lost faster than density-driven R-per-inch is gained (an R-19 batt made for a 6-inch cavity, compressed into 4 inches, delivers well under R-19).

How pros hit Grade I in practice, per the same sources plus the Building America Solution Center Grade-1 installation guide: cut batts to the exact cavity dimension rather than stuffing a slightly oversized batt (stuffing creates hidden voids at the back of the cavity, not visible compression at the face); split batts around wiring and boxes instead of compressing the batt in front of the obstruction; install a top-of-wall air-impermeable dam (blocking or a rigid baffle) at vented-attic eaves specifically to stop wind-washing before it starts; and inspect and correct at the pre-drywall stage, while the cavity is still open, rather than relying on a post-drywall infrared scan to catch problems that are already covered. https://constructioninstruction.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/BASC-Insulation-Installation-RESNET-Grade-1.pdf This lines up exactly with the envelope chapter's own testing program (section 8.13), which already sequences an infrared scan after insulation and before drywall specifically to catch these defects.

For this project's specific assemblies: the continuous-exterior-insulation wall strategy (envelope chapter section 8.4) sidesteps most cavity-insulation grading risk entirely, since there is no batt cavity in the primary wall assembly (rigid board over solid-grouted masonry); the residual defect risks there are board-to-board gaps at joints (addressed by the manuscript's staggered double-layer detail) and compression or misalignment at wall ties (addressed by the manuscript's tie-spacing and thermally-broken-tie guidance). Grade-I risk concentrates instead in any remaining framed assemblies: the wood-framed truss roof option (section 8.7.3) and any hybrid framed service walls (section 8.10). Recommend the manuscript state the RESNET 301 Grade I target explicitly wherever batt or blown insulation appears in a framed assembly, and require the pre-drywall infrared/visual grading inspection already scheduled in section 8.13 to record a Grade I/II/III call, not just a pass/fail blower-door number.

**5. Where are the worst thermal bridges in a concrete/masonry home (slab edge, shelf angles/balconies, lintels, ties, wall-floor junctions), and which thermal-break products and details cut them?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Slab edge and wall ties are already covered at full depth in the envelope chapter (sections 8.6.1 and 8.4's "Wall ties and thermal bridging" table, with the FRP/thermally-broken-tie options and their percentage bridging penalties). Two items in this question are not yet in the manuscript: shelf angles/balconies and masonry lintels as thermal-bridge locations specifically (lintels appear in section 8.8 as a structural/flashing item, not as a thermal bridge).

*Shelf angles and balconies.* Any cantilevered concrete element that penetrates the exterior insulation plane (a balcony slab, a steel shelf angle carrying a masonry veneer at a floor line) is a direct, uninsulated conductive path between interior and exterior structure, and is a materially worse bridge than a wall tie because it is a full structural member, not a small-diameter connector. The commercial product category that addresses this is the structural thermal break: a factory unit (rebar continuity plus a load-bearing insulating core, typically a fiber-reinforced polymer or basalt-fiber bearing element) cast into the joint between the interior floor slab and the cantilevered exterior element. Schöck Isokorb, the market-leading product line, publishes that its balcony thermal breaks reduce heat loss at the connection by up to 90 percent versus an uninsulated cast-in-place connection, eliminate the condensation risk at the connection, and raise the interior floor temperature near the balcony door or exterior wall by as much as 34 °F (19 °C) compared to an unbroken connection. https://www.schoeck.com/en-us/balcony The same manufacturer also sells a thermally broken shelf-angle bracket system for supporting exterior masonry veneer at floor lines, engineered so the continuous exterior insulation runs behind the brackets without a solid uninsulated angle running the full wall length. https://www.schoeck.com/en-us/isokorb-thermal-breaks Structural acceptance of any thermal-break connector, like the FRP ties already noted in section 8.4, requires an ICC-ESR and project-specific structural engineering sign-off; this is not a prescriptive-code item.

*Lintels.* The envelope chapter's section 8.8 already states that ORSC contains no prescriptive lintel table and that every masonry lintel is an engineered element (TMS 402 or ACI 318). Thermally, an uninsulated precast concrete or steel lintel spanning a window or door opening in an otherwise continuously insulated wall is a bridge in the same category as a shelf angle, sized to the opening width. The mitigation is detailing, not a product: extend the continuous exterior insulation over the exterior face of the lintel (the insulation does not need to stop at the opening head the way the structural member does), and where a steel angle lintel is used, specify it with a thermal break or an insulated cover plate on the exterior face rather than exposed steel bridging straight through the insulation line.

*Wall-floor junctions.* Already addressed structurally by the recommendation, elsewhere in the manuscript, to keep the exterior insulation continuous past floor lines in a way that does not require the floor structure itself to penetrate the insulation plane (i.e., design the floor to bear on the interior face of the wall, not to cantilever through it). Where a floor structure must bear on or penetrate an insulated wall, it becomes a shelf-angle-class bridge and the same thermal-break product category applies.

Whether the project's actual design includes any cantilevered balcony is an owner design decision; the products and the bridging severity are the researchable, settled part of this answer.

**6. Which water-resistive-barrier and drainage-plane mistakes cause bulk-water intrusion (reverse laps, failing seam tape, no drainage gap behind cladding, incompatible sealants), and what shingle-lap/rain-screen discipline prevents them?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The envelope chapter's section 8.9 already establishes the rainscreen assembly (cladding, drainage gap, WRB, insulation, air-control layer) and cites the governing standards (ORSC R703.1, R703.2, R703.4; ASTM E2178/E2357 for air-barrier materials and assemblies; ASTM C920 for sealants). This question asks specifically for the mistake list and the underlying principle, which is not yet stated as such.

The governing principle, from Building Science Corporation's Info-302 ("Pan Flashing for Exterior Wall Openings," which the manuscript's window section already implicitly follows): every layer must lap in shingle fashion so that water moving down the wall is always directed to the exterior face of the layer below, never behind it. https://buildingscience.com/documents/information-sheets/pan-flashing-for-exterior-wall-openings Four specific failure modes, each a violation of that one principle:

- **Reverse laps.** An upper course of WRB, tape, or flashing installed so its edge sits behind (rather than over) the course below tucks water directly into the wall assembly instead of shedding it. This is the single most common WRB installation error cited across residential building-science literature and the reason the shingle-lap rule exists as a stated discipline rather than an assumed default.
- **Failing seam tape.** Tape bonds fail from three causes: taping over dust, moisture, or mill-glaze residue on the WRB surface without cleaning first; using a tape not chemically compatible with the specific WRB facer (housewraps, fluid-applied membranes, and peel-and-stick membranes each have their own manufacturer-approved tape list); and UV exposure of tape left uncovered by cladding longer than the manufacturer's stated exposure window. The fix is procedural, not material: use only the WRB manufacturer's listed tape, and cover the WRB with cladding inside its published UV-exposure window.
- **No drainage gap behind cladding.** Cladding installed directly against the WRB with no rainscreen gap (common with adhered stone veneer, some stucco systems, and face-nailed lap siding without furring) traps any water that gets past the cladding against the WRB face with no path to drain or dry, which is the specific failure mode section 8.9's rainscreen gap (3/4-inch minimum) is designed to prevent. The manuscript's own cladding table already specifies a drained, back-ventilated rainscreen for every cladding option listed; the mistake to flag explicitly is any field substitution that omits the furring strip or gap to save cost.
- **Incompatible sealants.** Sealant chemistry can attack certain WRB facers and membrane adhesives (petroleum-based or some polyurethane sealants against some asphalt-based or PVC-faced membranes) or fail to bond to a low-surface-energy WRB. This is manufacturer-specific and not resolvable by a general rule: confirm sealant-to-substrate compatibility against the WRB manufacturer's published compatibility chart before specifying, at the same submittal stage the manuscript already requires for ASTM C920 sealant selection.

Lands in: Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, and Radon (new content, extends section 8.9 with the shingle-lap principle and the four-item mistake list).

**7. For a durable mass-wall envelope, what creative continuous-insulation and thermal-mass strategies work (interior vs exterior insulation on mass, insulation fully outboard for a "thermal battery," phase-change materials, bermed/earth-coupled walls), and what are the moisture/detailing tradeoffs?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Interior-vs-exterior placement and the "outboard insulation couples the mass to the interior" principle are already the core of the envelope chapter's section 8.4 (Wall Insulation Strategy) and are the project's chosen strategy. Two items this question raises are not yet in the manuscript: phase-change materials and the earth-coupled/bermed detailing tradeoff.

*Phase-change materials (PCM).* PCMs (commonly paraffin wax, fatty acids, hydrated salts, or butyl stearate) absorb and release heat at a nearly constant temperature during their solid-liquid phase transition, functioning as added thermal storage that is far lighter than concrete mass for the same stored-heat capacity (reported up to roughly 9 times lighter than dense concrete for equivalent thermal storage). https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1944/14/18/5328 A 2024 California Energy Commission-funded study specifically examined PCM-enhanced insulation for residential exterior wall retrofits. https://www.energy.ca.gov/publications/2024/phase-change-material-enhanced-insulation-residential-exterior-wall-retrofits Moisture is the detailing risk: unencapsulated or poorly sealed PCM is subject to leakage, moisture-transfer problems, evaporation, and degradation from water absorption; the proposed mitigation in the literature is to contain the PCM in sealed polymer pouches laminated in aluminum-foil sheets, which also blocks moisture ingress into the PCM layer itself. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306261914009428 PCM has no Oregon prescriptive code path (it does not appear in ORSC Table N1101.1(1) or the BCD trade-off calculator's material list) and no track record in this project's climate or wall type found in this research pass; it is a genuine but unproven-for-this-application option, best framed to the owner as a supplemental, not load-bearing, thermal strategy layered onto (not substituted for) the specified continuous-exterior-insulation mass wall.

*Bermed/earth-coupled walls.* CMHA TEK 06-07A ("Earth-Sheltered Buildings," the same technical-note series already cited elsewhere in this manuscript, e.g. TEK 19-05A for CMU flashing) directly addresses this. It recommends exterior insulation on below-grade masonry walls for the same reason section 8.4 already gives for above-grade walls: exterior insulation keeps the mass at conditioned temperature, and it adds a second, earth-sheltering-specific reason, that exterior insulation protects the waterproofing membrane from abrasion damage during backfill and from subsequent soil movement. https://www.cmha.org/resource/tek-06-07a/ The tradeoff TEK 06-07A names explicitly: exterior insulation blocks the wall's ability to function as a summer heat sink (a bermed wall with no exterior insulation cools toward stable earth temperature and can absorb interior heat gain in warm weather; insulated on the exterior, it cannot), which can raise summer indoor humidity and condensation risk enough to require supplemental mechanical dehumidification. Waterproofing for an earth-sheltered or bermed wall is a multi-layered system beyond the envelope chapter's existing below-grade table (section 8.6.2): a primary waterproofing membrane (rubberized asphalt, EPDM, or PVC sheet) directly on the structural wall, a drainage board or gravel drainage plane outboard of the insulation to relieve hydrostatic pressure, and, specifically for a berm (as opposed to a simple below-grade wall), an interceptor drain at the crest of the berm and/or a drainage swale partway down the berm slope to keep surface water from ever reaching the wall's waterproofing in the first place. https://www.cmha.org/resource/tek-06-07a/ ; https://www.motherearthnews.com/green-homes/earth-sheltered-house-zmaz81ndzraw This is a design option, not a fact to settle: whether any part of the house design is bermed or earth-coupled is the owner's choice, and the tradeoff (thermal stability and reduced heat loss, against a more complex waterproofing system and a summer humidity risk that may require added dehumidification) is what the owner needs to weigh.

**8. For a rural crawlspace or basement, is a vented or sealed/conditioned approach better in the Oregon marine climate, and what moisture failures does each risk (ground vapor, summer humid-air condensing on cool surfaces)?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Code baseline first. ORSC Section R408 (2021 IRC base, per the 2023 ORSC in effect at this writing) sets the vented-crawlspace default and the unvented alternative:

- R408.1/R408.2.2: naturally ventilated crawlspaces need vent openings totaling at least 1 square foot per 150 square feet of underfloor area in the seven radon-mandated counties (Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, Yamhill), an Oregon-specific amendment; outside those counties, with the ground surface covered per R408.2.1, the ratio drops to 1 square foot per 1,500 square feet.
- R408.3: ventilation openings may be omitted (an unvented/sealed crawlspace) where the exposed earth is covered with a continuous Class I vapor retarder (6-mil-minimum poly, joints lapped 6 inches, edges run up the stem wall) and the space is conditioned by one of: continuously operated mechanical exhaust at 1.0 cfm per 50 square feet of crawlspace floor area, conditioned supply air delivered at the same rate with a return-air path, or dehumidification sized to remove at least 70 pints per day per 1,000 square feet.

Sources: https://up.codes/viewer/oregon/irc-2021/chapter/4/foundations (Oregon-specific R408.2.2 radon-county amendment) and the base R408.3 text at https://up.codes/s/unvented-crawl-space (2021 IRC edition; confirm no further Oregon amendment to R408.3 itself at permit application, since only R408.2.2 was found amended). This is edition-tied to the 2023 ORSC (2021 IRC base); recheck against the 2026 ORSC (anticipated 2024 IRC base, adoption near October 1, 2026).

Performance evidence favors sealed/conditioned in Oregon's marine climate specifically. DOE Building America research on unvented, conditioned crawlspaces reports 15 to 18 percent heating/cooling energy savings and more than 20 percent reduction in crawlspace humidity compared to vented crawlspaces of the same house. https://www.energy.gov/cmei/buildings/articles/unvented-conditioned-crawlspaces-building-america-top-innovation The failure mode specific to marine and other humid climates is the one the question names: vented crawlspaces are built on the assumption that outside air is drier than crawlspace air, but in a humid marine climate that assumption reverses for large parts of the year, so vent-driven outside air is often more humid than the space it is ventilating, and that humid air condenses on the cool framing and foundation surfaces inside the crawlspace, producing rot, mold, and pest habitat rather than preventing it. https://www.finehomebuilding.com/project-guides/insulation/crawlspaces-that-work Sealing the vents, insulating the wall (not the floor above), and covering the ground with a sealed vapor barrier removes both failure paths at once: it blocks ground-sourced vapor at the source and it removes the humid-outside-air pathway that a vented crawl depends on.

For this project: the recommended approach is the sealed/conditioned crawlspace or basement, consistent with the envelope chapter's existing below-grade insulation strategy (section 8.6.2, insulation and waterproofing run continuously as one below-grade assembly) and its radon strategy (section 8.11, the sub-slab gas-permeable layer and soil-gas retarder already specified for a slab-on-grade apply equally to a crawlspace floor, since R408.3's vapor-retarder requirement and the radon soil-gas retarder are the same physical layer serving two functions). Ground vapor risk is addressed by the Class I retarder; summer condensation risk is addressed by conditioning the space rather than venting it with humid outside air.

Lands in: Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, and Radon (new content, cross-reference the below-grade table in section 8.6.2 and the radon system in section 8.11); cross-reference Foundation, Basement, Slabs, and Concrete Placement for the construction sequence if a crawlspace foundation type is used.

**9. What are the most common window/door flashing failures (no sill pan, reverse-lapped head flashing, sealant-only installs, missing back dam), and what pan-flashing sequence keeps openings watertight in a masonry wall?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The pan-flashing sequence is already fully specified in the envelope chapter's section 8.8 ("Applied: rough-opening sequence, continuous-exterior-insulation wall"), a 10-step sequence from structural rough opening through interior air-seal, already including the sill-pan-with-back-dam detail, jamb flashing lapped over the sill flashing, and head flashing continuous with the WRB. That sequence is settled; this question additionally asks for the failure modes it exists to prevent, which the manuscript states as a design rule ("leave the sill unsealed so incidental water drains out") without naming the failure each step blocks. Building Science Corporation's Info-302 and IIBEC's published field guidance name the four failures directly:

- **No sill pan.** Without a dedicated sill pan (a sloped, dammed sub-sill flashing independent of the window unit's own sill), any water that reaches the rough sill, whether from a failed perimeter seal or from wind-driven rain forced past the window frame, lands directly on wood sheathing or framing with no drainage path, and is absorbed rather than shed. https://buildingscience.com/documents/information-sheets/pan-flashing-for-exterior-wall-openings
- **Reverse-lapped head flashing.** Head flashing installed so it is tucked behind, rather than lapped over, the WRB above it directs water from the wall plane down into the rough opening instead of past it, turning the flashing into a funnel. This is the head-flashing version of the general reverse-lap failure described in item 6 above.
- **Sealant-only installs.** Relying on a bead of sealant at the window flange as the primary water defense, instead of a full flashing sequence, fails because sealant is a maintained, wear item (movement, UV, and adhesion loss all degrade it over years) standing in for what should be a passive, gravity-drained shingle-lap system with no maintenance dependency. The building-science framing is explicit: "correct flashing is a sequence, not a product," and sealant's role is to close the small gaps within an already-complete flashing sequence, not to replace it. https://buildingscience.com/documents/information-sheets/pan-flashing-for-exterior-wall-openings
- **Missing back dam.** Without a back dam (a raised lip at the interior edge of the sill pan, or a bead of sealant across the window's interior sill-to-jamb connection) any water that collects in the pan has no stop to prevent it running back toward the interior rough-opening framing instead of draining out the exterior face; the manuscript's step 3 (sill pan "turned up 6 inches at the jambs, with a 4-inch leg down the wall face") already builds the jamb dams, and the back dam is the corresponding interior-edge stop that should be named explicitly alongside it.

Recommend the manuscript's section 8.8 sequence add one clause explicitly naming the back dam at the interior edge of the sill pan (currently implied by "leave the sill unsealed" but not stated as a discrete dam element) and, optionally, a short callout naming which of the four failure modes each numbered step defeats, since that framing (what breaks if this step is skipped) is what a first-time owner-builder needs to retain the discipline under field pressure to shortcut it.

Lands in: Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, and Radon (extends existing section 8.8).

The compatibility risk to this project's HRV system specifically is smaller than the general vent-airflow question suggests, because R327.3.2's ember-resistant-mesh requirement targets passive attic, soffit, rafter, and underfloor vents, the openings that a wildfire ember can reach because they have no fan behind them, not the dedicated intake and exhaust hoods that terminate an HRV's ducted supply and exhaust runs. **18. How does a bare concrete roof's runoff pH and alkalinity change over the first several years as the surface carbonates, and does a concrete sealer or coating change that trajectory in a way that matters for downstream treatment design?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Two separate bodies of literature partially answer this; a study measuring both concrete-roof-runoff pH and carbonation depth together, over time, on the same roof, was not found in this research pass, so the trajectory below is a synthesis across two adjacent literatures, not a single directly-measured source, and should be labeled as such in the manuscript.

*Concrete carbonation, general mechanism and rate.* Fresh concrete's pore solution is strongly alkaline, pH 13-14, dominated by dissolved calcium hydroxide. Atmospheric CO2 dissolves into the pore fluid and reacts with that calcium hydroxide (and with calcium silicate hydrate) to form calcium carbonate, a reaction that measurably lowers the pore-fluid pH, converging toward roughly pH 9 as carbonation proceeds. https://precast.org/blog/understanding-carbonation/ Carbonation depth advances roughly with the square root of time (depth is proportional to time), not linearly: a concrete surface carbonated 1 mm deep at one year reaches roughly 3 mm by year 9, 5 mm by year 25, and 10 mm by year 100 in typical-quality concrete, with high-quality, low-water-cement-ratio concrete carbonating at roughly 0.039 inch (1 mm) per year. https://www.understanding-cement.com/carbonation.html This means the alkaline, high-pH reserve at the exposed surface is depleted fastest in the first several years (square-root-of-time growth is steepest early) and then slows.

*Rainwater-quality literature on concrete roof runoff.* Independently, rainwater-harvesting studies comparing roofing materials report concrete-roofed runoff is measurably more alkaline than runoff from other common roofing materials, because rainwater (naturally mildly acidic, pH roughly 4.8-5.9) reacts with and leaches alkaline compounds from the concrete surface as it runs off; measured concrete-tile-roof rainwater averaged pH 7.2 in one comparative study, and separate cistern-specific literature notes that concrete storage vessels can start very alkaline (pH 10-11) from residual manufacturing dust/lime before settling into a roughly 6-8 range after several fill-and-empty cycles. https://www.rainbrothers.com/concrete-cistern-ph ; runoff/roof-material comparison summarized via https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0269749111006622

*Synthesis (not independently verified against a single source that measured both together).* The mechanism connecting the two literatures is directionally consistent: a newly-cast, uncarbonated concrete roof surface presents the most soluble, most alkaline calcium hydroxide directly to rainwater contact, which is consistent with the high initial pH values (up to pH 10-11) reported in the cistern literature; as the surface carbonates over the first several years (fastest early, per the square-root-of-time relationship), the calcium hydroxide available at the exposed surface converts to less-soluble calcium carbonate, which is consistent with runoff pH trending down toward the roughly neutral-to-slightly-alkaline 6-8 range reported once a concrete surface or vessel has weathered through multiple wet cycles. This is a reasoned synthesis across two literatures that were not found combined in one study, not a directly measured, citable trajectory; it should be presented in the manuscript as a directionally-supported expectation (runoff alkalinity is highest when the roof is new and declines over the first several years as the exposed surface carbonates), not as a numbered, sourced fact with a specific pH-versus-year curve, because no source found in this pass measured that curve directly.

*Does a sealer change the trajectory?* Not found directly in this research pass. Reasoning from the two mechanisms above: a penetrating silane/siloxane sealer (already specified for this project's exterior concrete generally, per item 17) does not form a surface film and would not be expected to meaningfully block the CO2 diffusion that drives carbonation, nor would it be expected to significantly reduce direct rainwater contact with the exposed calcium hydroxide at the surface, since the sealer's function is to repel liquid water absorption, not to coat and isolate the surface chemically from contact. A true film-forming coating (not the penetrating sealer this project specifies) could plausibly slow both carbonation and lime leaching by physically isolating the concrete surface from both CO2 and rainwater contact, but this project has not specified a film-forming roof coating and no source in this pass tested this question directly for either sealer type. Flag this sub-question as genuinely open; it would need a dedicated literature search into concrete carbonation-inhibiting coatings and rainwater-quality effects specifically, beyond what this pass located, before the manuscript can state anything more than "not verified."

**19. Can an attached greenhouse (sharing a wall with the shop or the house) meaningfully contribute usable heat back to the adjoining structure in Oregon's climate, or does the vapor and humidity load it adds make an attached configuration a net problem for the adjoining wall assembly?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Practitioner and building-science sources agree the answer is season-dependent, not a fixed yes/no, which is exactly the tension the question anticipates. In winter, when the house is closed up and interior air is naturally drier, an attached solar greenhouse can pass usable heat to the adjoining structure and the added humidity is generally described as welcome rather than a problem, since it offsets winter's typically low indoor relative humidity. In summer, when solar gain and plant transpiration both peak, heat and humidity both build quickly in an attached greenhouse, opening windows alone is reported as insufficient to manage the summer heat load, and the same moisture load that was welcome in winter becomes the risk factor for the shared wall and its structure if it is not actively vented. https://permies.com/t/73458/moisture-attaching-greenhouse-house-Solar (a practitioner forum thread, not a peer-reviewed or standards-body source; treat as real-world account, not settled engineering data)

The construction-detail risk named across these accounts is at the attachment point itself: the wall-to-greenhouse junction needs its own dedicated flashing and vapor-control detail, separate from and in addition to the shared wall's normal WRB, because the greenhouse side of that wall runs at a different (generally higher and more variable) humidity than the house's conditioned interior on the other side, which is the reverse-direction case of the vapor-drive problem the envelope chapter's section 8.10 already frames generally. One practitioner-documented solution: a rubberized flashing system that runs under the house's siding at the greenhouse-to-house junction and diverts any water down and away from the house wall rather than letting it track behind the siding at that specific joint, a detail applicable regardless of siding material given the manuscript's own rainscreen/flashing rules in section 8.9. A solar chimney (a vented, thermally-driven exhaust path from a mass element inside the greenhouse) is cited as one mitigation for summer heat buildup, though the same source notes it does little to control humidity on its own, so humidity needs its own separate control (operable venting sized for summer, or, for a fully enclosed winter-only-heat-donor design, a dedicated exhaust fan with a humidistat).

No engineering study specific to Oregon's marine/continental climate zones, or specific to a greenhouse sharing a wall with concrete or masonry construction (as opposed to wood-framed), was found in this research pass; the sourced material above is general passive-solar-greenhouse practitioner literature, not Oregon-specific or concrete-specific engineering data, and the recommendation below is accordingly a design option with named tradeoffs rather than a settled fact.

For this project: an attached greenhouse is workable as a net winter heat contributor if (and only if) it is detailed with its own dedicated flashing at the wall junction (not assumed to be covered by the house's general WRB), designed with deliberate summer venting or an active exhaust/humidistat system sized for summer heat and moisture loads rather than relying on the winter-only benefit, and the shared wall assembly on the greenhouse side is treated in the vapor-control design (section 8.10) as facing a variable, sometimes-humid exterior condition rather than ordinary outside air. Whether to build an attached (versus detached) greenhouse at all, and how much active mechanical control to add for summer, is the owner's choice, weighing the winter heat-contribution benefit against the added detailing complexity and the season-reversing moisture risk at the shared wall.

**20. Does an attached or nearby root cellar create any radon, moisture, or structural interaction with the house's own foundation and radon-mitigation design that the envelope chapter's radon section needs to account for?**

**Answered (2026-07-12):** No root-cellar-specific study was found in this research pass; the answer here is drawn from general, well-established radon principles (already cited at full depth in the envelope chapter's section 8.11) applied to the root-cellar case, plus one direct check of ORSC Appendix AF's scope.

*Radon principle, applied to a root cellar.* Any underground or partially-underground structure in contact with soil is exposed to the same soil-gas entry mechanism described in item 1 above: cracks, joints, and gaps in its own walls and floor, under the same negative-pressure stack-effect drive. Underground spaces measure meaningfully higher radon concentrations than living space above grade in general population studies; one Swiss alpine study found cellar radon levels roughly 6 to 10 times higher than the living floor above. A root cellar, being fully or partly below grade by design and typically less airtight and less ventilated than a finished basement, should be assumed to be at least as radon-exposed as any other below-grade space, and probably more so if it is built with unsealed dirt or gravel floors and rubble or dry-stacked walls, a traditional root-cellar construction style, rather than the sealed, gasketed, continuously-poured construction the radon system in section 8.11 specifies for the house itself. https://foundationhandbook.ornl.gov/handbook/section2-1-radon.shtml

*Whether this triggers a code obligation depends on how it connects to the house, and that is a jurisdiction determination, not a fixed answer.* ORSC Appendix AF's own stated scope (AF101.1) applies to "new residential construction" and references Group R-2/R-3 buildings under ORS 455.365; a direct check of the appendix's scope language found it does not explicitly address accessory structures such as a root cellar, whether attached or detached. https://up.codes/viewer/oregon/irc-2021/chapter/AF/radon-control-methods This means: a fully detached root cellar with no shared wall, doorway, or connected pressure zone to the house is very unlikely to fall inside Appendix AF's mandated scope at all (it is not a dwelling), and is a separate design decision the owner can treat independently of the house's radon system; but a root cellar that shares a wall with the house, or is entered through an interior doorway from conditioned space, effectively joins the house's pressure zone, and soil gas entering the root cellar could migrate into the house through that connection even if the house's own slab-level radon system is performing correctly, which is exactly the kind of building-specific pressure-boundary question a local building official needs to weigh in on, since Appendix AF's text alone does not resolve it.

*Recommendation for the manuscript's radon section:* state the principle (an attached or pressure-connected accessory structure below grade should be treated as part of the same radon-control pressure boundary as the house, even if it does not trigger Appendix AF's mandatory scope on its own) and the practical response for an owner planning an attached root cellar: either bring the root cellar's own floor and wall construction up to the same sealed-and-vapor-retarded standard as the house's slab (extending the existing gas-permeable-layer-plus-soil-gas-retarder assembly under the root cellar as well), or physically and pressure-isolate the root cellar from the house's conditioned space (a sealed, weatherstripped door with no forced-air connection) so any soil gas in the root cellar cannot migrate into the house's pressure zone. For a fully detached root cellar with no shared structure, no radon accommodation beyond ordinary ventilation of the root cellar itself (for occupant safety during use, not code-driven) is indicated by anything found in this research pass, though anyone entering an unventilated below-grade space regularly should be aware that radon can accumulate there regardless of its distance from the house.
