1. What does ACI PRC-201.2-23 (Guide to Durable Concrete) state, chapter by chapter, and does its guidance conflict anywhere with ACI 318 Chapter 19's exposure-class minimums for a project deliberately targeting beyond-code service life? [CODE]
   Leads: ACI 201.2R-23 organizes by mass transport then per-mechanism chapters; verify against ACI 318-19/22 directly.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** ACI PRC-201.2-23's chapter structure, confirmed from the publisher's own preview/table-of-contents pages, is: Ch. 1 Introduction and Scope; Ch. 2 Definitions; Ch. 3 Mass Transport; Ch. 4 Concrete Quality; Ch. 5 Aggregate Reactions; Ch. 6 Sulfate Attack; Ch. 7 Chemical Attack; Ch. 8 Physical Salt Attack; Ch. 9 Corrosion of Metals Embedded in Concrete; Ch. 10 Abrasion; Ch. 11 Summary; Ch. 12 References. This matches the question's lead: mass transport (the shared mechanism behind nearly every other durability threat) comes first, then a chapter per specific degradation mechanism. On conflict with ACI 318 Chapter 19: 201.2R is an ACI Committee 201 "Guide" (non-mandatory reference document, not adopted code text), while 318 Chapter 19 is the mandatory minimum. The two documents are companion pieces by design; 318's commentary cites 201.2R as background for why the Chapter 19 exposure-class minimums exist. No direct textual conflict was identified in the accessible previews and secondary engineering literature (NRMCA, ACI Foundation's CRC-117 "Recommendations for Unified Durability Guidance in ACI Documents," which exists precisely because ACI's various durability-related committee documents are not always perfectly harmonized in scope and terminology, though this document addresses documented gaps/overlaps rather than outright contradictions). For a beyond-code service-life target, 201.2R's per-mechanism chapters function as the engineering rationale for going past 318's minimums (lower w/cm, added cover, SCM use), not as an alternate, looser standard; nothing found suggests 201.2R ever authorizes less protection than 318 Chapter 19 requires. The full text of both documents is behind ACI's paywall, so the "no conflict anywhere" claim could not be verified section-by-section against the primary text.

2. How does the Eurocode 2 / BS 8500 "design working life" and "structural class" system translate cover and mix requirements as design life increases from 50 to 100 years, and can that adjustment logic be extrapolated (with engineering judgment, not code authority) toward a longer target? [CODE]
   Leads: BS 8500 Table A.4 (50-year) vs Table A.5 (100-year); structural class tied to concrete strength class and cover.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** BS 8500's structure is confirmed: Table A.4 gives the specifier options for a 50-year design working life and Table A.5 the equivalent for 100 years, each cross-referencing concrete strength class (roughly C20/25 through C40/50), cement/combination type group, and minimum cover to reinforcement (roughly 45-80mm). The two tables let a specifier hold an exposure class fixed and trade off between two levers to meet a longer design life: increase cover, increase concrete quality (lower effective w/c via strength class and cement group), or both; a lower-quality concrete needs correspondingly more cover, and vice versa. General industry guidance (The Concrete Centre, BRMCA) confirms this trade-off logic exists but the exact cover delta between the 50-year and 100-year tables for a specific exposure class and cement combination sits inside the purchased standard and was not found reproduced free in this pass. This substitution logic (cover and quality as interchangeable durability levers, scaled to a stated design-life target) is the same principle embedded in Eurocode 2's cmin,dur cover formula, and it is a legitimate engineering-judgment extrapolation path: a structural engineer could reason, using the same logic BS 8500 uses to step from 50 to 100 years, toward what additional cover or quality increment a still-longer target would plausibly need. But BS 8500 itself stops at 100 years and provides no validated, code-sanctioned basis for a target beyond that; any such extrapolation is an engineer's judgment call, not a code-backed number, and should be presented in the manuscript as exactly that.
   Source: Concretecalculate.co.uk, "BS 8500 Quick Reference," https://concretecalculate.co.uk/downloads/bs-8500-quick-reference/; morrisandperry.co.uk, "How to use BS 8500 with BS 8110," https://morrisandperry.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/how-to-use-bs8500-with-bs8110.pdf; BRMCA, "Why the BS 8500 durability tables are the way they are," https://brmca.org.uk/documents/Why_the_BS_8500_durability_tables_are_the_way_they_are-Concrete_June2016.pdf (checked 2026-07-12).

6. What is the actual engineering and regulatory reasoning behind the 500-year barrier-integrity requirement for low-level radioactive waste containment, and which of its conservative-design principles (cover, density, redundancy, monitoring) transfer to a residential structural shell versus which are containment-specific and irrelevant here? [CODE] [METHOD]
   Leads: unverified; low-level waste facility guidance reportedly requires 500-year intruder-barrier integrity with passive performance after year 100.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Confirmed and more precise than the lead. Under 10 CFR Part 61 (NRC low-level radioactive waste land disposal licensing rule), an "intruder barrier" is defined as sufficient depth of cover over waste (or an engineered structure providing equivalent protection, such as a concrete cover) that keeps an inadvertent future intruder from contacting the waste; the rule requires this barrier's effective life to be 500 years, specifically for Class C waste (the most concentrated class allowed near-surface disposal, which is disposed at greater depth or behind engineered barriers precisely because of this requirement). The dose basis behind the classification limits in 10 CFR 61.55 is 500 millirem/year (5 mSv/year) to a hypothetical inadvertent intruder, calculated so that after the 500-year barrier-life period the remaining radioactivity no longer poses an unacceptable hazard. A separate, shorter 100-year period of active institutional control (site monitoring, access restriction, records) precedes the 500-year figure; after institutional control ends, the barrier is required to perform passively, without further monitoring or maintenance. Of the conservative-design principles this implies, several transfer directly to a residential structural shell and several do not: (1) Depth/mass of cover over the vulnerable element transfers directly — it is the same principle as concrete cover over rebar, just applied to nuclear material instead of steel. (2) Low permeability/density of the barrier material transfers directly — it is the same principle behind this project's own low-w/cm, SCM-enhanced mix. (3) Redundancy (multiple independent barrier layers: waste form, container, engineered barrier, site selection, institutional control) transfers as a design philosophy — belt-and-suspenders stacking of independent protective measures (see item 47). (4) Monitoring transfers in spirit but not in mechanism — a house benefits from an inspectable, periodically monitored condition in a way a sealed waste barrier does not. (5) The requirement for pure passive performance with zero maintenance after year 100 is the sharpest point of divergence and is containment-specific, not residential-relevant: a house is expected to receive ongoing owner maintenance (roof repair, sealer reapplication, drainage upkeep) indefinitely, and design that assumes zero future maintenance is neither how houses are built nor a stated goal of this project; relying on this principle would be borrowing containment-facility logic where it does not fit a lived-in, maintained building.
   Source: eCFR, 10 CFR Part 61, https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-10/chapter-I/part-61; NRC, "Technical Analysis Supporting Definition of Period of Performance," https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML1110/ML111030586.pdf (checked 2026-07-12).
   Lands in: Designing for Centuries, framing/precedent subsection.
SETTLED.

8. What carbonation depth, after how many years, is plausible for this project's specified long-life mix (0.45 or 0.40 w/c, with SCM) under Oregon's wet marine and inland climate conditions, using a recognized prediction model? [METHOD] [TEST-TO-PROVE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The recognized model is the Tuutti/fib Model Code approach: carbonation depth x = k√t, where k is a carbonation coefficient calibrated to the specific mix and exposure, and t is time. Two relevant, sourced facts bound the plausible answer without inventing a specific number for this project's untested actual mix: first, carbonation requires a moderate relative humidity (roughly 50-70%) to proceed; concrete that stays wet most of the time (a realistic description of much of Oregon's wet marine and inland-valley climate, especially anything below grade or shielded from sun and wind) carbonates markedly more slowly than concrete in an intermediate-humidity, wet/dry-cycling environment, because water-saturated pores block CO2 diffusion. Second, SCM replacement measurably increases carbonation depth relative to plain portland cement at the same w/c: research on high-volume fly ash concrete at 0.5 w/b found accelerated carbonation depths similar to plain concrete up to about 30% fly ash replacement, but roughly doubled at higher replacement and roughly tripled at the highest replacement levels tested; separately, published cover-for-50-year-service comparisons found concrete with up to 35% fly ash needing about 40% more cover than plain portland concrete to meet the same 50-year carbonation-related service-life target, and concrete with 40-70% fly ash needing more than double the cover. This project's specified mix (§6.13: 20-30% Class F fly ash or 30-50% slag, w/c 0.40-0.45) sits inside or just above the range where SCM-driven carbonation-rate increase becomes measurable, which is exactly why the manuscript already specifies extended curing (7-14 days) for SCM mixes — cutting that curing short is where the real risk concentrates (see item 9). No source in this pass ran the Tuutti model with this project's specific cement chemistry, actual site humidity data, and actual cover to produce a citable "X mm at Y years" number for this house; that calculation needs real mix-design and site inputs run through a tool like Life-365 or a fib-model spreadsheet by the structural engineer, not an invented figure here.
   Source: "Model for practical carbonation depth prediction for high volume fly ash concrete," ScienceDirect, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950061819307639; "Evaluation of concrete cover depth for green concretes exposed to carbonation," Wiley, https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/suco.202000086 (checked 2026-07-12).
   Lands in: Designing for Centuries, carbonation subsection; cross-reference §6.13 curing table.

9. Is there a real risk that a high-fly-ash-replacement mix carbonates faster per unit depth than a plain portland mix if curing is cut short, and if so what replacement rate and curing duration combination avoids it? [MATERIALS/INGREDIENTS] [PROBLEM-SOLUTION]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Yes, this is a real, literature-confirmed risk, and it is specifically tied to curing duration, not just replacement rate. Fly ash's pozzolanic reaction (which is what eventually densifies the pore structure and gives fly-ash concrete its long-term permeability advantage) is slower than plain portland cement's hydration; if moist curing ends before that reaction has progressed, the concrete is left with a coarser, more carbonation-permeable pore structure than either a properly cured fly-ash mix or a plain portland mix at the same age would have. The confirmed numbers: at a water-to-binder ratio of 0.5, accelerated carbonation depths stayed similar to plain concrete up to roughly 30% fly ash replacement, but were roughly doubled and tripled at higher replacement levels; separately, concrete with up to 35% fly ash needed roughly 40% more cover to hit the same 50-year carbonation-related service life as plain concrete, and concrete with 40-70% fly ash needed more than double the cover — these are cover/depth findings under adequate curing, meaning inadequate curing on top of high replacement compounds the problem further. The combination that avoids the risk, consistent with both this literature and the manuscript's own existing §6.13 table, is: keep fly ash replacement inside the already-specified 20-30% (Class F) range rather than pushing toward the 40%+ range where carbonation depth roughly doubles or triples, and hold to the already-specified 7-day minimum, 14-day-preferred moist curing for SCM mixes rather than the 3-day curing acceptable for plain mixes. This is not new guidance beyond what §6.13 already states; it is the literature-based justification for why that existing table specifies extended curing specifically for SCM mixes.
   Source: same as item 8, plus general fly-ash pozzolanic-reaction-rate description corroborated across both cited papers (checked 2026-07-12).
   Lands in: Designing for Centuries, carbonation subsection; direct cross-reference and reinforcement of §6.13's existing curing table (no change to that table needed).

Which specific locations on this house (garage slab, driveway-adjacent wall, any below-grade wall near a road, any surface exposed to wind-driven salt if coastal) carry meaningful chloride exposure risk in an inland or Cascades-foothill Oregon site, versus a genuinely coastal site, and how does the exposure-class assignment change between the two? [METHOD] [CODE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** The exposure-class framework (ACI 318 Category C: C0 dry/protected, C1 exposed to moisture but no external chloride source, C2 exposed to moisture and an external chloride source) applies identically wherever the site is; what changes between an inland/Cascades-foothill site and a genuinely coastal site is which locations trigger C1 versus C2. On a coastal site, the wind-driven salt-spray research (item 42) shows meaningfully elevated corrosion risk within roughly 1-3 miles of breaking surf (most severe within about 1,500 feet, moderate to about 1 mile, lighter to about 3 miles, with some studies finding accelerated corrosion effects as far as 5-10 miles under certain conditions), so any exterior concrete face on a coastal parcel within that band is a legitimate C2 (and, if also freeze-thaw exposed with deicing salts present, F3) candidate regardless of whether the wall is a garage, driveway apron, or ordinary above-grade wall. On an inland or Cascades-foothill site outside any coastal salt-spray band, wind-driven marine chloride is not a credible exposure driver, and most of the shell can reasonably sit at C0/C1 — but two self-inflicted, site-independent chloride sources remain live at specific locations regardless of distance from the ocean: the garage floor slab (vehicle tires track in whatever deicing chemical was applied to public roads or the driveway) and any driveway-adjacent wall or slab edge that receives runoff or splash from a deiced driveway surface. Those two locations are legitimate C1, and arguably C2, candidates even on a purely inland site, specifically because of what the owner chooses to put on the driveway and garage floor (see item 12), not because of the site's geography. A below-grade wall near a public road carries the same self-inflicted logic if that road is salted in winter (most Oregon state highways use deicing chemicals in winter storms; rural county roads vary and should be confirmed with the county). The final exposure-class assignment for this project's specific, not-yet-chosen parcel needs to be made once the actual site, its distance from the coast, and its adjacent road's winter maintenance practice are known.

11. What is the actual chloride threshold concentration at the rebar (by mass of cement or of concrete) accepted by current ACI/RILEM literature, given the range of figures found in this pre-research, and which value should the manuscript cite? [CODE]
   Leads: reported range 0.15-0.20% by mass of cement; verify against a single authoritative source before citing.

**Answered (2026-07-12):** There is no single authoritative value; the literature itself does not converge on one number, and citing a single figure as if it were settled would overstate the state of the science. Two distinct things are commonly conflated under "chloride threshold" and the manuscript should keep them separate. First, the corrosion-initiation threshold researched by RILEM Technical Committee 235-CTC and reviewed in FHWA and state-DOT literature: acid-soluble chloride threshold values for conventional black rebar were found to range roughly 0.17-0.26% by weight of cement depending on w/c ratio, averaging around 0.21%, while the broader research literature (accounting for different test methods — acid-soluble versus free/water-soluble chloride — and different exposure/temperature conditions) cites a wider recommended range of roughly 0.2-1.5% by weight of cement for the critical content that initiates corrosion. This is a research-derived, condition-dependent value, not a fixed code number. Second, and distinct from the above, ACI 318-19 Table 19.3.2.1 sets a maximum allowable water-soluble chloride ion content in the concrete mix itself, as a mix-design ceiling at time of placement (not the same as the field corrosion-initiation threshold): for nonprestressed concrete, secondary sources corroborate C0 (dry/protected) at 1.00%, C1 (moist, no external chloride source) at 0.30%, and C2 (moist with external chloride source) at 0.15% by weight of cement, with a flat 0.06% limit for all prestressed concrete regardless of class. For the manuscript's Code entry, the defensible citation is the ACI 318-19 Table 19.3.2.1 mix-design limit (0.15% by weight of cement for nonprestressed concrete in the C2 class most relevant to this project's higher-risk elements), because it is the actual adopted code number this project must design to, while the RILEM/ACI 222R research range (roughly 0.2-1.5%, with 0.21% average for conventional threshold studies) should be presented separately as the underlying research basis, explicitly flagged as a range rather than a single settled figure.
   Source: RILEM TC 235-CTC, https://www.rilem.net/groupe/235-ctc-corrosion-initiating-chloride-threshold-concentrations-in-concrete-237; FHWA-HRT-12-067, https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/infrastructure/structures/bridge/12067/index.cfm; ACI 318-19 Table 19.3.2.1 values as corroborated across https://www.vrmca.com/determining-chloride-content-of-concrete/ and https://ascconline.org/Portals/ASCC/Files/Position%20Statements/PS-31_CalciumChlorideWebSC.pdf?ver=tYHotJKHnFACtTTIC_LRPA%3D%3D (checked 2026-07-12).

12. What deicing practice (rock salt versus calcium magnesium acetate versus sand-only) should this project specify for its own driveway and walks to avoid self-inflicted chloride exposure at the exact structure the whole chapter protects? [PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [MATERIALS/INGREDIENTS]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Rock salt (sodium chloride) is documented as one of the worst choices specifically for concrete: it penetrates the porous surface and causes flaking, spalling, and long-term erosion, with newer concrete (under about one year old) especially vulnerable, and it is also the direct chloride source that would push this project's own driveway-adjacent walls and garage slab into a higher corrosion exposure class (see item 10). Are there any locations on this specific design (a slab edge above a chronically damp base, a retaining wall face, a planter-adjacent wall) where the wick-and-evaporate geometry behind physical salt attack could occur despite Oregon's generally wet climate, and how is each identified during design review rather than discovered as a later failure? Documented mechanism: salt in solution from groundwater or damp soil is drawn upward through concrete by capillary action; above ground level (or above any chronically wet base), the moisture reaches an air-exposed surface and evaporates, leaving salt crystals to grow in the near-surface pores, and the resulting sub-surface crystallization pressure causes scaling and spalling concentrated in a band "just above ground level" or just above the wet zone — exactly the geometry named in the question. The literature explicitly identifies the common field locations as "the exposed part of basement walls, piers, abutments, slabs on grade, service tunnels" — i.e., any location where a chronically damp zone (soil contact, a planter, a retaining wall's wet side) sits directly below an evaporative exposed face. For this project's specific design, the locations to flag during design review, before construction rather than after a failure appears, are: any slab edge or wall base that sits above soil or fill that stays damp longer than the surrounding grade (for example, from irrigation, a downspout discharge point, or poor drainage grading) with an exposed evaporative face just above it; any retaining wall face where the retained side is wet and the exposed face is not shielded from sun/wind-driven drying; and any planter built directly against a structural wall, where the planter's irrigation water wicks into the adjacent wall and evaporates at the exposed face above the planter's soil line. Independent of vendor marketing claims, does third-party (university or standards-body) test data show a measurable difference between a hydrophobic pore-blocking admixture and a crystalline admixture on the same concrete mix, or is the industry rivalry between the two categories overstated relative to real performance? [TEST-TO-PROVE] [MATERIALS/INGREDIENTS]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Independent literature (a peer-reviewed "critical review of the testing and benefits of permeability-reducing admixtures," ScienceDirect, and FDOT-funded research) does show a real, mechanism-grounded, measurable difference, not just marketing-driven rivalry, though the review also documents genuine gaps in independent field verification for both categories. The two categories work by different, non-interchangeable mechanisms: hydrophobic pore-blockers coat pore and capillary walls with a water-repellent lining (soaps, oils, long-chain fatty acids) that repels liquid water; crystalline admixtures are hydrophilic and react with water and unhydrated cement particles to grow an insoluble crystalline structure that physically seals pores, capillaries, and micro-cracks. The documented, mechanism-driven performance differences: hydrophobic pore-blockers are unlikely to coat every pore uniformly, cannot adequately protect larger voids or cracks, and typically resist only a few centimeters of hydrostatic water-head pressure before the repellent effect is overwhelmed and water penetrates the still-open capillary network anyway — meaning their real-world performance is materially weaker under any meaningful hydrostatic pressure (a below-grade wall with groundwater present) than under simple water-absorption testing, and independent test methods were found to "vary greatly" in how much credit they give hydrophobic products, with water-absorption-style tests specifically flagged as likely to overestimate their real effectiveness. Crystalline admixtures, by contrast, are documented to have a genuine self-healing capability distinct from hydrophobic products: because the crystal-growth reaction reactivates whenever water reaches an unreacted portion of the admixture, a new crack forming after initial cure can trigger new crystal growth at that crack, resealing it — a property new cracks in hydrophobically-treated concrete do not share, since the hydrophobic coating only exists where it was originally applied/mixed and does not "grow" to meet a new crack. The peer-reviewed critical review does note a real, independently-documented gap: "insufficient field-testing data to correlate to lab performance for PRAs" generally, meaning even the crystalline category's real-world, non-manufacturer field verification is thinner than lab data alone would suggest. Net: the categories are not interchangeable marketing variants of the same thing — crystalline admixtures have documented, mechanism-based advantages for below-grade/hydrostatic-pressure applications and for self-healing of later cracks specifically, which is directly relevant to this project's below-grade walls, while hydrophobic products are better suited to lower-pressure, primarily rain/splash-exposed applications.
   Source: "A critical review of the testing and benefits of permeability-reducing admixtures for use in concrete," ScienceDirect, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S266654922100013X; FDOT research report on PRAs, https://fdotwww.blob.core.windows.net/sitefinity/docs/default-source/research/reports/fdot-bdv31-977-130-rpt.pdf (checked 2026-07-12).

47. Across every durability technology gathered in this research (SCMs, corrosion-resistant rebar, crystalline admixtures, cathodic protection, sealers, detailing), which ones are genuinely additive (worth stacking together) and which are redundant (two ways of solving the same specific threat, where specifying both wastes money without adding protection)? [METHOD] [COST]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Organizing this chapter's technologies by which specific threat mechanism each one addresses shows that most of them are genuinely additive because they target different mechanisms or different points in the same mechanism's causal chain, while a smaller number are more properly understood as alternative routes to the same protection, where stacking both is a real-money, limited-benefit choice rather than a straightforward win. Genuinely additive (worth stacking, because each addresses a distinct mechanism or distinct stage): SCMs (reduce permeability/mass transport generally, item 8-9, 24) + corrosion-resistant reinforcement (removes the consequence of chloride/carbonation reaching the steel even if mass transport is not perfectly stopped, items 25-28) + added cover (increases the time before mass transport reaches the steel at all, item 21) + good drainage/waterproofing detailing (reduces the water available to carry any aggressive agent to the concrete in the first place, items 17-18, 36) + a crystalline admixture (adds self-healing capacity for cracks that inevitably form despite good mix design, item 33) — these five operate at genuinely different points (reduce water availability, slow ingress, increase the buffer distance, protect the steel itself if ingress occurs anyway, and self-heal cracks that appear anyway), so specifying several together is a defensible belt-and-suspenders strategy, not redundant spending, which is exactly the logic behind item 45's stacked specification. A proactive embedded galvanic anode (item 29) is additive to upgraded reinforcement rather than redundant with it, because it protects the surrounding steel electrochemically even if the reinforcement itself is ordinary black steel — pairing an anode with already-upgraded stainless steel, however, moves toward genuine redundancy, since stainless's own corrosion resistance already addresses much of what the anode would otherwise be protecting against; this is the clearest documented redundancy case in this research; specifying a galvanic anode is most cost-effective paired with black or galvanized steel at a confirmed high-risk spot, not paired with stainless at that same spot. A hydrophobic pore-blocking admixture and a crystalline admixture (item 33) are also largely redundant with each other rather than additive, because both primarily address the same mechanism (reducing water ingress into the pore structure) via competing rather than complementary means, and the research found the crystalline category's self-healing property gives it a documented advantage the hydrophobic category does not share — specifying both on the same element is unlikely to add meaningful protection over specifying the better-performing crystalline option alone. Impressed-current cathodic protection (item 30) is not a residential-scale option at all (cost and monitoring-burden prohibitive), so it is neither additive nor redundant with anything else in this project's actual toolkit — it simply is not available at this scale. Silane/siloxane sealers (item 19) are additive to everything else because they address the building's exterior face specifically (reducing bulk water absorption and efflorescence at exposed surfaces) rather than duplicating any mix-design or reinforcement measure.

48. Does reduced wall wetting from a deeper overhang measurably reduce freeze-thaw cycling and inward vapor drive at the exterior concrete or masonry face, in a way that changes the durability assumptions in Designing for Centuries: Durability Engineering? [PROBLEM-SOLUTION] [TEST-TO-PROVE]

**Answered (2026-07-12):** Yes on mechanism and general direction, though a precise numeric relationship (how many inches of overhang reduces freeze-thaw cycling by how much) was not found. Documented field-measurement research on wind-driven rain confirms roof overhangs measurably reduce facade wetting: larger overhangs provide greater protection, protection is strongest at the wall area directly under and near the roofline and decreases with distance from it (with a shallower falloff gradient for larger overhangs than for smaller ones), and this reduced-wetting effect is explicitly connected in the building-science literature to reduced freeze-thaw damage risk, since masonry or concrete that is kept drier during freezing spells is documented to be less susceptible to freeze-thaw cycling damage — a wall face that stays measurably drier has measurably fewer freeze-thaw cycles occurring in a saturated state, which is the specific condition freeze-thaw damage requires (dry or lightly damp freezing does not produce the same damage as freezing while saturated). This directly supports revising this chapter's durability assumptions in a favorable, cost-free direction: a deeper roof overhang is not merely an aesthetic or general moisture-management choice already covered elsewhere in the manuscript, it is itself a legitimate, evidence-based freeze-thaw durability measure for the exterior concrete or masonry face beneath it, meaning a generously overhung design reduces (though does not eliminate — wind-driven rain and splash from grade still reach the wall to some degree regardless of overhang) the F-class severity a given wall face effectively experiences compared to an unprotected, flush-eave wall face at the same site. No source in this pass quantified this into a specific "X inches of overhang reduces cycling by Y%" figure usable as a citable Code-style number, so the manuscript should present this as a qualitative, mechanism-confirmed design recommendation (favor generous overhangs at exterior concrete/masonry faces as a genuine durability measure, not just a rain-shedding one) rather than a quantified adjustment to the exposure-class table itself.
