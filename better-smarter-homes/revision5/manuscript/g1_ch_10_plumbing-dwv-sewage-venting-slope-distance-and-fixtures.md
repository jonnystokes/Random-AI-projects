# Chapter 10: Plumbing: DWV, Sewage, Venting, Slope, Distance, And Fixtures

This chapter covers the drainage side of plumbing: the drain, waste, and vent (DWV) system, fixture drains and traps, venting, cleanouts, the building drain and its outlet to sewer or septic, basement wastewater, and fixture placement. The potable supply system, hot water, and the nonpotable rainwater supply chassis are covered in the water-supply chapter; this chapter carries the drainage and disposal side and cross-references the supply chapter at every diverter point.

The supply side is pressure work. The drainage side is gravity, slope, air, and maintenance access. A drainage system drains by gravity wherever the outlet elevation allows and uses a pump only where gravity cannot reach the outlet.

The plumbing code of record is the 2023 Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code (OPSC), which places sanitary drainage in Chapter 7, vents in Chapter 9, and traps and interceptors in Chapter 10. Onsite wastewater (septic) is regulated by Oregon DEQ under OAR 340-071 and 340-073; a major DEQ amendment (DEQ 29-2025) takes effect January 1, 2026. Section values below are given for those editions; confirm the edition in force at permit time.

## 10.1 What The DWV System Does

The DWV system carries wastewater away by gravity, keeps sewer gas out of the house with water-filled traps, and gives the drains an air path so the traps are not siphoned dry.

**Simple**

DWV stands for drain, waste, and vent. Drains and waste pipes carry used water and solids away. Vents let air into the pipes so the water flows smoothly and so the U-shaped traps under each fixture keep their water seal. Without correct venting, a drain can gurgle, drain slowly, smell, or pull the trap seal out and let sewer gas into the room.

**Applied**

Draw the full DWV system before cutting or sleeving anything in concrete. The plan shows every fixture, trap, trap arm, vent, vertical stack, horizontal drain, and cleanout, plus the building drain, the sewer or septic connection, any backwater protection, and the basement fixture strategy. Settle the stack location before the foundation, slab, and wall details are fixed, because moving a bathroom on the plan is inexpensive and coring cured concrete for a missed stack is not.

**Professional**

DWV performance depends on the drainage fixture unit (DFU) load, pipe diameter, slope, developed length, fitting geometry, vent placement, trap-seal protection, and cleanout access. The system is designed and inspected as its own system, separate from the supply piping, even where the two share a wet wall. Self-cleansing velocity is maintained by matching slope and diameter to the load so the flow carries solids without stranding them.

**Code**

```
2023 OPSC Chapter 7: sanitary drainage; drain sizing (§703), slope (§708), cleanouts (§707), building drain and sewer.
2023 OPSC Chapter 9: vents; sizing (§906), termination (§906.1, §906.2).
2023 OPSC Chapter 10: traps and interceptors; trap-arm limits (Table 1002.2).
Oregon DEQ OAR 340-071 and 340-073: onsite wastewater (septic) systems; DEQ 29-2025 amendments effective January 1, 2026.
```

## 10.2 Wet-Zone And Stack Planning

The shortest, straightest, best-vented drain is the one whose fixtures are grouped and whose stack rises cleanly. Grouping wet rooms reduces branch length, vent complexity, and roof and slab penetrations.

**Simple**

Stack bathrooms over each other where the plan allows, and keep the kitchen and laundry near the main plumbing wall. Short, straight drains are easier to slope, vent, clean, and inspect than drains that wander across the house.

**Applied**

Use a wet-zone layout where the floor plan allows:

- Stack bathrooms vertically over a common wet wall.
- Keep laundry and, where practical, the kitchen near the main stack.
- Locate the main stack where it can rise straight through to the roof.
- Keep horizontal branches short and cleanouts reachable.
- Avoid drains buried in concrete that cannot be reached for service.

If a fixture moves away from the wet zone, trace its drain, vent, trap-arm length, and cleanout access before approving the layout.

**Professional**

Wet-zone concentration reduces branch length, vent count, roof and slab penetrations, and cleanout-access conflicts, and it simplifies coordination with structure, insulation, air sealing, and sound control. In a concrete structure the stack and branch penetrations are fixed in the penetration schedule before placement, so the fixture layout is reviewed at full scale before any sleeve or blockout is set.

**Code**

```
2023 OPSC Chapter 7: the plumbing drawings identify fixture locations, drainage and vent routes, pipe sizes, cleanouts, and the connection to the approved sewer or onsite system. Rough plumbing is inspected before it is covered.
```

## 10.3 Fixture Drain Sizing

Each fixture drains a known load measured in drainage fixture units, and each pipe size carries a capped number of DFUs. Sizing reads the fixture load against the code tables rather than copying common sizes.

**Applied**

Size drains as a code-table task, not from habit:

1. Assign the DFU value to each fixture from OPSC Table 702.1.
2. Total the DFUs on each branch, stack, and the building drain.
3. Read the minimum pipe size for that load and slope from Table 703.2.
4. Confirm the water-closet drain is 3 inches minimum.
5. Include large tubs, multi-head showers, utility sinks, and floor drains before finalizing the layout.

**Professional**

Typical single-family DFU values are 1 for a lavatory, 2 for a kitchen sink or shower, 2 to 3 for a tub, 3 for a clothes washer, and 4 for a 1.6 gpf water closet (3 for a high-efficiency 1.28 gpf closet); a bathroom group runs 5 to 8 DFUs. Under OPSC §703, a 3-inch building drain at 1/4-inch-per-foot slope carries up to 35 DFUs and a 4-inch drain carries 216; a 3-inch stack carries 30 DFUs over three stories and a 4-inch stack carries 240. A 4-inch building drain is the practical default for this project because the cost difference over 3-inch is small and it leaves capacity for later fixtures. Oversizing a branch is not automatically better, because low flow in an oversized pipe reduces the scouring that carries solids.

**Code**

```
OPSC Table 702.1: drainage fixture unit (DFU) values by fixture type.
OPSC Table 703.2: maximum DFU load permitted on each drain and stack size at a given slope.
OPSC §703: building drain and building sewer sizing; water-closet drain 3 inches minimum.
```

## 10.4 Slope And Flow

A horizontal drain needs enough slope to keep the flow moving without running so fast that the water outruns the solids. The slope is set from the pipe size and the code minimum, verified before the run is covered.

**Simple**

Drains run downhill. Too flat and the water stalls and solids settle. The standard slope for a house drain is a quarter inch of fall for every foot of run. Check the slope before the floor or the concrete covers the pipe, because a flat spot is a permanent clog point.

**Applied**

Before installing and covering a horizontal drain:

- Mark the pipe elevations and confirm the direction of flow.
- Set the slope to 1/4 inch per foot for pipe smaller than 4 inches.
- Use drainage-pattern fittings, not vent or pressure fittings.
- Support the pipe at the specified spacing so it cannot sag into a belly.
- Protect the pipe during backfill and concrete work.
- Verify the slope with a level before cover.

**Professional**

OPSC §708.1 sets a uniform slope of at least 1/4 inch per foot (2 percent) for horizontal drainage piping. Pipe 4 inches and larger may slope as low as 1/8 inch per foot (1 percent) only with prior Building Official approval; pipe smaller than 4 inches never goes below 1/4 inch per foot. The 1 percent provision is discretionary and is not designed against as a default. Long flat runs, excessive direction changes, sagging supports, and poor bedding are the chronic sources of stranded solids.

**Code**

```
OPSC §708.1: horizontal drainage piping at a uniform slope of at least 1/4 inch per foot.
  - Pipe 4 inches and larger may slope to 1/8 inch per foot only with prior Building Official approval.
  - Pipe smaller than 4 inches never slopes below 1/4 inch per foot.
OPSC §710: sewer and building-drain material, cover, and protection where the drain leaves the building.
```

## 10.5 Traps And Trap-Seal Protection

The trap under each fixture holds a water seal that blocks sewer gas from the room. A trap fails when siphonage, back-pressure, or evaporation pulls or drains the seal. Vent placement and trap-arm length protect the seal, and the trap-arm dimensions are the most commonly violated numbers in the rough-in.

**Applied**

For each trap, confirm:

- Correct trap size for the fixture and no double-trapping.
- The vent connects within the trap-arm distance for the trap size.
- The water-closet developed length from the closet flange to the vent does not exceed 6 feet.
- No trap is concealed where it cannot be serviced.
- Floor drains and rarely used fixtures have a trap primer or another method to keep the seal from evaporating.

**Professional**

OPSC Table 1002.2 caps the trap-arm distance from the trap to its vent by trap size: a 1-1/4-inch arm to a maximum of 2 feet 6 inches, a 1-1/2-inch arm to 3 feet 6 inches, a 2-inch arm to 5 feet, a 3-inch arm to 6 feet, and a 4-inch arm to 10 feet, each with a minimum length of twice the pipe diameter. The footnote water-closet rule limits the developed length from the top of the closet flange to the inner edge of the vent to 6 feet regardless of a 3-inch or 4-inch drain; above-floor water-closet vent geometry is the most common rough-in failure. A trap arm may change direction up to 90 degrees without a cleanout, and a trap arm of 3 inches and larger may change up to 135 degrees.

**Code**

```
OPSC Table 1002.2: trap-arm (fixture drain) length limits by size.
  - 1-1/4 inch: max 2 ft 6 in; 1-1/2 inch: max 3 ft 6 in; 2 inch: max 5 ft; 3 inch: max 6 ft; 4 inch: max 10 ft.
  - Minimum trap-arm length twice the pipe diameter.
  - Footnote: water closet developed length, closet flange to vent, not more than 6 feet.
OPSC Chapter 10: prohibited trap arrangements (no double-trapping, no S-traps), interceptor and floor-drain requirements, trap primers for floor drains.
```

## 10.6 Venting Strategy

Vents give the drain system an air path so flowing water does not siphon the traps. Individual vents are simple and robust; common, wet, and circuit vents reduce pipe when they are designed correctly; air-admittance valves solve difficult layouts but are mechanical devices with access and replacement needs.

**Simple**

When water runs down a drain it pulls air behind it. Without a vent, that pull acts like a straw on the fixture trap and can empty its seal. A vent lets air into the system so the drains flow and the traps stay full.

**Applied**

- Give each fixture a code-compliant vent path and keep vents accessible during rough-in.
- Size each vent to at least half the diameter of the drain it serves, and never smaller than 1-1/4 inch.
- Increase a vent one nominal size where its developed length exceeds 40 feet.
- Group vents into a clean stack where the code arrangement allows.
- Where an air-admittance valve is used for a difficult layout, confirm it is accepted for that location and keep it accessible for replacement.

**Professional**

OPSC Chapter 9 sizes vents from Table 906.1 against the DFU load and developed length. A vent is at least one-half the diameter of the drain it serves and never smaller than 1-1/4 inch, and a vent over 40 feet of developed length increases one nominal size. Wet vents, common vents, and circuit vents are code-approved arrangements that reduce pipe in a grouped bathroom; each has geometry rules that are drawn and checked before rough-in. Air-admittance valves are accepted for specific conditions at the Building Official's discretion and are installed accessibly, not buried in a wall.

**Code**

```
OPSC §906 and Table 906.1: vent sizing by DFU load and developed length.
  - Vent never smaller than 1-1/4 inch and at least one-half the diameter of the drain served.
  - Vent over 40 feet developed length increased one nominal size.
OPSC Chapter 9: wet-vent, common-vent, and circuit-vent arrangements; air-admittance valve acceptance is a Building Official discretion call.
```

## 10.7 Vent Terminations

A vent carries sewer gas out of the building and must terminate away from windows, doors, and air intakes, high enough to stay clear of snow and roof traffic. Roof termination is the prescriptive default.

**Applied**

Before setting the termination:

- Terminate each vent through the roof, at least 6 inches above the roof surface (confirm whether the local jurisdiction amends this to 12 inches).
- Keep the terminal at least 10 feet from, or 3 feet above, any openable window, door, opening, or air intake.
- Keep the terminal at least 3 feet from any lot line.
- Enlarge the terminal to at least 2 inches in diameter where snow and ice can close a smaller opening, making the diameter change inside the heated space.
- Coordinate the penetration with the roof membrane, flashing, and air barrier.

**Professional**

OPSC §906.1 requires each vent to extend through its flashing and terminate vertically at least 6 inches above the roof, and §906.2 sets the clearance to openings at 10 feet horizontally or 3 feet above. In a snow region the terminal diameter is not less than 2 inches, and any change in pipe diameter is made inside the building at least 12 inches below the roof in a heated space so the enlargement does not ice closed. Every roof penetration is a flashing and maintenance point, so the vent count is minimized by grouping without making the venting fragile. Sidewall venting is permissible only under the limited §906.1.1 conditions (downward termination, not under a vented overhang, 3/32-inch insect screen, the §906.2 clearances) and at the Building Official's discretion; this project terminates drainage vents through the roof and reserves sidewall venting for retrofits where roof access is impossible.

**Code**

```
OPSC §906.1: vent extends through its flashing and terminates at least 6 inches above the roof (local jurisdictions may amend to 12 inches).
OPSC §906.2: terminal at least 10 feet from or 3 feet above any openable window, door, opening, or air intake; at least 3 feet from any lot line.
OPSC §906.7: snow/frost closure; terminal not less than 2 inches diameter, diameter change made inside the heated space at least 12 inches below the roof.
OPSC §906.1.1: sidewall venting conditions where accepted (downward, not under a vented overhang, 3/32-inch screen, §906.2 clearances).
```

## 10.8 Cleanouts And Service Access

Cleanouts are the rodding and camera access points that make a clog a service call instead of a demolition. They are located at the stack bases, the terminals, the major direction changes, and the building-drain-to-sewer junction, and they are kept accessible.

**Simple**

A cleanout is a capped access point for clearing a clog. A drain without a reachable cleanout turns a simple blockage into a wall-opening repair. A cleanout hidden behind finished work is not usable, so each one is located where a service auger or camera can reach it.

**Applied**

Provide accessible cleanouts at:

- The base of each waste and soil stack.
- The upper terminal of each horizontal drain run.
- Each aggregate horizontal change of direction greater than 135 degrees.
- Every 100 feet of developed drain length.
- The junction of the building drain and the building sewer, as a two-way cleanout.

Keep the clearance in front of each cleanout (18 inches by 18 inches for pipe 2 inches and smaller, 24 inches by 24 inches for larger pipe), and map every cleanout in the project record.

**Professional**

OPSC §707 governs cleanout location, clearance, direction, and sizing. A two-way cleanout at the building-drain-to-building-sewer junction (inside the wall near the connection or outside at grade) is the default here because it allows rodding in either direction. Cleanouts open in or against the direction of flow, extend from a wye or equivalent sweep rather than a sanitary tee, and match the pipe size up to 4 inches (cleanouts on larger pipe may be 4 inches). An under-floor cleanout is extended to or above the finished floor, or outside the building, wherever the clearance falls below 18 inches vertical or 30 inches horizontal. Cleanouts are not concealed; a code-minimum cleanout facing a wall or buried behind equipment fails its purpose.

**Code**

```
OPSC §707.4: cleanout locations (stack base, upper terminal, 100-ft spacing, aggregate direction change over 135 degrees, building-drain/sewer junction). Exception: horizontal drains under 5 feet unless serving a sink or urinal.
OPSC §707.5 and §707.6: cleanout direction; extension from a wye or equivalent sweep, not a sanitary tee.
OPSC §707.8 and §707.9: cleanouts accessible and not concealed; clearance 18 by 18 inches for pipe 2 inches and smaller, 24 by 24 inches for larger pipe; under-floor cleanouts extended to floor level or outside when clearance is below 18 inches vertical or 30 inches horizontal.
OPSC §707.10: cleanout the nominal pipe size up to 4 inches; 4 inches for larger pipe.
```

## 10.9 Sewer Or Septic Connection

Wastewater leaves the house through a public sewer or an onsite septic system, and the site geometry decides which. The outlet elevation, the slope to reach it, and the septic approval sequence all have to be settled before the basement plumbing is designed.

**Applied**

Confirm the outlet path early:

- Determine whether public sewer is available and where the lateral connects.
- If septic is required, obtain the site evaluation before the layout is fixed.
- Locate every fixture that sits below the outlet elevation.
- Confirm the building drain reaches the connection with slope, cover, and cleanouts.
- Identify whether a pump or backwater valve is needed.
- Keep the building sewer at least 5 feet from any building or structure, with at least 1 foot of cover and freeze protection.

**Professional**

For septic, the DEQ sequence under OAR 340-071 is a site evaluation report (soil profile, slope, depth to a limiting layer, water table, setbacks), a construction permit, a mandatory pre-cover inspection, a start-up inspection (added by DEQ 29-2025), and a construction-site compliance certificate; an ongoing operation-and-maintenance contract is a permit condition for alternative treatment, sand-filter, and recirculating systems. Standard subsurface systems can often be permitted without a stamped engineer, while alternative systems require a registered design professional or sanitarian. Setbacks under OAR 340-071-0220 run to 100 feet from a well, 50 feet from streams and lakes, and 10 feet from property lines, water lines, and foundations, with site-specific variation. Under the 2026 DEQ rules a sewer is treated as available inside the urban growth boundary within 200 feet for a dwelling needing repair or alteration; outside the growth boundary, septic is presumptive. In nitrate-sensitive areas the agent may require an alternative treatment technology system with higher nitrogen removal, and an accessory dwelling unit is sized at 300 gallons per day for a one- to two-bedroom unit, added to the primary dwelling's design flow.

**Code**

```
Oregon DEQ OAR 340-071 (onsite wastewater) and 340-073 (construction standards); DEQ 29-2025 effective January 1, 2026.
  - Sewer availability: 200 feet inside urban-growth zoning for a dwelling needing repair or alteration; septic presumptive outside the growth boundary.
  - Approval sequence: site evaluation report, construction permit, pre-cover inspection, start-up inspection, construction-site compliance certificate.
  - ADU design flow 300 gpd for a 1- to 2-bedroom unit; alternative treatment technology may be required in nitrate-sensitive areas.
OAR 340-071-0220: setback table (100 ft to a well, 50 ft to surface water, 10 ft to property lines, water lines, and foundations).
OPSC §312.3 and §312.6: building sewer at least 5 feet from a structure, minimum 1 foot cover, freeze protection.
```

## 10.10 Basement Fixtures And Backwater Protection

A basement fixture that sits below the sewer or septic outlet cannot drain by gravity and is exposed to backup from the main. Protection is layered: a pump for the lift, a backwater valve against reverse flow, and accessible service for both.

**Simple**

Basement plumbing is the riskiest plumbing because the fixtures may sit below the point where the sewer leaves the property. Water does not run uphill on its own, and if the main backs up, the basement is where it shows first. A pump lifts the wastewater to the gravity drain, and a backwater valve keeps a main backup from flowing back into the basement.

**Applied**

Before placing any basement fixture, confirm:

- Whether it can drain by gravity or needs a sewage ejector.
- Whether it sits below the upstream sewer control point and needs a backwater valve.
- That the pump, basin, check valve, shutoff valve, and alarm are accessible for service.
- What happens on a power failure.
- That any below-grade fixture is in the approved plan.

**Professional**

A fixture below the next upstream sewer control point requires backwater protection or pumping under OPSC §710. A backwater valve complies with ASME A112.14.1 or IAPMO IGC 305, stays open during low flow to avoid stranding solids, and is installed accessibly, typically in a pit with a bolted, gasketed cover. The layered protection for a basement bathroom is the ejector for the lift, a backwater valve at the building-drain elevation, and, for combined-sewer jurisdictions, additional backflow protection near the property line. Avoiding wastewater fixtures below the slab is the simplest durability choice; where basement plumbing is included, it is built as a serviceable system with mapped access, not a buried assembly.

**Code**

```
OPSC §710: fixtures below the upstream sewer control point require pumping or backwater protection.
OPSC §710.6: backwater valve complies with ASME A112.14.1 or IAPMO IGC 305; accessible, with a removable cover; remains open at low flow.
```

## 10.11 Basement Toilet Options

A basement toilet needs an approved waste path to the gravity drain. Oregon recognizes several: a sewage ejector, a macerating toilet, a composting toilet, and an incinerating toilet. Each has a listing standard and a service profile. An owner-explored Venturi lifting concept is discussed at the end with its compliance path.

**Applied**

Compare the recognized options against the site outlet and the service commitment:

| Option | Where it fits | Service and approval |
|---|---|---|
| No basement toilet | Simplest, lowest failure risk | None |
| Gravity flush | Outlet elevation allows gravity drainage | Standard rough-in, no pump |
| Sewage ejector | Below outlet elevation | Basin, pump, check and shutoff valve, vent, alarm, power (OPSC §710) |
| Macerating toilet | Retrofit where a gravity drain location forces it | ASME A112.3.4 listed unit; one bathroom group; more service over time |
| Composting toilet | Site where conventional septic is prohibitive | NSF 41 listed; still requires a DEQ-approved graywater system |
| Incinerating toilet | Off-grid disposal | NSF 41 listed; roof exhaust; 3 to 5 kW electrical load |

**Professional**

The standard path is a sewage ejector under OPSC §710.3: a single-family residential ejector receiving water-closet discharge passes a 1-1/2-inch solid ball, discharges at least 20 GPM into a 2-inch or larger pressure-rated line, and carries a backwater valve and a gate or ball valve. A 1/2 HP ejector at about 25 GPM into a 30-to-40-gallon basin suits a basement bathroom group. The macerating toilet under OPSC §710.13 is a listed product (ASME A112.3.4 or CSA B45.9), permitted in one- and two-family dwellings for a single bathroom group where gravity flow is impossible. A composting toilet (OAR 918-770-0080, NSF 41) requires a plumbing permit and, critically, an approved DEQ graywater disposal system or a sewer connection, because it substitutes the blackwater stream rather than eliminating the wastewater system. An incinerating toilet (NSF 41) is approved through the Building Codes Division as a fixture, with independent roof exhaust and a significant electrical load.

An owner-explored Venturi-based lifting device is not a listed product class, so its compliance path is an OPSC §301.5 alternative engineered design: a registered design professional's stamped documents demonstrating performance equivalent to the listed methods, submitted for Building Official approval. That is the route by which a non-listed device is brought into compliance; the conventional ejector remains the default so the basement fixture is buildable while any alternative is being engineered and approved.

**Code**

```
OPSC §710.3: single-family sewage ejector passes a 1-1/2-inch solid ball, discharges at least 20 GPM into a 2-inch-minimum pressure-rated line, with a backwater valve and a gate or ball valve.
OPSC §710.13: macerating toilet system listed to ASME A112.3.4 / CSA B45.9; one- and two-family only; one bathroom group; check valve and full-way shutoff in the discharge.
OAR 918-770-0080: composting toilet listed to NSF/ANSI 41; plumbing permit; requires a DEQ-approved graywater disposal system or sewer connection.
NSF/ANSI 41: composting and incinerating (non-liquid-saturated) waste-treatment systems.
OPSC §301.5: alternative engineered design; the compliance path for any non-listed lifting or ejection device, requiring RDP-stamped documents and Building Official approval.
```

## 10.12 Sewage Ejector Detail

Where a sewage ejector is used, the basin, the discharge sequence, the vent, the materials, and the alarm are built to OPSC §710 so the pumped connection is as reliable and serviceable as a gravity drain.

**Applied**

Build the ejector to this arrangement:

- A watertight, gastight basin sized so the pump cycles on roughly a 45-second run (about 30 to 40 gallons for a 25 GPM pump).
- The discharge sequence from the pump: union, then check or backwater valve, then a full-way ball or gate valve, then the vertical riser.
- The riser connects to the top of the horizontal gravity drain through a wye fitting.
- A vent at least 1-1/2 inches, connected to the house vent system or through the roof.
- A high-water alarm with battery backup, audible and visible at an occupied location, not only in the basement.
- Schedule 40 PVC or other pressure-rated discharge material; not PEX.

**Professional**

OPSC §710.4 requires the discharge line to be approved pressure-rated material with an accessible backwater or swing-check valve and a full-way gate or ball valve, connecting to the top of a horizontal gravity drain through a wye. The basin under §710.5 is watertight and gastight with a vent sized per Table 703.2 for the connected fixtures. The high-water alarm is required by nearly every listed manufacturer and is placed where it can be heard. PEX is not used for ejector discharge because its listing does not cover pressurized sewage service.

**Code**

```
OPSC §710.4: pressure-rated discharge line; accessible backwater/check valve and full-way valve; connection to the top of the gravity drain through a wye.
OPSC §710.5: watertight, gastight basin; vent at least 1-1/2 inch, sized per Table 703.2.
OPSC Table 703.2: sump and connected-fixture vent sizing.
Discharge material: Schedule 40 PVC, cast iron, ductile iron, or copper; PEX not approved for sewage ejector discharge.
```

## 10.13 Graywater And Alternate Wastewater Disposal

Graywater is used water from showers, tubs, bathroom and kitchen sinks, and laundry, but not toilet waste. It can be routed to the sanitary system with no graywater permit, or diverted to a permitted reuse system under DEQ rules. This chapter carries the drainage and disposal side of any alternate-source system; the supply side is in the water-supply chapter.

**Simple**

Graywater is the used water from sinks, showers, tubs, and the clothes washer. It is not toilet water, but it still carries soap, food, grease, and germs, so it has an approved disposal path. Each category of water (drinking water, rainwater, graywater, toilet blackwater, stormwater, and foundation drainage) stays on its own separate, labeled path.

**Applied**

For a graywater fixture, choose the path:

- Sanitary path: connect the fixture through the normal DWV to the sewer or septic. No graywater permit is required because nothing is diverted. This is the default for a single basement handwash or sink.
- Reuse path: a DEQ graywater permit plus an OPSC Chapter 15 plumbing permit, an accessible corrosion-resistant diverter valve that can return flow to the sewer or septic, an overflow at least the inlet diameter running by gravity to the approved disposal with no shutoff on the overflow, the OAR 340-053-0090 setbacks, and annual reporting.
- Keep the laundry-to-landscape exemption in mind: it applies only to clothes-washer effluent with no pump and no storage, not to sink graywater.

**Professional**

DEQ classifies graywater under OAR 340-053 into Type 1 (untreated or filtered, for subsurface irrigation), Type 2 (treated, for surface or restricted irrigation), and Type 3 (highest treatment, for indoor toilet flushing). Any reuse system except the laundry-to-landscape exemption needs a DEQ permit (general permit 2401 or an individual permit) plus a plumbing permit under OPSC Chapter 15. A system under 1,200 gallons per day connected to an approved sewer or onsite system is exempt from a land-use compatibility statement, which is a paperwork exemption, not a permit exemption. Composting-toilet leachate piping and nonpotable rainwater tank overflow are drainage-side elements that connect to an approved disposal through an air break. Foundation drainage and condensate are clear water and are not discharged to the sanitary drain without Building Official approval under §714.2; they go to daylight, a drywell, or a storm system.

**Code**

```
OAR 340-053: graywater reuse and disposal; Type 1, 2, and 3 classification; permit pathway (general permit 2401 or individual) plus OPSC Chapter 15 plumbing permit.
OAR 340-053-0100: diverter valve accessible, durable, corrosion-resistant; overflow at least the inlet diameter to gravity disposal with no shutoff valve.
OAR 340-053-0090: graywater treatment thresholds and setback table.
OPSC §714.2: rain, surface, and subsurface (foundation drainage) water not discharged to the sanitary drain without Building Official approval.
OAR 918-770-0080: composting-toilet leachate to an approved graywater disposal system.
```

## 10.14 Fixture Placement And Clearances

A fixture needs clear space around it, a trap, a vent, a supply stop, and a drain, coordinated with the framing or masonry and the finishes. A code-sized fixture in a cramped location can still fail inspection or be unusable.

**Applied**

For each bathroom, kitchen, laundry, and utility area, confirm at full scale before rough-in:

- Fixture centerlines and clear floor space.
- Door swing and approach clearance.
- The trap location and the vent route within the trap-arm limit.
- Supply-stop and drain access, and cleanout access.
- Waterproofing and floor slope at showers and floor drains.
- An access panel wherever a serviceable part is concealed.

**Professional**

Fixture placement is a coordination task across plumbing, framing or masonry, waterproofing, accessibility, finishes, cabinets, doors, electrical, and ventilation. Water-closet clearances, fixture fittings, water-conservation flow limits, trap and vent requirements, and cleanout access are all read against the code and the approved plans, and the fixture plan is fixed before slab sleeves, wall cores, and cabinet rough-ins are set. Where the owner wants an accessible or visitable layout, the clear-floor and approach dimensions are set at this stage rather than retrofitted.

**Code**

```
OPSC Chapter 4: fixture clearances, fixture fittings, and water-conservation flow rates (water closets 1.28 gpf high-efficiency or 1.6 gpf; lavatory and shower flow limits).
OPSC Chapter 10 and Chapter 9: trap, vent, and cleanout requirements at each fixture.
Accessibility (where the owner adopts it): ICC A117.1 clear-floor and approach dimensions coordinated at rough-in.
```

## 10.15 Inspection, Testing, Commissioning, And Records

The drainage system is tested before it is covered and commissioned under real use before occupancy. A leak or a missed vent found before cover is a fitting; found after finishes it is a demolition. The records let a future owner find every cleanout, pump, and valve without opening a wall.

**Applied**

Before cover, record and photograph:

- The rough plumbing inspection approval.
- The drain and vent layout, pipe sizes, and slopes.
- Cleanout locations and a cleanout map.
- Vent routes and terminations.
- Any basement pump, basin, alarm, and backwater-valve locations.
- The sewer or septic approval documents.
- Photographs of buried pipe before cover.

At commissioning, confirm under real use:

- Fixtures drain quickly and traps hold their seal with no gurgle.
- Cleanouts are accessible and correctly oriented.
- Any ejector runs, cycles, and alarms correctly.
- Backwater valves are reachable.
- No fixture creates a pressure change that pulls another fixture's trap.

**Professional**

Final acceptance shows the DWV system drains by gravity where intended, holds trap seals, vents without gurgle, and is serviceable by someone who did not build it. The septic system carries its own DEQ inspection sequence, including the new start-up inspection before the construction-site compliance certificate. The as-built drawings, cleanout map, pump and alarm data, and the sewer or septic approval stay in the permanent house file.

**Code**

```
OPSC §712: DWV water or air test before the system is covered; do not conceal drainage or venting before the required inspection approval.
OPSC §710 and §707: final inspection of ejectors, backwater valves, and cleanout access.
Oregon DEQ OAR 340-071: septic pre-cover inspection, start-up inspection, and construction-site compliance certificate; operation-and-maintenance contract where required.
Records to retain: rough and final inspection approvals, DWV test result, cleanout map, ejector and alarm data, and the sewer or septic approval documents.
```
