# Chapter 14: Interiors, Finishes, Accessibility, And Livability

This chapter covers the interior: room geometry and egress, stairs and guards, the garage-to-dwelling separation, wall and ceiling coverings, floor finishes and their fit with radiant heat, wet-area waterproofing and tile, bathroom clearances and anti-scald protection, kitchens and cabinets, doors and safety glazing, storage, accessibility, interior materials chemistry, and move-in readiness.

The concrete structure is the long-service part of the house. The finishes serve the life inside it and are designed to be durable, repairable, and replaceable without disturbing the permanent shell. Several of these interior topics carry code requirements, so the chapter separates what the residential code requires from what is a design choice.

The residential code of record is the 2023 Oregon Residential Specialty Code (ORSC), based on the 2021 IRC, mandatory since April 1, 2024. A 2026 ORSC (2024 IRC base) is anticipated near October 1, 2026 and will revise some stair, egress, and wet-area provisions; values below are for the 2023 ORSC, and the ones most likely to change are noted. Plumbing fixture rules come from the 2023 Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code (OPSC) and electrical device rules from the branch-circuit chapter.

## 14.1 Finish Philosophy And Replacement Tiers

The finishes are chosen so the permanent assembly stays protected and every serviceable point stays reachable. Materials are grouped by how long they are meant to last, and the money follows that grouping.

**Simple**

The structure lasts for generations; the finishes serve daily life and change over time. That split is useful: the house can stay strong for many decades while the inside changes with the people in it. The rule that keeps it working is that a finish never hides a problem or buries a valve, junction box, cleanout, or shutoff that will need service.

**Applied**

Group interior materials by expected life and design each accordingly:

| Tier | Role | Examples | Design rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Permanent | Do not cover problems | Concrete, reinforcement, foundation, masonry | Keep inspection and repair logic clear |
| Semi-permanent | Worth investing in | Doors, cabinets, tile, stone, hardwood, built-ins | Install well, document products, keep replacement possible |
| Consumable | Planned future refresh | Paint, appliances, faucets, some lighting | Do not overbuild items that will be replaced |

Keep samples, model numbers, finish names, paint formulas, and photos in the project record. Do not bury inspection points, valves, junction boxes, cleanouts, dampers, manifolds, or shutoffs behind permanent finish.

**Professional**

Finish durability includes moisture tolerance, heat transfer, abrasion, cleaning chemistry, attachment method, and substrate compatibility, not just material strength. The wrong finish damages a good assembly: vapor-closed flooring over a wet slab, tile without movement joints, an unvented wet room, or an unrated shower backing turns interior design into a building failure. Interior finishes respect the air barrier, vapor behavior, thermal mass, radiant heating, acoustics, service access, and fire separation established by the structural and envelope work.

**Code**

```
Interior work touches ORSC provisions for room size (R304), ceiling height (R305), light and ventilation (R303), egress (R310), stairs and guards (R311, R312), alarms (R314, R315), garage separation (R302.5/R302.6), interior coverings (R702), and foam thermal barriers (R316), plus OPSC fixture rules and OESC device rules.
Follow the adopted code, approved plans, and product listings; a decorative decision does not override a required assembly.
```

## 14.2 Room Geometry: Area, Ceiling Height, Light, And Ventilation

Habitable rooms meet minimum floor area, dimension, and ceiling height, and each has natural light and ventilation or the mechanical equivalent.

**Applied**

- Size each habitable room (other than a kitchen) to at least 70 square feet with no horizontal dimension less than 7 feet.
- Provide at least 7 feet of ceiling height in habitable space and hallways, and at least 6 feet 8 inches in bathrooms, toilet rooms, and laundry rooms.
- Where a ceiling slopes, count only the floor area under at least 5 feet of height, and provide the required height over at least half the required floor area.
- Provide glazing for natural light equal to at least 8 percent of the floor area, with at least 4 percent openable for ventilation, or provide the mechanical ventilation covered in the HVAC chapter.

**Professional**

ORSC R304 sets the 70-square-foot minimum floor area and 7-foot minimum dimension for habitable rooms other than kitchens, and R305 sets the ceiling heights (7 feet habitable and hallway, 6 feet 8 inches bath and laundry), with sloped-ceiling allowances that let most loft configurations comply. ORSC R303 requires natural light glazing at 8 percent of floor area and openable area at 4 percent for ventilation, and mechanical ventilation under M1505 substitutes for the natural ventilation. A bathroom without bathing facilities meets either an aggregate 3 square feet of glazing with half openable or mechanical exhaust.

**Code**

```
ORSC R304: habitable rooms (other than kitchens) at least 70 sq ft, no horizontal dimension under 7 feet.
ORSC R305: ceiling height at least 7 feet (habitable, hallway), 6 feet 8 inches (bath, toilet, laundry); sloped-ceiling allowances.
ORSC R303: natural light glazing at least 8 percent of floor area, 4 percent openable for ventilation; mechanical ventilation per M1505 may substitute.
```

## 14.3 Emergency Escape And Rescue Openings

Every sleeping room and every basement with a sleeping room has an emergency escape and rescue opening sized for a person to get out and a firefighter to get in, operable from inside without a key or tool.

**Applied**

- Provide an emergency escape and rescue opening in every sleeping room, in a basement with a sleeping room, and in a habitable attic.
- Meet the net clear opening: at least 5.7 square feet (5.0 at grade-floor openings), at least 24 inches high and 20 inches wide, achieved by normal operation.
- Keep the sill no more than 44 inches above the floor.
- Make the opening operable from inside without a key, tool, or special knowledge; any bar, grille, or cover releases the same way.
- Provide a window well of at least 9 square feet, at least 36 inches in width and projection, with a ladder or steps where the well is deeper than 44 inches.

**Professional**

ORSC R310 (with the Oregon amendment effective October 1, 2024) sets the opening at 5.7 square feet net clear (5.0 at grade), 24 inches minimum height, 20 inches minimum width, and a 44-inch maximum sill. A sleeping room in a basement protected by an NFPA 13D sprinkler system may be exempt from the opening where the basement has a second means of egress. A door serving as the rescue opening is side-hinged or a slider, with an area well where it is below grade. The October 2024 amendment refined the basement-sprinkler exception and replacement-window edge cases; that amendment text is pulled directly where a replacement scenario is discussed.

**Code**

```
ORSC R310 (Oregon amendment effective October 1, 2024): emergency escape and rescue openings.
  - Net clear opening 5.7 sq ft (5.0 sq ft grade-floor), 24 in minimum height, 20 in minimum width.
  - Sill not more than 44 in above the floor; operable from inside without key, tool, or special knowledge.
  - Window well at least 9 sq ft, 36 in projection/width; ladder or steps where deeper than 44 in.
  - Sprinklered-basement (NFPA 13D) exception where a second egress is provided.
```

## 14.4 Stairs, Handrails, Guards, And Landings

Stairs, handrails, and guards follow fixed geometry that keeps them safe to use and consistent from step to step. These dimensions are among the most-checked at inspection.

**Applied**

- Build stairs at least 36 inches wide, with headroom of at least 6 feet 8 inches.
- Keep risers no higher than 7-3/4 inches and treads at least 10 inches deep, with no more than 3/8 inch variation between adjacent risers or treads or across a flight.
- Provide a landing at least 36 inches in the direction of travel at the top and bottom of each stairway.
- Provide a graspable handrail 34 to 38 inches above the nosing on at least one side of any stair with four or more risers.
- Provide a guard at least 36 inches high wherever a walking surface is more than 30 inches above the floor or grade, with openings that reject a 4-inch sphere.

**Professional**

ORSC R311 and R312 set these dimensions from the 2021 IRC: 36-inch stair width, 6-foot-8-inch headroom, 7-3/4-inch maximum riser, 10-inch minimum tread, 3/8-inch maximum variation, 36-inch landings, handrails at 34 to 38 inches with a Type I or Type II graspable profile for four or more risers, and 36-inch guards with 4-inch sphere openings (with the defined allowances at the triangular tread-riser-guard opening and the open side of a stair). The 2024 IRC revises some stair geometry, so a build permitted under the 2026 ORSC cross-checks these dimensions against the adopted edition.

**Code**

```
ORSC R311: stairways; 36-inch width, 6-foot-8-inch headroom, 7-3/4-inch maximum riser, 10-inch minimum tread, 3/8-inch maximum variation, 36-inch landings.
ORSC R311.7.8: handrails 34 to 38 inches above the nosing, graspable profile, required for four or more risers.
ORSC R312: guards at least 36 inches high where a walking surface exceeds 30 inches above grade; 4-inch sphere opening limit.
2024 IRC (future 2026 ORSC): revised stair geometry; confirm at permit time.
```

## 14.5 Garage-To-Dwelling Separation

Where an attached garage adjoins the dwelling, the separation is a specific gypsum assembly with a self-latching, self-closing door, and no opening into a sleeping room. A future garage triggers this assembly only when it is built and connected.

**Applied**

- Separate the garage from the dwelling with at least 1/2-inch gypsum board on the garage side; use 5/8-inch Type X where a habitable room is above the garage, and protect the structure supporting a dwelling above.
- Use a door between the garage and dwelling that is solid wood or solid or honeycomb-core steel at least 1-3/8 inches thick, or a 20-minute fire-rated door, and equip it to self-latch and self-close.
- Do not open a garage directly into a room used for sleeping.
- Duct through the separation in at least 26-gauge steel with no opening into the garage, and fireblock other penetrations.

**Professional**

ORSC R302.5 and R302.6 (Table R302.6) set the separation: 1/2-inch gypsum on the garage side, 5/8-inch Type X under a habitable room, and 1/2-inch gypsum protection on the supporting structure. The 2021 IRC (carried into the 2023 ORSC) requires the connecting door to be both self-latching and self-closing or automatic-closing, which changed from the prior edition. R302.5.1 prohibits an opening into a sleeping room. Where both the dwelling and garage are protected by an NFPA 13D sprinkler system, the door rating and self-closing requirements are waived, but the wall separation is not. A future garage is planned in the design without installing the door and sheathing, and the assembly is completed when the connection is made.

**Code**

```
ORSC R302.6 (Table R302.6): 1/2-inch gypsum on the garage side; 5/8-inch Type X where a habitable room is above; supporting structure protected.
ORSC R302.5.1: connecting door solid wood/steel 1-3/8 inch or 20-minute rated, self-latching and self-closing; no opening into a sleeping room.
ORSC R302.5.2: ducts through the separation at least 26-gauge steel, no garage openings.
Sprinkler exception (NFPA 13D): door rating and self-closing waived; wall separation retained.
```

## 14.6 Wall And Ceiling Coverings

Interior coverings follow the gypsum, backer, and fastening rules, and any foam plastic insulation is covered by a thermal barrier. A concrete or masonry wall may be left exposed or furred and covered.

**Applied**

- Use gypsum board of the thickness the framing spacing requires (1/2 inch standard at 16-inch on-center framing; 5/8 inch on ceilings framed wider than 16 inches on center and in fire-resistance assemblies).
- Cover foam plastic insulation on the interior with a thermal barrier of at least 1/2-inch gypsum or an approved equivalent.
- Use cement board or glass-mat gypsum, not regular drywall, as tile backer in wet areas (regular drywall is permitted only under a listed waterproofing membrane).
- Fasten and treat joints per the code table, and photograph wiring, piping, blocking, and shutoffs before closing a wall.

**Professional**

ORSC R702 governs gypsum (ASTM C1396), plaster, and paneling, with the thickness matrix in Table R702.3.5. ORSC R316 requires the thermal barrier over foam plastic, which is the rule that prevents an exposed-foam interior. A required fire-resistance-rated wall is built to a tested assembly (ASTM E119 or UL 263) or a prescriptive IBC-table assembly. Exposed concrete or CMU, drywall on furring, plaster veneer, and metal-stud partitions are all valid interior wall paths; the choice follows thermal-mass goals, acoustics, cost, and service access. An exposed-concrete ceiling reflects sound and shows forming quality, so acoustic treatment and lighting are planned with it.

**Code**

```
ORSC R702 and Table R702.3.5: gypsum board (ASTM C1396) thickness by framing spacing; fastener spacing and joint treatment.
ORSC R316: foam plastic separated from the interior by a thermal barrier (1/2-inch gypsum or equivalent).
ORSC R302.4: required fire-resistance-rated assemblies tested per ASTM E119 or UL 263.
Wet-area backer: cement board (ASTM C1325) or glass-mat gypsum (ASTM C1178); regular drywall only under a listed waterproofing membrane.
```

## 14.7 Floor Finishes And Radiant Compatibility

The floor finish is chosen for the room's use and for its fit with the radiant slab, and the slab's moisture is tested before any moisture-sensitive finish goes down. Polished concrete is the cleanest fit for a radiant concrete house.

**Applied**

- Match the finish to the room: polished or stained concrete in living areas and shops, tile in wet areas, and engineered wood or cork in bedrooms and offices.
- Confirm the finish is rated for the radiant floor and keep the tile surface temperature under the product limit (TCNA recommends a maximum of about 85°F), ramping the system up slowly.
- Test the slab moisture before installing a moisture-sensitive finish: ASTM F2170 in-situ relative humidity at or below 75 percent, or ASTM F1869 calcium-chloride emission at or below 3 pounds per 1,000 square feet per 24 hours.
- Install a crack-isolation membrane (ANSI A118.12) between the slab and tile over a heated slab.
- Keep the finish-floor R-value within the radiant manufacturer's limit; each additional R-1 cuts radiant output by roughly 10 percent, and carpet cuts it by about half.

**Professional**

Polished concrete preserves the thermal coupling of the radiant slab, avoids trapped moisture under a second floor, and is refinished rather than replaced. Tile and stone suit thermal mass with proper substrate preparation, crack isolation, and movement joints. Slab moisture testing (ASTM F2170 or F1869, per ASTM F710) is a manufacturer and warranty requirement rather than a code item, but skipping it is the most common cause of flooring failure over slab; acceptance can take 30 to 90 days post-pour. Engineered wood over radiant is generally limited to about 80°F surface temperature with manufacturer certification, and vinyl products are commonly capped near 85°F.

**Code**

```
ASTM F2170 (in-situ RH, at or below 75 percent) or ASTM F1869 (calcium-chloride MVER, at or below 3 lb/1000 sq ft/24 hr), per ASTM F710: slab moisture testing before moisture-sensitive flooring.
ANSI A118.12: crack-isolation membrane between a heated slab and tile.
TCNA methods for tile over radiant (F124/F125/F176/F189): about 85°F maximum tile surface temperature; slow ramp-up.
Finish-floor R-value per the radiant manufacturer's limit.
```

## 14.8 Wet Areas: Waterproofing And Tile

A tiled wet area is a waterproofing system, not a tile job. Tile is not waterproof by itself; the membrane, backer, slope, drain, and movement joints work together, and the tile is selected for slip resistance.

**Applied**

- Finish shower walls in a nonabsorbent surface to at least 6 feet above the floor (or 70 inches above the drain, whichever is greater).
- Install a listed waterproofing membrane (ANSI A118.10) over an approved backer, continuous across the shower floor and up the walls, with the shower liner turned up at least 2 inches above the finished threshold.
- Slope the shower floor 1/4 inch per foot to the drain, and use a drain assembly listed for the chosen membrane.
- Provide movement joints (soft sealant joints) at changes of plane, at the perimeter, and at the spacing the tile standard requires.
- Select tile with a dynamic coefficient of friction of at least 0.42 (at least 0.50 in showers) and record the product data in the project file.

**Professional**

ORSC R702.4 sets the wet-area minimums (nonabsorbent surface height, approved backer per Table R702.4.2), and OPSC 417.5.2 governs the shower lining. Regular gypsum is not a shower tile backer unless a listed membrane system permits it. The industry layer above the code is ANSI A118.10 (waterproofing membranes), A118.12 (crack isolation), A137.1 and A326.3 (DCOF slip resistance), and the TCNA Handbook methods that manufacturer warranties reference. Movement joints per the TCNA method (typically 8 to 12 feet in dry areas and 8 feet in wet areas) prevent the most common tile failure. A curbless shower with a linear drain keeps the membrane continuous across the threshold to the dry-area floor, and the drain and membrane are a matched, listed pair. Specific TCNA method details and manufacturer installation instructions govern the warranty and are followed for any specific detail.

**Code**

```
ORSC R702.4 and Table R702.4.2: wet-area nonabsorbent surface to 6 feet / 70 inches; approved backer (cement board ASTM C1325, glass-mat gypsum ASTM C1178).
OPSC 417.5.2: shower lining turned up at least 2 inches above the finished threshold; not perforated in the watertight portion.
ANSI A118.10 (waterproofing), A118.12 (crack isolation), A137.1 / A326.3 (DCOF at least 0.42, 0.50 in showers).
TCNA Handbook methods and manufacturer instructions govern the assembly and warranty.
```

## 14.9 Bathrooms: Fixture Clearances And Anti-Scald

Bathroom fixtures need clear space around them, and every shower, tub, and bidet has anti-scald temperature control. The temperature architecture pairs a hot storage setting with a limited delivery temperature.

**Applied**

- Provide at least 21 inches of clear space in front of a water closet and lavatory, and center the water closet at least 15 inches from any sidewall or fixture.
- Size a shower to at least 30 by 30 inches interior with at least 24 inches of clear space at the entry.
- Control shower and tub-shower temperature with a balanced-pressure, thermostatic, or combination valve (ASSE 1016), field-set to deliver no more than 120°F.
- Limit bathtub and bidet hot water to the code temperature with an ASSE 1070 device where a combination valve does not already do it.
- Provide bathroom exhaust of at least 50 CFM intermittent or 20 CFM continuous to the outdoors, and GFCI-protected receptacles within reach of the basin.

**Professional**

ORSC R307 and the OPSC set the fixture clearances (21-inch front clearance, 30-inch water-closet width, 15-inch centerline; 30-by-30-inch shower). OPSC 424.3 requires the shower and tub-shower valve to be an ASSE 1016 / ASME A112.18.1 balanced-pressure, thermostatic, or combination valve field-set to a 120°F maximum, and OPSC 424.5 requires an ASSE 1070 temperature-limiting device on bathtub hot water where a combination valve does not provide it. Storing water at 140°F for Legionella suppression while delivering 120°F through an ASSE 1017 master mixing valve at the heater, with point-of-use ASSE 1016 and 1070 devices, is the resilient configuration detailed in the water-supply chapter. Bathroom ventilation follows M1505.5, and the electrical protection follows the branch-circuit chapter.

**Code**

```
ORSC R307 and OPSC 402.5: fixture clearances (water closet 21-inch front, 30-inch width, 15-inch centerline; lavatory; shower 30 by 30 inches).
OPSC 424.3: shower/tub-shower valve ASSE 1016 / ASME A112.18.1, field-set to 120°F maximum.
OPSC 424.5: bathtub hot water limited by an ASSE 1070 device where not provided by a combination valve.
ORSC M1505.5: bathroom exhaust at least 50 CFM intermittent / 20 CFM continuous to the outdoors.
```

## 14.10 Kitchens, Cabinets, And Counters

The kitchen is planned by work zone with the appliances, circuits, and exhaust frozen before the cabinets are ordered, and cabinets and counters are attached to framing and substrate that carry their load.

**Applied**

- Plan the kitchen by zone (cooking, cleaning, food storage, prep, serving) and freeze the appliance models, sink location, receptacle and lighting locations, hood route, plumbing rough-in, and floor thickness before ordering cabinets.
- Attach wall cabinets through the back rails into framing (two screws per stud at top and bottom rails), with solid blocking behind the rails.
- Support a stone or quartz counter on a continuous plywood substrate over the base cabinets, anchor the cabinets to the floor, and bracket any unsupported overhang beyond 6 inches.
- Provide solid blocking in the walls for grab bars, heavy mirrors, and wall-hung fixtures, even where they are not installed now.
- Coordinate the kitchen circuits, GFCI and AFCI protection, and countertop receptacle spacing with the branch-circuit chapter.

**Professional**

The ORSC does not set cabinet attachment criteria; those come from the manufacturer and trade practice (screws into framing at each stud crossing, plywood substrate under stone, mechanical anchorage, and cantilever support for overhangs). Grab-bar blocking is standard at 33 to 36 inches above the floor at toilet and shower locations, full-width between studs with 2x backing so any screw pattern engages. Counter material follows use: quartz is low-maintenance but heat-sensitive, granite is heat-tolerant but may need sealing, marble stains and etches, and wood is repairable but needs maintenance. The kitchen countertop receptacle spacing, small-appliance circuits, and the GFCI and AFCI rules (including the Oregon Table 1-E amendments) are handled in the branch-circuit chapter and are coordinated before the cabinet layout is fixed.

**Code**

```
Cabinet and counter attachment: manufacturer instructions and trade practice (ORSC does not specify); plywood substrate under stone, mechanical anchorage, overhang support.
Grab-bar blocking: 2x backing at 33 to 36 inches, full width between studs (future-provision best practice).
Kitchen circuits, receptacle spacing, GFCI/AFCI: see the branch-circuit chapter (NEC 210.52(C), 210.8, 210.11(C), with Oregon Table 1-E).
```

## 14.11 Doors, Egress, And Safety Glazing

At least one exterior door provides the required egress, and glazing in hazardous locations is safety glazing. Interior door hardware on sleeping and bathroom rooms releases from outside without a key.

**Applied**

- Provide at least one side-hinged exterior egress door with a 32-inch clear width (door open 90 degrees) and 78-inch clear height, with a threshold no more than 1-1/2 inches above the interior floor.
- Use safety glazing (tempered or laminated) in doors, within 24 inches of a door edge below 60 inches, in and around tubs and showers, and adjacent to stairs and landings within the defined zones.
- Use privacy hardware on bedroom and bathroom doors that releases from outside without a key.
- Install exterior doors as part of the envelope, with flashing, air sealing, and a drainage path, not as loose finish carpentry.

**Professional**

ORSC R311.2 requires the egress door (32-inch clear width, 78-inch clear height, 1-1/2-inch maximum threshold). ORSC R308 requires safety glazing at hazardous locations (in doors, within 24 inches of a door where the glass is below 60 inches, in tub and shower enclosures, within 60 inches of a tub or shower where the glass is below 60 inches, and adjacent to stairs and landings within the defined zones), meeting ANSI Z97.1 or 16 CFR 1201. Exterior doors meet the Chapter 11 U-factor and air-leakage limits (typically 0.5 CFM per square foot tested to AAMA/WDMA/CSA 101/I.S.2/A440), and door flashing follows ASTM E2112 integrated with the water-resistive barrier. A smart lock still provides safe manual operation from inside where egress requires it.

**Code**

```
ORSC R311.2: at least one side-hinged exterior egress door, 32-inch clear width, 78-inch clear height, threshold not more than 1-1/2 inches above the interior floor.
ORSC R308: safety glazing (tempered or laminated, ANSI Z97.1 or 16 CFR 1201) at hazardous locations.
ORSC N1102.4: exterior door U-factor and air-leakage limits; ASTM E2112 flashing integrated with the WRB.
```

## 14.12 Storage, Pantry, Mudroom, And Laundry

Storage is planned space, not leftover space, and the utility rooms get the light, power, water, drainage, and ventilation their use requires. Closet lighting follows the fixture-clearance rules.

**Applied**

- Plan the entry or mudroom, pantry, closets, laundry, and basement storage for the specific loads they carry, with matching shelf strength.
- Give each area lighting, receptacles where needed, durable moisture-tolerant flooring, and exhaust where moisture collects.
- Use permitted closet fixtures at the required clearance from storage (enclosed LED or fluorescent, no exposed-lamp or pendant fixtures).
- Provide a laundry standpipe, drain, and exhaust, and leak protection at the washer.

**Professional**

Built-in storage reduces dependence on furniture and keeps circulation clear, and the pantry, mudroom, and laundry are planned before the final electrical, plumbing, HVAC, and cabinet decisions because they often need water, drains, exhaust, charging shelves, or special doors. Closet lighting follows NEC 410.16 (enclosed fixtures at 6 to 12 inches from storage depending on type; no exposed-lamp or pendant fixtures), because a fixture too close to stored material is a fire risk. The laundry follows the plumbing and mechanical chapters for the standpipe, drain, and dryer exhaust.

**Code**

```
NEC 410.16: closet luminaire types and clearances (enclosed fixtures; no exposed lamps or pendants).
ORSC M1505.5 and M1502: laundry exhaust and dryer duct (see the HVAC chapter).
OPSC: laundry standpipe, trap, and drain (see the drain-waste-vent chapter).
```

## 14.13 Accessibility And Aging In Place

A private single-family house is not covered by federal accessibility law, so accessibility here is a voluntary design standard the project adopts because it serves injury, aging, visitors, and daily life. It is designed in before construction, when it is inexpensive.

**Simple**

Accessibility helps far more than wheelchair users: it helps with injuries, aging, carrying groceries, moving furniture, and hard days. None of it is required for a private home a family builds and owns, but building it in from the start costs little and is expensive to add later.

**Applied**

Low-regret accessibility choices that trigger no code requirement:

- One no-step entry route into the house.
- Wider door openings (36-inch doors) on the ground floor where the layout allows.
- Reduced thresholds and a ground-floor full or half bath with maneuvering room.
- Blocking in bathroom walls for future grab bars.
- Curbless or low-threshold showers.
- Switches, outlets, and controls placed within a comfortable reach range.
- Strong lighting at entries, stairs, halls, bathrooms, and exterior paths.
- Stacked closets sized for a future lift or small elevator shaft where that is wanted.

**Professional**

The Americans with Disabilities Act does not apply to individually owned private single-family homes; it covers public accommodations and government facilities. The Fair Housing Act accessibility design requirements apply only to covered multifamily dwellings of four or more units, and a detached single-family house is not covered. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act applies only to federally funded housing. ANSI A117.1 (the 2017 edition referenced by the current code, with a 2024 edition published) is the technical accessibility standard, and for a private single-family house it is a voluntary best practice, not a requirement. Visitability (a no-step entry, 32-inch clear ground-floor doors, and a ground-floor bathroom with maneuvering room) is a voluntary framework that some jurisdictions promote through incentives; Oregon has no statewide mandatory visitability requirement for a private single-family house, so the specific site's jurisdiction is checked for any local incentive program. These features are stated as voluntary choices, and any dimension drawn from A117.1 is applied without invoking the federal regimes, as long as no federal or state housing funding flows through the project.

**Code**

```
ADA (28 CFR Part 36): does not apply to a private single-family residence.
Fair Housing Act (24 CFR 100.205): accessibility design applies only to covered multifamily dwellings of four or more units; detached single-family not covered.
Section 504 (Rehabilitation Act): federally funded housing only.
ANSI A117.1 (2017 referenced; 2024 published): voluntary best-practice technical standard for a private single-family house.
Visitability: voluntary; no statewide Oregon mandate; verify local incentive programs for the site jurisdiction.
```

## 14.14 Interior Materials Chemistry

Composite wood carries a mandatory federal formaldehyde limit, and finishes are selected to low-emission standards because source control is a more effective air-quality strategy than ventilation alone.

**Applied**

- Specify composite wood products (plywood, MDF, particleboard, and finished goods containing them) certified and labeled compliant with the federal formaldehyde standard, and retain the labels.
- Specify no-added-formaldehyde or ultra-low-emitting composite wood where available.
- Select paints, sealants, adhesives, and flooring meeting the California Section 01350 emission method or GREENGUARD Gold, and low-VOC content limits.
- Keep the product data and certifications in the project file.

**Professional**

TSCA Title VI (40 CFR Part 770) is the binding federal standard: all composite wood products sold or used since March 22, 2019 are certified and labeled compliant by an EPA-recognized third-party certifier, with emission limits identical to CARB ATCM Phase 2 (tested per ASTM E1333 or D6007). Solid wood is unregulated, and phenol-formaldehyde plywood is compliant by default because it emits far less than urea-formaldehyde. Above the mandatory floor, GREENGUARD Gold and FloorScore certify low VOC emissions, and the CDPH Standard Method (California Section 01350) is the chamber test behind those labels. VOC content limits follow the SCAQMD rules (paints under about 50 grams per liter for flat, sealants and adhesives under the Rule 1168 limits). Specifying products to Section 01350 for paints, sealants, adhesives, and flooring is a more effective indoor-air-quality strategy than ventilation alone.

**Code**

```
TSCA Title VI (40 CFR Part 770): mandatory formaldehyde certification and labeling for composite wood products; limits equal to CARB ATCM Phase 2; tested per ASTM E1333 or D6007.
CDPH Standard Method v1.2 (California Section 01350): chamber emission test behind GREENGUARD Gold and FloorScore.
SCAQMD Rule 1113 (architectural coatings) and Rule 1168 (adhesives and sealants): VOC content limits.
```

## 14.15 Lighting, Move-In Readiness, And Records

Interior lighting is designed from the furnished room, and move-in readiness means every code-required safety item is present and every normal activity works. The finish record and the pre-cover documentation let a future owner maintain the house without guessing.

**Applied**

- Design lighting in three layers (ambient, task, accent) placed from the furnished room and the work surface, coordinated with the branch-circuit and smart-home chapters.
- Before covering interior work, document wall and ceiling cavities, blocking, shutoffs, valve and cleanout access, electrical boxes, low-voltage pathways, radiant tubing, and waterproofing layers with photographs.
- At move-in, confirm the required safety items are present and operating: smoke and CO alarms interconnected, guards and handrails installed, egress doors and rescue openings operable, garage door self-latching and self-closing, anti-scald valves field-set, ventilation balanced, blower-door test submitted, and required GFCI, AFCI, and lighting outlets working.
- Keep the finish record (paint formulas, flooring and tile products, cabinet and hardware models, spare materials) and the manufacturer manuals in the permanent house file.

**Professional**

Interior final approval spans building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, energy, alarms, egress, stairs, guards, glazing, and documentation. The move-in checklist confirms both the visible quality and the hidden-system proof: doors swing and latch, cabinets and counters are secure, tile has slope and movement joints, floors are moisture-compatible, and service points are accessible. A concrete house is forgiving in structure but unforgiving in hidden routing, so photographing before cover and keeping the finish record complete is what prevents a future repair from starting with demolition. Best-practice records beyond the code minimum include the tile DCOF data, the composite-wood labels, the slab moisture-test results, and the anti-scald valve set points.
