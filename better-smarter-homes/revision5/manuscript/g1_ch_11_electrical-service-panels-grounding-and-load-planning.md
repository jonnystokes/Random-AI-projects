# Chapter 11: Electrical: Service, Panels, Grounding, And Load Planning

This chapter covers the electrical backbone: the utility service, meter, emergency disconnect, main panel and subpanels, the grounding electrode system and bonding, surge protection, service-capacity and load planning, and the provisions for solar, battery storage, generators, and electric-vehicle charging that a long-service house reserves space and capacity for.

Branch circuits, wire sizing, voltage drop, receptacle and lighting layout, and low-voltage cabling are covered in the branch-circuit and low-voltage chapter. This chapter sizes and locates the service and the distribution equipment; the circuit design follows from the service capacity established here.

The electrical code of record is the 2023 Oregon Electrical Specialty Code (OESC), effective October 1, 2023, based on the 2023 National Electrical Code (NEC) with Oregon amendments in OESC Table 1-E, plus the BCD 17-2025 interim amendments effective January 1, 2026. A 2026 OESC adoption based on the 2026 NEC is anticipated for October 1, 2026, which will move several citations (noted where they apply). Confirm the edition in force at permit time.

## 11.1 Electrical Scope And The Owner-Builder Permit

The electrical system is permitted, inspected, and documented work. An owner-builder may perform electrical work on their own primary residence under the Oregon homeowner exemption, with the same permit and inspection sequence a licensed electrician follows.

**Simple**

A bad electrical connection can shock someone, start a fire, or fail years later inside a wall. The electrical system is built to a code, inspected at each stage, and labeled so a future repair does not require guessing. This chapter plans the backbone (the service, the panels, the grounding, and the capacity) before any circuits are run.

**Applied**

Plan the backbone before the branch circuits:

- Utility service and meter location.
- The outdoor emergency disconnect.
- The main panel and any subpanels.
- The grounding electrode system and bonding.
- Whole-house surge protection.
- The service-size load calculation.
- Reserved space and conduit for solar, battery storage, a generator, EV charging, the shop, pumps, and large appliances.

Pull the electrical permit before starting. An owner-builder doing this work on their own primary residence uses the homeowner permit under the Oregon homeowner exemption; a contractor uses a master-electrician supervising permit.

**Professional**

Electrical planning starts from the service load, the utility interface, the grounding electrode system, the equipment locations and working clearances, and the future distributed-energy provisions. A concrete structure makes later routing and alteration harder, so the service-layer and conduit strategy carries more weight than in a stud-wall house. The objective is a backbone that can be inspected, isolated, upgraded, and maintained without cutting concrete, sized by the load calculation rather than by a target service number.

**Code**

```
2023 OESC: the adopted Oregon electrical code; NEC 2023 base with Oregon amendments in OESC Table 1-E, plus BCD 17-2025 interim amendments effective January 1, 2026.
  - A 2026 OESC (2026 NEC base) adoption is anticipated October 1, 2026; confirm the edition at permit time.
ORS 479.540(1)(a): homeowner exemption permitting an owner to perform electrical work on their own primary residence under permit.
OAR 918-305: electrical permit and inspection administration; OAR 918-305-0420(3) requires the permit posted near the main panel.
```

## 11.2 Concrete-House Electrical Strategy

A concrete structure is durable but unforgiving to later wiring changes, so the electrical routing is planned into accessible service layers and conduits rather than cut in after the fact.

**Simple**

Concrete is strong but hard to alter, so wiring is not buried where it cannot be reached. The plan brings the utilities to one controlled area, keeps the main equipment near the service entry, routes circuits through accessible service layers, and leaves spare conduit paths and spare panel spaces for later work.

**Applied**

- Bring the utility service, communications, and future energy equipment to one controlled exterior interconnect area.
- Locate the main panel near the service entry.
- Use service layers and conduit for accessible routing; avoid unplanned holes through the concrete envelope.
- Place panels where they can be reached and inspected.
- Leave spare breaker spaces and spare conduit sleeves into the service layer.
- Label every panel, feeder, conduit, and reserved path.

**Professional**

The concept is a single exterior interconnect area, a main panel near the service entry, floor-level subpanels to shorten branch runs, accessible service-layer routing, and reserved provisions for solar, EV charging, battery storage, outdoor utility loads, and low-voltage infrastructure. Final equipment and conductor sizes come from the load calculation, the utility requirements, the adopted code, and the listed equipment instructions, not from the concept alone.

**Code**

```
NEC 110.26 and 110.26(E): working clearance and dedicated equipment space for panels and service equipment (see the working-space section).
NEC 300.5: minimum cover for underground conductors and raceways.
The approved electrical drawings show the service entry, meter, disconnects, panels, feeders, grounding electrode system, bonding, working clearances, major loads, and reserved future provisions.
```

## 11.3 Utility Service, Meter, And The Exterior Interconnect

Power reaches the house at the utility transformer and meter, and the NEC governs the installation from the meter base inward. The utility service standards and the electrical code both apply, and the interconnect area is coordinated with the utility before the trench is opened.

**Applied**

Hold a service-planning meeting with the utility before trenching, and confirm:

- Overhead drop or underground lateral (underground is the default for durability, appearance, and wildfire exposure).
- Transformer type and location (padmount on an owner-poured pad, or pole), with the utility's setbacks.
- The meter-base model the utility approves.
- The lateral route, conduit size (typically 3-inch Schedule 40 PVC, Schedule 80 under driveways), and trench path.
- The meter-main combination location within sight of the dwelling.
- Temporary construction power.
- Separation from communications and other buried utilities.

**Professional**

The interconnect area organizes the meter, the outdoor service disconnect, communications equipment, and reserved space for a future solar AC disconnect, a generator inlet or transfer switch, and battery equipment where allowed outdoors. It is built from listed equipment with the required separation between systems rather than forced into one crowded cabinet. A meter-main combination (meter socket plus main breaker in one outdoor enclosure) commonly serves as the service equipment and satisfies the outdoor emergency-disconnect requirement in one device, with a four-wire feeder to the indoor distribution panel treated as a separate-building or separate-structure feed with isolated neutral and separate equipment ground.

**Code**

```
NEC Article 230: service conductors, service equipment, and the service disconnecting means.
NEC 300.5 and Table 300.5(A): minimum cover for underground service and branch circuits (service-entrance 24 inches; dwelling branch circuits under 150 V 18 inches, 24 inches under driveways; PVC conduit 18 inches; rigid metal conduit 6 inches).
NEC 250.32: feeder to a separate structure with separate equipment grounding conductor and isolated neutral.
Utility service standards: transformer, meter base, lateral, and clearances are set by the serving utility (Pacific Power, PGE, or the local co-op) and confirmed in a pre-construction service-planning meeting.
```

## 11.4 Emergency Disconnect

Every one- and two-family dwelling requires a service disconnect at a readily accessible outdoor location on or within sight of the dwelling, marked so a first responder can find and operate it.

**Applied**

- Locate the emergency disconnect outdoors, readily accessible, on the dwelling or within 50 feet and in sight of it.
- Use a meter-main, a service disconnect, or a listed disconnect ahead of the service disconnect.
- Rate the disconnect for the available fault current.
- Mark it with a red background, white letters, at least 1/2 inch high.
- Add an outdoor placard directory listing every energy source (service, solar, battery, generator) and where each disconnect is.

**Professional**

NEC 230.85 requires the outdoor emergency disconnect for one- and two-family dwellings, and replacing the service equipment triggers the requirement on an existing house. Under the anticipated 2026 OESC the same requirement moves into NEC 230.70(A)(1); a project permitted after that adoption cites the new location and labels accordingly. The disconnect short-circuit current rating is at least the available fault current at the service, which the utility provides on request.

**Code**

```
NEC 230.85 (2023 NEC): readily accessible outdoor service disconnect for one- and two-family dwellings; on or within sight (within 50 feet) of the dwelling; rated for the available fault current.
  - Consolidates into NEC 230.70(A)(1) under the 2026 NEC.
NEC 110.21(B): field-applied marking; emergency disconnect marked with a red background and white letters at least 1/2 inch high.
NEC 230.85(D) / 705.10: directory at the disconnect identifying all power sources.
```

## 11.5 Service Size And Load Planning

The service capacity is set by a dwelling load calculation, not by a sum of breaker sizes. An all-electric long-service house with a heat pump, heat-pump water heater, induction range, EV charging, and a shop calculates near the top of the residential range, and the choice is between a large unmanaged service and a smaller service paired with a listed energy-management system.

**Applied**

Build a load inventory, then run the calculation:

| Load group | Include |
|---|---|
| Base house | General lighting and receptacles, small-appliance and laundry circuits |
| Kitchen | Range or cooktop and oven, dishwasher, disposal, microwave, refrigerator |
| Water | Well pump, booster pump, recirculation pump, water heater, treatment equipment |
| HVAC | Heat pump, air handler, ventilation, any backup heat |
| Outdoor and shop | Utility bay, pumps, tools, welder, lighting, irrigation |
| Future | EV charging, hot tub, sauna, solar, battery storage, generator interface |

Run the NEC Article 220 dwelling calculation (the optional method under 220.82 usually yields a smaller demand than the standard method) and confirm the transformer and lateral capacity with the utility before ordering service equipment.

**Professional**

The standard method (NEC 220.42) sums general lighting at 3 VA per square foot (2 VA under the 2026 NEC), the small-appliance and laundry circuits, and the fixed appliances, applies the Table 220.42 demand factors, adds the larger of heating or cooling at 100 percent, and adds 25 percent of the largest motor. The optional method (NEC 220.82), available at 100 A and above on a 120/240 V three-wire service, takes the first 10,000 VA at 100 percent and the remainder at 40 percent, with heating and cooling per 220.82(C). Electric-vehicle supply equipment is added at 7,200 VA or nameplate, whichever is larger (NEC 220.57). A representative all-electric program (heat pump, heat-pump water heater, induction range, dryer, well and septic pumps, dual 48 A EV chargers, and a shop subpanel) calculates near 250 A of demand under the optional method, which points to a 400 A service without load management or a 200 A service with a listed energy-management system that curtails EV charging and other deferrable loads to the available headroom.

**Code**

```
NEC 230.79(C): minimum 100 A service for a single-family dwelling; a modern all-electric house is sized well above this.
NEC 220.42 (standard method) and 220.82 (optional method): dwelling load calculation methods.
NEC 220.57: EV supply equipment load at 7,200 VA or nameplate, whichever is larger.
NEC 220.50 and 220.60: largest-motor and noncoincident (heating/cooling) load treatment.
NEC 625.42 and 625.48: EVSE as a continuous load (circuit at 125 percent of nameplate); a listed energy management system (EVEMS) limits the calculated load to the managed maximum (the 125 percent multiplier is waived under the 2026 NEC 625.48).
```

## 11.6 Main Panel, Subpanels, And Working Space

The main panel is the distribution center; floor-level subpanels shorten branch runs in a house where routing is expensive to change. Every panel needs code working clearance and dedicated space that stays clear for the life of the installation.

**Applied**

- Locate the main panel near the service entry and subpanels near the loads they serve.
- Keep at least 36 inches of clear working depth in front of each panel, at least 30 inches of width, and at least 6.5 feet of headroom.
- Keep the dedicated space above the panel (to 6 feet or the ceiling) clear of plumbing, ducts, and other systems.
- Do not place a panel in a bathroom, a clothes closet, over steps, above a sink, or behind an appliance.
- Provide lighting at each panel and a clear path to it.
- Leave spare breaker spaces and label the panel directory accurately.

**Professional**

NEC 110.26 sets the working clearance: at least 3 feet of depth in front of the equipment (Condition 1), 30 inches of width or the equipment width, and 6.5 feet of headroom, with the 110.26(E) dedicated space above the panel kept free of foreign systems. NEC 240.24 prohibits overcurrent devices in bathrooms, clothes closets, and over steps. Floor-level subpanels reduce branch length and simplify maintenance at the cost of added feeders, neutral-to-ground separation at each subpanel, and coordination of the available fault current; the feeder and panel sizing come from the calculation set. A smart panel that provides energy management and per-circuit control can serve as the main panel where the design uses a 200 A service with managed peak loads.

**Code**

```
NEC 110.26: working space (3 ft depth minimum, 30 in width, 6.5 ft headroom) and, at 110.26(E), dedicated space above the panel clear of foreign systems.
NEC 240.24: overcurrent devices not in bathrooms, clothes closets, or over steps; readily accessible.
NEC 408 and 215: panelboard ratings, feeder sizing, overcurrent protection, and subpanel neutral-to-ground separation.
```

## 11.7 Grounding And Bonding

Grounding connects the electrical system to earth; bonding ties metal parts together so a fault has a low-impedance path back to the overcurrent device. A concrete house turns its footing reinforcement into a strong grounding electrode, but the concrete-encased electrode has to be connected and inspected before the pour.

**Simple**

Grounding gives the system a connection to earth. Bonding connects the metal parts together so a fault current returns quickly and trips the breaker. In a concrete house the steel in the footing makes an excellent grounding electrode, but the connection to it has to be made and inspected before the concrete is placed, because it cannot be reached afterward.

**Applied**

Plan grounding before the pour:

- Identify the concrete-encased electrode: at least 20 feet of 1/2-inch (#4) rebar or 20 feet of bare #4 copper in the footing, in direct earth contact under at least 2 inches of concrete.
- Tie the footing rebar into a continuous grid and bring two #4 bare copper grounding tails above the slab, one to the main panel and one spare for future equipment, connected to the rebar by exothermic weld.
- Bond metal water piping, any gas piping, and structural metal where required.
- Keep neutral and grounding conductors separated in every subpanel.
- Photograph and test the grounding connections before cover.
- Schedule the concrete-encased electrode inspection before the foundation pour.

**Professional**

NEC 250.50 requires all present electrodes to be bonded into the grounding electrode system, and 250.52(A)(3) requires the concrete-encased electrode (20 feet of #4 rebar or bare #4 copper, 2-inch cover, direct earth contact) to be incorporated in new construction. Only one concrete-encased electrode must be bonded where several are present, but tying the rebar lifts together with exothermic welds at the major intersections makes the whole shell a coherent low-impedance electrode. The grounding electrode conductor is sized from Table 250.66 (never larger than #4 copper for a concrete-encased-electrode-only connection). The copper tail is connected above the slab where it stays dry, aluminum is not used in concrete contact, and a continuity check from the tail to a far rebar exposure targets under 0.1 ohm and is recorded. The active rebar stays below frost depth so the concrete-to-earth interface remains moist.

**Code**

```
NEC 250.50 and 250.52: grounding electrode system; all present electrodes bonded together.
NEC 250.52(A)(3): concrete-encased electrode; 20 feet of #4 (1/2 inch) rebar or 20 feet of bare #4 copper, encased in at least 2 inches of concrete in direct earth contact; incorporated in new construction.
NEC Table 250.66: grounding electrode conductor sizing (250.66(B) caps a concrete-encased-electrode connection at #4 copper).
NEC 250.104: bonding of metal water, gas, and structural piping systems.
NEC 250.24 and 408.40: main bonding jumper at the service only; neutral-to-ground separation at subpanels.
Inspection: concrete-encased electrode inspected before the foundation pour.
```

## 11.8 Surge Protection And Lightning

A whole-house surge protective device at the service protects the wiring and connected equipment from transient overvoltage, and it is required at every dwelling service. Lightning protection is separate, is not code-required for a dwelling, and is worth evaluating on an exposed concrete structure.

**Applied**

- Install a Type 1 or Type 2 surge protective device at or immediately adjacent to the service equipment.
- Protect sensitive low-voltage lines (network, coax, antenna, gate, and outdoor camera cabling) at their entry.
- Bond all surge and low-voltage protection to the same grounding system.
- Keep the device accessible and check its status indicator during maintenance.
- On an exposed or hilltop site, evaluate a listed lightning protection system and bond it to the electrical grounding system at a single point.

**Professional**

NEC 230.67 requires a Type 1 or Type 2 surge protective device at every dwelling service, and the 2023 NEC extends the requirement to feeders supplying dwelling units (215.18) and outdoor branch circuits and feeders (225.42). Surge performance depends on short conductor paths and correct bonding, and low-voltage lines carry surges as readily as power conductors. A lightning protection system per NFPA 780 uses air terminals, down conductors, and dedicated ground electrodes bonded to the electrical grounding system at one point (NEC 250.106); the concrete-encased electrode is strong for power-frequency fault current but a dedicated low-impedance ring supplements it for lightning impulse. Properly bonded DC sources (solar strings and battery systems, per NEC 690.41 and 706.30) prevent stray DC current from driving rebar corrosion.

**Code**

```
NEC 230.67: Type 1 or Type 2 surge protective device required at every dwelling service.
NEC 215.18 and 225.42: surge protection extended to feeders and outdoor branch circuits/feeders serving dwellings.
NFPA 780: lightning protection system design (air terminals, down conductors, dedicated electrodes).
NEC 250.106: lightning protection ground bonded to the electrical grounding electrode system at a single common point.
```

## 11.9 Backup Power And Generators

Backup power needs a listed transfer method that prevents backfeeding the utility. The choices are a portable generator with a listed interlock, a permanent standby generator with an automatic transfer switch, or a battery system as the primary backup with a generator for deep outages.

**Applied**

Decide the backup loads first (refrigeration, well and booster pumps, heat pump, ventilation, water treatment, any sump or sewage pump, network, and lighting), then choose the architecture:

- Portable generator: an outdoor inlet, a backfeed breaker, and a listed mechanical interlock for the specific panel; operate the generator outdoors, away from doors and windows.
- Permanent standby: a listed automatic transfer switch (service-rated units can double as the service disconnect) on an engineered pad, with the manufacturer's separation from openings.
- Battery-primary: a listed energy storage system with a transfer relay, and a generator only for multi-day outages.

For a fixed generator on LP gas, Oregon requires a licensed LP installer; there is no homeowner exemption for LP work.

**Professional**

NEC 702 governs the optional standby system. A two-pole transfer switch that does not switch the neutral keeps the generator bonded to the service neutral and is the residential default; the generator's internal neutral-to-ground bonding jumper is removed to avoid a parallel neutral path. A three-pole transfer switch that switches the neutral makes the generator a separately derived system with its own grounding electrode and its internal bond retained. Permanent standby placement follows NFPA 37 and the manufacturer's clearances from openings and combustible walls. A battery-primary architecture with a generator for deep backup is the default here because it has fewer moving parts, no daily fuel logistics, and it operates every day for self-consumption rather than sitting idle.

**Code**

```
NEC 702: optional standby systems; generator sized to the load served; caution marking at the service (702.7).
NEC 250.30 and 250.34: grounding of separately derived (three-pole transfer) and portable generator systems; single neutral-to-ground bond.
NFPA 37: separation of stationary combustion engines from structures and openings.
ORS 480.432: LP gas installation requires a licensed installer; no homeowner exemption.
Listed mechanical interlock: required on any panel using a backfeed breaker for a portable generator.
```

## 11.10 Battery Energy Storage Readiness

A permanently installed battery system is a listed energy storage system with siting, fire-separation, and disconnect rules. Kept below the residential aggregate threshold and mounted outdoors or in a fire-separated space, it stays in the prescriptive path.

**Applied**

- Specify a UL 9540-listed self-contained energy storage system, confirmed on the manufacturer's certification, not battery-cell or inverter listings alone.
- Mount it on an exterior wall (the default) or in a mechanical room; not in a habitable space.
- Keep the aggregate below the residential threshold (commonly 20 kWh per unit and 40 kWh per location) to avoid a hazard mitigation analysis.
- Provide the working clearance, an emergency shutdown outside the building marked per code, and lockable DC and AC disconnects.
- Add smoke detection in the storage area, interconnected with the dwelling alarm.

**Professional**

NEC Article 706 and UL 9540 govern the system listing, and NFPA 855 governs siting, spacing, and the hazard mitigation analysis threshold. A concrete house provides inherent fire separation exceeding the one-hour rating NFPA 855 seeks for an interior installation. NEC 706.15 requires an emergency shutdown for one- and two-family dwellings, readily accessible and marked per 110.21(B), and an outdoor placard directory lists the service, solar, battery, and generator disconnects together for first responders. Residential lithium systems typically do not require active ventilation; the UL 9540 listing confirms this.

**Code**

```
NEC Article 706: energy storage systems; NEC 706.15 emergency shutdown for one- and two-family dwellings.
UL 9540: complete energy storage system listing (battery, inverter, controls).
NFPA 855: siting, spacing, aggregate thresholds (commonly 20 kWh per unit, 40 kWh per location for dwellings), and hazard mitigation analysis above threshold.
NEC 110.26 and 110.21(B): working clearance and disconnect marking.
```

## 11.11 Solar Readiness

Solar is far cheaper to accommodate during construction than to retrofit. Reserved conduit, a roof or ground array location, an equipment zone, and service headroom make a later installation a connection rather than a demolition, and the fire-access pathways are set by the residential code.

**Applied**

- Reserve a conduit path from the array area to the electrical equipment and pull a string in it.
- Reserve an inverter or combiner location and an AC disconnect location, co-located with the meter-main cluster.
- Observe the residential PV roof-access pathways (a module setback from the ridge and clear access pathways) at the roof-layout stage.
- Coordinate the roof structure for the planned PV dead load and wind and snow loads.
- Keep panel busbar headroom for the interconnection, or plan a smart-panel power-control interconnection.

**Professional**

A microinverter architecture keeps all high-voltage conversion at the module and satisfies NEC 690.12 rapid shutdown inherently, with individual module replaceability and no rooftop DC arc-fault path. Interconnection follows NEC 705.12: the sum of the PV backfeed breaker and the main breaker stays within 120 percent of the busbar rating (a 200 A busbar with a 200 A main allows a 40 A PV breaker), or a supply-side connection or a listed power control system per 705.13 is used where the busbar rule is exceeded. Oregon Residential Specialty Code R324 sets the roof-access pathways and fire-access setbacks. New concrete or heavy-timber roofs rarely reach a structural limit for roof-mounted PV; a ground-mount array needs an engineered foundation. Net-metering enrollment and a signed interconnection agreement precede energization.

**Code**

```
NEC 690.12: rapid shutdown; controlled conductors to 30 V within 30 seconds outside the array boundary (microinverters comply inherently).
NEC 705.12: interconnection; 120 percent busbar rule, or supply-side connection, or a listed power control system (705.13).
NEC 690.13, 690.54, 690.56, 705.10: PV disconnect and labeling; directory of all sources at the service.
ORSC R324: residential PV roof-access pathways and fire-access setbacks; structural coordination under ORSC R301 and ASCE 7 loads.
Utility interconnection: net-metering enrollment and a signed interconnection agreement before energization.
```

## 11.12 EV Charging And Future Large Loads

Electric-vehicle charging is a large continuous load that a long-service house plans conduit and capacity for now, even when the equipment comes later. Load management keeps dual-charger capability within a moderate service size.

**Applied**

- Stub a 2-inch conduit with a pull string from the panel area to every plausible charging location (garage, driveway pad, and any future accessory dwelling), and label both ends with the destination and intended ampacity.
- Install one outdoor-rated charger (NEMA 3R minimum, 4X near the coast) on day one and leave the second stub empty.
- Size the charging circuit at 125 percent of the charger nameplate, or use a listed energy-management system that limits the aggregate EV draw.
- Reserve panel space and conduit for the shop, a hot tub, and other future large loads rather than reserving breaker spaces in place of a real calculation.

**Professional**

NEC 220.57 adds EV supply equipment at 7,200 VA or nameplate, and NEC 625.42 treats it as a continuous load sized at 125 percent. A listed energy management system (NEC 625.48) limits the aggregate EV load to the managed maximum, which lets dual 48 A chargers fit within a 200 A service alongside the heat pump, water heater, range, and shop; without management, dual chargers push the service to 320 or 400 A. Empty conduit and pull strings installed during construction cost a fraction of retrofitting through finished walls, and a smart panel that provides the energy management also provides per-circuit monitoring and control.

**Code**

```
NEC 220.57: EV supply equipment load at 7,200 VA or nameplate.
NEC 625.42: EVSE continuous-load circuit sized at 125 percent of nameplate.
NEC 625.48: listed energy management system (EVEMS) limits the aggregate EV load; the 125 percent multiplier is waived under the 2026 NEC where an EVEMS actively limits load.
NEC 110.27: physical protection for equipment subject to damage (bollards at a pedestal charger).
```

## 11.13 Inspection, Testing, And Records

Electrical work is inspected at each stage before it is covered or energized, and the records let a future owner or electrician understand the system without opening walls. The concrete-encased electrode inspection is the one most often missed because it happens before the pour.

**Applied**

Schedule the inspections in sequence and record each:

1. Service or temporary-service inspection.
2. Underground or trench inspection before backfill.
3. Concrete-encased electrode inspection before the foundation pour.
4. Rough-in inspection before insulation and drywall.
5. Final inspection with devices terminated, the panel labeled, the surge device installed, and the emergency disconnect marked.

Keep the one-line diagram, the panel schedules, the load calculation, the grounding and bonding record and continuity test, the equipment cut sheets, the inspection approvals, the as-built conduit routes, and the pre-cover photographs in the permanent house file.

**Professional**

Final acceptance confirms the panel directory is accurate, the disconnects operate, the surge device shows normal status, the grounding and bonding continuity is verified where tested, any transfer equipment operates, and the working clearances are maintained. The permit is posted near the main panel through construction. The as-built record documents the reserved solar, battery, generator, and EV provisions so the future work connects to a known infrastructure rather than an assumed one.

**Code**

```
OAR 918-305: electrical inspection sequence (service, underground, concrete-encased electrode, rough-in, final); permit posted near the panel (918-305-0420(3)).
NEC 110.26 and 408: working clearance and panel labeling verified at final.
NEC 250: grounding and bonding continuity verified and recorded.
Records to retain: one-line diagram, panel schedules, load calculation, grounding/continuity record, inspection approvals, as-built conduit routes, and pre-cover photographs.
```
