# Chapter 3: Land, Site, And Feasibility

This chapter is the parcel-evaluation pass that should happen before a purchase contract becomes binding, and before any house design is committed to a specific lot. Most items here are questions, not answers, because the answers depend on the specific parcel. The goal is to know what to ask, what to verify, and what makes the project unworkable on a given lot.

The house design itself works across a wide range of climates and terrain. Insulation thickness, snow loads, foundation depth, drainage strategy, and several mechanical decisions scale to the site. This chapter helps determine which end of each design range applies and which lots cannot support the project at any reasonable cost.

The Soil, Ground Prep, Drainage, And Foundation Risk chapter handles the geotechnical side once a parcel is under serious consideration. The Legal, Permits, Owner-Builder Rules, And Inspections chapter handles the title, ownership-structure, and owner-builder posture. This chapter is the early filter that decides whether either of those next chapters needs to be opened for this parcel.

## 3.1 The Feasibility Filter

Before making an offer, or during the inspection period after an offer is accepted, sort each issue into one of these severity bands.

| Result | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Dealbreaker | A core requirement is missing or legally blocked | Walk away, or make the contract contingent on the issue being resolved before closing |
| Expensive risk | The parcel remains possible, but the fix is costly or uncertain | Price the risk, add written contingencies, obtain agency or contractor written answers |
| Design constraint | The parcel works if the house adapts to it | Carry the constraint into the site plan and the budget |
| Lifestyle tradeoff | The parcel works, but daily life changes | Decide whether the tradeoff fits the way the household wants to live |
| Future-flexibility check | The parcel supports the initial build but may block later plans | Check outbuilding allowances, accessory-dwelling rules, work-area limits, access |
| Strong candidate | Land, utility, access, septic, water, zoning, and title questions all pass | Move toward design with the site facts in hand |

The order in which the questions get answered matters. Septic, water, legal access, zoning, and title are the items that can kill a project. Resolve those first. Climate, drainage, topography, and distance are usually design or lifestyle questions, not dealbreakers.

A dealbreaker is anything for which there is no buildable workaround at any reasonable cost: no legal dwelling path under the zoning, a failed septic site evaluation with no alternative system that fits, no legal access to a public road, no usable water source, or a recorded restriction that prohibits residential use.

## 3.2 Climate And Design Loads

Oregon spans coastal, valley, mountain, and high-desert climates. Design loads change substantially across that range, and a parcel-specific value set is required at permit time.

**Simple**

Climate is what the house has to work against. Cold, hot, snow, wind, earthquake, rain, and frost all show up as numbers the structural and envelope designs have to handle. Different parts of Oregon land in different bands of those numbers, so the same house plan needs different insulation, different foundation depth, and sometimes different roof framing depending on where the lot is.

**Applied**

For the candidate parcel, collect the values below before finalizing the structural design.

- winter design temperature (heating-design outdoor low)
- summer design temperature (cooling-design outdoor high)
- ground snow load
- roof snow-load method (flat-roof or sloped, with shape coefficients)
- basic wind speed and exposure category
- seismic design category
- frost depth or frost-protection requirement
- annual rainfall pattern
- wildfire exposure where applicable
- floodplain status where applicable
- elevation and exposure

The Oregon Building Codes Division provides a Design Criteria Hub for jurisdiction-level values where the local jurisdiction participates. The local building department may also direct the engineer to a specific source. The OSU/SEAO snow-load lookup tool at snowload.seao.org/lookup.html is referenced by the 2021 ORSC and later for site-specific ground snow loads. Save the answer, the source, and the date in the project binder.

The values below are early planning ranges, not permit values. They are useful for early sizing and early conversations only.

| Condition | Early planning range across Oregon |
|---|---|
| Winter design temperature | -10 °F (high-elevation east of Cascades) to 25 °F (Willamette Valley) |
| Summer design temperature | 85 °F (coast) to 105 °F (high desert east of Cascades) |
| Ground snow load | 15 psf (valley lowlands) to 25–50 psf (rural hills) to 100–300+ psf (mountain elevations above approximately 3,000–4,000 ft) |
| Basic wind speed | 95–115 mph in most of Oregon, with higher exposure near the coast and in the Columbia River Gorge |
| Seismic design category | Most of western Oregon is SDC D0, D1, or D2 (Cascadia Subduction Zone influence); eastern Oregon is largely SDC C |
| Frost depth | 12–18 inches in most valleys, 24–36 inches in cold mountain zones |
| Annual rainfall | 15–20 inches east of the Cascades, 60–100+ inches on the coast and west slopes |

**Professional**

The structural set requires design values for ground snow load, roof snow load, basic wind speed, exposure category, seismic design category, flood hazard, and frost protection. The envelope and mechanical design also require outdoor design temperatures, indoor design temperatures, humidity and solar-exposure assumptions, rain-exposure assumptions, and wildfire-smoke assumptions where applicable.

For a concrete house, wall mass does not absolve a loose load path. Gravity load, lateral load, uplift, sliding, overturning, and foundation demand still have to close into one permitted design. Snow drifting on flat concrete roofs, ponding instability, and parapet detailing introduce design issues that wood-framed homes do not face.

**Code**

```
ORSC R301: design criteria for residential structural loading.
  - Establishes the baseline for gravity, snow, wind, seismic, and flood loads.
  - Refers prescriptive provisions to the climatic and geographic table at R301.2(1).

ORSC Table R301.2(1): climatic and geographic design criteria.
  - Adopted by the local jurisdiction with its specific values for ground snow load, basic wind speed, exposure category, seismic design category, weathering, frost depth, termite, decay, winter design temperature, ice barrier requirements, flood hazard, air freezing index, and mean annual temperature.
  - The table is filled in by the local jurisdiction; values are not statewide.

ORSC R301.2.1: wind design and exposure categories.
  - Exposure category B (suburban, wooded), C (open terrain, scattered obstructions), or D (open water, smooth flat terrain).
  - Special wind regions (Columbia Gorge, parts of the Oregon coast) may have site-specific design wind speeds above 130 mph.

ORSC R301.2.2: seismic provisions.
  - Adopts seismic design category and the reinforcing requirements that follow.
  - SDC D0/D1/D2 (most of western Oregon) trigger expanded reinforcement for foundations, walls, and floor diaphragms.
  - BCD publishes a how-to guide for determining SDC at oregon.gov/bcd.

ORSC R301.2.3: snow loads.
  - Refers to the OSU/SEAO snow-load lookup tool (snowload.seao.org/lookup.html) for site-specific ground snow load.
  - Roof snow load is calculated from ground snow load using shape, exposure, and thermal coefficients per ASCE 7.

ORSC R322: flood-resistant construction.
  - Applies where flood hazard applies under the local jurisdiction's adopted FEMA mapping.
  - Special foundation, elevation, and venting requirements for buildings in Special Flood Hazard Areas.

ASCE 7: Minimum Design Loads and Associated Criteria for Buildings and Other Structures.
  - Provides the load combinations, snow shape coefficients, wind pressure calculation, and seismic spectral values that engineered designs use.

Oregon Design Criteria Hub (oregon.gov/bcd): BCD design-criteria tool for jurisdiction-level values.
  - Confirms ground snow load, basic wind speed, exposure category, seismic design category for participating jurisdictions.
```

## 3.3 Zoning And Allowed Use

Zoning is the legal label on the parcel. It controls allowed use, lot dimensions, setbacks, building height, accessory uses, and the local review path. Zoning failures are dealbreakers; nothing built on the parcel changes the zoning, and the building permit cannot override the zoning approval.

**Simple**

Every piece of land has rules about what can be built on it. Some land lets a person build a primary residence with a normal permit. Some land requires special approval. Some land does not allow a residence at all. Zoning has to be confirmed before money is committed; nothing about the building design fixes a zoning problem.

**Applied**

Before making an offer, contact the planning department directly. Identify the parcel by address, map number, or tax lot, not by description. Confirm in writing:

- whether a primary single-family residence is allowed outright, by conditional use approval, or not at all
- whether the parcel meets the minimum lot size for the zone
- the front, side, rear, stream, road, and resource setbacks
- whether the setbacks leave room for the house, driveway, septic, well, drainage features, and staging
- the maximum building height
- the maximum lot coverage
- whether outbuildings are allowed, at what size, and at what count
- whether an accessory dwelling unit (ADU) is allowed
- whether home occupations or home-based businesses are allowed
- any limits on outdoor storage, equipment, animals, farming, structure type, or exterior work areas

Save the planner's name, the date, the parcel identifier they confirmed against, and the answer in writing. A verbal answer in passing is not a basis for a purchase decision.

**Professional**

Common rural Oregon zoning categories, ranked by typical fit for an owner-built single-family residence:

| Zone family | Typical posture | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Rural Residential (RR-2, RR-5, RR-10, RR-20) | Often the cleanest target | Number indicates the minimum lot size in acres. Primary residence typically allowed outright. ADU, home occupation, and outbuildings often permitted with limits. |
| Mixed Use Agricultural (MUA-10, MUA-20) | Often workable | County-dependent. Often allows a primary residence outright alongside farm and timber uses. Douglas County uses MUA zones in many areas. |
| Farm-Forest (F1, F2) | Conditionally workable | Usually requires more review than RR zones. County-dependent. |
| Non-Resource Rural Lands | Workable when present | Rare designation. Tends to be flexible. |
| Exclusive Farm Use (EFU) | Restrictive | Building a residence usually requires qualifying as a farm income parcel, meeting template-dwelling criteria, or another defined exception. Second dwellings often prohibited. Treat with care. |
| Urban residential (R1, R2) | Workable but constrained | Typical small-lot rules. May not match the testing-space, outbuilding, and privacy goals of an owner-built concrete home. |

Land-use feasibility controls the legal envelope of what can be built before the building code controls how it can be built. Zoning, overlay zones, resource-land rules, setbacks, road-access rules, floodplain rules, wildfire overlays, and home-occupation limits can all change the project before the first footing is designed.

For an owner-built concrete home with research, testing, and content-creation activity on site, several routine items deserve specific zoning verification:

- long driveways and equipment delivery routes
- staging areas for materials, formwork, and equipment
- outbuildings (workshop, garage, storage, equipment shed)
- testing space (compaction tests, concrete-mix trials, block-press operation if applicable)
- material storage (rebar, block, lumber, sealants, insulation)
- any future business use under home occupation rules

A parcel that allows a residence does not automatically allow all of those activities. Confirm each one before the budget and design commit to needing it.

**Code**

```
ORS Chapter 215: county land-use planning and resource-land authority.
  - Establishes the framework for county comprehensive plans and zoning.
  - Includes the EFU framework, template-dwelling criteria, and resource-land rules.

ORS Chapter 227: city planning and zoning authority.
  - City-side equivalent of ORS 215 for incorporated areas.

Local comprehensive plan and development code.
  - Zoning district map, allowed uses, setbacks, conditional uses, home-occupation rules, ADU rules, overlay zones, and the local review path.
  - Adopted by the city or county under ORS 215 or 227 authority.

County or city GIS portals (Lane, Marion, Washington, Multnomah, Clackamas, Deschutes, Douglas, others).
  - Parcel identity, zoning layer, tax lot, overlays, mapped constraints, contour data.
  - Most are public and accessible without account creation.

Oregon DLCD: Department of Land Conservation and Development.
  - Statewide planning goals and statewide review of resource-land changes.
  - Background reference for understanding why certain rural zones are restrictive.
```

## 3.4 Property Rights, Title, And Recorded Restrictions

Buying land does not automatically include every right associated with it. Several rights are routinely separated from the surface estate, and several recorded restrictions can sit on a parcel without showing up in casual marketing materials.

**Applied**

Before closing, review the preliminary title report and the recorded property records for each item below.

| Right or restriction | What to verify |
|---|---|
| Access easements | Legal and physical access from a public road; obligations to maintain the access |
| Utility easements | Routes, widths, limits on planting or building over them |
| Road maintenance agreements | Cost-sharing rules, maintenance responsibility, dispute mechanisms |
| Mineral rights | Whether oil, gas, and hard-rock mineral rights are owned with the surface |
| Timber and forestry rights | Whether standing timber and future cut rights transfer with the land |
| Water rights | Surface, spring, and in some cases groundwater rights are separate from the surface estate under Oregon's prior-appropriation doctrine |
| Riparian rights | Rights and obligations related to creeks, rivers, ponds, and lakes touching or crossing the parcel |
| Hunting and fishing rights | Sometimes deeded separately, especially adjacent to timber-company holdings |
| Air rights and height limits | Restrictions on building height, antennas, wind equipment |
| Grazing rights | Where adjacent to federal land, these may attach to the parcel |
| Conservation easements | Recorded permanent restrictions on certain uses; bind future owners |
| Covenants, conditions, and restrictions (CC&Rs) | Private subdivision or HOA rules that may control building style, materials, or uses |
| Liens, judgments, recorded claims | Any encumbrance affecting transfer, financing, or use |

If the parcel does not directly touch a public road, slow down. Confirm legal access, physical access, road-maintenance responsibility, emergency access, and delivery access. A legal easement on paper is not the same as a road that can carry a fully loaded concrete truck in February.

Walk the access route in person. Drive it in poor weather if possible.

**Professional**

Title review and site feasibility belong together. A parcel can be physically buildable and still be constrained by easements, severed rights, private covenants, conservation restrictions, road-maintenance obligations, water-rights limits, or a survey gap. The preliminary title report, assessor records, water-rights records, seller disclosures, survey, road agreement, and county records all function as source documents for the feasibility decision.

A few specific issues that show up regularly:

- **Severed mineral rights.** Older Oregon parcels (especially in former timber-company areas and areas with historic mining activity) may have mineral rights owned by a separate party. The surface owner cannot prevent reasonable access to extract the minerals.
- **Severed timber rights.** Standing-timber rights may have been sold separately. The surface owner can prevent harvesting only if those rights have expired or been bought back.
- **Water rights ambiguity.** Oregon uses prior-appropriation doctrine. A well permit for domestic use is usually available, but irrigation water rights require a specific allocation. A spring on the property is not automatically usable as a water source; the right may belong to a downstream holder.
- **Road maintenance disputes.** Private-road agreements that look benign in summer can become contentious in winter when one neighbor wants to plow and another does not want to pay.
- **Conservation easements.** Often signed by a previous owner for tax benefits. They run with the land and bind every future owner. Read the easement carefully; some prohibit any new construction.

**Code**

```
ORS Chapter 93: conveyancing and recording framework for deeds and recorded property interests.
  - Establishes the legal effect of recording, the priority of recorded interests, and the basic conveyance rules.

County clerk or recorder records.
  - Deeds, easements, covenants, liens, plats, and recorded restrictions.
  - Searchable by parcel or grantor/grantee name.

Preliminary title report.
  - Working index of recorded matters that may affect ownership or use.
  - Standard part of any purchase transaction; review carefully before contingencies expire.

Oregon Water Resources Department records (oregon.gov/owrd).
  - Water rights and well-record research where private water supply is part of the plan.
  - Domestic well permits, irrigation rights, and groundwater registration.

Oregon Department of Forestry.
  - Timber-related encumbrances where applicable.
  - Forest practice rules (harvest restrictions, riparian setbacks).

BLM records (blm.gov).
  - Federal mineral-rights research where the parcel is adjacent to federal land or has historic federal ownership.

ORS 105.700–105.795: Oregon recreational immunity statute.
  - Background reference if neighbors or the public access the property recreationally.
```

## 3.5 Utilities And Access

The cost of bringing utilities to a remote parcel can rival the cost of the land itself.

**Applied**

Verify each system directly with the provider. Coverage maps and listing descriptions are not adequate substitutes for a direct quote.

| System | Questions to confirm in writing |
|---|---|
| Electrical service | Distance to the nearest transformer or service point; extension cost estimate; utility company (PGE, Pacific Power, local cooperative); service-entrance and metering requirements; voltage and ampacity available |
| Water | Whether municipal water is available, or whether a well must be drilled; if a well exists, yield, recovery, and water-quality test results; nearby well depths and yields if known; legal water-use limits |
| Wastewater | Whether sewer is available; if not, whether the parcel has passed or can pass an on-site septic site evaluation |
| Internet | Which providers serve the exact address; the speed actually deliverable, not the speed advertised; installation cost; latency and reliability if known |
| Road access | Public or private; who maintains it; whether trucks reach the site year-round; bridge and culvert weight limits; bridge and overhead clearance limits |
| Natural gas | Whether available and at what cost; the design may not require it, but the answer affects future options and resale |
| Cellular service | Carrier coverage at the building site (not at the property line); voice and data; signal strength on multiple carriers |

Save names, dates, and written answers in the project binder.

A practical rule of thumb for utility extension costs in 2026 Oregon dollars: budget approximately $15–$30 per foot for overhead electrical extension to a new transformer, $20–$50 per foot for underground extension, and $5,000–$25,000 minimum for a new transformer if one is needed. Internet fiber drop costs vary wildly; some providers extend for free, others charge $1,000–$10,000+. Always get a written quote.

**Professional**

Utility feasibility sets early geometry. Service routes, transformer location, and trenching paths affect the layout. Water source affects pump curve, pressure, storage, treatment, and backup-power planning. Wastewater feasibility affects the location of the building before the floor plan is committed. Access feasibility affects concrete delivery, pump-truck setup, excavation equipment, reinforcement delivery, waterproofing schedules, and emergency response.

The site must also support construction logistics. The Build Sequence And Site Coordination chapter handles the equipment and staging detail; for the early feasibility pass, confirm that:

- a concrete truck can enter, position, and turn around without backing into traffic
- a concrete pump truck can set up where its boom can reach the work
- a lowboy or equipment trailer can deliver an excavator and compaction equipment
- block, rebar, roofing material, septic tanks, windows, and other staged material have a safe location that does not block the road
- emergency vehicles can reach the house

A parcel that looks excellent in aerial photography can still be a poor construction site.

**Code**

```
Oregon utility-extension rules.
  - Each investor-owned utility (PGE, Pacific Power) and each cooperative has its own published extension policy and rate schedule.
  - PGE Schedule 300 and Pacific Power line-extension policies set the cost-recovery formula for new service extensions.

Local road-access permit rules.
  - Driveway connection to a county or state road requires a permit from the county road department or ODOT.
  - Sight distance, grade, and drainage requirements apply.

ORS Chapter 374: county road authority.
  - Background framework for county road-access decisions.

Oregon ePermitting: oregon.gov/bcd/epermitting.
  - State-supported online permit portal used by many cities and counties.
  - The land-use, building, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical permits route through this system in participating jurisdictions.
```

## 3.6 Septic And Water Feasibility

When sewer is unavailable, the parcel needs a septic system. When municipal water is unavailable, the parcel needs a well or another approved water source. Either question can stop the project. These are the two checks most likely to convert an attractive parcel into a dealbreaker.

**Applied**

If sewer is not available, make the offer contingent on a satisfactory septic site evaluation by the local onsite-program authority. The evaluation must answer:

- whether the soil can drain at acceptable rates
- whether there is enough usable area for the primary system
- whether there is a reserve area for replacement
- the type of system required (standard gravity, pressure-distribution, sand filter, ATT, ETPS, or other approved system)
- the required setbacks from wells, surface water, slopes, property lines, and structures
- whether the system fits alongside the planned house location, driveway, drainage features, and any future outbuildings

Site evaluation cost is typically $500–$1,200 in 2026 Oregon dollars depending on county and complexity. The cost is small relative to the consequences of buying an unbuildable parcel.

If municipal water is not available, investigate the well path before closing.

| Item | Source |
|---|---|
| Existing well log | Oregon Water Resources Department records (oregon.gov/owrd) |
| Well yield and recovery | Existing log or new draw-down test |
| Static water level and drawdown | Existing log or new test |
| Water quality test results | Recent lab test from an accredited Oregon lab |
| Nearby well depths and yields | OWRD database for adjacent parcels |
| Legal water-use limits | Oregon water-rights records and OWRD guidance |
| Setback conflicts | Local septic, well, and building-setback rules |

Draw the house, driveway, septic primary area, septic reserve area, well, utilities, and drainage features on the same site sketch before committing the building location. Do not place the house first and force water and wastewater into leftover space.

**Professional**

Septic feasibility integrates soil texture, percolation behavior, the limiting layer (a clay seam, water table, or rock that controls drainage), seasonal high groundwater, slope, drainfield sizing, reserve-area sizing, and setback distances into a single site layout. Water feasibility integrates well yield, pump curve, static water level, drawdown, storage volume, treatment, pressure regulation, freeze protection, and backup power into the same plan.

Cost ranges in 2026 Oregon dollars to anchor decisions:

| System | Typical cost range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Standard gravity septic | $8,000–$15,000 | Suitable soils, gravity drainfield |
| Pressure-distribution septic | $12,000–$25,000 | Marginal soils requiring even distribution |
| Sand filter, ATT, ETPS | $20,000–$50,000 | Difficult soils or shallow groundwater |
| Standard well (Willamette Valley) | $8,000–$20,000 | Shallow water table, moderate depth |
| Deeper well (Cascade foothills) | $15,000–$45,000 | Greater depth, harder rock |
| Storage and pump system | $3,000–$10,000 | Storage tank, pressure tank, pump, plumbing |
| Water treatment | $1,500–$8,000+ | Iron, manganese, hardness, bacteria treatment |

The septic primary and reserve areas are protected infrastructure on the site, not leftover land for landscaping or outbuildings.

**Code**

```
OAR Chapter 340 Division 71: Oregon onsite wastewater treatment and disposal rules.
  - Adopts the technical and procedural rules for septic systems statewide.
  - Administered by DEQ; often delegated to county environmental health departments.

OAR 340-071-0600: sewage-disposal-service licensing.
  - Installer licensing requirement.
  - Property-owner exception: a property owner may install a system on their own property under a properly issued construction permit.

ORS 537.747: water-supply well constructor licensing framework.
  - Establishes the licensing requirement for commercial well drilling.

OAR 690-205-0175: landowner well permit path.
  - Available to a property owner who is not a licensed well constructor.
  - Construction must meet the same OAR 690-200 through 690-240 standards as licensed drillers.
  - Permit valid for 6 months; bond required.

OAR 690-200 through 690-240: water-supply-well construction standards.
  - Drilling, casing, sealing, abandonment, and well-log requirements.

Local onsite program or county sanitarian.
  - Site evaluation, system approval, pre-cover inspection, and certificate of satisfactory completion where applicable.
  - Local administration varies; some counties contract back to DEQ for site evaluations.

OAR Chapter 333 Division 61: Oregon Health Authority drinking water rules.
  - Apply if the well serves more than the owner's household (rare for a single-family home).

ORS 197.015 through 197.460: Oregon land-use planning and DLCD framework.
  - Background context for understanding why certain rural zones constrain building sites.
```

## 3.7 Topography, Drainage, And Buildability

Slope and drainage affect water management, truck access, foundation type, and erosion-control obligations.

**Applied**

Walk the site after rain if possible. Look for:

- where surface water flows and pools
- soft ground or springs
- erosion in driveway routes, swales, and slopes
- low spots near the planned building area
- uphill water aimed at the future house
- evidence of old grading or fill (different soil colors, debris, abandoned terraces, rip-rap)
- room for excavator setup, concrete delivery, pump-truck position, and material staging
- root systems that suggest seasonal saturation
- vegetation patterns (water-loving species in low spots, drought-stressed species on dry slopes)

Use topographic maps and county GIS data before the visit. Trust the in-person walk more than the map. A contour line does not show standing water on the surface.

**Professional**

Topography drives cut and fill, positive drainage strategy, retaining-wall demand, driveway grade, erosion-control planning, staging-area selection, waterproofing demand, flood exposure, and foundation complexity. A simple rectangular footprint on a contour map can become stepped footings, tall retaining walls, imported fill, difficult compaction, or a driveway that fails in winter.

A few specific topographic patterns deserve attention:

- **Slope orientation.** A south-facing slope is preferred for solar gain in winter. A north-facing slope is colder, darker, and slower to dry after rain. East and west slopes are intermediate.
- **Cold-air pooling.** Valley sites and basin sites can pool cold air on still winter nights, producing local microclimate temperatures 5–15 °F colder than the regional design value. This affects heating-system sizing and freeze-protection planning.
- **Wind exposure.** Ridgeline sites get more wind, which increases lateral loads and air infiltration. Valley sites get less wind but may pool cold air or smoke.
- **Flood exposure.** Even properties not in a mapped flood zone may have local drainage that floods in extreme events. Ask neighbors.
- **Old grading.** Visible terraces, rip-rap, or unusual contours often indicate prior earthwork. Investigate before relying on the soil at footing depth.

For a concrete house, staging is part of feasibility. Concrete trucks, pump-truck setup, excavation, reinforcement, and waterproofing work all require physical space that a steeply sloped or constrained site may not provide. The Soil, Ground Prep, Drainage, And Foundation Risk chapter takes the geotechnical side once the parcel is under serious consideration.

**Code**

```
ORSC R401.3: surface drainage around foundations.
  - Required slope: 6 inches in the first 10 feet from the foundation.
  - Surface water must be directed away from the building.
  - Where 10 feet is not available, swales or drains are required.

ORSC R403.1.7: footings on or adjacent to slopes.
  - Setback from descending slope: minimum 1H:1V slope setback measured on an imaginary 45° plane from the toe.
  - Greater setback may be required where soil conditions warrant.

ORSC R322: flood-resistant construction.
  - Applies in Special Flood Hazard Areas as mapped by FEMA and adopted by the local jurisdiction.
  - Foundation, elevation, and venting requirements differ from standard construction.

Local grading, erosion-control, driveway, stormwater, and floodplain rules.
  - Each jurisdiction adopts its own permit requirements for grading and erosion control.
  - DEQ 1200-C permit applies to disturbances over 1 acre.

USGS topographic data (usgs.gov).
  - Free 1/3 arc-second digital elevation model for Oregon, suitable for early feasibility.

FEMA flood maps (msc.fema.gov).
  - Authoritative source for flood-hazard mapping; the local jurisdiction may have adopted older or revised mapping.
```

## 3.8 Distance And Logistics

Distance affects how the project actually gets built and how daily life works during and after construction.

**Applied**

Drive the route to the site before committing. Check it for:

- hardware and material runs
- grocery and basic-services trips
- medical access
- emergency response time
- winter road conditions, ice, and snow
- steep grades, sharp curves, and bridge/culvert weight limits
- delivery-truck clearance (overhead, width, weight)
- concrete-truck and pump-truck access
- internet and cellular service along the route

Drive the route in poor weather if possible. The condition of a rural road in summer is not predictive of its condition in January.

**Professional**

Distance affects labor productivity, subcontractor willingness to travel, delivery cost, inspection scheduling, emergency response, utility options, the resale pool for the finished home, and owner fatigue during the build. The more remote the site, the more important staging discipline becomes: extra fittings, backup tools, weather-protection supplies, temporary power, temporary water, fuel storage, and clearly scheduled deliveries.

Trade-offs by distance band, in 2026 Oregon dollars and time:

| Distance to nearest hardware/services | Effect on the build |
|---|---|
| Under 15 minutes | Easy material runs; emergency response minutes; higher land cost; less privacy; more restrictive zoning likely |
| 15–30 minutes | Reasonable runs; faster than rural; medium land cost; reasonable privacy; usually buildable zoning |
| 30–60 minutes | Each missing fitting is a half-day; cheaper land; significant privacy; more flexible zoning often available |
| Over 60 minutes | Plan around scarce trips; expect to keep a much larger inventory of fasteners, fittings, and consumables; emergency response can be 30+ minutes; cheapest land; most flexibility |

For an owner-builder whose schedule already runs against the clock, distance is rarely a dealbreaker but is almost always a design constraint on staging discipline and inventory planning.

## 3.9 The Purchase-Decision Review

Before the offer becomes binding, sort each issue by severity.

| Severity | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Dealbreaker | Do not buy unless solved before closing | No legal dwelling path, failed septic evaluation with no alternative system, no legal access, unusable water source, prohibitive recorded restriction |
| Expensive risk | Possible, but the price or uncertainty must be carried into the offer | Long utility extension, major driveway work, difficult drainage, uncertain title issue, marginal septic with expensive alternative system |
| Design constraint | The house must adapt | Slope, snow load, wind exposure, solar orientation, staging limits, floodplain |
| Lifestyle tradeoff | The land works, but daily life changes | Long drive, weak internet, winter access, distance from services, cellular dead zones |
| Future-flexibility check | Initial plan supported, future plans may not be | Outbuilding limits, ADU rules, home-occupation limits, equipment storage, business-use restrictions |

Then confirm each of the following before signing:

- zoning allows the intended primary residence
- parcel size meets the zoning minimum
- setbacks leave room for the house, driveway, septic, well, drainage, and staging
- height limits accommodate the intended house
- septic feasibility is confirmed if sewer is unavailable
- water source is confirmed
- electrical service is available, or extension cost is acceptable
- road access is legal, physical, and usable year-round
- internet service is acceptable for the project and for daily use
- title report does not reveal a dealbreaker
- easements, conservation restrictions, and CC&Rs are understood and acceptable
- mineral, timber, and water rights are understood
- floodplain and drainage exposure are acceptable
- climate design values can be obtained for the parcel
- topography supports excavation, drainage, staging, and delivery
- solar orientation works if solar is part of the plan
- distance to town, services, and emergency response is acceptable
- outbuilding, ADU, and home-occupation rules fit the long-term plan

If a risk cannot be priced, permitted, engineered, insured, accessed, or inspected, treat it as a dealbreaker until proven otherwise.

A parcel that looks excellent on listing photos can still be the wrong site for a long-life concrete house. The cost of a wrong land purchase shows up in every chapter that follows: harder foundation, worse drainage, more expensive utilities, longer drive for every mistake. The cost of a few weeks of careful evaluation is small relative to the cost of building on the wrong lot.
