# Chapter 19: Testing, Commissioning, And Quality Control

This chapter covers how the house proves itself: which tests are required and by whom, what the owner-builder may test versus what a certified party must, the concrete and soil testing framework, the plumbing and electrical tests, the envelope and energy tests, HVAC and smart-home commissioning, radon and water-quality testing, the owner's commissioning plan, and the records the program produces. The system-specific values (mix design, pressures, R-values, flows) live in the trade chapters; this chapter is the testing and commissioning program that ties them together.

Two framing points control the chapter. First, Oregon's code structure is not the IRC's: the ORSC adopts the residential mechanical and energy chapters but not the plumbing and electrical chapters, so plumbing tests are cited to the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code (OPSC) and electrical tests to the Oregon Electrical Specialty Code (OESC), never to ORSC plumbing or electrical sections. Second, several tests have no standing unless a certified party performs them, and two tasks (refrigerant work and backflow-assembly testing) are gated by federal or state certification regardless of the owner-builder exemption. The chapter is written to keep the owner's role inside its lawful boundary.

## 19.1 Scope And The Testing Program

Testing checks assumptions before the work is buried, commissioning proves the finished systems work together, and quality control keeps the build from drifting from the approved design. Every test is scheduled early enough that its result can still change the work, and every test produces a record.

**Simple**

Testing answers one question: did the house do what the design said it would? The answer is found early, while the work is still reachable, not at move-in. Some tests the owner-builder can run; others only count when a certified technician or an accredited lab performs them, and the plan respects that line.

**Applied**

Run the program across the tracks, and record every result:

| Track | What it checks |
|---|---|
| Soil and site | Bearing and drainage screening (design values require a geotech) |
| Concrete | Acceptance testing where required (by a certified agency) |
| Plumbing | Drain-waste-vent, supply, shower pan, gas, and backflow |
| Electrical | Polarity, protection devices, grounding, and labeling |
| Envelope and energy | Air leakage and duct leakage on the chosen compliance path |
| HVAC | Airflow, charge, ventilation balance, and controls (charge by a certified tech) |
| Radon and water | Radon test and well-water panel |
| Smart home | Local operation, fallback, alerts, and backups |
| Occupancy | Whole-house readiness and the records |

**Professional**

The program distinguishes tests the owner performs, tests a certified party performs, and diagnostics that guide fixing but do not establish compliance. It sequences the tests to the build (pre-cover tests before drywall, code tests before the certificate of occupancy, and post-occupancy tests after move-in), and it feeds every result into the commissioning binder. The inspection gates that witness the code tests are in the inspection chapter; this chapter is the program that prepares for them and adds the commissioning and monitoring the code does not require.

**Code**

```
ORSC adopts the residential mechanical (Chapters 12–24) and energy (Chapter 11) chapters, not the plumbing or electrical chapters; cite plumbing tests to the OPSC and electrical tests to the OESC.
Tests classified by who performs them: owner-performed, certified-party-required, and diagnostics (not compliance).
Every result recorded in the commissioning binder (see the materials and documentation chapters).
```

## 19.2 Which Tests Are Required, And By Whom

Most testing on a prescriptive single-family house is limited, and much of what people assume is required is condition-triggered or optional. The required-by-source matrix keeps the program honest.

**Applied**

Sort every test by its source:

- Code-mandated: the OPSC plumbing tests (drain-waste-vent, supply, shower pan), the gas-piping pressure test, the smoke and CO alarm function check, and the energy-path tests where a testing option or a testing-based measure is chosen.
- Condition-triggered: concrete acceptance testing only where the engineer, the plans, or the building official requires it; a soils investigation only where the official determines weak or questionable soils are likely; duct leakage only where ducts leave the conditioned envelope; radon-resistant construction only in the seven mandated counties.
- Utility- or provider-required: the backflow test on the water connection and the utility service verification.
- Manufacturer-required (warranty-gating): water-heater and heat-pump startup and commissioning per the product instructions.
- Building-official discretion: any test the official requires as evidence of compliance where the evidence is otherwise insufficient.

**Professional**

The building official's authority to require a test at the permit holder's expense (ORSC R104.11.1) is the legal hook behind every "the inspector asked for a test" situation, and per-county practice varies, so the project confirms the county's testing expectations and its chosen energy-compliance path at the design pass. The structural engineer's general notes, where the project uses engineered concrete or masonry, override every default in this chapter and name the tests, frequencies, and testing agency directly.

**Code**

```
Code-mandated: OPSC plumbing tests, gas-piping pressure test, smoke/CO function, energy-path tests where the testing option is chosen.
Condition-triggered: concrete (engineer/plans/official), soils (official), duct leakage (ducts outside the envelope), radon (seven mandated counties).
ORSC R104.11.1: building official may require tests as evidence of compliance at the permit holder's expense.
The structural engineer's general notes override the defaults and control the testing program for engineered elements.
```

## 19.3 The Owner-Builder Testing Boundary

Some tests only have standing when a certified technician or an accredited lab performs them, and two tasks are gated by certification the owner-builder exemption does not waive. The owner's role on those is scheduling, access, and support, not running the test.

**Applied**

- Do not treat owner-performed concrete slump, air, or cylinder results as acceptance; a certified agency performs acceptance testing, and the owner provides the specimen curing environment.
- Do not treat any do-it-yourself soil test as a design value; screening informs whether to call a geotechnical engineer.
- Hire an EPA Section 608-certified technician for all refrigerant work, including connecting a mini-split line set, attaching gauges, and charging.
- Hire a certified backflow-assembly tester for the backflow test at installation and annually.
- Keep every owner-performed test to de-energized work or a device's own listed test feature.

**Professional**

Owner-performed concrete tests have no acceptance standing because acceptance testing requires an ASTM C1077-conforming agency with certified field and lab technicians (ACI 318 §26.12.1.1); the owner's job on an engineered shell is the ASTM C31 initial-curing logistics and the pour scheduling. Do-it-yourself soil screening (ribbon, jar, hand-auger, pocket penetrometer, dynamic cone) is uniformity and red-flag screening under the visual-manual practice, never a source of bearing values, settlement predictions, expansive-soil parameters, seismic site class, or a liquefaction conclusion. Refrigerant work is gated by EPA Section 608 under 40 CFR 82 Subpart F, and backflow testing by the state certified-tester rule; both are routed to hired certified parties, which also preserves the equipment warranties that reference proper commissioning.

**Code**

```
Concrete acceptance: ASTM C1077 agency, ACI-certified technicians, ACI 318 §26.12.1.1; owner performs ASTM C31 curing logistics only.
Soil: do-it-yourself methods are screening under ASTM D2488; design values require an approved agency (see the soil chapter).
EPA Section 608 (40 CFR 82 Subpart F): refrigerant work, including mini-split line-set connection and charging, by a certified technician.
Backflow-assembly testing: certified tester at installation and annually (Oregon certified-tester rule; confirm the current cite).
```

## 19.4 Concrete Acceptance Testing

Concrete acceptance testing is a certified-technician, accredited-lab activity that is usually not required on a prescriptive house and is invoked by the engineer, the plans, or the building official. Where it applies, the owner's role is the curing environment and the schedule.

**Professional**

Where acceptance testing is required, the testing agency conforms to ASTM C1077 (typically accredited, under a professional engineer's direction), the field technician holds the ACI Concrete Field Testing Technician Grade I credential, and the lab technician holds the corresponding certification (ACI 318 §26.12.1.1 and ACI 301). The field tests in that scope are ASTM C172 sampling, C1064 temperature, C143 slump, C138 density and yield, C231 or C173 air content, and C31 specimen making, with C39 compressive strength (and C42 cores where a question arises) at the lab. Acceptance cylinders are standard-cured per C31 with the initial on-site curing temperature controlled and recorded; field-cured cylinders inform form-removal and loading decisions, not acceptance. Testing errors on C31 and C39 read almost always low, which is why the accredited-lab quality standard matters. On an engineered shell the agency is named in the engineer's general notes and budgeted per pour, and the owner provides the secure, temperature-controlled initial-curing box. The mix design, exposure class, and target values are in the structural and foundation chapters.

**Code**

```
ASTM C1077: concrete testing agency requirements; ACI-certified field (Grade I) and lab technicians (ACI 318 §26.12.1.1, ACI 301).
Field tests: ASTM C172, C1064, C143, C138, C231/C173, C31; lab: C39 (and C42 cores as needed). Confirm current editions before normative use.
Acceptance cylinders standard-cured per C31; field-cured cylinders for form-removal/loading only.
Usually not required on a prescriptive ORSC house; invoked by engineer, plans, or building official.
```

## 19.5 Soil And Geotechnical Testing

Prescriptive foundation design rests on the code's presumptive bearing values, and any value above them or any settlement, site-class, or liquefaction determination is a professional geotechnical activity. Do-it-yourself screening tells the owner whether to call a geotechnical engineer, not what the soil will carry.

**Professional**

ORSC and IRC R401.4 leave the decision to require a soils investigation to the building official where expansive, compressible, or shifting soils are indicated, with the test by an approved agency and method; otherwise the presumptive values of Table R401.4.1 apply (about 1,500 psf for clay and silt). Where the seismic design category is C or higher and soil testing is performed, the geotechnical report includes the seismic site class, which is directly relevant in western Oregon. The professional tests are the visual-manual and laboratory soil classification (ASTM D2488 and D2487), Atterberg limits (D4318), the Proctor compaction reference (D698 or D1557), field density (D1556 sand cone or D6938 nuclear gauge), and the dynamic cone (D6951), with editions confirmed before normative use. Do-it-yourself screening supports using the conservative presumptive row, flags the conditions (plastic clays, organics, uncontrolled fill, groundwater) that require the geotechnical call, and documents conditions for the pre-pour inspection; compaction acceptance itself requires a laboratory Proctor plus certified field density testing. The soil chapter carries the screening procedures and the red-flag list.

**Code**

```
ORSC/IRC R401.4 and Table R401.4.1: presumptive bearing values (about 1,500 psf clay/silt); soils investigation at the building official's determination, by an approved agency and method.
Professional standards: ASTM D2488/D2487 (classification), D4318 (Atterberg), D698/D1557 (Proctor), D1556/D6938 (field density), D6951 (dynamic cone); confirm editions.
Do-it-yourself methods are screening only; no design value, settlement, site class, or liquefaction conclusion.
```

## 19.6 Plumbing Testing

Plumbing tests are held under the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code, and the drain-waste-vent, supply, shower-pan, and gas tests are witnessed at the rough and final inspections. Plastic drainage is never air-tested.

**Applied**

- Test the drain-waste-vent rough with a 10-foot water head above the highest fitting for 15 minutes (or an approved air test); do not air-test plastic drainage.
- Test the water supply at working pressure or 50 psi for 15 minutes, with the gauge holding when the inspector arrives, and install a pressure-reducing valve where static pressure exceeds 80 psi.
- Test the shower pan by plugging the drain and holding at least 2 inches of water for 15 minutes.
- Test gas piping at 1.5 times working pressure (at least 3 psi for natural gas) for the required interval.
- Have a certified tester test the backflow assembly at installation and annually.
- Commission the water heater per the manufacturer (fill before power or fire, purge, check the temperature-and-pressure relief valve, set the temperature, and precharge the expansion tank).

**Professional**

The OPSC (2021 UPC base) sets the drain-waste-vent test (UPC 712), the supply test (UPC 609), and the shower-pan test, and it prohibits air-testing plastic drainage because a brittle failure under air is explosive. Backflow assemblies are tested at installation, after any repair or relocation, and annually, by a state-certified tester under the drinking-water rules. Water-heater and pump startup follow the manufacturer's model-specific procedures, which are collected at specification time and gate the warranty. The inspector witnesses the code tests; the owner's binder keeps the timestamped gauge photographs and the pass record. The supply pressures, pipe sizing, and treatment detail are in the water-supply chapter, and the drainage detail in the drain-waste-vent chapter.

**Code**

```
OPSC (2021 UPC): drain-waste-vent test (UPC 712, 10-foot water head or approved air test, 15 minutes; plastic drainage not air-tested); supply test (UPC 609, working pressure or 50 psi, 15 minutes); shower-pan test (2 inches of water, 15 minutes).
Pressure-reducing valve where static pressure exceeds 80 psi.
Gas piping pressure test (1.5 times working, at least 3 psi natural gas); confirm the Oregon fuel-gas adoption.
Backflow: certified tester at installation and annually; water-heater startup per manufacturer.
```

## 19.7 Electrical Testing

Electrical testing is cited to the Oregon Electrical Specialty Code, and the owner's test set is limited to de-energized work and a device's own listed test feature. Anything requiring opening energized equipment is a hired-electrician task.

**Applied**

The owner's defensible electrical test set:

- Verify receptacle polarity and ground on every device with a tester.
- Verify GFCI and AFCI protection with the device's own TEST button (a plug-in tester does not reliably trip an AFCI), and repeat monthly per the manufacturer.
- Check voltage at the panel and the farthest outlets, and the continuity of grounding and bonding connections.
- Verify the panel-directory accuracy and the smoke and CO alarm interconnection function.
- Torque terminations to the manufacturer's value with a calibrated tool.

Leave earth-resistance testing, thermal panel scans under load, and any test that opens energized equipment to a hired electrician.

**Professional**

The OESC (NEC base) governs, and the owner-level tests are on de-energized work or the listed test features. The grounding-electrode requirement is met without earth-resistance testing by driving the supplemental rod under the two-rod alternative (NEC 250.53(A)(2)), which is the practical residential answer; actual electrode testing uses professional equipment. Torque of terminations follows the calibrated-torque requirement (NEC 110.14(D)), and the panel directory follows the circuit-identification requirement (NEC 408.4). Insulation-resistance (megger) testing is not a residential code requirement. Structured cabling is certified to the TIA-568 family with a cable certifier where resale documentation is wanted; a function verifier suffices otherwise. The service, grounding, and branch-circuit detail is in the electrical chapters, and confirm the adopted OESC edition and section numbers at permit time.

**Code**

```
OESC (NEC base): owner tests on de-energized work or listed test features.
NEC 250.53(A)(2): two-rod alternative avoids earth-resistance testing; NEC 110.14(D) calibrated-torque; NEC 408.4 panel directory.
GFCI/AFCI verified with the device TEST button (plug-in testers do not reliably trip AFCIs); monthly per manufacturer.
Energized testing (electrode testing, thermal scans) is a hired-electrician task. Confirm the adopted OESC edition.
```

## 19.8 Envelope And Energy Testing

The envelope air-leakage test is one of two compliance options, not an unconditional statewide mandate, and the chosen compliance path determines whether a blower-door test is required at all. Duct leakage is tested where ducts leave the conditioned envelope.

**Applied**

- Determine the energy-compliance path at design; air sealing is met either by the prescriptive sealing criteria or by a blower-door test at or below the path target.
- Where the testing path is chosen, have an approved third-party tester run the multi-point blower-door test, and file the result on the state form before the certificate of occupancy.
- Test duct leakage where any duct runs outside the conditioned envelope, and design ducts and the air handler inside the envelope to avoid the test.
- Use smoke tracing and thermal imaging as diagnostics to find and fix leaks and insulation voids, not as compliance evidence.

**Professional**

The 2023 ORSC N1104.8.2 makes sealing compliant either by the Table N1104.8 criteria or by a blower-door result at or below 3.25 ACH50, and a separate Additional Measure is earned by a third-party-verified 3.0 ACH50 with heat-recovery ventilation; the code text controls over secondary sources that describe testing as an unconditional mandate, and local reach codes may set a lower target. The test follows ASTM E779 or E1827 (and the RESNET-ICC air-leakage standard), with ACH50 computed as CFM50 times 60 divided by the conditioned volume, and the result filed on the BCD form. Field water-spray testing of windows (ASTM E1105) is a hired forensic tool, not a code requirement on a house; the code path is manufacturer-instruction flashing verified at inspection. Hot-box and heat-flux insulation tests are research-grade and are named and excluded. The envelope values and assemblies are in the envelope chapter.

**Code**

```
ORSC N1104.8.2: sealing by Table N1104.8 criteria OR a blower-door result at or below 3.25 ACH50; a 3.0 ACH50 + HRV Additional Measure is separate. The chosen path (and any reach code) determines whether a test is required.
Blower door: ASTM E779/E1827 by an approved third-party tester; result on the BCD form before the certificate of occupancy; ACH50 = CFM50 × 60 / conditioned volume.
ORSC N1103.3.3: duct leakage where ducts are outside the envelope.
ASTM E1105 window water-spray and hot-box/heat-flux tests: hired/research-grade, not code required on a house.
```

## 19.9 HVAC Commissioning

HVAC commissioning verifies that the installed system delivers its design performance, following the residential grading standard, with the refrigerant work performed by a certified technician. The owner installs everything mechanical except the refrigerant circuit.

**Applied**

- Verify the equipment was sized by a load calculation (Manual J) and selected within the oversize limit (Manual S), with the duct design to Manual D.
- Commission to the five grading-standard tasks: design review, total duct leakage, blower-fan airflow, blower-fan watt draw, and refrigerant-charge verification.
- Have the EPA-certified technician connect the line set, evacuate, charge, and verify the charge; the owner installs the rest.
- Balance the ventilation: verify the whole-house balanced ventilation rate, the heat- or energy-recovery balance to the manufacturer's tolerance, and the local exhaust flows with a flow box.
- Pressure-test hydronic and radiant tubing before and during the slab pour with the gauge visible, then purge, fill, and verify the temperature difference.
- Verify the condensate trap, slope, and overflow switch, and walk through the heat, cool, and auxiliary staging and the smart-thermostat configuration.

**Professional**

The residential commissioning standard is ANSI/ACCA/RESNET/ICC Standard 310, whose five tasks form the commissioning checklist, with ACCA Standard 5 as the quality-installation specification behind it (confirm the standard text before normative use). The refrigerant boundary is a bright line: any line-set connection, gauge attachment, charging, or recovery requires EPA Section 608 certification, with substantial daily penalties for uncertified work, so the owner installs everything mechanical and hires the certified technician for the refrigerant circuit and the charge verification, which also preserves the manufacturer's warranty. The ventilation rates, equipment selection, and radiant detail are in the HVAC chapter; this chapter is the commissioning that confirms the installation performs.

**Code**

```
ANSI/ACCA/RESNET/ICC Standard 310: residential HVAC commissioning; five tasks (design review, total duct leakage, blower airflow, blower watt draw, refrigerant charge). ACCA Standard 5 quality installation. Confirm standard text.
ACCA Manual J/S/D: load, equipment selection, duct design (ORSC M1401.3).
EPA Section 608: refrigerant connection, charging, and recovery by a certified technician; owner installs the rest.
Hydronic/radiant tubing pressure-tested before and during the slab pour, gauge visible.
```

## 19.10 Radon, Indoor Air, And Water Quality

Radon-resistant construction is required in seven Oregon counties and is inexpensive to rough in everywhere, and the house is tested for radon after occupancy. A new well gets a full water panel before the plumbing is commissioned.

**Applied**

- Build radon-resistant construction (a passive sub-slab vent rough-in) in the seven mandated counties, and rough it in elsewhere because it is inexpensive at build time.
- Test radon after occupancy with a closed-house test per the measurement standard (winter preferred), activate the passive system with a fan only if the result is at or above the action level, and retest periodically.
- Test a new well for the full panel (bacteria, nitrate, arsenic, and the site-appropriate items) before commissioning the house plumbing, using an accredited lab.
- Treat moisture and mold checks (crawl-space humidity, attic sheathing, window condensation) as maintenance-schedule observations, not tests.

**Professional**

Radon-resistant new construction applies in Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, and Yamhill counties under the ORSC-adopted radon appendix, and radon is measured per the ANSI/AARST closed-house protocol with the EPA action level at 4.0 pCi/L (mitigation considered at 2 to 4). Oregon does not license radon professionals and points to nationally certified providers. Well water is governed at sale by the Domestic Well Testing Act (ORS 448.271, OAR 333-061), which requires arsenic, nitrate, and total-coliform testing with results to the health authority and the buyer, valid one year; outside a sale the health authority recommends annual coliform and nitrate and periodic arsenic, with thresholds of 10 ppb arsenic, 10 mg/L nitrate, and coliform absent. The radon rough-in and the well-water treatment detail are in the envelope and water-supply chapters.

**Code**

```
Radon-resistant new construction: seven mandated counties (Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, Yamhill) per the ORSC radon appendix; radon test per ANSI/AARST, EPA action level 4.0 pCi/L.
ORS 448.271 and OAR 333-061: well-water testing (arsenic, nitrate, total coliform) at sale, valid one year; health-authority recommendations for ongoing testing (arsenic 10 ppb, nitrate 10 mg/L, coliform absent).
```

## 19.11 Smart-Home Commissioning

No residential code or consensus standard governs smart-home commissioning, so this is practice guidance, and its center is proving the house works with the internet disconnected. The detailed architecture and the full commissioning checklist are in the smart-home chapter; here it is one track of the whole-house program.

**Applied**

Commission the smart-home layer by test, not by assumption:

- Local operation: disconnect the wide-area connection and confirm every controlled load still works from a physical control.
- Automation fallback: confirm a manual switch or override for every automated device.
- Camera and lock behavior: confirm motion events record and retain, and confirm the mechanical key, power-loss behavior, and low-battery behavior on every lock.
- Backup power: measure the rack runtime under real load and confirm a clean automatic shutdown.
- Alerts and safety chains: confirm end-to-end notification for leak, freeze, smoke-interconnect, and power-loss events, and confirm the leak-sensor-to-shutoff-valve chain with a wet sensor.

**Professional**

The only citable anchors are the product listings and the consumer-IoT baseline guidance, so the smart-home commissioning is framed as practice guidance and recorded in the binder. It is the same internet-out, power-out, and local-control testing detailed in the smart-home chapter, run here as part of the whole-house commissioning so the "works without the internet" design is verified rather than assumed.

**Code**

```
No residential code or consensus commissioning standard for smart-home systems; practice guidance recorded in the binder.
Commissioning tests: local operation (internet disconnected), automation fallback, camera/lock behavior, backup runtime, and safety-chain alerts (see the smart-home chapter).
```

## 19.12 The Owner's Commissioning Plan And Records

The house is commissioned against an owner's plan assembled from the code tests, the HVAC grading tasks, the manufacturer startups, and borrowed program checklists used only as internal quality assurance. The plan claims no certification label it has not earned, and it produces the record set that carries into warranty, insurance, and resale.

**Applied**

Sequence the commissioning plan:

1. Pre-drywall: the rough plumbing and electrical tests, and photographs of the insulation and air barrier before cover.
2. Pre-occupancy: the final code tests, the filed energy forms, and the trade finals.
3. Move-in plus 30 and 90 days: the radon test, an indoor-air baseline, and system tuning.
4. One-year review: the seasonal tuning and the whole-binder review.

Keep, organized by system with dates, photos, and signatures: the inspection record and filed state forms, the plumbing and gas gauge photos, the concrete batch tickets and any lab reports, the soils report, the radon results, the well tests, the HVAC commissioning sheet (with the technician's EPA 608 number), the electrical panel directory and torque log, the product serials and warranties, and the pre-cover photographs of every concealed assembly.

**Professional**

Formal commissioning standards and certification programs (ENERGY STAR, DOE Zero Energy Ready, PHIUS, and HERS ratings) are sources of excellent checklists, but claiming their labels requires the program's own verifiers, so the owner's plan borrows the checklists as internal quality assurance and does not imply certification. Post-occupancy, the program continues as monitoring: an energy log compared seasonally, a water meter and leak-sensor check, indoor humidity kept roughly 30 to 60 percent, a radon retest on the recommended cycle and after any foundation or HVAC change, an annual well panel, and the routine equipment logs and safety-device checks. Oregon's well-test and radon-disclosure environment means these records convert directly into a smoother sale, and the closeout binder is retained per the materials and documentation chapters.

**Code**

```
Owner's commissioning plan: code-required tests + Standard 310 HVAC tasks + manufacturer startups + borrowed program checklists as internal QA; no certification label claimed without the program's verifier.
Sequence: pre-drywall, pre-occupancy, move-in +30/90 days, one-year review.
Records retained by system with dates/photos/signatures; well tests (valid one year) and radon results carry into resale disclosure (ORS 105.848).
```
