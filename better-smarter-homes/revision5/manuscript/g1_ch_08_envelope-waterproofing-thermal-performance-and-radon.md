# Chapter 8: Envelope, Waterproofing, Thermal Performance, And Radon

The envelope is the building shell that separates the conditioned interior from the exterior environment. For this project, the envelope spans the roof, the above-grade walls, the windows and doors, the slab edge, the below-grade walls, the foundation drainage interface, and every penetration through any of those surfaces.

The envelope handles five distinct jobs: rejecting bulk water, controlling air movement, managing vapor diffusion, controlling heat flow, and rejecting soil gas (radon). Each job is handled by a control layer that has to be continuous across the building. A control layer that stops at a corner, transition, or penetration creates a failure point that no amount of effort elsewhere can compensate for.

This chapter covers the five envelope control layers, the Oregon residential energy code framework that drives the thermal performance, the foam-plastic protection requirements that affect insulation choices in concrete and masonry assemblies, the radon-control provisions for the seven mandated Oregon counties, and the envelope testing program that verifies the work was done correctly.

The Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement chapter handles below-grade waterproofing in detail. The Structural System And Concrete Design chapter handles wall ties as structural elements. The HVAC, Ventilation, Humidity, And Air Quality chapter handles the mechanical ventilation that pairs with a tight envelope. This chapter integrates the envelope as a system across all of those interfaces.

## 8.1 The Five Envelope Jobs

The envelope handles five jobs. Each job has a dedicated control layer; each control layer has to be continuous across the building.

**Simple**

A house has to keep the rain out, keep the air controlled, keep the moisture in vapor form from condensing inside walls, keep the heat where it belongs, and keep gas from the soil out of the conditioned space. Each of those is a separate job, and each one is handled by a different layer in the wall, roof, or floor assembly. When all five layers are continuous from one part of the house to the next, the envelope works. When any one layer has a gap, water or air or moisture or heat or soil gas finds the gap.

**Professional: the five control layers**

| Job | Control layer | Typical material |
|---|---|---|
| Bulk water (rain, snow, groundwater, splashback) | Water-resistive barrier (WRB) on walls; roofing membrane on roofs; waterproofing membrane below grade | House wrap, fluid-applied WRB, peel-and-stick membrane; modified bitumen, EPDM, TPO, PVC; 60-mil peel-and-stick polymer-modified asphalt |
| Air | Air-control layer (continuous from foundation to roof) | Sealed sheathing, sealed drywall, sheet membranes (peel-and-stick), fluid-applied air barriers, foam taped at seams |
| Vapor | Vapor retarder (placement and perm rating depend on climate and assembly) | Class I (≤ 0.1 perm), Class II (≤ 1.0 perm), Class III (≤ 10 perm); polyethylene, smart membranes, vapor-retarder paint |
| Heat | Thermal insulation, continuous and complete | Mineral wool, XPS, EPS, polyiso, spray foam, cellulose, fiberglass; placement varies by assembly |
| Soil gas | Soil-gas retarder + passive subslab depressurization (radon counties) | 6-mil polyethylene retarder OR 15-mil ASTM E1745 Class A serving both vapor and soil-gas function; 3-inch passive vent pipe |

The five control layers are conceptually distinct. In practice, several layers can share materials. A peel-and-stick membrane can serve as both the WRB (bulk water) and the air-control layer. A 15-mil ASTM E1745 Class A vapor retarder serves as both vapor retarder and soil-gas retarder. A foam plastic insulation board serves both as thermal insulation and (with sealed seams) as part of the air-control layer.

**Continuity is non-negotiable**

Each layer must be traceable around the entire building cross-section. The pencil test: starting at any wall section, can a single line be drawn around the building (across all assemblies: slab, walls, roof) representing each control layer, without lifting the pencil at any transition or penetration? If not, that layer has a gap.

Common discontinuities:

- Slab-to-wall transition: where the wall sole plate meets the foundation
- Wall-to-roof transition: at the eave or parapet
- Window and door rough openings: head, jambs, sill
- Penetrations: pipes, conduits, vents, exhaust fans, hose bibs, condensate drains, structural ties
- Below-grade-to-above-grade transition: where the foundation wall meets above-grade construction
- Mechanical and electrical service entries: water service, electrical service, gas line, HVAC equipment

For each discontinuity, the project drawings should show the detail that maintains continuity. The Window and door rough opening detail in §8.10 illustrates the pattern.

## 8.2 Oregon Residential Energy Compliance

The thermal performance of the envelope is governed by ORSC Chapter 11 (residential energy provisions). The 2023 ORSC Chapter 11 became effective October 1, 2023 and mandatory April 1, 2024. The 2021 ORSC is no longer the minimum standard. The 2023 edition is based on the 2021 IRC and adopted via OAR 918-480-0005.

Confirm the adopted edition with the local jurisdiction at permit application; some jurisdictions phase in by chapter.

### 8.2.1 The Compliance Path

Every new dwelling must meet:

- the prescriptive envelope requirements in Table N1101.1(1) (or an equivalent UA tradeoff per Table N1104.1(1) for unusual assemblies), and
- at least one additional measure from Table N1101.1(2)

The additional measure is required by ORSC Section N1101.1 for all new construction. It is not optional.

| Path | Description |
|---|---|
| Standard prescriptive | Table N1101.1(1) values + one additional measure from Table N1101.1(2) |
| UA tradeoff | Table N1104.1(1) performance-based UA calculation; one additional measure still required from N1101.1(2) |
| Ducts outside thermal envelope (Exception 3 of N1105.3) | Two additional measures required from N1101.1(2) instead of one |

For a concrete house with a concrete roof or unusual envelope assemblies, the prescriptive path may not apply directly; the UA tradeoff path or another accepted performance path is the typical route. The UA tradeoff calculation lets a stronger-than-required value in one element compensate for a weaker-than-required value in another.

**Permit submittal:** Form 440-4854j (Residential Energy Additional Measure Selection) must be submitted with the permit application. The form declares which additional measure is being used. Permits are placed on hold until the form is submitted.

### 8.2.2 Table N1101.1(1) Prescriptive Envelope Requirements

The 2023 ORSC Table N1101.1(1) prescriptive envelope values, sourced from the BCD slab-on-grade technical bulletin which reprints the table verbatim:

| Assembly | Required performance | Equivalent value |
|---|---|---|
| Wall, above grade | U-0.059 | R-21 intermediate framing |
| Wall, below grade | C-0.063 | R-15 c.i. or R-21 |
| Flat ceiling | U-0.021 | R-49 |
| Vaulted ceiling | U-0.033 | R-30 rafter or R-30A scissor truss |
| Underfloor | U-0.033 | R-30 |
| Slab-edge perimeter | F-0.520 | R-15, 24-inch minimum depth |
| Heated slab interior | not specified at table value | R-10 entire underside |
| Windows | U-0.27 | n/a |
| Skylights | U-0.50 | n/a |
| Exterior doors | U-0.20 | n/a |
| Doors with > 2.5 sq ft glazing | U-0.40 | n/a |

Solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC): the 2023 ORSC Table N1101.1(1) does not set a mandatory SHGC value. SHGC remains a design choice that affects overheating, passive solar gain, glare, and cooling load. It should not be written as a statewide Oregon residential code minimum unless the adopted compliance path requires it.

### 8.2.3 Table N1101.1(2) Additional Measures

ORSC Table N1101.1(2) lists additional efficiency measures. At least one is required. Available measures include enhanced envelope (improved walls, ceiling, slab edge, or windows), enhanced air sealing with blower-door verification, ducts located inside the conditioned envelope, heat-recovery ventilation, high-efficiency heating equipment, high-efficiency water heating, solar thermal, and photovoltaic generation.

The most common selections for a concrete-and-reinforced-masonry house in Oregon:

- enhanced air sealing with blower-door test result ≤ 2.75 ACH50 plus heat recovery ventilation (Measure 7), well-aligned with a tight, mechanically ventilated envelope
- ducts located entirely inside the conditioned envelope (this is the default for many concrete-house designs where mechanical equipment lives in conditioned space)

Form 440-4854j lists the current measures with full performance criteria.

### 8.2.4 Concrete Roof Energy Compliance

A concrete roof creates a specific energy-compliance challenge. The prescriptive table values are calibrated for typical wood-framed roofs with attic insulation. A concrete deck has little inherent insulating value; the assembly's R-value comes almost entirely from the insulation placed above or below the deck.

For a concrete roof, the practical compliance paths:

- UA tradeoff (Table N1104.1(1)): submit a calculation showing the actual concrete-roof assembly with continuous insulation above the deck (typical 4–6 inches XPS or polyiso), demonstrating that the building's overall UA value meets or beats the prescriptive requirement
- performance-based path: submit a whole-building energy model showing compliance

Both paths require additional engineering work at permit time. The work is straightforward; the calculation tools are available from BCD.

For thermal-bridge accounting in a concrete roof, the deck itself is part of the heat-flow path. The total assembly UA includes the concrete deck plus the insulation above. With 4 inches of polyiso (R-25 nominal), the assembly approaches the prescriptive R-49 target after thermal-bridge derating; with 6 inches the assembly typically meets it cleanly.

## 8.3 Air Sealing And Blower-Door Testing

Air sealing controls the air-control layer. Blower-door testing verifies the result.

**Applied: air sealing scope**

The air-control layer is continuous around the conditioned space. Common air-sealing locations:

- band joist or rim joist at floor levels (if any wood framing exists in a hybrid concrete/wood assembly)
- top plate to ceiling drywall transition
- wall-to-roof transition at eaves and parapets
- penetrations: plumbing pipes, electrical conduit, HVAC ducts, exhaust fans, dryer vent, range hood, gas line, hose bibs
- window and door rough openings (head, jambs, sill)
- attic hatch
- mechanical equipment penetrations (water heater, furnace, AC condenser)
- electrical boxes on exterior walls (use airtight boxes or seal at the box-to-drywall interface)

Sealing materials:

- caulk: silicone, polyurethane, or acoustic sealant for permanent gap-and-crack sealing
- spray foam: expanding polyurethane for larger gaps and around penetrations
- sheet membrane: peel-and-stick at major transitions (window flashing, deck-to-wall, parapet)
- gaskets: at sill plates, electrical boxes, attic hatches

The sealing work is verified by the blower door test. Air leakage is measured in cubic feet per minute at 50 pascals pressure difference (CFM50), then divided by the conditioned volume in cubic feet times 60 to produce air changes per hour at 50 pascals (ACH50). The formula: ACH50 = (CFM50 × 60) / Conditioned Volume.

**Professional: Oregon thresholds**

| Threshold | Required by | Applies when |
|---|---|---|
| 3.25 ACH50 | ORSC N1104.8.2 (tested path for envelope air sealing) | When testing is used as the compliance path instead of the prescriptive air-sealing checklist in Table N1104.8 |
| 2.75 ACH50 | ORSC N1101.1(2) Additional Measure 7 | When Measure 7 is selected as the additional measure |

The standard sealing path under prescriptive Table N1104.8 does not require a blower-door test; the builder follows the air-sealing checklist instead. Where the project uses testing as the compliance route, 3.25 ACH50 is the pass threshold.

For Measure 7, the third-party-tested 2.75 ACH50 is paired with a heat-recovery ventilation system: minimum 66% sensible heat recovery efficiency, total fan efficacy of 1.6 CFM/Watt (combined supply and exhaust). The HVAC, Ventilation, Humidity, And Air Quality chapter handles HRV selection in detail.

**Test timing and documentation**

Blower-door test results must be provided to the local building official before the certificate of occupancy is issued where testing is part of the compliance path. The building has to be substantially complete and testable before the test can be run. Standard ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380 test protocol governs.

Measure 7 requires third-party testing. The test must be performed by someone other than the builder. The reporting form requires the testing company's CCB or EEAST number, technician name, and signature.

**Code**

```
ORSC N1104.8: air leakage.
  - N1104.8.2 testing: 3.25 ACH50 threshold.
  - Table N1104.8: air-sealing checklist (prescriptive sealing path, no test required).

ORSC N1101.1(2) Additional Measure 7: enhanced air sealing + HRV.
  - 2.75 ACH50 maximum, third-party tested.
  - HRV 66% minimum sensible heat recovery efficiency.
  - Total fan efficacy 1.6 CFM/Watt (combined supply and exhaust).

ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380: standard for testing airtightness of building enclosures.

BCD Blower Door Results Reporting Checklist (oregon.gov/bcd/Formslibrary/orsc-blower-door.pdf).

  - Requirements for submittal before CO.
```

## 8.4 Wall Insulation Strategy

The wall is the largest envelope surface. For a concrete or reinforced-masonry wall, insulation strategy is a design choice with implications for thermal performance, vapor drying, fire performance, and cost.

**Professional: placement options**

| Placement | Description | Strengths | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Continuous exterior insulation | Rigid foam (XPS, polyiso, mineral wool board) on the exterior of the structural wall, with rainscreen cladding outside the insulation | Best thermal performance; structural mass on the warm side; minimal thermal bridging at slab-to-wall and wall-to-roof transitions | Adds wall thickness; rainscreen cladding adds cost; fire-protection considerations for foam plastic |
| Insulation in the cavity (double-wythe walls) | Rigid foam, mineral wool, or spray foam between two structural wythes | Insulation is protected from weather and impact; slim wall section | Limited cavity width by tie-spacing rules; thermal bridging at ties; no path for double-wythe loadbearing walls under prescriptive code |
| Interior insulation | Rigid foam or spray foam on the interior face of the structural wall | Lowest cost; warm structural mass is unavailable for tempering; fastest install | Structural wall is on the cold side and exposed to thermal cycling; vapor management more critical |

For a long-life concrete-and-reinforced-masonry house in Oregon, continuous exterior insulation with rainscreen cladding is the recommended strategy. The structural wall stays in the conditioned-temperature zone, vapor migration is straightforward (drying is to both sides through the wall and through the rainscreen gap), and thermal bridging at transitions is minimized.

**Materials for continuous exterior insulation**

| Material | Strengths | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Rigid mineral wool (Rockwool ComfortBoard, Owens Corning Thermafiber) | Vapor-open, fire-resistant, no foam-plastic protection issue | Lower R-per-inch (~R-4.2/in.); more expensive than foam |
| XPS rigid foam (DOW, Owens Corning Foamular, Atlas) | Closed-cell, water-resistant, R-5/in. | Foam-plastic protection requirements in some assemblies; long-term R-value reduction (off-gassing) |
| Polyiso rigid foam (Hunter, GAF, Atlas) | High R-per-inch (~R-6/in. nominal; ~R-5/in. derated for cold) | R-value reduces in cold weather; foam-plastic protection requirements |
| Closed-cell spray foam | Continuous air seal; high R-per-inch (~R-6/in.) | Expensive; foam-plastic protection requirements; off-gassing concerns during cure |
| EPS rigid foam | Low cost; R-4/in. | Lower compressive strength; not for ground contact unless rated |

For a long-life envelope, mineral wool board has the most favorable durability profile (no off-gassing, no R-value loss, vapor-open for drying), at the cost of slightly thicker walls for the same R-value. Rockwool ComfortBoard 80 (R-4.2 at 1 inch nominal) at 4 inches gives R-16.8, sufficient with the structural wall to meet R-21 above-grade prescriptive when accounting for thermal bridging from ties.

**Wall ties and thermal bridging**

Continuous exterior insulation requires ties through the insulation to attach the cladding. The Structural System And Concrete Design chapter handles the structural side of wall ties. The envelope side is the thermal bridge created by the tie.

| Tie material | Thermal bridge effect |
|---|---|
| Stainless steel (304 or 316) at typical spacing | ~0.5–1% point thermal bridge; reduces 4-inch foam cavity from R-26 to ~R-23 effective |
| Carbon steel | Higher thermal conductivity than stainless; should be coated or galvanized for corrosion resistance |
| Thermally-broken ties (Halfen FM, Bekaert Murfor THERM, Wagner ThermaTIE) | Thermal-break polymer or basalt section reduces the bridge to ~0.1–0.3% point reduction |
| FRP ties | Very low thermal conductivity; structural acceptance requires ICC ESR submittal and per-project building-official approval |

For aggressive R-value targets, specify thermally-broken ties. For typical residential targets, stainless steel ties at the spec frequency are acceptable.

## 8.5 Foam Plastic Protection (R316)

Foam plastic insulation in a residential building is regulated by ORSC R316 because of fire-performance concerns. The general rule: foam plastic must be separated from interior space by a thermal barrier (typically 1/2-inch gypsum board) unless a specific exception applies.

For a concrete or masonry wall with foam plastic insulation, the most useful exception is the concrete/masonry coverage exception: foam plastic in a masonry or concrete wall, floor, or roof system where the foam is covered on each face by not less than 1 inch of masonry or concrete satisfies R316 without a separate thermal barrier.

**Applied: R316 implications for the envelope**

For continuous exterior insulation:

- mineral wool board has no R316 concern (not a foam plastic)
- XPS, polyiso, EPS, or spray foam on the exterior of a masonry or concrete wall, covered with cladding on the outside, requires either a thermal barrier on the interior or the concrete/masonry-coverage exception
- if the structural wall is reinforced CMU and the cladding is masonry veneer, the foam can be sandwiched between two masonry wythes and the concrete/masonry-coverage exception applies (with the full cover requirements); this turns the assembly into a sandwich wall and changes the structural classification (engineered design)

For a single-wythe CMU structural wall with continuous exterior insulation and rainscreen cladding:

- the CMU wall on the interior side of the foam acts as the thermal barrier (CMU thicker than 1 inch satisfies the protection in the inward direction)
- the rainscreen cladding does not protect the foam in the outward direction
- the foam is "exposed to weather" but not exposed to interior space; R316 is generally satisfied by the masonry-on-one-side configuration plus appropriate cladding-side protection
- confirm the specific assembly with the building official; some jurisdictions interpret R316 differently for the exterior side

For a wood-framed assembly above the masonry walls (only on the prescriptive path with manufacturer-engineered wood floor and roof), R316 protection applies in the conventional sense (1/2-inch gypsum on the interior).

**Code**

```
ORSC R316: foam plastic.
  - General requirement for a thermal barrier between foam plastic and interior space.
  - Standard thermal barrier: 1/2-inch gypsum board.

ORSC R316.4: foam plastic in masonry or concrete wall, floor, or roof construction.
  - Exception for foam plastic insulation where covered on each face by minimum 1-inch concrete or masonry.
  - Useful for sandwich-wall construction.

UpCodes thermal-barrier exception collection: up.codes/s/thermal-barrier-not-required.
  - Lists the exceptions including the concrete/masonry coverage exception.
```

## 8.6 Slab Edge And Below-Grade Envelope Interface

The slab edge is a thermal-bridge problem and a water-management problem at the same time. Below-grade walls are subject to hydrostatic pressure and have to handle both insulation and waterproofing as an integrated assembly.

### 8.6.1 Slab Edge

The 2023 ORSC Table N1101.1(1) requires slab-edge insulation at F-0.520 / R-15, 24-inch minimum depth. The 24-inch dimension is measured horizontally or vertically below grade as part of the perimeter insulation.

**Applied: slab-edge detail options**

| Configuration | Description |
|---|---|
| Vertical perimeter insulation | Rigid foam (XPS Type IV minimum) attached to the foundation wall exterior, full 24 inches below grade or to the top of the footing |
| Horizontal wing insulation | Rigid foam (XPS Type VII minimum at 60 psi) extending horizontally outward from the foundation, buried under 6–12 inches of gravel/soil; combined with vertical insulation at the wall |
| Heated slab interior | R-10 rigid foam (XPS Type IV minimum) under the entire slab, in addition to perimeter insulation |
| Insulated stem wall (combined approach) | Insulation continuous from above-grade exterior wall, around the stem-wall corner, and down 24 inches below grade |

For continuous exterior insulation on the above-grade wall, the slab-edge insulation is typically continuous with the wall insulation: the wall insulation extends down past the sill plate, around the foundation top, and continues down the foundation exterior to the prescribed depth. This produces a continuous thermal layer from the roof to below grade.

### 8.6.2 Below-Grade Wall Interface

Below-grade walls combine multiple control layers:

| Layer | Position | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation drainage | Outboard of the wall | Reject subsurface water before it reaches the wall |
| Waterproofing or dampproofing membrane | On the soil side of the wall | Reject any water that does reach the wall |
| Drainage board | Between membrane and backfill | Channel water down to the perimeter drain |
| Below-grade insulation | Outboard of the membrane (or in some assemblies, inboard of the wall) | Thermal control |
| Protection board | Between insulation and backfill (where impact damage is a concern) | Mechanical protection |

The Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement chapter takes the drainage and waterproofing detail. The envelope-side responsibility in this chapter is making sure the insulation, the membrane, and the air-control layer are continuous from the above-grade wall down to the footing.

**Materials for below-grade insulation**

- XPS Type IV (25 psi) for typical residential below-grade
- XPS Type VI (40 psi) for backfill-pressure-loaded conditions or soils with rocky backfill
- Mineral wool board (Rockwool Comfortboard or similar) is acceptable below grade but compressive strength is lower; XPS is the typical choice
- EPS is acceptable but absorbs more moisture over time; lower R-value retention than XPS in wet exposure

**Code**

```
ORSC Table N1101.1(1): slab-edge insulation F-0.520, R-15, 24-inch minimum depth.

ORSC R406: foundation dampproofing and waterproofing.
  - R406.1 dampproofing default.
  - R406.2 waterproofing where high water table or severe soil-water conditions exist.

ORSC R405: foundation drainage.
  - 4-inch perforated pipe, 6-inch aggregate, filter fabric.

ASTM C578: rigid cellular polystyrene (XPS, EPS) by type.

ASTM C516: mineral fiber thermal insulation for low-temperature applications.

BCD slab-edge insulation technical bulletin: oregon.gov/bcd/codes-stand/Documents/res-techb-slabedge-insulation.pdf.
```

## 8.7 Roof Assembly

Roof assembly options depend on the structural choice (concrete deck, wood-framed). The Structural System And Concrete Design chapter delivered the structural roof; this chapter addresses the envelope side: drainage, waterproofing, insulation, vapor control, and continuity at parapets and edges.

### 8.7.1 Concrete Deck With Protected Membrane Roof (PMR)

The recommended roof for a long-life concrete house. The structural concrete deck (8-inch CIP slab or hollow-core with 2–3-inch topping) is sloped at minimum 1/4 inch per foot toward roof drains. The membrane is applied directly to the deck. Insulation is placed above the membrane, protected by ballast.

**Layer order, top to bottom (PMR):**

1. ballast: river rock (10–20 pcf required for wind uplift) or concrete pavers on pedestals
2. filter fabric (under ballast)
3. rigid insulation: XPS Type VI (40 psi), 4–6 inches thick (R-20 to R-30 nominal); slip-sheet between insulation layers if multi-layer
4. roofing membrane: fully adhered modified bitumen (SBS or APP, 2-ply hot-mopped or torch-applied), EPDM, TPO, or PVC; 60-mil minimum
5. primer (membrane-specific)
6. concrete deck: 8-inch CIP slab or hollow-core with 2–3-inch CIP topping

**Strengths of PMR:**

- the membrane is protected from UV, foot traffic, and impact by the insulation and ballast above it
- the membrane reaches end of service life on a 30–50 year cycle; replaceable without disturbing the deck
- the concrete deck stays in the conditioned-temperature zone (the insulation is above it)
- the design-life mismatch between the membrane (50 years) and the deck (centuries) is intentional and managed

**Drainage on a flat concrete deck:**

- minimum 1/4 inch per foot slope toward roof drains
- minimum two roof drains per primary drainage area (per IPC 1106)
- overflow scupper or secondary drainage sized for 100-year storm + drain failure
- ASCE 7 ponding-instability check on the concrete deck
- snow load per OSU/SEAO snow lookup tool with 25 psf minimum

### 8.7.2 Concrete Deck With Sloped Wood-Framed False Roof

A second option for a concrete-deck house. The concrete deck handles structure and waterproofing (membrane on the deck); a sloped wood-framed false roof above the deck supports a metal or slate weather surface.

This decouples the weather surface from the structure with a gap that can dry. Higher cost than PMR, more maintenance, but matches some aesthetic preferences (visible sloped roof, eaves, traditional appearance).

### 8.7.3 Wood-Framed Truss Roof On Bond Beam

The prescriptive-path option. Manufacturer-engineered wood trusses (the manufacturer's stamp carries the engineering) on a bond beam at the top of the masonry wall. Standing-seam metal roofing on synthetic underlayment over plywood sheathing. Vented attic with insulation at the ceiling level (R-49 prescriptive).

**Roofing material selection for a long-life envelope:**

- standing-seam metal panel (24-gauge minimum): 50–70 year service life; the recommended roofing material
- natural slate: 75–150 year service life; high cost; requires structural design for the load
- asphalt shingle: 15–30 year service life; rejected for this project's design horizon
- concrete tile: 50+ years; high weight; requires structural design

For the prescriptive-path roof, the long-life-friendly compromise is manufacturer-engineered wood trusses with 24-gauge standing-seam metal roofing. The roof structure is replaceable on a 60–80 year cycle; the metal surface lasts 50–70 years.

### 8.7.4 Roof Penetrations And Parapets

Every roof penetration (vent, plumbing stack, exhaust, skylight, equipment curb) and every parapet condition is a control-layer transition.

For PMR roofs, penetrations use a manufacturer-detailed flashing that maintains membrane continuity. Parapets receive a continuous membrane up the inside face, over the top, and down the outside face with a counter-flashing or coping detail.

For wood-framed truss roofs, penetrations use traditional sheet-metal flashing with the underlayment lapped over and around. Parapets are uncommon on this assembly.

**Code**

```
  - Vented and unvented (R806.5) roof assembly requirements.
```

## 8.8 Windows And Doors

Windows and doors are the most-penetrated part of the envelope. Each one is a hole in the wall that has to be flashed, sealed, and integrated with five control layers.

**Professional: performance specifications**

ORSC Chapter 11 sets the performance values:

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Windows U-factor | U-0.27 maximum |
| Skylights U-factor | U-0.50 maximum |
| Exterior doors U-factor | U-0.20 maximum (where the door has ≤ 2.5 sq ft glazing) |
| Exterior doors with > 2.5 sq ft glazing | U-0.40 maximum |

Air leakage: NFRC 400 testing per ASTM E283 (≤ 0.3 cfm/sq ft for fixed; ≤ 0.5 cfm/sq ft for operable, typical industry maximums).

Water resistance: AAMA 101/I.S.2/A440 or NFRC 700 ratings; window must meet the design pressure for the project's wind exposure.

Emergency escape (R310): every habitable basement and every sleeping room requires an egress window or door. Net clear opening 5.7 sq ft minimum (5.0 sq ft at grade); minimum 24 inch height, 20 inch width; sill not more than 44 inches above the floor.

**Applied: rough-opening detail**

The window or door rough opening is the highest-failure-rate envelope detail. The detail has to integrate with all five control layers.

A standard sequence for a window in a continuous-exterior-insulation wall:

1. structural wall complete with rough opening framed
2. apply primer for the WRB to the rough-opening surfaces
3. apply sill flashing membrane (peel-and-stick): pan flashing turned up at jambs 6 inches, with 4-inch leg up the wall
4. apply jamb flashing (peel-and-stick): up the jambs, lapping the sill flashing
5. install the WRB on the wall plane, integrated with the head flashing
6. install the window per the manufacturer's instructions: shimmed, plumb, square; backer rod and sealant at jambs and head; sill not sealed (allow drainage)
7. apply head flashing (peel-and-stick): over the top of the window, continuous with the WRB head lap, drainage path to the WRB outboard side
8. install the rigid foam exterior insulation, cut to the window frame
9. install the head flashing over the foam (integrated with the foam-side WRB if the assembly has one)
10. install the rainscreen cladding with appropriate trim around the window
11. interior side: caulk or gasket between window frame and rough opening; spray foam around the perimeter to seal the gap; air-control-layer continuity at the interior face

The Window-Wall Interface Detail Standard (referenced by AAMA 711) covers this sequence in industry-standard form.

## 8.9 Above-Grade Wall Assembly: Drainage And Air Control

The above-grade wall handles bulk water, air, vapor, and heat. The key design move is the rainscreen: an air gap between the cladding and the WRB that lets water drain and the wall dry.

**Professional: drained and back-ventilated rainscreen**

| Layer | Position | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Cladding | Outermost | Aesthetic; primary weather surface; rejects most bulk water |
| Rainscreen gap | Between cladding and WRB | Drainage and ventilation; typically 3/4 inch minimum |
| WRB (water-resistive barrier) | On the rigid foam or sheathing | Secondary water barrier; rejects water that gets past the cladding |
| Rigid exterior insulation | On the structural wall | Continuous thermal layer |
| Air-control layer | Typically the WRB or a sheet membrane on the structural wall | Continuous air barrier |
| Structural wall (CMU or concrete) | Inboard of the insulation | Structural load-bearing |
| Vapor control | Inherent in the assembly (concrete and masonry are low-permeance; vapor migration is controlled by the assembly characteristics) | Manages vapor diffusion |
| Interior finish | Drywall, plaster, or other | Aesthetic and protection of the structural wall surface |

For a long-life envelope, the cladding is replaceable on a multi-decade cycle without disturbing the rest of the assembly. The rainscreen makes that practical: removing and replacing cladding does not require disturbing the WRB or the insulation.

**Cladding options for a long-life concrete house:**

| Cladding | Service life | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Brick veneer (a second wythe of brick or stone) | 100+ years | Low maintenance; integrates well with the structural masonry |
| Fiber-cement panels (James Hardie, Equitone, Nichiha) | 50+ years | Lower cost; paint maintenance |
| Metal panels (corrugated, standing-seam) | 50–70 years | Low maintenance; good for accents or full cladding |
| Stone veneer (natural or manufactured) | 50–100+ years | Heavy; structural support required |
| Wood siding (cedar, redwood) | 20–40 years | Maintenance-intensive; not recommended for the long-life envelope of this project |

The cladding choice is partly aesthetic and partly performance. For a multi-century house, brick-veneer or stone-veneer cladding pairs naturally with the structural masonry.

## 8.10 Vapor Control

Vapor control is the most-misunderstood envelope topic. The conservative approach is to design the assembly to dry to one or both sides; a vapor retarder is placed only where calculated condensation analysis shows it is needed.

**Professional: vapor retarder classification (IECC and ASHRAE 160)**

| Class | Permeance (perms) | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Class I | ≤ 0.1 perm | Polyethylene sheet; sheet metal; aluminum foil |
| Class II | > 0.1 to ≤ 1.0 perm | Kraft-faced fiberglass; vapor-retarder paint; some smart membranes |
| Class III | > 1.0 to ≤ 10 perm | Latex paint on drywall; many mineral-fiber sheathings |

Oregon climate zone 4C and 5B: a Class III vapor retarder (typically painted drywall) on the warm side of the assembly is acceptable. Class I (polyethylene) on the warm side can produce trapped moisture in some assemblies and is not recommended for continuous exterior insulation walls.

For a concrete or reinforced-masonry wall with continuous exterior insulation and a rainscreen, vapor control is typically inherent: the masonry itself has low permeance, the foam exterior insulation has low permeance, and the rainscreen-side cladding allows drying outward. Painted drywall on the interior provides Class III control without trapping moisture.

For wood-framed assemblies above masonry walls (prescriptive path), traditional vapor control rules apply: painted drywall on the interior side; WRB on the exterior; cavity insulation between framing.

The HVAC, Ventilation, Humidity, And Air Quality chapter handles indoor humidity control, which affects the vapor-drive across any envelope assembly.

**Code**

```
ORSC N1102.5 / R702.7: vapor retarder requirements.
  - Class I, II, or III vapor retarder required on the interior side of frame walls in climate zones 5 and higher (ORSC adopts Oregon-specific provisions).
  - Class III is acceptable in zones 5 and warmer with continuous exterior insulation that meets the table-specified R-values.
```

## 8.11 Radon Control

Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas produced by the decay of uranium in soil and rock. Soil-gas radon enters buildings through cracks, joints, and openings in the slab and lower walls. Cumulative exposure raises lung cancer risk; the U.S. EPA recommends mitigation when indoor levels exceed 4 pCi/L.

Oregon mandates radon-resistant new construction in seven counties through ORSC Appendix AF.

**Mandated counties (ORSC Appendix AF):**

- Baker
- Clackamas
- Hood River
- Multnomah
- Polk
- Washington
- Yamhill

These seven counties were identified by the Oregon Health Authority as having elevated radon-potential mapping. The county list can expand by board action under ORS 455.365; the current list is the seven above.

For projects outside these counties, Appendix AF is not required by the state, but radon-resistant construction is still recommended as a long-life best practice. The marginal cost of including the passive system at construction is small; retrofit after construction is much more expensive.

**Applied: Appendix AF passive subslab depressurization system**

The required system has the following components, integrated with the slab stack from the Foundation, Basement, Slabs, And Concrete Placement chapter:

| Component | Specification |
|---|---|
| Permeable aggregate base under slab | 4 inches minimum of clean #57 stone or equivalent gas-permeable material (AF103.5) |
| Soil-gas retarder | 6-mil polyethylene above the aggregate base, OR a 15-mil ASTM E1745 Class A vapor retarder serving both vapor and soil-gas function (AF103.3) |
| Vent pipe | 3-inch ABS, PVC, or equivalent gas-tight pipe, vertical, embedded in the aggregate before the slab is cast (AF103.6.1) |
| Vent pipe routing | Vertical from sub-slab T-fitting through the slab, through the conditioned space (typically chase or interior wall cavity), through the roof; outlet 12 inches above roof and 10 feet from any operable opening |
| Sealing of slab joints | All slab joints, control joints, and isolation joints sealed with polyurethane caulk or elastomeric sealant (AF103.4.2) |
| Sealing of penetrations | All slab penetrations sealed (AF103.4.1) |
| Sumps | Tight-fitting gasketed covers (AF103.4.4) |
| Foundation walls (hollow CMU) | Continuous solid course or grouted course at or above grade (AF103.4.5) |
| Vent pipe access for future fan | Accessible in the attic for future fan installation if post-occupancy testing requires activation (AF103.8) |
| Electrical for future fan | Electrical circuit terminated in the attic for future fan power (AF103.12) |

The system is passive at construction (no fan installed). After occupancy, the owner conducts a radon test (typically 90+ day long-term test). If the test result exceeds 4 pCi/L, an in-line fan is added in the attic between the sub-slab pipe and the roof outlet, converting the passive system to active. The conversion takes several hours and the prepared electrical circuit and pipe location make it straightforward.

**Professional: Appendix AF (binding) versus ANSI/AARST CCAH (supplemental)**

ORSC Appendix AF is the binding code requirement in Oregon. It specifies the rough-in components.

ANSI/AARST CCAH (Reducing Radon in New Construction of One- and Two-Family Dwellings and Townhouses) is a supplemental best-practice standard published by the American Association of Radon Scientists and Technologists. CCAH adds testing protocols, fan-activation criteria, and post-occupancy verification practices that Appendix AF does not require. EPA references CCAH as the comprehensive new-construction standard; Oregon adopts the rough-in subset through Appendix AF.

For a long-life house, following CCAH practices in addition to Appendix AF is straightforward and produces better long-term radon control:

- post-occupancy radon test (90+ day long-term test or repeated short-term tests)
- annual or biennial re-testing
- fan activation if any test result exceeds 2 pCi/L (CCAH lower threshold) or 4 pCi/L (EPA action level)
- documentation of test results and any system changes

**Radon and the structural shell**

The Appendix AF passive system pairs naturally with concrete-and-masonry construction. The slab stack already includes the aggregate base, vapor retarder, and sealed slab joints. The only additions for radon-county compliance are the 3-inch vent pipe (installed before slab placement) and the attic accessibility for future fan.

For a project outside the seven mandated counties, the same components can be installed at construction as a long-life best practice. Cost is small (~$300–$800 in 2026 Oregon dollars for materials and labor) compared to retrofit cost ($1,500–$5,000+) or the public-health cost of unmanaged radon exposure.

**Code**


## 8.12 Envelope Testing

Envelope testing verifies that the work was done correctly. Testing is more useful early (before finishes are installed and corrections are easy) and at completion (to document final performance).

**Professional: envelope test types and timing**

| Test | When to perform | Standard | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blower-door (initial) | After insulation and air sealing complete; before drywall | ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380 | Identify leaks while they are accessible for repair |
| Blower-door (final) | After all finishes complete; for compliance documentation | ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380 | Compliance with ORSC N1104.8.2 (3.25 ACH50) or N1101.1(2) Measure 7 (2.75 ACH50, third-party tested) |
| Duct leakage test | Before drywall (rough) and at completion | ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380 or ASTM E1554 | Compliance with ORSC duct sealing requirements; required where ducts are outside the conditioned envelope |
| Infrared thermography | After insulation, with temperature differential between inside and outside | RESNET-recommended | Identify thermal bridges, missing insulation, air leaks |
| Water-spray test (windows) | After window installation, before WRB and cladding cover | AAMA 501.1 or 501.2; ASTM E1105 | Identify water leaks at window installations |
| Smoke-pencil test | During blower-door negative-pressure | Industry standard | Trace specific leak locations during depressurization |
| Moisture monitoring (slab) | After cure | ASTM F2170 (relative humidity probes) or F1869 (calcium chloride) | Verify slab moisture is acceptable for floor coverings |

Blower-door testing during construction (before drywall) is the highest-value test on the project. With the air barrier exposed, leaks can be found by hand, smoke pencil, or infrared imaging and sealed with caulk, foam, or membrane patches. After drywall, every leak hidden behind finishes requires invasive repair.

**Applied: testing program**

A typical envelope-testing program for a long-life house:

1. **Pre-drywall blower door + smoke pencil**: identify and seal leaks while the air barrier is exposed. Target: ≤ 2.0 ACH50 at this stage so the final test (after drywall) lands well under 2.75 ACH50.
2. **Infrared thermography after insulation**: with a temperature differential of at least 18 °F between inside and outside, scan the wall and ceiling assemblies for missing insulation or thermal bridges.
3. **Water-spray test at window installations**: spray water at each window for 15+ minutes per AAMA 501.2; observe for water entry through the frame, sill, or rough-opening flashing. Repair any leak before WRB and cladding cover.
4. **Final blower-door test**: documented compliance test, performed by a third party for Measure 7 compliance. Submit the result to the building official before CO.
5. **Post-occupancy radon test**: 90+ day long-term test in the lowest occupied level. Activate the sub-slab depressurization fan if result exceeds the action level.

The Testing, Commissioning, And Quality Control chapter covers the full project testing program (concrete, soil, MEP, envelope, IAQ) in coordinated detail. The envelope-side responsibility in this chapter is the testing list above.

## 8.13 IAQ Coordination

A tight envelope requires mechanical ventilation. The two systems are paired by design: the envelope keeps uncontrolled air out; the ventilation system supplies controlled fresh air at the right rate.

The HVAC, Ventilation, Humidity, And Air Quality chapter handles ventilation system selection, sizing, distribution, and commissioning in detail. The envelope-side responsibility in this chapter is to identify the coordination points.

**Coordination points**

| Item | Envelope side | HVAC side |
|---|---|---|
| Whole-house ventilation rate | Envelope airtightness sets the baseline; tighter envelope means ventilation system carries more of the fresh-air load | Mechanical ventilation sized to ASHRAE 62.2 or local code requirement |
| HRV/ERV intakes and exhausts | Envelope-penetration locations and detail | Equipment efficiency, airflow, distribution |
| Combustion appliances | Envelope airtightness affects combustion-air supply for atmospheric-vented equipment | Sealed-combustion equipment recommended for tight envelopes |
| Bathroom and kitchen exhaust | Envelope-penetration detail at fan housings | Fan sizing, controls, integration with whole-house ventilation |
| Dryer vent | Sealed envelope penetration | Vent run length, makeup air |
| Whole-house dehumidification | Slab and below-grade detail; vapor retarder integrity | Equipment selection, drainage |

**Applied**

For a long-life house with the envelope and HVAC designed together:

- Specify the envelope to Measure 7 air-tightness (≤ 2.75 ACH50) and HRV with 66% sensible heat-recovery efficiency or better
- Install all combustion appliances (water heater, furnace where used, fireplace) as sealed-combustion units; do not rely on atmospheric venting in a tight envelope
- Locate HRV intakes and exhausts to avoid short-circuiting (intake away from exhaust; exhaust away from operable windows)
- Detail all envelope penetrations for the mechanical system to maintain air-control-layer continuity
- Pair the slab stack vapor retarder with whole-house humidity control to keep interior moisture in the design range (40–60% RH typical)

**Code**

```

ORSC M1507: mechanical ventilation.
  - Whole-house ventilation rate per ASHRAE 62.2.

ORSC G2407 (and IRC equivalent): combustion air for atmospherically vented appliances.
  - Tight envelopes typically push the project to sealed-combustion equipment.

ASHRAE 55: thermal environmental conditions for human occupancy.
```

## 8.14 Pre-Cover Acceptance And Records

The envelope work happens in stages, and each stage has to be inspected and recorded before the next stage covers it. Once cladding is on, the WRB is hidden; once drywall is up, the air-control layer is hidden; once finishes are applied, almost everything is hidden.

**Applied: pre-cover inspection points**

| Stage | What to verify and record | Cover that follows |
|---|---|---|
| Below-grade waterproofing | Membrane continuity, terminations, penetrations sealed; drainage board installed; foundation drains in place | Backfill |
| Slab stack | Subgrade, capillary break aggregate, vapor retarder lapped and sealed, soil-gas retarder, under-slab insulation, radon piping, sub-slab plumbing and electrical | Concrete slab |
| Air-control layer | Continuity around the building cross-section; sealing at penetrations; integration at transitions | Drywall and finishes |
| WRB on walls | Lapped and sealed at all openings, transitions, and terminations; integrated with window flashing | Rigid foam insulation and rainscreen |
| Window installations | Pan flashing, jamb flashing, head flashing per the manufacturer's instructions; sealant beads | Trim and finishes |
| Continuous exterior insulation | Thickness, joints, attachment to structural wall, integration with window and door openings | Rainscreen and cladding |
| Roof membrane | Continuity, terminations at parapets and penetrations, drain detailing | Insulation and ballast (PMR) or covering |
| Insulation in cavities or above ceiling | Coverage, depth, no gaps or compression | Drywall, ceiling finish |

**Records**

For each stage, the project binder receives:

- date and weather of installation
- materials installed (product name, manufacturer, batch number where available, cut sheets)
- photographs at each stage with reference scale
- any inspector approval (formal inspection where required)
- any test results from that stage
- any deviations from the approved drawings, with the reason and the as-built record

The Documentation, Maintenance, And Future Upgrades chapter handles the long-term archive strategy. The envelope records are kept for the life of the building; they support repair, alteration, insurance, and resale.
