# Chapter 17: Build Sequence And Site Coordination

This chapter sequences the build: how an empty site becomes a dried-in concrete house, how the permits and inspections chain together, how the owner-builder's own work and the licensed specialists are scheduled around each other, and how the project reaches occupancy without losing control of quality. It coordinates the technical chapters rather than repeating them; where a step needs a detail (the concrete mix, the waterproofing assembly, the branch-circuit layout), the owning chapter carries it.

Three independent timing systems have to align: the Oregon permit chain (land use, then building, then trade, then inspection), the trade-licensing limits on what the owner-builder may install at each stage, and the material curing and conditioning windows set by the standards and the manufacturers. On a rural owner-builder build, the binding order is land-use clearance and septic and well feasibility before the building permit issues, not the reverse.

Phase durations, costs, equipment rental rates, and the current wildfire-hazard-map designation are verified for the actual site, season, and jurisdiction; they are treated as dated facts, not manuscript constants.

## 17.1 The Build As A Dependency Map

The build moves through stages in an order set by dependencies, not by preference. A step done out of order traps the next trade or buries an uninspected assembly, so the sequence is planned before the first load of dirt moves.

**Simple**

A house is built in stages: land, permits, site work, foundation, structure, roof and windows, rough systems, insulation, finishes, and move-in. The order matters because one step done too early can trap the next one. A drain has to slope before finishes hide it, an electrical box has to be located before drywall, and waterproofing has to be inspected before backfill.

**Applied**

Keep the stage map current in the project binder:

| Stage | Goal |
|---|---|
| Land use and feasibility | Prove the parcel can support the house and its water and waste |
| Design and permits | Turn the design into approved documents |
| Site preparation | Make the land buildable and serviceable |
| Foundation | Place and protect the base |
| Structure | Build the concrete and masonry body |
| Roof and openings | Dry in the house |
| Rough systems | Install plumbing, mechanical, electrical, and low voltage |
| Insulation and cover | Close assemblies after inspection |
| Finishes | Make the house livable |
| Occupancy | Prove the required systems are safe and usable |

**Professional**

The sequence is a dependency graph in which downstream permits and inspections cannot close until upstream ones do. Gravity drains need slope before cover, electrical boxes need location before drywall, waterproofing needs inspection before backfill, radiant tubing needs a pressure test and photographs before the topping slab, and windows need egress and rough-opening verification before ordering. This chapter names when to use each technical chapter and how the pieces coordinate in time; the technical chapters carry the how.

**Code**

```
ORSC R109: required inspection sequence for a one- and two-family dwelling; each stage gated by the prior inspection.
The permit chain: land use (ORS 215/197) → building (ORSC R105) → trade (ORS 479/693/447) → inspection (R109) → certificate of occupancy (R110).
```

## 17.2 The Permit Dependency Chain

Permits issue in a fixed order, and the building permit cannot issue until the land-use, septic, and well approvals close. On a rural parcel this front-end work takes months, so the schedule is built backward from a target occupancy date with those realities included.

**Applied**

Work the chain in order:

1. Land-use clearance from the county or city planning department (rural zoning under ORS 215 and statewide planning under ORS 197), plus any wildfire-hazard-zone designation and its envelope requirements.
2. Septic site evaluation (DEQ OAR 340-071) and well feasibility (OWRD OAR 690) on a parcel without sewer and water.
3. Driveway or approach permit (county, city, or ODOT for a state highway) meeting fire-access standards.
4. Building permit (ORSC R105) with the construction documents (R106), the Stage 1 approvals, and any required PE stamps; plus the erosion-control permit where the disturbance reaches one acre.
5. Trade permits for electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work.
6. Inspections and the certificate of occupancy.

**Professional**

Land use and feasibility routinely take three to nine months in rural Oregon counties, and building-permit plan review runs weeks to months longer in growth-pressured jurisdictions, so no permit is assumed to be obtainable on demand. The wildfire-hazard map has been reissued and challenged repeatedly, and a high- or extreme-hazard designation triggers the Oregon wildland-urban-interface provisions (ORSC R327: Class A roofing, ignition-resistant siding, ember-resistant venting, and defensible space) that materially change the envelope, so the current designation is obtained in writing from the county before the envelope is designed. The DEQ 1200-C construction stormwater permit applies to a disturbance of one acre or more (or smaller in a regulated area), and the septic site evaluation must be approved before the building permit issues on a parcel without sewer.

**Code**

```
ORS 215 and 197: county zoning and statewide land-use planning; land-use clearance before the building permit.
ORSC R327: wildland-urban-interface envelope requirements where the parcel is in a high/extreme hazard zone; verify the current map designation in writing.
DEQ OAR 340-071: septic site evaluation approved before the building permit on a parcel without sewer; OWRD OAR 690: well feasibility.
ORSC R105 and R106: building permit and construction documents.
DEQ 1200-C (ORS 468B, OAR 340-045): construction stormwater permit for one acre or more of disturbance.
Oregon Fire Code 503 and Appendix D: driveway fire-access dimensions.
```

## 17.3 Permit Lifecycle And The Occupancy Gate

A permit expires if work does not start or is suspended, so extensions are obtained in writing before expiration. Partial approvals let discrete work proceed, and the certificate of occupancy is the legal gate to moving in.

**Applied**

- Start work within 180 days of permit issuance and avoid a 180-day suspension; request an extension in writing before the permit expires.
- Request inspections through the jurisdiction's portal or phone (same-day requests often by early morning), and document every request and result with dated photos of the inspection record.
- Do not call for an inspection until the work is complete and accessible, to avoid a reinspection fee.
- Use a partial approval (such as a foundation-only permit) to let the pour and cure proceed while superstructure plan review continues, when the plans examiner allows it.
- Treat a temporary certificate of occupancy as discretionary, not a right, and not as a planning crutch.

**Professional**

ORSC R105.5 sets the 180-day commencement and suspension limits and allows the building official to grant extensions of up to 180 days each for justifiable cause; reviving a lapsed permit usually means a new application and fee. A temporary certificate of occupancy under R110.4 requires the life-safety items complete (smoke and CO alarms, egress, guards and handrails, working water and waste, working heat, no exposed conductors) plus a written list of remaining work and a deadline, and failing the deadline can trigger revocation. The certificate of occupancy under R110 requires all inspections signed off, the energy-code documentation filed, the required testing documented (blower door, well-water potability, septic startup where applicable), and any conditions of approval satisfied. Selling before the certificate of occupancy is a disclosure risk under the seller-property-disclosure framework covered in the legal chapter.

**Code**

```
ORSC R105.5: permit expires if work is not commenced within 180 days or is suspended for 180 days; extensions in writing before expiration.
ORSC R110.4: temporary certificate of occupancy, discretionary, life-safety items complete plus a written remaining-work list and deadline.
ORSC R110: certificate of occupancy; all inspections, energy documentation, required testing, and conditions complete.
```

## 17.4 Owner-Builder Scope At Each Stage

The owner-builder is the labor for the bulk of the work, and the licensed specialists are scheduled around that work, not the reverse. The trade exemptions cover the owner's own work on the owner-occupied residence, but several tasks require a licensed or federally certified specialist regardless.

**Applied**

Assign each task to the right party:

- Owner-builder (on the owner-occupied, not-for-sale residence, with permits): general construction (no CCB license required), and the owner's own electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work.
- EPA-certified HVAC technician: brazing the refrigerant lines, evacuation, charging, and startup of a split-system heat pump (the owner may mount the units and run the line-set conduit).
- Licensed LP-gas installer: all LP-gas work (no homeowner exemption).
- Licensed septic installer and OWRD-bonded well constructor: the septic tank and pipe install and the well construction (no homeowner exemption; the owner may do supervised site work).
- Professional engineer: any structural element outside the prescriptive code (a service purchase; the owner still pulls the permit).

**Professional**

The trade exemptions (ORS 479.540 electrical, ORS 693.020 plumbing, ORS 447.115 mechanical) parallel the CCB owner-builder exemption established in the legal chapter and turn on the same intent test, and permits are still required. The federal EPA Section 608 certification for refrigerant work does not yield to the Oregon owner-builder exemption, so the certified technician is scheduled for a single startup visit rather than spread across the build. The owner-builder does not schedule the trades the way a general contractor does, because the owner is the trades for most of the work; the external dependencies (the refrigerant technician, the well driller, the septic installer, the surveyor, and the inspector) are the fixed points the owner's own work is planned around.

**Code**

```
ORS 479.540, 693.020, 447.115: owner exemptions for the owner's own electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work on the owner-occupied residence; permits still required.
Clean Air Act Section 608 (federal): EPA-certified technician required for refrigerant brazing, evacuation, and charging; no Oregon owner-builder waiver.
OAR 340-071-0150 (septic install) and OAR 690-210 (well construction): licensed installer and bonded well constructor; no homeowner exemption.
ORS 480.430–480.460: LP-gas work by a licensed installer.
```

## 17.5 Utility Coordination

Utility timelines do not bend for the build schedule, so temporary power, the transformer, and the meter set are applied for early. The trench is dug once for power, communications, and any low-voltage conduit, with locates called before any digging.

**Applied**

- Apply early for temporary construction power: a permitted, inspected construction service (up to 100 A under the manufactured-construction-service rule) with a 2-to-8-week utility lead time.
- Coordinate permanent service: the transformer pad and conduit are the customer's responsibility to the utility's spec (commonly 4-inch Schedule 40 PVC, 24 inches or deeper, with tracer wire), and the meter is set after the electrical inspection signs off the service equipment.
- Call 811 at least two business days before any digging; locates are required by law even on raw land.
- Dig one utility trench for power, communications, and low-voltage conduit with the required separations, and photograph it before backfill.
- Install a communications conduit with a pull string from the demarcation to the street even where the house is initially served by satellite or fixed wireless.

**Professional**

The temporary service is a permitted, inspected installation under the OESC and ORS 479, using a manufactured construction service (OAR 918-261-0038) that is exempt from licensing but not from permit and inspection. The permanent service follows the utility's standard for the transformer pad, conduit, service-entrance conductors, and meter base, with a conduit run longer than about 150 feet checked for voltage drop. Underground burial depths follow NEC 300.5 for power and Article 800 for communications, with the typical 12-inch power-to-communications separation, and the 811 locate requirement (ORS 757.541 to 757.571) is non-waivable. The outdoor emergency disconnect and the meter and disconnect placement are detailed in the electrical service chapter.

**Code**

```
OAR 918-261-0038: manufactured construction service for temporary power; permit and inspection required, licensing exempt.
NEC 300.5 and Article 800: minimum burial depths and power-to-communications separation.
ORS 757.541–757.571: 811 utility locate required at least two business days before digging.
Utility service standards: transformer, conduit, meter base, and energization per the serving utility (see the electrical service chapter).
```

## 17.6 Site Preparation

Site preparation makes the land buildable and serviceable and installs the erosion control, driveway, and address before the structure. Erosion control goes in at the start of disturbance, not after.

**Applied**

- Have a registered land surveyor verify the boundary and set corner and grade stakes where any setback is tight or the lot lines are unclear, and document the staking.
- Install erosion control at the start: silt fence at the lower perimeter, inlet protection, a gravel construction entrance, and covers on soil stockpiles left more than about two weeks in the wet season.
- Build the driveway to the approach permit and the fire-access standard (width, vertical clearance, turn radii, grade, and a turnaround for a driveway over 150 feet), with a culvert sized to the design storm where a ditch is crossed.
- Post address numbers at least 4 inches tall, visible from the street, with a separate sign at a long driveway entrance, during construction as well as after.
- Plan phased material staging so lumber, drywall, and finish materials each land protected and out of the access path.

**Professional**

The boundary survey is a registered-professional-land-surveyor service under ORS 672.025, and setback violations are expensive to correct after construction. Erosion control follows the DEQ 1200-C erosion-and-sediment-control plan where the disturbance reaches one acre, and a simplified local version below that threshold. The driveway meets the Oregon Fire Code 503 and Appendix D fire-access dimensions, and a culvert crossing in fish-bearing water triggers ODFW fish-passage review (ORS 509.605 to 509.910). Permanent stormwater management (dispersal, a bioswale or rain garden, or a drywell) is required by the local stormwater code and may need a civil-engineer stamp. Temporary sanitation and jobsite safety obligations under OR-OSHA attach once any paid worker is on site.

**Code**

```
ORS 672.025: boundary survey by a registered professional land surveyor.
DEQ 1200-C erosion-and-sediment-control plan (one acre or more); local erosion control below that threshold.
Oregon Fire Code 503 and Appendix D: driveway width, clearance, turn radii, grade, and turnaround.
Oregon Fire Code 505.1: address numbers at least 4 inches tall, visible from the street, during construction.
ORS 509.605–509.910: ODFW fish-passage review for a crossing in fish-bearing water.
```

## 17.7 Excavation And Trench Safety

Excavation is the most dangerous common construction activity, so a trench 5 feet or deeper gets a protective system and the spoil is set back from the edge. These rules apply to any paid worker on site and are sound practice for solo work.

**Applied**

- Provide a protective system (sloping, benching, shoring, or a trench shield) for any excavation 5 feet or deeper, and a registered-engineer design at 20 feet.
- Slope to the soil type (assume Type C granular or wet soil at about 1.5 horizontal to 1 vertical unless the soil is classified stiffer by a competent person).
- Keep spoil piles at least 2 feet from the trench edge, provide access and egress within 25 feet of any worker in a trench 4 feet or deeper, and do not enter a trench with accumulated water without controls.
- Inspect the excavation daily before each shift and after rain, vibration, or added surcharge load.

**Professional**

OR-OSHA Division 3/P (29 CFR 1926 Subpart P) governs excavation, with the maximum allowable slopes by soil type (Type A 53 degrees, Type B 45 degrees, Type C 34 degrees); most Oregon residential soils are Type B or C. Typical foundation footing excavations to about 4 feet need no protective system but keep the spoil setback, full-height foundation-wall excavations to 8 to 10 feet need sloping or shoring, and narrow deep utility trenches need a protective system at 5 feet. The standard applies to any paid worker; for solo owner work it is not a regulatory requirement but is followed because trenches kill more people per worker-hour than almost any other activity. The specific frost depth and footing depth are set by the foundation chapter.

**Code**

```
OR-OSHA Division 3/P (29 CFR 1926 Subpart P): protective system at 5 feet, engineer design at 20 feet; soil-type slope limits; spoil setback; access within 25 feet; daily inspection.
Applies to any paid worker on site; recommended practice for solo work.
```

## 17.8 Equipment Selection

The build needs earthmoving, compaction, concrete, lifting, and power equipment, and each item is a buy, rent, or hire-with-operator decision by frequency of use and storage cost. Rental rates and the specific machines available are local facts confirmed at estimating.

**Applied**

- Match the equipment to the phase: an excavator or compact track loader for earthwork, a plate compactor or rammer for lift compaction, a mixer or pump for concrete placement, a vibrator for consolidation, a telehandler or boom truck for lifting, and a generator sized to the tool load and any well-pump startup.
- Buy tools used across more than one phase with low storage cost; rent equipment used once or twice with high storage and maintenance cost; hire-with-operator for crane, large excavation, and concrete pumping, where the operator carries the certification and insurance.
- Get current rental and purchase quotes from at least two Oregon rental yards and suppliers; the planning ranges are placeholders until then.
- Confirm operator-certification, towing, and fuel-transport rules for any equipment the owner will move or operate, and confirm equipment-floater and builder's-risk insurance coverage.

**Professional**

The minimum equipment list for a concrete-and-masonry shell and the buy-versus-rent-versus-hire decision are developed in the owner-fabrication-and-equipment research; the cost ranges there are national planning figures pending local quotes, and the local Oregon rental-yard and purchase prices are the project-level facts confirmed before a budget line is fixed. Operator training and the OSHA requirements for equipment operation attach to any paid worker, and a rental yard that delivers and operates equipment carries its operator's workers' compensation. On-site concrete mixing equipment overlaps the make-versus-buy concrete decision in the foundation and concrete-placement chapter, and the block press for owner-manufactured units overlaps the materials chapter.

**Code**

```
Equipment selection: buy multi-phase low-storage tools; rent single-use high-storage equipment; hire-with-operator for crane, large excavation, and pumping.
Local rental and purchase quotes (at least two Oregon sources) confirmed at estimating; national ranges are planning placeholders.
Operator certification, towing/CDL, and fuel-transport rules confirmed for any owner-operated equipment; workers' compensation carried by any paid operator's employer.
```

## 17.9 Concrete And Masonry Placement Sequencing

Concrete placement is governed by the weather window, the cure, and the backfill readiness, and the foundation inspection happens after the reinforcement and embeds are set but before the pour. Masonry has its own mortar and grout timing.

**Applied**

- Schedule foundation pours into the practical weather window (roughly March through November in the Willamette Valley without extensive protection; narrower east of the Cascades) and plan hot- or cold-weather protection outside it.
- Call the foundation inspection after the forms, reinforcement, anchor bolts, holdowns, and plumbing rough-ins through the footing are set and before concrete placement, with enough lead time that a failed inspection does not lose the concrete delivery slot.
- Do not backfill against a foundation wall until it has cured to design strength and the first floor is framed or braced to resist lateral earth pressure (typically 7 to 14 days plus a framed first floor for an 8-foot wall).
- Use mortar within 90 minutes of mixing and grout the masonry in the code lift heights, with cleanouts at the base of high-lift pours and temporary bracing on walls over 8 feet.
- Schedule a concrete pump where the truck cannot reach the placement point, and coordinate it with ready-mix delivery for continuous flow.

**Professional**

Hot-weather placement follows ACI 305.1 (concrete at delivery at or below 95°F, evaporation-rate control), and cold-weather placement follows ACI 306R (minimum placement temperatures and protected curing for several days). Formwork supporting structural load is not removed until the concrete reaches the specified strength (ACI 318 and 301), and backfilling too early is a leading cause of foundation-wall cracking. Waterproofing membranes require a cured, dry substrate within the manufacturer's temperature window (commonly a 7-to-14-day minimum cure), and the manufacturer's instructions control under ORSC R102.4. Masonry mortar follows the 90-minute board life of ASTM C270, and grout lifts follow TMS 602 (fine grout to 5 feet, coarse to 4 feet, with cleanouts above those heights). The concrete mix design, reinforcement, curing, and the waterproofing assembly are detailed in the foundation and concrete-placement chapter and the structural chapter; this chapter sequences them in time.

**Code**

```
ACI 305.1 (hot weather) and ACI 306R (cold weather): placement temperature and curing windows.
ACI 318 and ACI 301: strength before form removal; backfill after design strength and lateral bracing.
ORSC R109.1.1: foundation inspection after reinforcement and embeds, before concrete placement.
ASTM C270 (90-minute mortar life) and TMS 602 (grout lift heights, cleanouts); temporary bracing per OSHA Subpart Q and the MCAA standard.
```

## 17.10 Dry-In: Roof And Openings

Drying in the house (roof, windows, and the water-resistive barrier) is scheduled into a single reliable weather window, because an open structure in the rain damages the work already done. Windows are verified for egress before ordering.

**Applied**

- Schedule roof tear-off or dry-in into the reliable dry window (mid-June to mid-October in western Oregon), and never open the structure with rain in the 48-hour forecast unless the dry-in is ready.
- Order windows and exterior doors at the framing-decision point (6-to-16-week lead times are verified at order), and confirm each specified unit meets the egress net-clear-opening dimensions before ordering, not just the rough-opening size.
- Flash windows and doors in the shingle-style sequence (sill flashing first, unit, jamb flashing over the flange, head flashing lapped under the barrier above) per ASTM E2112 and the manufacturer's instructions.
- Install kick-out flashings where a roof slope meets a vertical wall, and the ice barrier where the climate requires it.
- Use fall protection for any roof work above 6 feet, and a crane or boom truck rated at the required boom angle for truss and heavy-member lifts.

**Professional**

Roof coverings and flashings follow ORSC R905, gutters and downspouts follow R903, and the kick-out flashing at a roof-wall intersection is required by R903.2.1; the roofing manufacturer's listed system (underlayment, starter, nailing pattern, ridge ventilation) controls the warranty under R102.4. Window and door installation follows ASTM E2112 with AAMA 711/712 flashing products, and the manufacturer's rough-opening tolerance and fastening pattern govern the installation warranty. Egress openings meet ORSC R310 (5.7 square feet net clear, 24-inch height, 20-inch width, 44-inch maximum sill), and a unit that meets the rough opening but not the operable clear opening is a costly ordering error. Fall protection follows OR-OSHA Subpart M for any paid worker. The envelope control layers and the window and door details are carried by the envelope and interiors chapters.

**Code**

```
ORSC R905 (roof covering and flashing), R903 (drainage), R903.2.1 (kick-out flashing); manufacturer's listed roofing system controls the warranty (R102.4).
ASTM E2112 and AAMA 711/712: window/door installation and flashing; manufacturer rough-opening tolerance governs the warranty.
ORSC R310: emergency escape and rescue opening dimensions; verify the specified unit's operable clear opening before ordering.
OR-OSHA Subpart M: fall protection above 6 feet for paid workers.
```

## 17.11 Rough-In Sequence And The Inspection Cascade

After the house is dried in, the rough systems go in from largest to smallest, and the framing inspection cannot proceed until every rough-trade inspection is signed off. A missed inspection stalls the whole cascade.

**Applied**

Sequence the rough-in by clearance demand:

1. Plumbing drain-waste-vent and supply first (the heaviest pipes with the strictest slope), including vent penetrations for the roofer to flash.
2. Mechanical second (ducts, ventilator runs, refrigerant line-set sleeves with the brazing left for the certified technician, condensate, and exhaust).
3. Electrical third (branch circuits routed around the fixed plumbing and mechanical, boxes at finished-wall depth).
4. Low voltage last (structured cabling, coax, and control wiring, routed to keep the required separation from power).

Install firestopping and fireblocking before insulation, air-seal every penetration, and use only insulation-contact-rated recessed lights. Call the rough-trade inspections before the framing inspection, because framing cannot pass until they are signed off.

**Professional**

The inspection cascade under ORSC R109 runs foundation, rough plumbing, rough electrical, rough mechanical, framing (after all rough-ins and firestopping), insulation and air sealing, and drywall where required, then final; some jurisdictions combine steps, which is confirmed locally. The energy-code rough-in enables the later tests: the air-sealing detail sets up the blower-door result, and duct sealing sets up any duct-leakage requirement. The detailed plumbing, mechanical, electrical, and low-voltage designs are carried by their own chapters; this chapter fixes the order and the inspection dependencies, and the schedule is built with buffer because a failed inspection blocks everything downstream.

**Code**

```
ORSC R109: inspection cascade (foundation, rough plumbing/electrical/mechanical, framing, insulation/air-sealing, drywall, final); framing gated by all rough-trade sign-offs.
ORSC R302 firestopping and fireblocking installed before insulation.
Energy-code rough-in (air sealing, duct sealing, IC-AT recessed lights) enables the blower-door and duct-leakage results at insulation and final.
```

## 17.12 Insulation, Slab, And Cover Readiness

Insulation, the radiant topping slab, and the wet-area and fire-rated assemblies are installed and inspected before cover, and the slab moisture is verified before any moisture-sensitive floor. These steps set up the finish work carried by the interiors chapter.

**Applied**

- Pressure-test the radiant tubing before the topping slab (1.5 times operating pressure, held and documented) and photograph the tubing before it is covered.
- Insulate to the climate-zone R-values after the framing and rough-trade inspections, and air-seal the assembly to the blower-door target.
- Test the slab moisture (ASTM F2170 in-situ relative humidity, at or below the flooring manufacturer's limit) before installing any moisture-sensitive floor.
- Install the wet-area backer and waterproofing membrane and the fire-rated garage-dwelling assembly to their listed systems, and the foam thermal barrier over any interior foam.
- Install and photograph the interconnected smoke and carbon monoxide alarms before final.

**Professional**

Insulation R-values follow ORSC N1102.1 by climate zone (the western Oregon valley is generally Climate Zone 4, with higher values at elevation and east of the Cascades), and the air-sealing detail is verified by the blower-door test on the BCD form before the certificate of occupancy. The topping slab, slab moisture testing, and finish-floor readiness are detailed in the interiors chapter, as are the wet-area waterproofing assembly, the fire-rated separation, and the foam thermal barrier. The radiant tubing, insulation, and slab work are sequenced here so the pressure test and the pre-cover photographs happen before the pour and the inspection.

**Code**

```
ORSC N1102.1: insulation R-values by climate zone; blower-door result on the BCD form before the certificate of occupancy.
ASTM F2170: slab in-situ relative-humidity test before moisture-sensitive flooring (see the interiors chapter).
ORSC R302.6 (garage-dwelling separation), R316.4 (foam thermal barrier), R314/R315 (smoke and CO alarms) installed and inspected before final.
```

## 17.13 Occupancy, Schedule, And Cost Discipline

Occupancy is a major milestone, not the end of the project, and it is reached through the certificate-of-occupancy checklist. The schedule and cost are tracked against dated quotes and the actual hours the build takes, which for an owner-builder run well beyond a contractor crew's.

**Applied**

- Reach the certificate of occupancy through the life-safety and utility checklist: alarms, egress, guards and handrails, GFCI and AFCI verification, energized permanent power, working water, waste, and heat, commissioned water heater and HVAC, potable well-water test and septic startup where applicable, and address numbers.
- Build the schedule backward from a target occupancy date with the land-use, permit, utility, and inspection lead times included, and add buffer for inspection re-work.
- Track cost against dated quotes (refresh within 30 days of order for meaningful items) and record the actual hours and cost per phase as the build's own labor database.
- Plan the licensed-specialist visits (refrigerant startup, well, septic, LP gas) as fixed points around the owner's own work.

**Professional**

The certificate of occupancy requires all inspections signed off, the energy documentation filed, and the required testing documented, and a temporary certificate is discretionary. A solo owner-builder takes roughly two to four times as long as a contractor crew for the same scope, so the schedule is built with the actual labor pool rather than a contractor timeline; published labor-hour references (from the home-builders' association and the residential estimating manuals) are starting points adjusted upward for the owner-builder learning curve. Phase durations and costs are verified at final scope and treated as dated facts. The occupancy documentation, the as-built record, and the maintenance handoff are carried by the documentation and maintenance chapter, and the inspection gates in detail by the inspection and approval chapter.

**Code**

```
ORSC R110: certificate of occupancy; life-safety and utility checklist complete, energy documentation filed, required testing documented.
Schedule built backward from occupancy with land-use, permit, utility, and inspection lead times and re-work buffer.
Cost and labor tracked against dated quotes and actual per-phase hours; refresh quotes within 30 days of order.
```
