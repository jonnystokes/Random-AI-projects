# Chapter 16: Materials, Suppliers, Procurement, And Substitution

This chapter turns the design into material orders without letting a sales counter, a shortage, a substitute product, or an old price quietly change the house. It covers the verification principle that keeps a specification durable, the substitution-authority framework that governs who may approve a change, a material-standards reference by trade, the owner-manufactured-component decision, the product-listing and documentation chain, jobsite chemical safety, energy incentives, and the supplier and procurement process.

The chapter separates the verifiable from the volatile. Material standards, product-listing rules, substitution authority, batch-ticket requirements, and hazard-communication rules are stable enough to specify in writing. Prices, lead times, supplier availability, rebate dollar amounts, and current product-listing numbers change too quickly to fix in the manuscript and are re-verified at the estimating pass and again at order time.

## 16.1 The Verification Principle

The specification names the standard the material must meet, not the brand that meets it today. A standard is durable across the design-to-build window; a brand and part number are one reformulation or one phone call away from being wrong.

**Simple**

Every block, pipe, wire, membrane, valve, and fastener becomes part of the house, and a wrong material can create a leak, a crack, or a failed inspection. The way to keep an order from drifting is to write down what the material has to meet (the standard), why it was chosen, and who is allowed to approve a change, then verify the actual product and price at the time of purchase.

**Applied**

Sort every material decision into three reference tiers:

- Permanent references (do not revisit per build): the consensus-standard families (ASTM, ACI, AWS, NSF/ANSI, IAPMO, AHRI, NEMA, AAMA/WDMA/CSA, UL, ICC-ES). These do not move quarter to quarter.
- Slow-moving references (revisit per code cycle): the Oregon code adoptions and their amendment tables. Revisit when the next adoption is announced.
- Volatile data (verify per build, never fixed in the spec text): unit prices, supplier minimums, lead times, rebate amounts, and current product-listing numbers. Treat any older estimate as a historical note until replaced by a dated quote.

Write the standard into the spec ("concrete conforming to ASTM C94/C94M, designed per ACI 318 and ACI 301, with batch tickets per ASTM C94 Section 14"), not the brand and mix number.

**Professional**

The material system connects design intent, code compliance, product listings, manufacturer instructions, supplier capability, installation method, warranty, inspection evidence, and future maintenance. A concrete house with a long-service goal depends on compatibility across the concrete, reinforcement, waterproofing, insulation, drainage, and mechanical systems, so a cheaper substitute is evaluated against the assembly it joins, not in isolation. The specification anchors to the standard so the document stays defensible while the market underneath it changes.

**Code**

```
Specify by standard, not brand: the consensus standard is the durable anchor; the product and price are verified at order.
ORSC R102.5: referenced standards are part of the code to the extent referenced.
Volatile data (price, lead time, rebate amount, current listing number): verified at the estimating pass and at order, not fixed in the specification.
```

## 16.2 Substitution Authority

A change to a specified material is approved only by the authority whose scope covers it, and there are five distinct authorities. Confusing them is the most common procurement error on an owner-builder job.

**Applied**

Match every substitution to its authority:

- The building official approves an alternative material, design, or method that departs from the prescriptive code (ORSC R104.11), on evidence of equivalence.
- The inspector enforces what the building official approved and cannot approve a field substitution; a change to an approved detail goes back through the building official.
- The design professional in responsible charge approves substitutions within the scope of any PE-stamped work before the building official accepts them.
- The manufacturer's warranty controls its listed assembly: substituting a fastener, primer, or accessory not in the installation instructions can void the warranty even where the substitute meets the standard.
- The installer carries independent professional liability for what they install for pay, even when the owner or designer approved the product.

Tag each material spec with its substitution tier: no substitution (Tier 1), substitution with written designer and manufacturer approval (Tier 2), equivalent product with documentation (Tier 3), or no approval required (Tier 4).

**Professional**

ORSC R104.11 makes the building official the only authority who can accept an alternative that departs from the prescriptive code, requiring it to be at least equivalent in quality, strength, effectiveness, fire resistance, durability, and safety, with test data under R104.11.1 or a documented history of successful use. A denial can be escalated to the Building Codes Division for a statewide alternate-method ruling under OAR 918-008-0095, which then binds every Oregon jurisdiction but takes months; prescriptive paths and the code's alternative appendices are used first, and statewide petitions are reserved for what the design genuinely requires. ORSC R102.4 gives the manufacturer's listing precedence where a code provision would violate the listing, which is the rule that makes warranty-critical assemblies (waterproofing, fenestration, roofing, manufactured veneer) name the listed accessory by part number and document any substitution with the designer and the manufacturer's representative.

**Code**

```
ORSC R104.11 and R104.11.1: building official authority over alternative materials/methods; equivalence by test data or documented history of use.
OAR 918-008-0095 and 918-008-0110: statewide alternate-method ruling; binds all Oregon jurisdictions once issued.
ORSC R106.3.4: design professional in responsible charge governs substitutions within stamped scope.
ORSC R102.4 (Exception): manufacturer's listing and instructions control where a code provision would violate the listing.
```

## 16.3 Material Standards Reference By Trade

Each trade's materials anchor to a family of consensus standards. The specification cites these; the specific product and lot number live in the submittal log, not the manuscript. This reference lets the reader write a durable spec and verify a submittal without opening each standard.

**Code**

```
Concrete and reinforcement:
  ASTM C94/C94M: ready-mixed concrete (batch-ticket content in Section 14).
  ACI 318 and ACI 301: structural concrete design and specification; ACI 117 tolerances; ACI 305.1/306R hot- and cold-weather concreting.
  ASTM C150/C595/C1157: cement. ASTM C33 aggregates. ASTM C494/C260 admixtures. ASTM C618/C989/C1240 supplementary cementitious materials.
  Reinforcement: ASTM A615 (billet), A706 (low-alloy, required by ACI 318 for special seismic), A1064 (welded wire); epoxy-coated A775/A934; stainless A955.

Masonry:
  ASTM C90: load-bearing concrete masonry units (specify weight class and strength).
  ASTM C270 mortar (type S typical structural), C476 grout; TMS 402 (code) and TMS 602 (specification) via OSSC.

Structural steel:
  ASTM A992 (W-shapes), A500 (HSS), F1554 (anchor bolts), F3125 (high-strength bolts).
  AWS D1.1 (structural welding), AWS D1.4 (reinforcing steel welding); AISC 360 (design).
  Galvanizing: ASTM A123 (products), A153 (hardware), A653 (sheet, G60/G90).

Anchors and fasteners:
  ICC-ES AC193 (mechanical anchors), AC308 (adhesive anchors); require a current ICC-ES Evaluation Service Report (ESR) referencing ACI 318 Chapter 17.
  Wood connectors per NDS-2024 and manufacturer ESR; stainless fasteners ASTM F593/F594 (grade 304/316 by exposure).
```

```
Waterproofing, flashing, and sealants:
  ASTM D6135 (self-adhering sheet), C836 (hot-applied), D7832 (fluid-applied) foundation waterproofing; ORSC R406 governs where waterproofing vs. dampproofing applies.
  ASTM E2112 (window/door/skylight installation practice); AAMA 711/712 (flashing); ASTM C920 (elastomeric sealants); UL 1479/ASTM E814 (firestop).
  WRB verified by ASTM E2178 (air permeance) and E2357 (assembly); ORSC R703 requires a water-resistive barrier.

Insulation:
  ASTM C665 (mineral-fiber batt), C578 (XPS/EPS), C1289 (polyiso), C739 (cellulose), C612 (mineral wool board); spray foam with a thermal barrier per ORSC R316; R-values per ORSC Chapter 11.

Fenestration:
  AAMA/WDMA/CSA 101/I.S.2/A440 (NAFS): Performance Class and Grade. NFRC 100/200 labels (U-factor, SHGC) for energy compliance. Fire-rated assemblies NFPA 252/UL 10C (doors), NFPA 257/UL 9 (windows).

Plumbing:
  Lead-free: NSF/ANSI/CAN 61 (health effects) and 372 (0.25% weighted lead) on every potable-water product; federal SDWA Section 1417.
  PEX ASTM F876/F877; copper ASTM B88 (lead-free solder B32); backflow ASSE 1013/1015/1024; water heaters ANSI Z21.10.1 (gas)/UL 174 (electric).
  Oregon adopts the UPC via the OPSC; verify product acceptance at submittal.

Electrical:
  NEC 110.3 listing requirement; ORS 479.530 certified electrical product via an NRTL (UL, ETL, CSA).
  Conductors UL 83/719; conduit UL 6/797/651; devices UL 498 (receptacles), 489 (breakers), 943 (GFCI), 1699 (AFCI); surge UL 1449.
  Low-voltage cabling NEC Articles 722/725/760/770/800 (see the smart-home chapter).

HVAC:
  AHRI Directory listing (with the AHRI reference number on the submittal) for any energy-code or rebate claim; SEER2/EER2/HSPF2 ratings (effective January 1, 2023).
  ERV/HRV per AHRI 1060 and HVI certification; ductwork per SMACNA and UL 181; filters per ASHRAE 52.2 (MERV).

Interior finishes:
  Gypsum ASTM C1396; cement board ASTM C1325; tile per ANSI A108/A118 and the TCNA Handbook; waterproofing membranes ANSI A118.10.
  Cabinetry ANSI/KCMA A161.1 with TSCA Title VI formaldehyde compliance; paint VOC per the SCAQMD reference limits and low-emission certification.

ICC-ES Evaluation Service Reports:
  For any non-prescriptive product, cite the ESR (or IAPMO UES, Intertek CCRR, DrJ TER) number and retain a copy current at the install date; the building official has discretion to accept a private evaluation service.
```

## 16.4 Owner-Manufactured Components: CMU Make-Versus-Buy

An owner-builder can manufacture concrete masonry units on site with a block press, and Oregon permits it when the units are tested to the masonry-unit standard and the production is documented and accepted by the building official. This is a make-versus-buy decision with a real testing and approval path, not a shortcut around it.

**Applied**

If owner-manufactured CMU is considered:

- Hold a pre-application meeting with the building official for the specific county before committing, and get the accepted testing program, lot definition, and sampling frequency in writing.
- Plan a testing program to the masonry-unit standard (ASTM C90), with unit sampling and testing (ASTM C140) by an accredited laboratory.
- Design the dry-cast mix to reach the required unit strength and absorption class, and cure the units under the manufacturer's or the lab-verified process.
- Document each production lot (mix, date, cure, dimensions, and test results) as the acceptance evidence.
- Compare the realistic per-block cost and production rate against commercial CMU delivered to the site before deciding.

**Professional**

ORSC R606 (reinforced masonry) and TMS 402 govern masonry structural design, and owner-manufactured units enter that path through the building official's authority to accept test reports (ORSC R104.4) and require tests (R104.11.1) rather than through a formal special-inspection program, which is an OSSC concept. The controlling unit values (ASTM C90 strength and absorption limits by density class, and the ASTM C140 per-lot sampling counts) are confirmed against the purchased current-edition standards before the normative production table is finalized, and the specific county's building official and environmental-health acceptance is confirmed at the pre-application meeting. The concrete batching for owner-manufactured units overlaps the on-site mixing covered in the foundation and concrete-placement chapter, and the block-press and curing logistics are covered with the equipment planning in the build-sequence chapter.

**Code**

```
ORSC R606 and TMS 402/602: reinforced masonry design and specification (via OSSC).
ASTM C90: load-bearing concrete masonry units; ASTM C140: unit sampling and testing (confirm per-lot counts and limit values against the purchased current edition before writing the production table).
ORSC R104.4 and R104.11.1: building official acceptance of test reports and authority to require tests; confirm the accepted testing program at a pre-application meeting for the project county.
```

## 16.5 Product Listing, Labels, And The Documentation Chain

A material's compliance is proved by an unbroken document chain from specification to closeout. The label disappears into the work, so the photograph taken before cover is often the only remaining evidence.

**Applied**

Carry each material through the chain:

- At specification: cite the standard and the listing (for example, "Type X gypsum, ASTM C1396, UL-listed for the U419 assembly").
- At submittal: submit the product cut sheet showing the standard, listing mark, and any ICC-ES number; the designer or building official approves.
- At delivery: verify the product matches the submittal and photograph the batch numbers and listing labels.
- At install: keep the packaging label until the relevant inspection is signed off; photograph it before it is covered.
- At inspection: record the date and the products the inspector verified.
- At closeout: assemble the binder.

**Professional**

The closeout binder is the defensible record of how the house was built, and it holds the approved construction documents, the submittal approvals, the ICC-ES reports current at install, every concrete batch ticket, the product cut sheets and label photographs, the AHRI and NFRC labels, the equipment startup reports, the warranty registrations, the alternate-method approvals, the inspection records, and the required test reports (concrete strength, soil bearing, blower door, and the energy-compliance forms). Batch tickets are retained in full because they are the substantive evidence of what was delivered if a structural question arises; the paper copies fade, so each is photographed on site and stored digitally. The binder is kept as a paper copy in a fireproof location and as a digital copy in two independent locations, and it is the evidence the business-and-brand and documentation chapters rely on.

**Code**

```
ASTM C94 Section 14: concrete delivery-ticket content; ACI 301 requires slump, air, and temperature verification at 1.5 hours regardless of the stated discharge-time limit.
Closeout binder: approved documents, submittals, ESRs, batch tickets, label photographs, AHRI/NFRC labels, startup reports, warranties, alternate-method approvals, inspection records, and required test reports.
Retain paper (fireproof) plus digital in two independent locations.
```

## 16.6 Jobsite Chemical Safety: SDS And Hazard Communication

The moment a paid worker steps onto the site, Oregon's hazard-communication rules attach, and several common construction chemicals carry real exposure hazards. For the owner's own labor the rules are not a regulatory requirement, but the safety-data-sheet information is still how the exposure is managed.

**Applied**

- Where any paid helper, employee, or subcontractor is on site, maintain a written hazard-communication program, a chemical inventory, and a safety data sheet for every hazardous chemical, accessible during all shifts (a digital copy is acceptable if it opens without an internet connection).
- Read the safety data sheet for storage temperature, shelf life, ventilation, personal protective equipment, and disposal before using a product.
- Use the manufacturer-specified respiratory protection and reentry time for spray polyurethane foam.
- Control respirable silica when cutting, drilling, or grinding concrete or masonry with water suppression or local exhaust, and use the specified respiratory protection.
- Use eye protection and acid-resistant gloves for masonry acids and skin protection for two-part epoxies.

**Professional**

OR-OSHA's Hazard Communication standard (OAR 437 Division 2/Z and Division 3/D, GHS-aligned since 2012) requires the written program, inventory, safety data sheets, labels, and worker training whenever workers other than the owner are on site; a federal 2024 update to GHS Revision 7 is being evaluated by OR-OSHA, so the 2012 baseline remains the enforced training level as of writing. The respirable-crystalline-silica standard (29 CFR 1926.1153, enforced by OR-OSHA under OAR 437 Division 3) requires Table 1 controls for concrete and masonry cutting and grinding. The safety data sheet's sections on handling, exposure controls, and disposal drive the practical decisions (foam reentry time, admixture alkali-burn protection, coating ventilation and ignition control, and epoxy skin sensitization).

**Code**

```
OAR 437 Division 2/Z and Division 3/D: OR-OSHA Hazard Communication (written program, inventory, SDS access, GHS labels, training) where any paid worker is on site.
29 CFR 1926.1153 (OR-OSHA OAR 437 Division 3): respirable crystalline silica; Table 1 controls for concrete/masonry cutting and grinding.
Safety data sheet (16-section HCS format): storage, shelf life, ventilation, PPE, and disposal per Sections 7, 8, 9, 13, and 16.
```

## 16.7 Energy Incentives

Incentive programs and their dollar amounts are the most volatile content in the chapter, so the specification captures the eligibility framework and defers every dollar figure to the estimating pass. Equipment is specified to keep the incentive doors open rather than committed to one program.

**Applied**

- Specify equipment that is AHRI-listed and, where the category has it, ENERGY STAR-certified, which keeps most incentive paths open at a small cost premium.
- Build an equipment-decision spreadsheet at estimating with three columns: AHRI/ENERGY STAR status, current incentive eligibility per program (pulled from current program documents), and net post-incentive cost.
- Do not budget any specific rebate dollar amount into a build until the administering agency confirms the owner-builder install pattern qualifies in writing.
- Confirm program availability at the time of the build; funding tranches can close mid-quarter.

**Professional**

The federal Section 25C and 25D residential energy credits ended for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, so a build completed in 2026 or later cannot claim them, and no older source that assumes otherwise is relied on. The IRA-funded HOMES and HEAR rebate programs are state-administered through the Oregon Department of Energy with a phased launch and generally require an enrolled contractor, which makes the owner-builder pattern awkward for some measures and worth confirming with the agency before budgeting. The state Heat Pump Purchase Program and the utility-funded Energy Trust of Oregon incentives continue independent of the federal sunset, with Energy Trust offering measure-based incentives that update at least annually. The durable rule is to specify to the standards and pull the current incentive documents at estimating; the frameworks are stable, the dollars and funding flags are not.

**Code**

```
Federal Section 25C and 25D: ended for property placed in service after December 31, 2025 (do not budget for 2026+ builds).
IRA HOMES and HEAR (Oregon Department of Energy): eligibility framework stable; enrolled-contractor requirement and owner-builder qualification verified with the agency before budgeting.
State Heat Pump Purchase Program and Energy Trust of Oregon: continue; specific amounts and eligibility verified at estimating.
Specify AHRI-listed and ENERGY STAR-certified equipment to preserve eligibility.
```

## 16.8 Suppliers, Procurement, And Buy-Versus-Rent

Supplier names, lead times, and minimums change too quickly to fix in the manuscript, so the chapter captures the procurement process and the buy-versus-rent framework. Anyone paid to do licensed work on the site carries the corresponding license and workers' compensation.

**Applied**

- At estimating, confirm for each material category: which plant or distributor serves the site, the standard compliance held, the minimum order and delivery terms, and the current lead time.
- Order long-lead items (fenestration, custom steel, special-order masonry, fabricated reinforcement) early and stage the balance to the pour and rough-in schedule.
- Buy tools used across more than one phase with low storage cost; rent equipment used once or twice with high storage and maintenance cost; hire-with-operator for crane, large excavation, and concrete pumping, where the operator carries the certification and insurance.
- Ensure anyone paid to perform CCB-licensed work holds a CCB license, and route unskilled labor through a temp agency that carries workers' compensation.

**Professional**

Ready-mix, aggregate, masonry, reinforcement, and structural steel are served by regional producers and fabricators; the balance of trades is served by national wholesalers with Oregon branches. The verification at estimating is the plant or distributor that serves the specific site, the mix designs or gradations they hold compliance for, the minimums and delivery cadence, and the return policy for unused stock. Rental yards that deliver and operate equipment are providing a service, so the operator's employer carries the workers' compensation and OR-OSHA training; hiring a friend to help without an agency is the workers'-compensation exposure documented in the legal chapter. The equipment selection for owner-operated machines is detailed with the build-sequence chapter.

**Code**

```
Procurement: verify plant/distributor, standard compliance, minimums, and lead time at estimating; order long-lead items early.
Buy-versus-rent: buy multi-phase low-storage tools; rent single-use high-storage equipment; hire-with-operator for crane, large excavation, and pumping.
CCB licensing: anyone paid to perform licensed work holds a CCB license; temp-agency labor carries workers' compensation (see the legal chapter).
```
