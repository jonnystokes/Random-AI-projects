# Chapter 20: Documentation, Maintenance, And Future Upgrades

This chapter covers owning the house after the build: the records to keep and why, the project binder and its backup, warranty documentation, the maintenance program, the budget contingency and maintenance reserve, technology replacement planning, the provisions that make future upgrades cheap, the documentation future owners and responders need, and how to handle a pause or an incident. The house is designed for a long service life, but it still needs records, maintenance, and a way for future people to understand what was built.

Some of this is legally consequential in Oregon and some is practice guidance. The first sale of the house requires the permit record verbatim, several sale disclosures draw on the binder, and one structural-code exemption records into the deed. The maintenance intervals and technology planning bands are recommendations anchored to industry sources, not requirements, and every question of sale timing, insurance, or tax is routed to the relevant professional rather than answered here.

## 20.1 The Record As The House's Memory

The project binder is the memory of the house, and it should not depend on one person's recollection, one laptop, one installer, or one paper folder. The record is useful during construction, at move-in, during repairs, during an insurance claim, and at resale.

**Simple**

The walls, pipes, wires, drains, and equipment outlive the moment they were installed, and if no one records where things are and why choices were made, future maintenance becomes guessing. The binder is the house's instructions for its future self: what was permitted, what was installed, where the hidden things run, and what needs service.

**Applied**

Document these record areas:

| Record area | What it helps with |
|---|---|
| Property and permits | Ownership, approvals, inspections, and the first sale |
| Design | What the house was meant to be |
| As-built | What was installed and where |
| Materials and warranties | Products, suppliers, and replacement parts |
| Systems | Plumbing, electrical, HVAC, network, and shutoffs |
| Testing | What was tested, when, by whom, and what failed |
| Maintenance | What needs service, cleaning, inspection, or replacement |

**Professional**

The binder is built during construction (the materials and inspection chapters describe the closeout binder it grows from) and carried for the life of the house. Its most valuable content is the record that cannot be recovered later: the permit numbers, the as-built routes, the test results, and the photographs of every concealed condition before it was covered. The record habit is what makes any later professional consultation cheaper, because the professional starts from fact rather than from an excavation.

**Code**

```
The project binder carries forward from the closeout binder in the materials and inspection chapters.
Highest-value records are the unrecoverable ones: permit numbers, as-built routes, test results, and pre-cover photographs.
```

## 20.2 Records To Keep Permanently

Oregon gives the owner-builder a concrete reason to keep the permit record forever: the first sale of the house requires the permit numbers stated verbatim, and any later sale draws the seller's disclosure answers from the binder. The keep-forever list is short and specific.

**Applied**

Keep permanently:

- The building permit numbers and issuing jurisdiction (required word-for-word at the first sale).
- The inspection record and the certificate of occupancy.
- The approved, stamped plan set and every revision, and the engineering documents (calculations, general notes, and any special-observation letters).
- The energy-path documents (the measure-selection form and the blower-door and duct results).
- The septic record (the construction permit, the as-built diagram, and the completion certificate) and the well record (the log, the pump record, and the initial water panel).
- The utility service records, the product approvals and evaluation reports relied on at plan review, all test reports, and the photo archive of concealed work.
- Any structural-code-exemption permit that was recorded into the deed.

**Professional**

The first sale of a never-occupied dwelling is excluded from the full seller's property disclosure only if the seller gives the buyer the statutory statement naming the building permit numbers and the issuing jurisdiction (ORS 105.464 and the ORS 105.470 exclusion). Any later sale requires the full disclosure form answered from actual knowledge, where a "yes" answer often requires attaching the underlying report, so the binder becomes the seller's defense file (ORS 105.462 to 105.490). Oregon's statute of repose for construction claims runs a decade-scale clock (ORS 12.135; confirm the exact period), and warranty, insurance, and resale value never expire, so "permanent" is the honest retention recommendation. The jurisdiction keeps its own official copies, but retrieval decades later is not guaranteed, which is why the owner's copy is the reliable one.

**Code**

```
ORS 105.464 and 105.470: first sale of a never-occupied dwelling excluded from full disclosure only if the seller provides the statutory permit-number statement (building permit numbers and issuing jurisdiction, verbatim).
ORS 105.462–105.490: later sales require the full seller's property disclosure from actual knowledge; "yes" answers may require attaching the report.
ORS 12.135: statute of repose for construction claims (decade-scale; confirm the exact period).
Legally required at sale: the disclosure or first-sale permit statement, and the well-water testing results (see the testing chapter).
```

## 20.3 The Selling-Too-Soon Caution

The owner-builder exemption rests on owner-occupancy intent, so developing a house with intent to sell can convert the owner into a contractor requiring a license. This is a legal risk pattern to understand and route to an Oregon attorney, not a timeline to plan around here.

**Professional**

Oregon treats a property owner who develops a structure with intent to sell as a contractor or developer requiring CCB licensure; the owner-builder exemption depends on building for one's own occupancy, and the legal chapter documents that framework. Separately, where the owner-builder uses the narrow structural-code exemptions available under ORS 455.320 (for certain ceiling-height, room-size, and temperature items), the permit noting that noncompliance is recorded with the county clerk into the permanent deed record, so that record travels with the title and is kept accordingly. The manuscript states these statutes and the risk pattern; the question of how soon a particular house can be sold is a legal question routed to an Oregon attorney, because the time-window presumptions are fact-specific and change.

**Code**

```
ORS 701.005/701.010: developing a structure with intent to sell can require CCB licensure; the owner-builder exemption rests on owner-occupancy intent (see the legal chapter).
ORS 455.320: structural-code exemptions require the permit noting noncompliance to be recorded with the county clerk into the deed record.
Sale-timing questions are routed to an Oregon attorney; this chapter does not give legal advice.
```

## 20.4 The Project Binder

The binder is one physical set of originals plus a full digital mirror, protected by a three-copy backup rule so a single fire or theft does not erase the house's history. It is organized by system and named consistently so a future user can find a record without the person who built it.

**Applied**

- Keep one physical binder of originals (permits, certificate of occupancy, stamped plans, and warranties) in a fire-resistant box, and a full digital mirror organized by system folder (site, foundation, structure, envelope, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, energy, smart home, and administration).
- Follow the three-two-one backup rule: three copies, on two kinds of media, with one off-site (a local drive, a network drive, and one encrypted cloud copy).
- Name files by date, system, and document, and label every photo with location, date, and phase; the pre-cover photographs are the most valuable records.
- Record a narrated video walkthrough before insulation and again before drywall.
- Secure the digital archive (encryption, unique passwords with multi-factor authentication) and leave a "where everything is" note so the archive survives its creator.

**Professional**

No regulation sets a binder standard, so this is practice guidance built on the three-two-one backup principle, which exists for exactly the lightning strike or fire that destroys both the house server and the local backup at once. The digital mirror is organized so a contractor about to open a wall, a buyer's inspector, or the household's next steward can find the relevant record, and the owner-drawn as-builts (the utility trench routes with depths and photos, the septic and well coordinates, and the in-slab radiant and conduit maps) are cheap at build time and irreplaceable later. The binder is the owner handoff, and its extracts serve the audiences described later in the chapter.

**Code**

```
Practice guidance: three-two-one backup (three copies, two media, one off-site); physical originals in a fire-resistant box plus a full digital mirror by system.
Owner-drawn as-builts recommended: trench routes with depths and photos, septic/well coordinates, in-slab radiant and conduit maps.
Panel circuit identification is a code requirement (NEC 408.4); other as-builts are recommended, not required.
```

## 20.5 Warranty And Manufacturer Documentation

Equipment warranties condition coverage on correct installation, documented startup, and proof of required maintenance, so a one-page record per product keeps the coverage intact. The record is assembled at installation, not reconstructed at the claim.

**Applied**

For each major product, keep a one-page record with the model and serial, the purchase date and receipt, the installer and license number, the registration confirmation, the startup or commissioning sheet, and the service log. Register the product within the manufacturer's stated window, keep the installation to the manufacturer's instructions, and keep the maintenance records (annual HVAC service, water-heater flush and anode records) that the warranty requires.

**Professional**

Residential equipment warranties follow a pattern (confirmed per selected product at specification time): registration within a window often activates or extends coverage, coverage is conditioned on installation per instructions and documented startup (heat pumps in particular), and a claim requires the model, serial, proof of purchase, and installer identity. Written consumer warranties are governed federally by the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, which limits tie-in service restrictions (confirm the specifics before stating them). The per-product record template belongs in the project-binder appendix, and the manufacturer startup procedures collected here are the same ones the testing chapter routes to a certified installer where certification is required.

**Code**

```
Warranty pattern (verify per product): registration window; coverage conditioned on installation per instructions, documented startup, and proof of required maintenance; claim requires model, serial, proof of purchase, and installer identity.
Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act governs written consumer warranties (confirm specifics before stating them).
Per-product record: model, serial, purchase date/receipt, installer and license, registration, startup sheet, and service log.
```

## 20.6 Maintenance: Replacement Horizons And The Recurring Calendar

Maintenance has two layers: the replacement horizons that say when a component reaches end of life, and the recurring tasks that keep components reaching that horizon. The horizons are anchored to an industry life-expectancy source and are guidelines, not warranties.

**Applied**

Plan replacement against representative horizons (assuming recommended maintenance):

| Component | Representative horizon |
|---|---|
| Tank water heater | About 10 years (tankless 20 or more) |
| Furnace / heat pump / AC | 15 to 20 / about 16 / 10 to 15 years |
| Aluminum gutters | About 20 years (copper 50 or more) |
| Bituminous foundation waterproofing | About 10 years (and compromised immediately if cracked) |
| Security systems and heat/smoke detectors | 5 to 10 years |
| Concrete footings and foundation | Lifetime, if properly built |

Run the recurring calendar season by season: clean gutters twice a year (more under conifers), check the roof and flashing annually and after wind events, walk exterior sealants annually, survey concrete and masonry cracks annually with logged widths, verify footing-drain outlets and grading each fall, exercise plumbing shutoffs and the temperature-and-pressure valve annually, function-test pumps quarterly, test GFCI, AFCI, and smoke and CO devices monthly, change HVAC filters every one to three months, and confirm the radon fan monthly on its manometer.

**Professional**

The replacement horizons are drawn from the InterNACHI standard life-expectancy chart, which states plainly that its values assume recommended maintenance and are guidelines rather than warranties; one figure from that chart (a fifty-year life for wireless networks and home automation) is not used, because vendor support ends long before the hardware fails (the technology section carries the real planning bands). The recurring-task intervals are practice guidance published as a season-by-season calendar. Roof flashing connections, not the roof surface, are the usual failure point, so the seasonal roof check focuses there; surge protective devices are sacrificial and are replaced after major events; and paint life is driven by preparation and product quality, so exposed elevations are inspected annually rather than on a fixed clock.

**Code**

```
Replacement horizons anchored to the InterNACHI standard life-expectancy chart (guidelines assuming recommended maintenance, not warranties).
Do not repeat the chart's fifty-year figure for wireless networks/automation; use the technology planning bands.
Recurring-task intervals are practice guidance published as a season-by-season calendar.
```

## 20.7 Recurring Outside-Party Inspections

Several tests recur on a schedule set by a code, a utility, a warranty, or an insurer rather than by the owner. The program tracks who can demand proof and what proof satisfies them.

**Professional**

Backflow assemblies are tested annually by a certified tester (from the testing chapter); septic pumping and inspection cadence, and the mandatory service contracts for alternative-treatment and sand-filter systems, are DEQ and county specific; well-water testing is mandatory at sale and recommended annually; and radon retesting follows the EPA and AARST cycle. Smoke and CO alarm maintenance follows the manufacturer and the statutes that apply at a rental or sale transition. A utility may run a backflow program or a net-metering interconnection inspection where solar is added, and a warranty may require annual professional HVAC service. Insurers in Oregon increasingly require wood-stove, roof-age, and wildfire-mitigation inspections; those are policy-level facts confirmed with the insurer. The specific county, utility, warranty, and policy requirements are verified rather than assumed.

**Code**

```
Backflow: annual certified-tester test (testing chapter). Septic: pumping/service-contract cadence per DEQ/county. Well: mandatory at sale, recommended annually.
Radon: retest per EPA/AARST cycle. Smoke/CO: manufacturer plus statutes at rental/sale transitions.
Utility (backflow program, solar interconnection), warranty (annual HVAC service), and insurer (wood-stove, roof-age, wildfire) requirements verified with the counterparty.
```

## 20.8 Budget Contingency And The Maintenance Reserve

A build carries a contingency reserve that shrinks as the design matures, and an owner-builder carries the high end because owner-builder overruns run larger than a professional builder's. Ongoing maintenance is funded by a component sinking fund built from the replacement horizons rather than by a single percentage.

**Applied**

- Carry a design/feasibility contingency of roughly 15 to 25 percent early, stepping down to 5 to 10 percent at complete construction documents and 3 to 5 percent during construction.
- Structure the reserve as three labeled buckets: owner scope-change, construction-unknowns, and design, and track drawdowns like a budget line.
- Plan for the owner-builder overrun reality by carrying the high end of the range.
- Fund ongoing maintenance with a component sinking fund built from the replacement horizons (set aside each year the annual share of each component's replacement cost).

**Professional**

Standard planning contingency runs 5 to 10 percent for well-documented new construction and 10 to 20 percent for complex or high-risk work, higher at early design and lower as documents mature; the owner-builder evidence (professional-builder overruns of 5 to 15 percent versus owner-builder overruns up to 30 percent) supports carrying the high end. The three-bucket structure follows the guidance that a single undifferentiated contingency invites overruns and disputes. Schedule, rework, seasonal, and inspection-delay reserves have no citable percentage, so they are planned as mechanisms (pours and dry-in scheduled around the wet season, a one-inspection-cycle buffer per phase, and a predefined pause procedure) rather than numbers. The common one-to-four-percent-of-value maintenance reserve is not printed as a rule without a source; the defensible alternative is the component sinking fund, which the binder can compute honestly from the horizons.

**Code**

```
Contingency (publish as ranges, per phase): 5–10% well-documented new construction, 10–20% complex; 15–25% early design → 5–10% at complete documents → 3–5% during construction.
Owner-builder overruns run up to ~30% versus 5–15% for professional builders; carry the high end.
Three buckets: owner scope-change, construction-unknowns, design; track drawdowns.
Maintenance reserve: a component sinking fund from the replacement horizons, not a printed percentage without a source.
```

## 20.9 Technology Replacement Cycles

Consumer network gear, hubs, cameras, and cloud-dependent devices reach end of life when vendor support and standards move on, not when the hardware wears out. The plan uses support-driven planning bands, checked at purchase.

**Professional**

Technology replacement is support-driven rather than wear-driven, and the planning bands are published with an "as of 2026, verify at purchase" tag rather than as fixed lifespans or year-specific product recommendations. Security systems and smoke and heat detectors run 5 to 10 years, hubs and controllers about 5 to 7 years (a 2019-or-earlier hub typically never received the current interoperability standard), uninterruptible-power-supply batteries 3 to 5 years, network video-recorder drives 3 to 5 years under continuous write, access points and routers and switches 5 to 8 years, cameras 5 to 8 years, and mains-powered smart switches 10 or more years. Locks are the split case: the hardware runs 10 or more years, the batteries run months, and the electronics are support-limited. The interoperability standard's own churn (a border-router certification rule that changed at the start of 2026) illustrates why the bands are support-driven and are re-checked at purchase.

**Code**

```
Technology end-of-life is support-driven (vendor firmware and standards), not wear-driven; publish planning bands "as of 2026, verify at purchase," no year-specific product recommendations.
Representative bands: security/detectors 5–10 yr; hubs/controllers 5–7 yr; UPS batteries and NVR drives 3–5 yr; APs/routers/switches and cameras 5–8 yr; mains smart switches 10+ yr; locks hardware 10+ yr / batteries months / electronics support-limited.
```

## 20.10 Smart-Home Backup And Vendor-Sunset Defense

The smart-home stack is protected by encrypted backups on the three-two-one rule, by local-control device choices that survive a vendor outage, and by keeping the pairing and account records that a rebuild needs. The detailed architecture is in the smart-home chapter; here it is the ownership-phase discipline.

**Applied**

- Run automated encrypted controller backups on the three-two-one rule, and test a full restore onto spare hardware once a year.
- Keep the controller configuration in version control (or rely on the controller's snapshot lineage).
- Choose devices that work locally (a local mesh radio or a local-API connection) so automations survive an internet or vendor outage, and prefer open-protocol or interoperability-certified devices.
- Save every device's pairing and setup codes in a password manager, and record each device's account and firmware version in the binder.
- Never expose the controller directly to the internet; use a VPN or the controller's own cloud relay for remote access, with multi-factor authentication.

**Professional**

A mature local-first controller ships automated encrypted scheduled backups with off-site targets and restores across hardware, which makes the three-two-one rule practical for the smart-home stack. The vendor-sunset defenses are to prefer interoperability-certified or open-protocol devices with local fallback, to save the pairing codes that a device rebuild requires, and to record the account and firmware state so the archive survives the vendor. The network segmentation and the consumer-IoT security baseline carry over from the smart-home and testing chapters.

**Code**

```
Practice guidance: automated encrypted controller backups on the three-two-one rule; annual full-restore test onto spare hardware.
Prefer local-control, open-protocol/interoperability-certified devices; save pairing/setup codes in a password manager; record account and firmware state.
Never expose the controller to the public internet; VPN or vendor cloud relay with multi-factor authentication (see the smart-home chapter).
```

## 20.11 Future-Upgrade Provisions

The cheapest future upgrade is the one the build left room for. Spare conduit, reserved panel capacity, kept-clear roof and wall zones, and accessible shutoffs turn a future addition into a connection instead of a demolition.

**Applied**

Leave these provisions:

- Spare conduit: at least a 1-inch run from the panel area to the attic and to the crawl space or basement, 2-inch sweeps from the service area to the garage (EV), roof (solar), and exterior mechanical pad, and site conduits to any gate or outbuilding trench while it is open.
- Service and panel capacity: size the service and leave labeled reserved breaker spaces for EV charging, a heat pump, and a battery.
- Solar and battery readiness: a penetration-free, reinforced south-roof mounting zone documented on the as-builts, and reserved wall space with clearances by the panel for an inverter and battery.
- A ventilated network-rack location on a dedicated circuit with conduit home-runs and spare patch capacity.
- Accessible shutoffs and listed equipment clearances documented on the emergency map, spare capped roof penetrations where a future flue is plausible, a water-treatment bypass loop with a drain and power stub, and an equipment-swap path sized into the doors and halls.
- Accessibility provisions: a zero-step-capable entry, grab-bar blocking in bathroom walls, and 36-inch door rough openings where they are cheap.

**Professional**

These are practice-guidance provisions with a few code anchors; the EV-ready and solar-ready provisions exist in Oregon's residential energy code, and the exact one- and two-family section numbers are verified at design. Empty conduit and reserved capacity cost little during construction and a great deal to retrofit through finished concrete, and the photo archive and as-builts are themselves the provision that makes any future remodel start from knowledge. Bidirectional charging and utility demand-response are moving targets, so the chapter's job is the conduit and the panel capacity, not the product.

**Code**

```
Provisions (practice guidance): spare conduit (panel-to-attic, panel-to-crawl, 2-inch sweeps to garage/roof/mechanical pad, site conduits), reserved and labeled panel capacity, solar/battery readiness zones, network rack, accessible shutoffs and clearances, and accessibility blocking.
EV-ready and solar-ready provisions exist in Oregon's residential energy code; confirm the one- and two-family section numbers at design.
```

## 20.12 Documentation For Future Parties And Handling A Pause

The binder produces a tailored extract for each future party, and a pause or incident is handled by process (weather-in, secure, document, and notify) with every decision routed to its professional. The book teaches the procedure, not the outcome.

**Applied**

Provide the right extract to each party:

- Buyers: the disclosure-supporting set (permits and certificate of occupancy, well and septic records, radon results, warranty transfers, and manuals).
- Tenants: an operating subset (system operation, filter schedule, emergency shutoffs, and alarm testing).
- Contractors: the as-builts, panel schedule, and the photo archive of the wall about to be opened.
- Emergency responders: a laminated one-page map at the panel showing the gas, water, and power shutoffs, the solar rapid-shutdown, the battery location, and the septic and well locations.
- Inspectors, insurers, and appraisers: the permits and approved plans, and the construction-quality dossier of specifications, engineering, photos, and receipts.

For a pause: weather-in and secure the structure, drain or winterize as needed, photograph the condition, notify the insurer, lender, and building official factually, and calendar the permit deadlines. For a failed inspection, get the correction in writing (the code section cited), fix it, re-inspect, and document.

**Professional**

The editorial rule is to describe processes and factual triggers, not outcomes or entitlements, and to route each domain to its professional: sale timing, liability, and liens to an Oregon attorney; insurance coverage during a vacancy to the insurer in writing; lender draw and completion terms to the lender; and injuries to medical care first and then the factual reporting duties. The book can teach the mothball procedure, the inspection-failure hygiene, and the records habit that makes any later consultation cheaper; it does not give medical, employment, or financial-planning advice beyond building the pause into the contingency structure. Oregon's developing landlord well-testing obligations for rentals on exempt wells are tracked where the house may be rented.

**Code**

```
Binder extracts by audience: buyers (disclosure set), tenants (operating subset), contractors (as-builts/panel/photos), emergency responders (laminated shutoff one-pager), inspectors/insurers/appraisers (quality dossier).
Pause procedure (practice guidance): weather-in, secure, winterize, photograph, notify insurer/lender/building official factually, calendar permit deadlines.
Route sale timing, liability, insurance, and lender questions to the relevant professional; the book teaches the procedure, not the outcome.
```
