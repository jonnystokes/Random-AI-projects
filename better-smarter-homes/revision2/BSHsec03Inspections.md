# Section 3 — Inspections

*When inspectors come, what they check, and how to pass*

Every major stage of the build ends with an inspection. The inspector is a county or state official who verifies the work meets building code before the next stage covers it up. Pass the inspection and you move on. Fail and you fix it, then re-inspect. Inspectors are generally professional and helpful — they want the home built right — but they do not forgive sloppy work or ignore code violations.

The owner-builder schedules each inspection after the relevant work is complete. Inspections are usually free (covered by the permit fee) but a failed inspection that requires re-inspection may incur a small additional fee. More importantly, a failed inspection delays the whole build because no further work can happen until the fix is verified.

This section lists each inspection in the order it occurs, what the inspector is looking for, and how to be ready before the inspector arrives.

---

## 3.1 Overview of the Inspection Sequence

**Plain language:** Inspections happen in a specific order, matching the order the house is built. First the dirt work, then the foundation, then the walls, then the systems inside the walls, then insulation, then finishes, then final sign-off. Missing an inspection or doing work out of order can force tearing things apart to inspect what was covered up too soon.

**Technical detail:** Standard inspection sequence for this design:

1. **Site / Setback inspection** (before excavation) — verifies the building location matches the approved plans
2. **Septic inspections** (separate permit track, runs parallel to building permit)
3. **Footing / Foundation inspection** (before concrete pour) — verifies excavation depth, rebar, and formwork
4. **Underground utility trench inspection** (before backfill) — verifies buried water and sewer lines coming into the building
5. **Underslab inspection** (before pouring the basement floor slab) — for this design, limited because no pipes run under the slab
6. **Framing / Structural inspection** (after walls are complete, before closing them up) — for this design, after the concrete block walls and steel/concrete floor assemblies
7. **Rough-in Plumbing inspection** (before insulation) — inspects all plumbing in the walls and service layer
8. **Rough-in Electrical inspection** (before insulation) — inspects all wiring in the walls and service layer
9. **Rough-in Mechanical inspection** (before insulation) — inspects HVAC, ductwork if any, and ventilation
10. **Insulation inspection** (before drywall or interior finish)
11. **Final Plumbing, Electrical, and Mechanical inspections** (after fixtures and devices are installed)
12. **Final Building inspection** (after all interior and exterior work complete)
13. **Certificate of Occupancy issued** (after all finals pass)

**How to schedule:**
- Most counties use an online portal (Oregon ePermitting) or a phone-in inspection request line
- Inspections are typically scheduled 24-48 hours in advance
- Inspector arrives in a 2-4 hour window on the scheduled day
- Owner-builder or representative should be on site to answer questions

**General tips for passing every inspection:**
- Have the approved plans on site at all times
- Keep the permit placard posted on site
- Photograph every stage of work for personal records
- Clean up the work area before the inspector arrives — a tidy site signals care
- Ask questions during the inspection — inspectors often share useful tips

**Key code references:**
- ORSC R109 (required inspections — building code inspections)
- ORS 455.055 (uniform permit, inspection and certificate of occupancy requirements)

## 3.2 Site and Setback Inspection

**Plain language:** Before any digging, an inspector verifies the building location is correct. The inspector checks that the stakes marking the building corners are the right distances from the property lines, roads, and any other required setbacks. This prevents a $200,000 home from being built in the wrong spot.

**Technical detail:** The inspector verifies:
- Setback distances from all property lines, roads, septic fields, wells, and any other required separations
- Property corner pins or stakes are in place
- Building corners are staked and the stakes match the approved site plan
- Any required tree protection, erosion control, or environmental measures are in place

**How to prepare:**
- Have the approved site plan on site
- Stake the building corners with string lines connecting them
- Have a tape measure on site to verify distances if requested
- Clear brush and flag any temporary features needed for the inspector to see the site clearly

**When this inspection is sometimes combined with the footing inspection:** In simple rural builds, the building official may combine site/setback verification with the footing inspection. Ask the local department when in doubt.

## 3.3 Footing and Foundation Inspection

**Plain language:** After digging the basement hole and setting up the forms for the concrete mat foundation, but before pouring the concrete, an inspector checks that the ground is properly prepared, the reinforcement steel is placed correctly, and the formwork matches the plans.

**Technical detail:** The inspector verifies:
- Excavation depth meets or exceeds the frost depth for the area (12-36 inches depending on climate zone)
- Excavation is on undisturbed native soil or properly compacted fill
- If imported fill was used, a geotechnical compaction report from an engineer must be provided
- Forms are erected, squared, and secured
- Rebar size, spacing, and overlap length match the approved plans
- Rebar chairs (small supports that keep rebar at the right height) are in place
- The U-FER ground (a copper conductor bonded to the rebar that becomes the home's electrical ground) is installed and tied to the rebar
- Any underslab plumbing or conduit that must pass through the foundation is sleeved properly (for this design, none)
- The capillary break layer (clean crushed rock under the slab that prevents moisture wicking up into the concrete) is in place with the specified thickness

**How to prepare:**
- Lay out rebar with calibrated spacing, tied at every intersection
- Photograph the rebar and formwork before the inspector arrives
- Have a concrete delivery scheduled for 24 hours after the inspection to allow for corrections
- Never schedule the concrete pour for the same day as the inspection — always give a full day of buffer

**Crucial note:** No concrete can be poured until this inspection passes. Inspectors are strict about this because once concrete is poured, the rebar is permanently hidden.

**Key code references:**
- ORSC R403 (footings — footing size and depth)
- ORSC R404 (foundation walls — reinforcement requirements)

## 3.4 Underground Utility Trench Inspection

**Plain language:** For this design, the only underground utility work is the pipes running from the house to the septic tank and field, and the incoming water line (either from a well or city service) coming up to the exterior interconnect box at the side of the house. All of it terminates at ground level or comes up into the exterior interconnect box — nothing buried under the slab. An inspector verifies these outside-the-house utility trenches before they get backfilled.

**Design-specific note:** Because this design routes all utilities through an exterior interconnect box above grade (a protected enclosure on the outside wall of the house where water, electrical, and sewer connections come together before entering the building), the utility entry into the home is entirely inspectable for the life of the building. No plumbing runs under the concrete slab. The only buried utilities are the trench runs from the property boundary or well/septic locations up to that exterior box.

**Technical detail:** The inspector verifies:
- Trench depth meets code (water lines below frost depth, typically 18-36 inches minimum)
- Pipe materials are code-approved (PEX, PVC, HDPE, copper depending on use)
- Pipe bedding (the material the pipe sits on and is covered by) is clean sand or fines-free gravel, not rocks or debris
- Warning tape is installed in the trench 12 inches above the pipe (a bright colored plastic tape that alerts future diggers)
- Tracer wire is included on non-metallic pipes (required for locate services to find the pipe underground later)
- Separation between water and sewer lines meets code (typically 10 feet horizontal or water above sewer with specific vertical separation)
- Slope of drain lines is correct (1/4 inch per foot minimum for 3-inch pipe)
- Cleanouts are provided at required locations
- Backflow prevention is installed where required (at the incoming water line)

**Sewer / drain lines running to the septic tank:**
- 4-inch schedule 40 PVC or ABS typical
- Slope: 1/4 inch per foot
- Cleanouts at the building, at any change of direction, and every 100 feet of straight run
- Properly bedded and backfilled

**Water supply line coming in:**
- 1-inch minimum (larger for high-flow designs like this one — plan for 1.5-inch minimum given the 2-inch interior trunk philosophy)
- Below frost depth
- Tracer wire required if non-metallic pipe
- Shutoff valve accessible

**Exterior interconnect box considerations (for this design):**
- Above-grade enclosure where incoming utilities terminate and transition into the building
- Functions like a commercial utility entrance
- All connections visible and accessible forever
- Weatherproof, pest-proof, and insulated
- Treated as part of the building for inspection purposes
- Allows every utility to be shut off, serviced, and replaced without excavation

**How to prepare:**
- Keep the trench open until inspected
- Have manufacturer documentation on hand for all pipe and fitting materials
- Leave tape measures in the trench at the inspector's expected points of interest (pipe depth, separation distance, warning tape height)
- Do not backfill until inspection passes

**Key code references:**
- OPSC 609 (water service piping — materials and installation)
- OPSC 717 (building sewer — materials and installation)
- ORSC R403.1 (foundation drain — if applicable)

## 3.5 Underslab Inspection (Limited Scope for This Design)

**Plain language:** For typical homes with pipes running under the basement floor, an inspector checks the pipes and vapor barrier before concrete is poured. This design has no pipes under the slab, so the underslab inspection is very simple — just the vapor barrier, insulation, rebar, and the U-FER ground (which was also checked at the footing inspection).

**Technical detail:** Even with no plumbing under the slab, the inspector verifies:
- Compacted gravel base meets thickness specification (typically 4-6 inches of 3/4-inch crushed rock)
- Capillary break layer is present (clean 1-inch+ crushed rock with no fines)
- Vapor barrier (6-mil polyethylene minimum, ideally 10-mil or 15-mil for durability) is installed with seams overlapped and taped
- Under-slab insulation (Type XIV EPS, 40 psi, in this design) is laid flat with staggered seams
- Second vapor barrier is in place above the insulation (optional but recommended in this design)
- Radiant heat tubing is secured to the rebar grid (if included in the first pour)
- Rebar is elevated on chairs, with proper size, spacing, and overlap
- No plumbing penetrations exist (verified — consistent with design)

**How to prepare:**
- All under-slab work complete and visible
- Rebar tied and elevated on chairs
- Radiant tubing pressure-tested (typically 100 psi for 24 hours) and still pressurized during the inspection so the inspector can verify no leaks
- Photos of every layer for personal records

**Key code references:**
- ORSC R506 (concrete floors on ground — preparation and reinforcement)
- ORSC N1102 (thermal envelope — slab insulation)

## 3.6 Framing and Structural Inspection

**Plain language:** After the walls are complete and the floor slabs between levels are poured and cured, but before anything gets covered up, an inspector verifies the structure is built per the plans. For this design, that means verifying the concrete block walls, the rebar in the grouted cores, the horizontal reinforcement at floor levels, and the floor slab thickness and reinforcement.

**Technical detail:** The inspector verifies:
- Wall thickness matches plans (8-inch structural inner wythe, 4-inch decorative outer wythe)
- Rebar in grouted cells is the correct size and spacing
- Grout is fully consolidated in reinforced cells (no voids)
- Bond beam reinforcement at the top of each wall and at each floor level is in place
- Horizontal joint reinforcement or bed joint reinforcement between courses, per plans
- Wall ties between the two wythes are installed at the specified spacing (FRP ties per this design)
- Lintels over openings (the beams that span doors and windows) are sized correctly
- Anchor bolts or hurricane straps connecting walls to the floor slabs above are in place
- Floor slab thickness, rebar, and fiber content match plans
- Service layer framing (the walkable utility level between floors) is structurally sound
- Roof structure is complete per approved plans (concrete slab with parapet or steel joist with deck, depending on design choice)
- All required seismic bracing is in place

**Unique considerations for this design:**
- Double-wythe cavity wall — inspector verifies both wythes and the cavity insulation is in place
- Sideways course of block at each floor level for horizontal steel joists (when that option is used) — inspector verifies joists are properly supported and anchored
- Service layer — inspector verifies the perforated metal walkway is securely bolted to the walls and the structure above is supported independently

**How to prepare:**
- Approved plans on site
- Mortar joints clean and consistent
- Grout pours documented (photos of each cell being grouted)
- Reinforcement steel left exposed where feasible for visual verification
- Any specialty engineered items (FRP ties, steel joists, etc.) with manufacturer documentation

**Key code references:**
- ORSC Chapter 6 (wall construction — masonry walls)
- ORSC R606 (general masonry construction)
- ORSC R608 (reinforced masonry construction)
- ORSC R301 (design criteria — structural loads and wind/seismic provisions)

## 3.7 Rough-in Plumbing Inspection

**Plain language:** After all the plumbing pipes are installed in the walls and service layer, but before insulation or interior finishes cover them up, an inspector checks every pipe, fitting, and connection. This is one of the more detailed inspections because plumbing mistakes are expensive to fix later.

**Technical detail:** The inspector verifies:
- All pipes are the correct size for the fixtures they serve
- Pipe materials are code-approved for the application (hot vs. cold, potable vs. drain)
- Slope of drain lines is correct (1/4 inch per foot for 3-inch pipe, 1/8 inch per foot for 4-inch+)
- Venting is properly sized and terminates correctly (through roof or sidewall per design)
- Air admittance valves (AAVs) are in approved locations and within the allowed count
- Cleanouts are installed at code-required locations
- Hangers and supports are spaced per code (typically 4-10 feet depending on pipe material and size)
- Water supply pipe is tested under pressure (80 psi hydrostatic test for 15 minutes minimum)
- Drain, waste, and vent piping is tested (water test or air test, 5 psi for 15 minutes)
- Backflow prevention is installed at required locations (hose bibs, boiler connections, irrigation)
- Fixture rough-in dimensions are correct for the planned fixtures
- Shutoff valves are accessible and in correct locations
- Pressure regulators are installed where required (per-fixture in this design)

**Unique considerations for this design:**
- 2-inch trunk pipe throughout — inspector verifies this is properly sized and supported
- Per-fixture pressure regulators — inspector verifies they are listed devices
- Hot water recirculation loop — inspector verifies the loop, return line, circulator pump, and check valve
- Utility bay fixtures (hot outdoor hose bib, etc.) — inspector verifies backflow prevention
- Non-potable system (if installed for rainwater) — inspector verifies labeling and air gap separation

**How to prepare:**
- Pressure test both water and DWV systems before the inspector arrives
- Leave test gauges connected and pressurized during the inspection
- Have all fixtures and their rough-in specifications on site
- Label any unusual features (non-potable lines, specialty fixtures) clearly

**Key code references:**
- OPSC 312 (testing — water and DWV tests)
- OPSC 604 (water distribution — materials and sizing)
- OPSC 701-717 (drainage systems — materials, sizing, and installation)
- OPSC 901-913 (vents — sizing, materials, termination)

## 3.8 Rough-in Electrical Inspection

**Plain language:** After all the electrical wiring is pulled through the walls and service layer, but before insulation or drywall covers it, an inspector checks the wiring, boxes, panel, and grounding. Electrical mistakes are dangerous as well as expensive, so this inspection is thorough.

**Technical detail:** The inspector verifies:
- Service entrance conductors sized correctly for the main panel (typically 200 amp or 400 amp for a home this size)
- Main panel labeled correctly with all circuits identified
- Grounding electrode conductor connects the panel to the U-FER ground
- All branch circuit wire sizes match the breakers they connect to
- Every room has at least the minimum number of receptacles (outlets) required by code
- Ground-fault circuit interrupters (GFCI) are installed in kitchens, bathrooms, laundry, garages, outdoors, and unfinished basements
- Arc-fault circuit interrupters (AFCI) are installed on required circuits (most living areas)
- Tamper-resistant receptacles are installed in required locations
- Smoke alarms and carbon monoxide detectors are hardwired with battery backup and interconnected
- Boxes for outlets, switches, and fixtures are at proper heights
- Wire fill in each box does not exceed code limits (box fill calculation)
- Staples and wire supports are at required spacing
- Wires are protected from nails and drywall screws with metal plates at wall penetrations
- No wire splices outside of electrical boxes
- Dedicated circuits for large appliances, HVAC, and kitchen per code
- Low-voltage wiring (Ethernet, audio/video) is separate from line-voltage wiring with proper separation

**Unique considerations for this design:**
- Every room has its own breaker — inspector verifies the panel schedule matches
- Kitchen has multiple breakers for high-power areas — inspector verifies adequate circuit capacity
- Service layer wiring routing — inspector verifies protection where wires pass through walls or structural elements
- Exterior interconnect box entry — inspector verifies the service entrance, meter base, and disconnect are properly installed and accessible
- Future solar provisions — inspector verifies any reserved breaker space and conduit stubs

**How to prepare:**
- Panel completely wired, labeled, and closed (cover on)
- All boxes installed with wires terminated (pigtails ready for devices)
- Grounding system visibly complete
- Have a panel schedule printed and laminated at the panel
- Label low-voltage wiring clearly to distinguish it from line-voltage

**Key code references:**
- OESC (Oregon Electrical Specialty Code, based on NEC — the National Electrical Code)
- OESC Article 210 (branch circuits — requirements and sizing)
- OESC Article 230 (services — service entrance requirements)
- OESC Article 250 (grounding and bonding — grounding electrode system)
- OESC Article 406 (receptacles — spacing and type requirements)

## 3.9 Rough-in Mechanical Inspection

**Plain language:** After the heating, cooling, and ventilation systems are installed in rough form, but before insulation and finishes, an inspector checks the HVAC equipment, ductwork if any, exhaust systems, and any combustion equipment.

**Technical detail:** The inspector verifies:
- Heating system is properly sized for the building and installed per manufacturer instructions
- Ductwork (if any) is sized correctly, sealed at joints, and supported properly
- Exhaust fans are installed in every bathroom and the kitchen
- Kitchen hood ventilation meets the required airflow for the range
- Clothes dryer exhaust routes directly outside with no restrictions (if electric dryer included)
- Combustion air is provided for any combustion appliance
- Flue pipes (vent pipes for combustion appliances) are properly sized, supported, and terminate correctly
- Condensate drains from cooling equipment route to an approved location
- Fresh air intakes (for ventilation systems) are at required distances from contamination sources

**Unique considerations for this design:**
- Radiant floor heating — inspector verifies tubing layout, manifold location, pressure test, and circulator pumps
- Ground-source heat exchange (if installed) — inspector verifies ground loop piping, heat pump equipment, and associated controls
- Whole-house ventilation (HRV or ERV when powered) — inspector verifies airflow rates, supply and exhaust locations, and duct runs
- Per-room hydronic valves — inspector verifies the valve layout and manifold
- Passive ventilation openings (if used) — inspector verifies they meet airflow requirements

**How to prepare:**
- All equipment installed and labeled with manufacturer model and serial numbers
- Tubing pressure-tested and documented
- Manufacturer installation instructions on site for reference
- Duct sealing visible (mastic or foil tape at joints)

**Key code references:**
- ORSC Chapter 13-24 (mechanical — general requirements, equipment installation)
- ORSC M1506 (exhaust systems — bathroom and kitchen exhaust)
- ORSC M1501-M1505 (clothes dryer exhaust — ducting requirements)

## 3.10 Insulation Inspection

**Plain language:** After all the rough-in work is done and passed, and before drywall or interior finish covers the insulation, an inspector checks that insulation is installed correctly with the right R-value in the right places.

**Technical detail:** The inspector verifies:
- Wall insulation meets the R-value required by the energy code for the climate zone
- Ceiling insulation meets the R-value required
- Floor insulation meets the R-value required
- Insulation fills the cavity completely with no gaps, compression, or voids
- Air sealing is complete — gaps around wiring, plumbing, and structural elements are sealed with spray foam or appropriate sealant
- Vapor barriers are installed on the correct side of the insulation (warm side in cold climates)
- Any continuous insulation (rigid foam on the exterior or interior of the structural wall) is properly installed with taped seams
- Blower door test results are available (if required by the energy code path chosen) — this is a test measuring how airtight the building is

**Unique considerations for this design:**
- Cavity wall insulation (4 inches of polyiso in the double-wythe cavity) — inspector verifies the insulation is continuous, with staggered seams, filling the cavity
- Under-slab insulation (Type XIV EPS, 40 psi) — already inspected at the underslab stage
- Roof / ceiling insulation — varies by roof option chosen:
  - Concrete slab roof: insulation on top of or below the slab depending on assembly
  - Steel deck shed roof: closed-cell spray foam on the underside of the deck
- Service layer insulation — inspector verifies the service layer thermal envelope if any portion is inside the conditioned space

**How to prepare:**
- All insulation installed and complete before calling for inspection
- Air sealing visible at every penetration (spray foam, caulk, or gaskets)
- Blower door test scheduled and results documented if required
- Vapor barriers clearly visible

**Key code references:**
- ORSC Chapter 11 (energy efficiency — insulation and air sealing)
- ORSC N1101 (thermal envelope — insulation R-values)
- ORSC N1102 (insulation — installation requirements)

## 3.11 Final Plumbing, Electrical, and Mechanical Inspections

**Plain language:** After all the fixtures, outlets, switches, light fixtures, and equipment are installed and connected, an inspector verifies everything works and is complete. These are separate final inspections for each trade.

**Technical detail:** For each trade, the inspector verifies:

**Final Plumbing:**
- All fixtures installed and connected
- All shutoff valves accessible and functional
- Hot water system delivers water at code-compliant temperature (120°F max at fixtures)
- Water pressure at fixtures is within code range (20-80 psi)
- No leaks anywhere in the system
- Water heater installation meets code (seismic strapping, drain pan, T&P relief valve)
- Backflow prevention is functional

**Final Electrical:**
- All devices installed (outlets, switches, fixtures)
- All circuits labeled correctly in the panel
- GFCI and AFCI protection functional (tested)
- Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms interconnected and functional
- All outlets test for proper wiring (hot, neutral, ground in correct positions)
- Exterior receptacles have weather-resistant covers
- Any site lighting and outlets function properly

**Final Mechanical:**
- Heating system operates and reaches target temperatures
- All exhaust fans operate and vent properly
- Any ventilation system delivers required airflow
- Thermostats function correctly
- All registers and grilles installed (if ductwork system)

**How to prepare:**
- All fixtures, devices, and equipment fully installed
- Run the house for a week before the inspection to catch any issues
- Test every outlet with a plug-in tester
- Test GFCIs and AFCIs with their trip buttons
- Test smoke and CO alarms

## 3.12 Final Building Inspection and Certificate of Occupancy

**Plain language:** After all the trade finals pass, the building inspector does a final walkthrough to verify the house is safe to occupy. If it passes, the Certificate of Occupancy is issued and the home can be legally lived in.

**Technical detail:** The inspector verifies:
- All prior inspections are passed
- Exterior grading slopes away from the building (positive drainage)
- Driveway and site work are complete per permit conditions
- Exterior surfaces and weatherproofing are complete
- Handrails and guardrails are installed at stairs, decks, and elevated surfaces
- Stair rise and run comply with code
- Egress windows in bedrooms meet minimum size and accessibility requirements
- Attic access is provided if required
- Address numbers are visible and meet size requirements
- Any conditions attached to the building permit are satisfied

**When all finals are passed and the building inspector signs off, the Certificate of Occupancy is issued.** This triggers the start of the 3-year residency clock (assuming the homeowner actually occupies the home, which they do, with supporting documentation — see Section 2.3).

**Temporary Certificate of Occupancy (TCO):** If most of the house is complete but a few minor items remain (landscape, final grading, minor mechanical), a TCO can be issued instead. TCOs are typically valid for 30 days and can be extended in 30-day increments. The residency clock starts when the homeowner moves in under a TCO.

**How to prepare:**
- Complete all exterior work (siding, roofing, weatherproofing, grading)
- Install handrails, guardrails, and all required life-safety items
- Install permanent address numbers
- Clean the site
- Have all inspection records on site
- Have a checklist of everything the permit required, verified done

**Key code references:**
- ORSC R110 (certificate of occupancy — issuance)
- OAR 918-480-0140 (certificate of occupancy — residential requirements)
- ORSC R303 (light, ventilation, and heating — habitable requirements)
- ORSC R311 (means of egress — stairs, doors, windows)
- ORSC R312 (guards — guardrails)

## 3.13 Septic System Inspections (Separate Permit Track)

**Plain language:** The septic system has its own permit and its own inspections, separate from the building permit. These inspections run on a parallel track and do not hold up the building permit, but both the building CO and the septic approval must be in place before the home can be legally occupied.

**Technical detail:** Septic inspections typically include:

- **Pre-construction site verification** — confirms the installation location matches the approved design
- **Tank installation inspection** — before the tank is buried, an inspector verifies the tank type, size, placement, inlet/outlet elevations, and risers
- **Drainfield / disposal trench inspection** — before the trenches are backfilled, the inspector verifies trench depth, width, distribution piping, gravel bedding, and slope
- **Final inspection** — after backfill and final grading, the inspector verifies the system is complete and functional

**Homeowner can install their own septic system:** In Oregon, a homeowner can install their own on-site septic system on their own property without hiring a licensed installer, provided they follow the permitted design exactly and pass all inspections.

**Key code references:**
- OAR Chapter 340, Division 71 (onsite wastewater treatment systems — DEQ rules)
- OAR Chapter 340, Division 73 (onsite wastewater standards)

## 3.14 What to Have Ready Before Each Inspection (Master Checklist)

**Plain language:** Consistent preparation prevents failed inspections. This checklist applies to every inspection.

**Master checklist for every inspection:**

- [ ] Approved permit placard posted and visible
- [ ] Approved plans on site, in a weatherproof container
- [ ] Work complete and accessible (no covering up what needs to be seen)
- [ ] Work area clean — sweep up debris, remove unnecessary materials
- [ ] Any specialty items have manufacturer documentation available
- [ ] All photos taken of the work before the inspection (for personal records)
- [ ] Test pressures applied and gauges visible where applicable
- [ ] Homeowner or representative on site for the inspection window
- [ ] Questions for the inspector prepared (learn from every visit)
- [ ] Tape measure, flashlight, and ladder available if the inspector needs them

**After each inspection:**
- [ ] Record any issues identified and the fixes needed
- [ ] If passed, note the date and inspector name in the project records
- [ ] If any corrections required, schedule re-inspection after fixing
