# Appendix A — Glossary

*Comprehensive A–Z glossary of terms used throughout the BSH Design and Engineering Documentation, including geotechnical and soil science terminology from Section 14*

This glossary defines every technical term, abbreviation, brand name, code reference, and concept used across the document. Where terms have multiple uses (e.g., "PI" can mean Penetration Index in DCP work or Plasticity Index in soil chemistry), both meanings are included with context.

Cross-references appear in **bold** within definitions when one glossary term references another. Section references point to the section of the main document where the term is most extensively discussed.

---

## A

### AAV — Air Admittance Valve

A mechanical one-way valve installed on a plumbing drain line in place of a traditional vent pipe that runs up through the roof. Allows air into the drain when water is flowing (to prevent siphonage of traps) but closes to prevent sewer gas from escaping into the room. **Decision in this design: AAVs are NOT used.** Every fixture receives an individual full-size vent to the outside. See **Individual Vent, Stack Vent, Trap Seal**.

### ABS — Acrylonitrile Butadiene Styrene

A black thermoplastic used for plumbing drain, waste, and vent piping. Competitor to PVC. Joined with solvent cement. ABS has fallen out of favor in many markets due to past quality issues; PVC is the default for this design. See **DWV, PVC**.

### AC — Air Conditioner / Air Conditioning

Also used to refer to mechanical cooling equipment. Context determines meaning. In this document, cooling is primarily provided by a ground-source heat pump with radiant cooling loops, not a traditional AC condenser.

### AC — Alternating Current

Electrical current that reverses direction many times per second (60 times per second in North America). The power coming from the utility is AC. Contrast with **DC** (direct current, which flows in one direction — what batteries and most electronics use internally).

### ACCA — Air Conditioning Contractors of America

Industry organization that publishes standards and manuals used throughout the HVAC trade. **Manual J** (heat load calculation), **Manual D** (duct design), **Manual T** (air terminal devices), and **Manual S** (equipment selection) are the most-referenced.

### ACH50 — Air Changes per Hour at 50 Pascals

A standardized measurement of envelope airtightness. The house is pressurized to 50 Pascals above or below outdoor pressure, and the air leakage rate is measured. The result — total leakage volume per hour divided by house volume — expresses how "leaky" the envelope is. Oregon code allows 3.0 ACH50 for new construction; this design targets ≤ 1.0 ACH50; Passive House certification requires ≤ 0.6 ACH50. See **Blower Door Test, Envelope**.

### ACI — American Concrete Institute

The authoritative body for concrete practice in North America. Publishes standards such as **ACI 318** (Building Code Requirements for Structural Concrete) and **ACI 332** (Residential Code Requirements for Structural Concrete). Referenced throughout Section 4.

### Active Layer (expansive clay)

The depth range of soil that experiences seasonal moisture-driven volume change. Above this depth, soil swells in winter and shrinks in summer. Below this depth, moisture is more stable. In Oregon, typical active layer is 3–5 feet for moderately expansive clays. See **Section 14.16, Expansive Clay**.

### ADA — Americans with Disabilities Act

Federal civil rights legislation that includes accessibility standards for public buildings. Not directly applicable to a private single-family residence, but the **ANSI A117.1** accessibility standard derived from ADA informs "aging-in-place" design choices in Section 9.12.

### Admixture

A chemical additive mixed into concrete to modify its properties — plasticizers, retarders, accelerators, air-entraining agents, corrosion inhibitors, waterproofing admixtures. This design uses a water-reducing plasticizer and a calcium nitrite corrosion inhibitor. See **Calcium Nitrite, Plasticizer, Pozzolan**.

### AFCI — Arc Fault Circuit Interrupter

A specialized circuit breaker that detects the characteristic electrical signature of an arcing fault (a damaged or loose wire sparking) and cuts power before a fire can start. Required by the NEC in bedrooms, living rooms, kitchens, hallways, and most habitable spaces. Often combined with **GFCI** protection in "combination" AFCI/GFCI breakers.

### AFF — Above Finished Floor

Measurement reference used in architectural drawings. "The switch is mounted 48 inches AFF" means 48 inches above the finished floor surface. Contrast with **AFG** (above finished grade) for exterior measurements.

### Aggregate

The stone and sand that makes up most of the volume of concrete. Coarse aggregate (gravel, usually 3/4-inch nominal) provides bulk and strength. Fine aggregate (sand) fills the spaces between coarse particles and provides workability. See **Concrete, Portland Cement**.

### AHRI — Air-Conditioning, Heating, and Refrigeration Institute

Industry standards body that certifies HVAC equipment performance (efficiency ratings, capacity ratings). When a heat pump is rated "ARI-certified" or "AHRI-certified," it means its published ratings were measured according to AHRI standards.

### AI — Artificial Intelligence

In the context of this document, refers to machine-learning-based object detection and analysis running on local hardware (not cloud services). Used for camera object detection (person, vehicle, package) and potentially voice recognition. See **Frigate, Local-First**.

### Air Barrier

A continuous layer in the building envelope that stops air movement through the envelope. Distinct from a **vapor barrier**, though sometimes the same material serves both functions. In this design, the air barrier is formed by the continuous concrete masonry walls themselves, with sealing at every penetration. See **Envelope, Vapor Barrier**.

### Air Handler

The fan-and-coil unit in a conventional forced-air HVAC system that moves heated or cooled air through ductwork. This design does not use a traditional air handler — the ERV handles air movement, and heat and cooling are delivered radiantly. See **ERV, Forced Air, Radiant**.

### Alluvium / Alluvial

Soil deposited by flowing water (rivers, streams). Found in valleys, river floodplains, deltas. Highly variable — can include clay, silt, sand, gravel, and cobbles in any proportion. Most of the Willamette Valley sits on alluvium. See **Section 14.15**.

### ANSI — American National Standards Institute

A non-governmental organization that coordinates and publishes voluntary consensus standards. ANSI standards are referenced throughout building codes. **ANSI A117.1** (accessibility) and **ANSI A137.1** (tile slip resistance) are notable in this document.

### AP — Access Point (wireless)

A networking device that provides Wi-Fi coverage. Distinct from a "router" (though consumer devices often combine both). Multiple APs spread throughout a home provide uniform Wi-Fi coverage. See **PoE, Wi-Fi**.

### API — Application Programming Interface

A defined way for software components to communicate. In smart home contexts, a "local API" means a device can be controlled directly over the local network without going through a cloud service. This design prefers devices with local APIs.

### ASCE — American Society of Civil Engineers

Professional society publishing many standards. Section 14 references:
- **ASCE 7** — Minimum Design Loads and Associated Criteria for Buildings and Other Structures (seismic site classes, snow loads, wind loads)
- **ASCE 32** — Design and Construction of Frost-Protected Shallow Foundations

See **Appendix B Supplement**.

---

### ASHRAE — American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-Conditioning Engineers

The primary professional body for HVAC engineering in North America. Publishes **ASHRAE 55** (thermal comfort), **ASHRAE 62.2** (ventilation for dwellings), **ASHRAE 90.1** (energy standard), and the **ASHRAE Handbook** series. Heavily referenced in Section 8.

### ASSE — American Society of Safety Engineers

Older name; now called **American Society of Sanitary Engineering** in the plumbing context. Publishes standards for plumbing devices and backflow preventers (e.g., ASSE 1013 for reduced-pressure backflow preventers).

### ASTM — American Society for Testing and Materials

Publishes materials and testing standards. ASTM standards are referenced throughout construction — ASTM C31/C39 (concrete cylinder testing), ASTM C78 (concrete flexural strength), ASTM C177 (thermal conductivity), ASTM C518 (heat flow meter), ASTM C1609 (fiber-reinforced concrete flexural testing), ASTM C1396 (drywall), ASTM C1178 (cement board). See **Section 12** for their use in testing programs.

### Atterberg Limits

Three specific moisture contents that define the engineering behavior of fine-grained soils:
- **Liquid Limit (LL)** — the moisture content at which the soil flows like a liquid
- **Plastic Limit (PL)** — the moisture content at which the soil can just barely be rolled into a 3 mm thread
- **Plasticity Index (PI)** — the difference: PI = LL − PL

The Plasticity Index measures the range of moisture contents over which the soil behaves plastically. **High PI = expansive clay risk.** Named after Albert Atterberg (Swedish soil scientist, early 1900s). Determined in lab, not field. See **Section 14.3, 14.16**.

### Autoclaved Aerated Concrete (AAC)

A lightweight concrete product with millions of tiny air bubbles, produced by a chemical reaction during manufacture. Not used in this design (CMU is used instead), but worth knowing as an alternative concrete masonry product.

### AV — Audio/Video

Collective term for home entertainment equipment — speakers, displays, amplifiers, source devices, and the wiring that connects them. See **Section 10.8**.

### AWG — American Wire Gauge

The standard system for specifying electrical wire diameter in North America. Counterintuitively, **smaller AWG numbers mean thicker wire**. 14 AWG is thinner than 12 AWG, which is thinner than 10 AWG, and so on. For context: 14 AWG is standard for 15A lighting circuits; 12 AWG for 20A general circuits; 10 AWG for 30A dryers; 6 AWG for 50A EV chargers; 2/0 AWG for 200A feeders.

### Aw — Wet Area / Water Equivalent

Variable used in some plumbing and building-science calculations. Context-dependent.

## B

### Backfill

Soil or gravel placed back into an excavation after the foundation or utility is installed. Quality backfill (compacted, drainable) is essential for foundation performance.

### Backflow Preventer

A plumbing device that prevents non-potable water from flowing backward into the potable water supply. Required by code at any cross-connection between potable and non-potable water (irrigation, fire suppression, pressure washers, hose bibs). See **RPZ, Cross-Connection**.

### BACnet — Building Automation and Control network

A communications protocol used in commercial HVAC and building automation. Can be used in high-end residential systems for equipment-level communication. Alternative to Modbus or proprietary protocols.

### Baffle

A flat piece of material used to direct airflow (in ventilation), absorb acoustic energy (in acoustic treatment), or filter grease (in range hood baffle filters).

### Ball Valve

A plumbing shut-off valve with a spherical ball inside that rotates 90 degrees to open or close the flow. Reliable, long-lasting, quarter-turn operation. Preferred in this design over gate valves (which are older technology, prone to sticking).

### Band Joist / Rim Joist

The structural member at the perimeter of a floor system that caps the ends of the floor joists. In this design with concrete floors, there's no traditional rim joist, but the term is used to describe the analogous perimeter of the floor slab where the wall meets it.

### Basement

A floor level partially or fully below grade. In this design, the basement is a full story with 9-foot ceilings, mechanical room, storage, and living spaces. Often kept minimally conditioned to stay naturally cool. See **Grade, Below Grade**.

### Base Rock / Base Course

Compacted crushed stone placed beneath a concrete slab or pavement to provide a stable, drainable foundation. Typically 4-12 inches of 3/4-inch minus or 1-1/2-inch minus depending on application.

### BE365

Schlage model number for a common keypad deadbolt — referenced as a "dumb" keypad lock option. Manufacturer part numbers appear throughout the document for specific products.

### Beam

A horizontal structural member that spans between supports and carries loads to them. In this design, beams appear rarely — the structural system uses load-bearing walls and slabs, not a post-and-beam approach. See **Girder, Lintel**.

### Bedrock

Solid rock beneath soil. Foundations on bedrock are ideal (no settlement), but excavation to bedrock is more expensive. Geotechnical investigation determines depth to bedrock on a specific site.

### Bedrock — Competent

A geotechnical term for solid rock that can support virtually any building load without movement. Distinct from "weathered bedrock" or saprolite, which is rock-derived but partially decomposed. See **Section 14.7**.

### Bitumen

Petroleum-based material used in waterproofing (modified bitumen roof membranes) and damp-proofing (liquid-applied foundation coatings). "Modified bitumen" indicates synthetic polymers added for improved performance. See **EPDM, Membrane, TPO**.

### BLM — Bureau of Land Management

Federal agency managing public lands in the western United States. Some rural Oregon properties border BLM land, which affects setbacks, access, and sometimes water rights.

### Blower Door Test

A diagnostic test that quantifies envelope airtightness. A large calibrated fan temporarily mounted in a doorway pressurizes or depressurizes the house, and the airflow needed to maintain a 50 Pa pressure difference measures leakage. See **ACH50, Envelope, Section 12.7**.

### BLUMOTION

Blum's brand name for their soft-close drawer slides and hinges. Referenced in Section 9 as a quality-indicating specification for cabinet hardware.

### Board Foot

Unit of volume for lumber (12 inches x 12 inches x 1 inch = 1 board foot). Insulation is sometimes sold by "board-foot" in thickness-area form (1 board-foot of 1-inch insulation = 1 square foot). Section 5 references under-slab EPS pricing in board-feet.

### Bond Beam

A horizontal reinforced concrete or masonry element running continuously around the perimeter of a wall at specific levels. Provides lateral stability, ties walls together, and distributes loads. In this design, bond beams run at each floor level and at the top of walls.

### Borings

Drilled holes that bring up soil/rock samples from depth. Run by truck-mounted or trailer-mounted rigs. Standard professional geotechnical investigation. Each boring includes Standard Penetration Test (SPT) blow counts at intervals plus extracted samples for lab testing. See **Section 14.5.4, SPT**.

---

### Boro-Silicate (Borosilicate)

A type of glass with boron and silica that has superior thermal shock resistance. Not typically used in windows but appears in some scientific and cooking contexts.

### Brace / Bracing

Temporary or permanent structural members that provide lateral stability during construction or throughout the building's life. Wall bracing is required during masonry construction while mortar cures.

### Branch Circuit

In electrical, a circuit running from a panel to one or more outlets/lights/fixtures. Distinct from a **feeder** (panel-to-panel) or a **service** (utility-to-panel). See **Feeder, Service**.

### Breaker — see **Circuit Breaker**

### BSH — Better Smarter Homes (LLC)

The brand and content entity associated with this design. See **Section 13**.

### BTU — British Thermal Unit

Unit of energy. One BTU is the energy required to raise one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit. HVAC equipment capacity is measured in BTUs per hour (BTU/hr). A ton of cooling = 12,000 BTU/hr. A typical home needs 20,000–60,000 BTU/hr for heating or cooling at design conditions.

### Butterfly Valve

A type of quarter-turn valve with a disc that rotates to open or close flow. Used in larger plumbing and HVAC applications.

### Butyl Tape

A non-hardening, rubber-based adhesive tape used for sealing joints in roofing, glazing, and air barriers. Excellent long-term elasticity and adhesion. Used at metal roof panel laps in this design's Option B roof.

## C

### Cable

In electrical: a bundled group of insulated conductors in a sheath (**NM-B cable**, **MC cable**, **coaxial cable**). In data: a single cable can contain multiple conductors (**Cat6A cable** contains 4 twisted pairs of conductors).

### CAD — Computer-Aided Design

Drafting done on a computer rather than a drawing board. Produces dimensioned, printable drawings. Professional architects and engineers use programs like AutoCAD, Revit, or ArchiCAD.

### CAFCI — Combination AFCI

An AFCI that combines parallel-arc and series-arc detection. The "combination" part means it detects both types of arc fault. Modern AFCIs are typically CAFCIs. See **AFCI**.

### Calcium Chloride

A concrete accelerator (speeds up cure time). **Not** used in this design because it promotes steel rebar corrosion.

### Calcium Nitrite

A corrosion inhibitor admixture for concrete. Chemically passivates the surface of embedded steel rebar, extending the life of reinforced concrete dramatically. Core component of this design's "1000-year" concrete strategy. See **Admixture, Corrosion Inhibitor**.

### CalDAV

A protocol for calendar data. Not used in the building design, but relevant to local-first automation philosophy in Section 10.

### Cantilever

A structural member supported at one end and free at the other. Balconies and some overhangs are cantilevers. Rare in this design (which favors simpler structural geometries).

### Capillary Break

A layer of material that stops water from "wicking" upward through capillary action. Typically a coarse gravel layer (1-inch or larger stone with no fines) between the soil and the slab or foundation. Critical for dry basement design. See **Section 4.2** for the stack-up.

### Carbon Monoxide — see **CO**

### Cartridge (faucet)

The internal valve mechanism of a modern faucet. Ceramic disc cartridges are the quality standard (long life, smooth operation). Compression cartridges are older technology. This design specifies ceramic cartridge faucets throughout.

### Casement Window

A window that opens on side hinges, swinging outward. Seals tightly when closed (compressed weatherstripping). Superior air-sealing compared to sliders.

### Casing

The trim molding around a door or window on the interior side. Wood or MDF, painted or stained. See **Section 9.6**.

### Cat6A — Category 6 Augmented ethernet cable

A high-performance data cable supporting 10 Gbps to 100 meters. The recommended ethernet standard for this design (future-proof through ~2045). Contrast with Cat5e (older, 1 Gbps), Cat6 (similar to Cat6A but less shielding, shorter 10 Gbps range), Cat7/Cat8 (higher performance, less common).

### Caulk

A flexible sealant used to fill gaps and joints. Many formulations: silicone (bathroom/kitchen), polyurethane (exterior), acrylic latex (paintable interior), butyl (industrial). Not a structural material — caulk bridges small gaps but doesn't fill structural voids.

### Cavity Wall

A wall assembly with two separate layers ("wythes") of masonry with a gap between them, typically filled with insulation and drainage space. This design uses a double-wythe cavity wall with 4 inches of polyiso foam in the cavity. See **Section 4, 5, Wythe**.

### CBR — California Bearing Ratio

A standardized measure of soil strength expressed as a percentage. Originally developed by the California Division of Highways for pavement design but widely used for foundation evaluation. CBR of 100 = the strength of a standard reference crushed-stone material; CBR of 1 = very weak soil. Modern correlation: CBR can be estimated from DCP penetration index using CBR = 292 / PI^1.12. See **Section 14.4.2, DCP**.

### CCB — Construction Contractors Board (Oregon)

The Oregon state agency that licenses and regulates general contractors. An owner-builder building their own home is exempt from CCB licensing under ORS 701.010, a critical legal framework for this design. See **Section 2**.

### CCID — Charging Circuit Interrupting Device

An integrated ground-fault detection system built into EV chargers. Because CCID provides the equivalent protection of a GFCI, a separate GFCI breaker is not required in front of a hardwired EV charger per NEC 625.54.

### CCPA — California Consumer Privacy Act

Privacy law that can affect data handling in smart home contexts. Similar to Europe's GDPR. Local-first smart home design minimizes exposure to these regulations.

### Cedar

A softwood species used for exterior siding, decking, and some interior finishes. Aromatic, rot-resistant. Not used structurally in this design (no wood in the envelope). Western red cedar and Port Orford cedar are both native to Oregon.

### CEE — Concrete-Encased Electrode

An electrical grounding electrode formed by bare copper or rebar embedded in concrete in contact with the earth. Also called a **Ufer ground** after its inventor, Herbert Ufer. NEC 250.52(A)(3) requires 20 feet of #4 rebar or larger, encased in at least 2 inches of concrete. This design uses the foundation rebar cage itself as the CEE — the rebar was going to be there anyway, and it's a dramatically lower-resistance electrode than ground rods. See **Section 7.5, Ufer Ground**.

### CEE — see **Concrete-Encased Electrode** (in main glossary)

Note: also relevant to Section 14 because the CEE rebar stub from the foundation grounding electrode is verified during the same inspection sequence as soil/foundation prep.

### Cement

The binder in concrete and mortar. Most commonly **Portland cement** (produced by heating limestone and clay). The term is often incorrectly used to mean "concrete" — cement is an ingredient; concrete is the mixed material. See **Concrete, Mortar, Portland Cement**.

### Cement Board

A rigid panel made from cement and reinforcing fibers (or cellulose), used as a backer behind tile in wet areas. Trade names include **Durock, HardieBacker, WonderBoard**. ASTM C1325 standard. Replaces drywall in wet zones. See **Drywall, Wet Wall**.

### Cementitious

"Of or relating to cement." A "cementitious material" is one that acts as a binder — Portland cement, fly ash, slag, silica fume are all cementitious.

### Certificate of Occupancy — see **CO**

### CFM — Cubic Feet per Minute

The standard unit for airflow rate. A range hood exhausts at 300–600 CFM; a bathroom fan at 50–100 CFM; a whole-house ventilation system at 100–200 CFM. HVAC equipment airflow is also rated in CFM.

### Chair (rebar)

Small plastic or metal supports that hold rebar at the correct height above the ground or form before concrete is poured. Ensures proper concrete cover on all sides of the rebar.

### CH (soil classification)

Unified Soil Classification System code for **clay of high plasticity**. Plasticity Index typically over 25. Often expansive. See **Section 14.3, USCS**.

### CHSPT2Ultra

Eaton's model number for a Type 2 whole-house surge protective device. Product-specific reference in Section 7.17.

### Circuit Breaker

An electrical device that automatically interrupts current flow when an overload or short circuit is detected. Resets manually after tripping. Standard sizes: 15A, 20A, 30A, 40A, 50A, and up for residential. See **AFCI, Breaker, GFCI**.

### Circuit — Electrical Branch

A single path of wires from a breaker to one or more outlets or fixtures. A house has many branch circuits — each room may have one or more. See **Branch Circuit**.

### Cladding

The exterior skin of a building. In this design, the exterior cladding is a decorative course of CMU block (the outer wythe of the cavity wall). Traditional cladding materials include wood siding, vinyl siding, stucco, fiber cement, and masonry veneer.

### Clear Span

The unobstructed horizontal distance between supports. A floor with a 30-foot clear span has no columns or walls between its two support lines. Clear span is structurally demanding — longer spans require deeper, stronger members.

### Closed-Cell Foam

Polyurethane foam insulation where the individual cells are sealed, making the foam water-resistant and structurally rigid. Higher R-value per inch (~R-6.5) than open-cell foam (~R-3.6). See **Open-Cell Foam, Spray Foam**.

### Closed-Loop Heat Pump

A ground-source heat pump where a sealed loop of fluid circulates between the heat pump and buried pipes. Distinct from open-loop (pump-and-dump) systems. See **GSHP, Heat Pump, Section 8.2**.

### CL (soil classification)

USCS code for **clay of low plasticity**. Plasticity Index typically below 25. Less expansive than CH but still clay. See **Section 14.3, USCS**.

### CMU — Concrete Masonry Unit

The technical name for a concrete block. Standard sizes include 8x8x16 (the common "cinder block"), 6x8x16, 12x8x16, and many specialty shapes. This design uses 8-inch CMU for the inner structural wythe and 4-inch CMU or stone for the outer decorative wythe. See **Block, Masonry, Wythe**.

### CO2 — Carbon Dioxide

A byproduct of combustion and respiration. Elevated indoor CO2 levels (over ~1000 ppm) indicate inadequate ventilation. Monitored indirectly via CO2 sensors in some smart HVAC systems.

### Coaxial Cable / Coax

A cable with a central conductor, insulation, a shield, and an outer jacket. Used for cable TV, cable internet, and older security systems. **RG6** is the standard residential coax. Still wired in this design even though streaming has replaced cable TV, because it's cheap to install and supports MoCA (Ethernet over coax) as a backup.

### CO — Carbon Monoxide

An invisible, odorless, toxic gas produced by incomplete combustion of fossil fuels. Required detectors in sleeping areas and near fuel-burning appliances per ORSC R315. This design is all-electric to minimize CO risk.

### CO — Certificate of Occupancy

The legal document issued by the building department certifying that the building complies with all applicable codes and is approved for occupancy. See **TCO** for the temporary version. See **Section 2** for the owner-builder context.

### Coefficient of Friction

A measure of slip resistance. **DCOF** (Dynamic Coefficient of Friction) per ANSI A137.1 applies to tile — 0.42 minimum for wet areas.

### Coefficient of Performance — see **COP**

### Cohesive Soil

A soil whose particles stick to each other through electrostatic and chemical forces (clay being the prototypical example). Holds vertical excavation walls. Distinct from **cohesionless** or **granular** soils (sands, gravels) where particles slide past each other freely. See **Section 14.2**.

### Collapsible Soil

A soil that has stable structure when dry but loses volume when wetted. Common types: loess, some volcanic ash. The structural arrangement of particles is held in place by capillary tension and weak chemical bonds; water destroys these and the soil collapses. Mitigated by pre-wetting and compacting. See **Section 14.12, Loess**.

### Compacted Fill

Soil or gravel placed in layers and compacted to achieve structural stability. Critical under foundations and driveways. Compaction is measured by Proctor density tests.

### Compaction

The process of reducing soil voids by mechanical action — pressing, vibrating, or rolling — to increase density. Critical for engineered fill. Measured by comparison to maximum density achievable with a Proctor test. Target is typically 90–95% of Proctor maximum. See **Section 14.11, Proctor Test**.

### Compressive Strength

The ability of a material to resist being crushed. Concrete is characterized primarily by compressive strength in psi (pounds per square inch). Structural concrete in this design is 4,000–5,000 psi. See **Cylinder Test, Section 12.3**.

### Concrete

A composite material of Portland cement, water, aggregate, and admixtures. Cures through chemical reaction (hydration), not drying. Strong in compression, weak in tension (reinforcement compensates). See **Cement, Rebar, Reinforced Concrete**.

### Condensation

Water droplets forming on a surface when humid air contacts a surface below the dew point. A risk in poorly-detailed wall assemblies and cold-surface conditions. Mitigated by continuous insulation, air sealing, and humidity control. See **Dew Point, Vapor Drive**.

### Conductor

A wire or cable that carries electrical current. Copper is the standard; aluminum is used for large-diameter applications (service conductors). See **AWG**.

### Conduit

A protective raceway for electrical wiring. Types include **EMT** (electrical metallic tubing — thin-wall steel), **RMC/RGC** (rigid metal conduit — thick-wall steel), **PVC** (rigid plastic, typically Schedule 40 or 80). This design routes most wiring in conduit for long-term replaceability. See **EMT, PVC, RMC**.

### Consolidation

The slow process by which clay soils lose volume under sustained load as water is squeezed out of pore spaces. Distinct from immediate (elastic) settlement. Can take months to decades to complete. Pre-loading addresses consolidation by accelerating it before construction. See **Section 14.13.3, Settlement**.

### Continuous Insulation

A layer of insulation without interruptions by structural members (studs, rafters). Typically rigid foam board applied to the exterior of the structural framing. This design's cavity foam in the masonry wall is continuous insulation. Contrast with "cavity insulation" (insulation between framing members, which allows thermal bridging through the studs). See **Thermal Bridging**.

### COP — Coefficient of Performance

The efficiency ratio of a heat pump. COP = heat output ÷ electrical input. A COP of 3.5 means the heat pump delivers 3.5 units of heat energy for every 1 unit of electricity consumed. Ground-source heat pumps routinely achieve COP 3.5–4.5; air-source heat pumps achieve COP 2.5–4.0. See **EER, Heat Pump, SEER**.

### Coping

The cap at the top of a parapet wall. Prevents water from entering the top of the wall. Can be stone, metal, or precast concrete. See **Parapet**.

### Core Fill

Grout poured into the hollow cores of CMU to create reinforced concrete within the block wall. Usually combined with vertical rebar. See **Grout, Reinforcement**.

### Corrosion Inhibitor

An admixture that protects embedded steel from rusting. **Calcium nitrite** is the primary example in this design. See **Admixture, Calcium Nitrite**.

### Cover (rebar)

The distance from the surface of concrete to the nearest rebar. Required minimums vary by exposure condition (slab on ground, exposed to weather, etc.). This design specifies 3–4 inches of cover for critical reinforcement, significantly above code minimum, as part of the 1000-year strategy.

### CPT — Cone Penetrometer Test

A truck-mounted soil testing method using a hydraulically-pushed instrumented cone. Records continuous resistance vs. depth, plus pore water pressure. Higher quality and more expensive than DCP or SPT. Specialty equipment, professional only. See **Section 14.4.6, DCP**.

---

### Crawlspace

A partial-height underfloor space. Older Oregon homes often have crawlspaces instead of basements. This design has a full basement, not a crawlspace.

### Cross-Connection

A plumbing connection between potable and non-potable water supplies. Prevented by backflow preventers. See **Backflow Preventer**.

### CSA — Canadian Standards Association

A standards body similar to ANSI. Some products carry CSA certification (common for products sold in both countries).

### CT — Current Transformer

A sensor that measures AC current without direct electrical connection — it clamps around a wire and senses the magnetic field. Used in whole-house energy monitoring (Emporia Vue, Sense). See **Section 10.9**.

### Curing (concrete)

The chemical process by which concrete hardens. Requires adequate moisture and temperature for full strength development. Concrete gains about 65% of its strength by 7 days, 85% by 14 days, 100% of its "28-day strength" by 28 days, and continues to gain strength slowly for years (especially with pozzolans).

### Cylinder Test

The standard method for measuring concrete compressive strength. 6-inch-diameter by 12-inch-tall cylinders are cast from the batch, cured in water, and crushed in a compression testing machine at 7, 14, 28, and sometimes 56 and 90 days. Result expressed in psi. See **ASTM C31/C39, Section 12.3**.

## D

### Damp-Proofing

A coating applied to the exterior of foundation walls to resist water seepage. Less performance-demanding than **waterproofing**. Often liquid asphalt-based. Code minimum for many locations, but this design specifies full waterproofing (membrane-based) for the 1000-year goal.

### DC — Direct Current

Electrical current that flows in one direction only. Batteries produce DC; solar panels produce DC; most electronics use DC internally. The utility supplies AC; conversion to DC happens in power supplies. See **AC, Inverter**.

### DCOF — Dynamic Coefficient of Friction

Slip resistance measurement per ANSI A137.1. Wet-area tile requires DCOF ≥ 0.42. See **Section 9.2, Tile**.

### DCP — Dynamic Cone Penetrometer

A field-portable soil testing instrument consisting of a steel rod with a hardened conical tip, an anvil, and a sliding hammer that drops a fixed distance. The number of blows required to drive the cone a specified distance correlates to soil bearing capacity. Per **ASTM D6951**. The owner-builder's primary geotechnical tool. See **Section 14.4** for complete usage guide.

### DCV — Double Check Valve

A type of backflow preventer. See **Backflow Preventer**.

### Deadbolt

A lock mechanism where the bolt is extended and retracted by a key or thumb-turn, with no spring mechanism. More secure than a latch because it cannot be retracted by force against the latch bevel. See **Section 9.13**.

### Dead Load

The weight of the building itself — walls, floors, roof, permanent fixtures. Distinct from **live load** (people, furniture, snow). Structural design must account for both.

### Deck (roof)

The structural surface of a roof, over which the weather layer is applied. Can be wood sheathing, concrete slab, or steel decking. This design's two roof options differ in deck type.

### Dehumidifier

A device that removes moisture from indoor air. This design uses a whole-house ducted dehumidifier to manage summer humidity and support radiant cooling. See **Section 8.7**.

### Delta-T (ΔT)

The temperature difference between two points. Used throughout HVAC — supply-to-return delta-T in hydronic systems is typically 10–20°F. Also used in insulation calculations (the temperature difference across a wall).

### Demand / Load Calculation

The calculated electrical or HVAC load a building imposes on its systems. Used to size service, equipment, wire, and piping. NEC Article 220 and ACCA Manual J are the primary methods.

### Denon

A manufacturer of AV receivers, referenced in Section 10.8 as a home theater option.

### DEQ — Department of Environmental Quality (Oregon)

The state agency that regulates septic systems, water discharge, and environmental protection. See **Section 11.5**.

### Dewpoint

The temperature at which water vapor in air begins to condense. A warmer, more humid environment has a higher dewpoint. Critical for radiant cooling design (floor surface must stay above dewpoint to prevent condensation) and for avoiding mold in wall assemblies. See **Condensation, Humidity**.

### Differential Settlement

When parts of a foundation settle at different rates, causing cracks and structural distortion. Prevented by uniform soil-bearing, mat foundations (this design), or deep foundations in problematic soils.

### Differential Settlement

When parts of a foundation settle different amounts, causing the building to distort. The most damaging type of settlement — small differential movements (½ inch across a building) crack foundations, while large uniform settlement (3 inches across the entire building) causes minimal structural damage. See **Section 14.2, 14.7**.

### DIN Rail

A standardized metal rail for mounting electrical components in panels and control cabinets. Common in European and industrial systems; appearing more in residential smart panels.

### Disconnect

An electrical switch that completely disconnects a circuit or equipment from power. Required for service entry (see 2020 NEC 230.85), for HVAC equipment (within sight of the equipment), and for certain other applications. See **Section 7.2**.

### Dishwasher Air Gap

A fitting that prevents dirty water from siphoning back into the dishwasher. Required by some jurisdictions; a "high loop" is an alternative. See **OPSC**.

### DIY — Do It Yourself

The practice of homeowners performing work on their own home, rather than hiring contractors. Oregon's owner-builder exemption (ORS 701.010) provides the legal framework for this practice. See **Section 2, 11.3**.

### DLNA — Digital Living Network Alliance

A set of standards for sharing media between networked devices. Referenced in Section 10.8 as one option for whole-house media distribution.

### DNS — Domain Name System

The internet service that translates website names into IP addresses. Referenced in Section 10.12 for network security (PiHole filters DNS at home to block unwanted connections).

### DOGAMI — Department of Geology and Mineral Industries (Oregon)

The Oregon state agency responsible for geological mapping and hazard assessment. Publishes:
- **SLIDO** (Statewide Landslide Information Database for Oregon)
- Liquefaction susceptibility maps
- Tsunami hazard maps
- Earthquake hazard maps
- Various geological surveys

Free public access at oregongeology.org. Essential resource for Oregon site evaluation. See **Section 14.6, 14.15**.

### Double Check Valve — see **DCV**

### Double Pane / Triple Pane

Window glass with two or three sheets of glass with sealed air or inert-gas spaces between. More panes means better insulation (higher R-value) and lower U-factor. Triple-pane is specified for high-performance homes.

### Downspout

A vertical pipe that carries roof drainage from gutters to the ground or to a dry well / cistern. This design routes downspouts to a rainwater cistern for non-potable use.

### Drainage Mat

A dimpled plastic sheet installed against a foundation wall, providing a void space for water to flow downward to the perimeter drain. Common brand: Cosella-Dörken DELTA-MS. See **Section 5, 14.18**.

### Drain Tile / Perimeter Drain

A perforated pipe at the base of the foundation that collects water and carries it away from the structure. Typically wrapped in filter fabric and surrounded by gravel. Critical for dry basement performance.

### Drilled Shafts (Caissons)

Large-diameter (24–72 inch) reinforced concrete columns extending to bedrock or competent bearing. Used for very heavy loads or very deep weak soil. Specialty contractor only. See **Section 14.13.8**.

### DSC — Digital Security Controls

### DSL — Digital Subscriber Line

### Duct

### Durock

USG's brand of cement board. See **Cement Board**.

### DWV — Drain, Waste, Vent

The plumbing system that collects wastewater from fixtures (drain), carries sewage (waste), and admits air to equalize pressure (vent). Typically PVC or ABS plastic. See **Section 6, OPSC**.

## E

### Earthen / Earthwork

Excavation, grading, and other dirt-moving work. Usually done with excavators, dozers, or loaders.

### Easement

A legal right to use another's land for a specific purpose. Common easements: utility easements (power lines, buried utilities), access easements (driveway across neighboring land), drainage easements. Identified during title search. See **Section 1, 11.4**.

### Eave

The lower edge of a sloped roof, usually extending past the wall. The design's Option B (metal roof) has eaves; Option A (flat roof with parapet) does not.

### EER — Energy Efficiency Ratio

A cooling efficiency measurement: BTU/hr of cooling divided by watts of electrical input, at specific rating conditions (95°F outdoor). Higher = more efficient. Modern heat pumps achieve EER 12–18. Contrast with **SEER** (seasonal, weighted average). See **COP, SEER**.

### Efflorescence

A white crystalline deposit on masonry surfaces caused by water dissolving salts in the masonry, traveling to the surface, and evaporating. Aesthetic only, not structurally damaging, but indicates water intrusion that should be investigated.

### EFU — Exclusive Farm Use (Oregon zoning)

A restrictive Oregon zoning designation that limits non-farm uses. Building a residence on EFU land requires specific exemptions. Generally avoided for this design. See **Section 1**.

### Egress

A means of exit, especially for emergency. Code requires every sleeping room to have an egress window (minimum size for escape and rescue). See **ORSC R310, Section 9**.

### Elastomeric

Rubber-like, highly flexible. "Elastomeric coating" describes flexible waterproofing materials that can bridge small cracks without tearing.

### Electrical Service

The utility's supply to the building: drop (overhead wires to the house) or lateral (underground conduit), meter, main disconnect. Service size is in amps: 200A standard, 320–400A for this design. See **Section 7.2**.

### Electrician

A trade professional who installs electrical systems. In Oregon, journeyman electricians are licensed by the state Electrical Board. Owner-builders may do their own electrical work under the exemption. See **Section 2**.

### EMT — Electrical Metallic Tubing

Thin-wall steel conduit for electrical wiring. Common in commercial and industrial work; used in this design's basement and service layer. See **Conduit, Raceway**.

### Encroachment

A building or structure that extends onto another's property. Often uncovered during title search or survey. See **Easement, Setback**.

### Engineered Fill

Soil or aggregate placed and compacted in lifts to specified density to create a stable, predictable building substrate. Distinct from "uncontrolled fill" or "common fill" which is dumped without compaction control. See **Section 14.13.1, Compaction**.

### Envelope — Building Envelope

The physical separator between the interior conditioned space and the outside environment. Includes walls, roof, foundation, windows, doors. Performance characterized by thermal resistance (R-value), airtightness (ACH50), and moisture management. See **Air Barrier, R-Value, Vapor Barrier**.

### EPA — Environmental Protection Agency

Federal agency. Relevant: EPA 608 certification for refrigerant handling; EPA WaterSense certification for water-efficient fixtures; EPA Energy Star labeling.

### EPDM — Ethylene Propylene Diene Monomer

A synthetic rubber used for roof membranes and pond liners. Durable (30+ year life), UV-resistant. One of three common flat-roof membranes (EPDM, TPO, modified bitumen). See **Section 4, Roof**.

### EPS — Expanded Polystyrene

A rigid foam insulation made by expanding polystyrene beads. Generally less expensive and more moisture-stable than XPS. Type XIV (40 psi compressive strength) is used under this design's slab. See **Section 4.3, XPS**.

### ERV — Energy Recovery Ventilator

A mechanical ventilation system that exchanges both heat and moisture between incoming and outgoing air streams. Preferred for Oregon's climate over HRV (which exchanges only heat) because ERV helps manage summer humidity. See **HRV, Section 8.6**.

### Escutcheon

A decorative metal plate around a pipe or fixture where it penetrates a wall or floor. Primarily cosmetic.

### Euro Hinge

A concealed cabinet hinge standard. European-style concealed hinges are common in modern cabinetry. See **Section 9.7**.

### EV — Electric Vehicle

A battery-powered automobile. This design provisions for two Level 2 EV chargers. See **Section 7.15**.

### EVOH — Ethylene Vinyl Alcohol

The oxygen-barrier coating on PEX-A tubing used for radiant heat. Prevents oxygen from diffusing through the tube wall and corroding iron components in the system. See **PEX-A, Radiant**.

### Excavation Walls (Stability)

The vertical or sloped sides of a hole dug for foundation construction. Must hold their shape during the work. Cohesive soils hold vertical walls for short periods; granular soils require sloping or shoring. OSHA requires sloping or shoring for excavations over 4–5 feet deep. See **Section 14.5.1, Shoring**.

### Excavator

A tracked or wheeled machine with an articulated arm and bucket, used for digging. Essential for foundation excavation. Typically hired with an operator. See **Section 11.6**.

### Expansion Joint

A gap filled with flexible material that allows two rigid elements to move independently due to thermal expansion or settlement. Concrete slabs and masonry walls require expansion joints at specified intervals.

### Expansion Tank

A small tank with a flexible diaphragm used in closed plumbing and heating systems to accommodate the expansion of water as it heats. Required on water heaters in pressurized systems.

### Expansive Clay

A clay soil that swells with moisture and shrinks when dry, with volume changes large enough to damage foundations. Caused by clay minerals (montmorillonite, smectite, bentonite) whose layered structure absorbs water between layers. Plasticity Index >25 indicates expansive potential; PI >50 is highly expansive. Common in Oregon's Willamette Valley. See **Section 14.16**.

A synthetic stucco assembly — rigid foam insulation covered by mesh-reinforced coating. Not used in this design (which uses masonry walls), but common in conventional construction.

## F

### F1 / F2 (Oregon zoning)

Forest zoning designations in Oregon. Limit residential construction; generally avoided for this design. See **Section 1**.

### Face-Bedded

A masonry defect where a stone or block is laid with its natural bedding plane vertical rather than horizontal. Weakens the unit. Avoided by proper block orientation.

### Faucet

A fixture that controls water flow to a sink, tub, or shower. Modern faucets use ceramic disc cartridges. See **Cartridge**.

### FEMA — Federal Emergency Management Agency

Produces flood zone maps used to determine flood insurance requirements and certain building requirements. See **Section 1, 11.4**.

### FEMA — Federal Emergency Management Agency

Federal agency. For site evaluation, the relevant FEMA product is the Flood Insurance Rate Map (FIRM), which shows areas subject to flooding. Sites in Special Flood Hazard Areas have insurance and construction implications. See **Section 14.6, Section 1**.

### Fiberglass

Reinforcing fibers of glass used in composite materials. Used in insulation (glass wool batts), in shingles, in some window frames, and in the FRP wall ties specified in this design.

### Fiber-Reinforced Concrete (FRC) / Steel Fiber-Reinforced Concrete (SFRC)

Concrete with short fibers mixed in — steel, polypropylene (macro synthetic), or micro-synthetic. Fibers improve crack resistance and post-crack toughness. Used in this design's floor slabs. See **Section 4, 12.4**.

### Field Identification (soil)

The process of identifying soil type by direct observation and simple tests in the field, without sending samples to a laboratory. Sufficient for most owner-builder decisions. Tools: visual inspection, ribbon test, jar test, shake test, smell test. See **Section 14.3**.

### Filter Fabric / Geotextile

A permeable synthetic fabric used in drainage and filtration applications. Wrapped around drain tile to prevent soil intrusion while allowing water through. See **Drain Tile**.

### Filter Fabric / Geotextile

A non-woven synthetic fabric used to separate soil layers and prevent fines from migrating into drainage gravel. Wrapped around perimeter drains and gravel envelopes. See **Appendix C, Drainage**.

### Fire Rating

A measure of how long a wall, floor, or ceiling assembly resists fire — typically 1 hour, 2 hour, or 3 hour per ASTM E119 testing. Residential doors between garage and house require 20-minute rating per ORSC R302.5.1.

### Fixture (electrical)

A permanently-installed light, receptacle, switch, or similar device.

### Fixture (plumbing)

A water-using appliance or outlet — sink, toilet, tub, shower, dishwasher, washer.

### Flashing

Thin metal or synthetic sheet used to direct water away from vulnerable joints in building construction — around windows, doors, chimneys, roof penetrations, wall-to-roof junctions. Critical for moisture management.

### Flex Duct

Flexible, corrugated ducting used for short runs. Easy to install but has high airflow resistance and collapses if compressed. Used sparingly in this design (only the final 18 inches to register boots). See **Section 8.11**.

### FLIR — Forward-Looking Infrared

A brand name that became synonymous with thermal imaging cameras. FLIR ONE is a smartphone thermal camera attachment. See **Thermal Camera, Section 12.6**.

### Flood Zone

A FEMA-designated area subject to flooding. Determines flood insurance requirements and sometimes building elevation requirements.

### Floor Drain

A drain in a floor surface, required in mechanical rooms, laundry areas, and some other locations. Fed to the DWV system with a trap primer to keep the trap sealed.

### Fly Ash

A pozzolanic byproduct of coal combustion. Replaces a portion of Portland cement in concrete, improving long-term strength and durability while reducing carbon footprint. Type F fly ash (from bituminous coal) is the standard in this design's pozzolanic mix. See **Pozzolan, Section 4**.

### Footing

A widened base of a foundation that distributes building loads over a larger soil area. This design uses a **mat foundation** (a continuous slab) rather than strip footings, but the edge of the mat acts as an integral footing.

### Footing — Spread / Strip / Combined

Different foundation footing geometries:
- **Spread footing** — isolated pad supporting a column or post
- **Strip footing** — continuous footing under a wall
- **Combined footing** — single footing supporting multiple columns
- **Mat foundation** — a single continuous footing covering the entire building footprint (this design's choice)

See **Section 4**.

### Forced Air

Heating/cooling delivered by fans blowing heated or cooled air through ducts. The default approach in conventional homes. This design uses radiant heating and cooling instead, with forced air only for ventilation. See **Radiant, Section 8**.

### Forma / Formwork

Temporary structures (usually plywood, engineered forms, or specialty panels) that shape concrete while it cures. Removed after the concrete hardens. Can be reused many times.

### FPSF — Frost-Protected Shallow Foundation

A foundation design that uses horizontal insulation to prevent the soil under and around the foundation from freezing. Allows shallow foundations in cold climates without deep footings. Standard reference: **ASCE 32**. See **Section 14.17**.

### Free Swell Test

A laboratory test for expansive clay potential. A measured volume of dry sample is placed in water and allowed to swell. The volume increase ratio indicates expansion potential. See **Section 14.16**.

### Frost Heave

Upward soil movement caused by ice formation. In frost-susceptible soils (silty), water continues to feed the freezing front, building thick ice lenses that lift the soil dramatically more than simple water expansion. Can heave a foundation 6+ inches. Mitigated by foundation depth, drainage, and material selection. See **Section 14.17, 14.12**.

### Frost-Susceptible Soil

A soil that forms ice lenses when frozen with water available. Silty soils are most susceptible (worst). Fine sand with silt fines, glacial till also susceptible. Clean gravel and clean sand are NOT frost-susceptible. See **Section 14.17**.

A composite material of glass fibers in a polymer matrix. Used in this design for masonry wall ties instead of steel, eliminating a thermal bridge. See **Section 4.5, Thermal Bridge, Tie**.

### FS140

Siemens' model number for a Type 2 whole-house surge protective device. See **SPD, Section 7.17**.

### Furring Strip

A narrow piece of wood or metal fastened to a wall to create a cavity for insulation, wiring, or to level an uneven wall. Used in this design as one option for finishing the interior face of exterior CMU walls.

The level of the ground. "Grade" can refer to the original natural grade or the finished grade (after earthwork). **Above grade** means above the ground surface; **below grade** means below (basement, foundation).

## L

### Lacustrine

Soil deposited in a lake. Typically fine-grained (clay, silt) with horizontal layering. Can be expansive. Common in Oregon basins. See **Section 14.15**.

### Lag Bolt / Lag Screw

A heavy-duty threaded fastener with a hex head, typically used to anchor into wood through metal connectors.

A material made of multiple thin layers bonded together. Can refer to flooring, countertops (Formica), or glass (laminated safety glass).

Areas with history or potential for slope failure. Indicators: curved tree trunks, hummocky ground, scarps, water seeps, past slide deposits. Oregon Coast Range is the most landslide-prone region in the state. See **DOGAMI's SLIDO database**, **Section 14.15**.

A spring-loaded mechanism that holds a door closed but can be retracted by the doorknob. Distinct from a **deadbolt**, which is more secure.

The horizontal force exerted by soil against a retaining structure (basement wall, retaining wall). Increases with depth. Higher for saturated or clay soils. Calculated using Rankine or Coulomb earth pressure theory. See **Section 14.8, Section 4**.

Horizontal force on a structure (wind, earthquake). Contrast with **gravity load** (vertical).

### LED — Light-Emitting Diode

A semiconductor light source. Energy-efficient, long-lived, specified throughout this design. See **Section 7.11, 9.10**.

### LEED — Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design

A building certification program. Not pursued in this design, which achieves similar or higher performance without the certification process.

A bacteria that grows in warm (77–108°F) water systems and causes Legionnaires' disease. Controlled by storing hot water at 140°F or hotter, then mixing down at fixtures. See **Section 6.8, Thermostatic Mixing Valve**.

### Level (accessibility)

Short for "Level Bolt" — a brand of smart lock retrofit that motorizes an existing deadbolt's thumb-turn without replacing the mechanical lock. See **Section 9.13, 10.7**.

A tool with a vertical or horizontal bubble indicating plumb or level. Essential.

### Level (drywall)

The industry-standard finish levels for drywall:
- Level 0–1: fire walls, concealed
- Level 2: garages, shops
- Level 3: textured walls

See **Section 11.14**.

### Lien

A legal claim against property for unpaid debt. Relevant to construction contracts. Title search identifies existing liens. See **Title Search**.

### Lift (compaction)

A single layer of fill placed before compaction. Lift thickness depends on equipment capacity:
- Hand tamper: 4–6 inches
- Plate compactor: 6–8 inches
- Sheep's foot or roller: 8–12 inches

Thick lifts compact only at the surface; the rest stays loose. See **Section 14.11**.

The vertical distance of concrete placed in a single pour, before allowing initial set. Masonry grout lifts are typically 4–8 feet; slab lifts are typically the full slab depth.

### Lighting Layers

See **Ambient, Task, Accent** lighting. Section 9.10 describes the three-layer approach.

### Lime Stabilization

Mixing lime (typically quicklime CaO or hydrated lime Ca(OH)₂) into clay soil to chemically reduce plasticity and increase strength. Reaction takes days to weeks; result is permanent. Specialty contractor work. See **Section 14.13.4**.

### Lintel

A horizontal structural member over a door or window opening. In masonry, often a specialty precast or cast-in-place concrete element. See **Header, Section 4**.

### Liquefaction

The temporary loss of soil bearing capacity during an earthquake, caused by elevated pore water pressure in saturated loose sands. The soil briefly behaves like a liquid; foundations sink, tilt, or fail. Most dangerous along Oregon coast and in some Willamette Valley alluvial flats. See **DOGAMI's liquefaction maps**, **Section 14.12**.

### Liquid-Applied Membrane

A roofing or waterproofing material applied as a liquid that cures to a continuous seamless film. Brands include **Tremco, Carlisle, BASF**. Used in this design's roof waterproofing (Option A).

### Live Load

The weight of occupants, furniture, snow, wind, and other non-permanent loads. Contrast with **dead load**. Residential floors: 40 psf live load per ORSC R301.5.

### Load-Bearing Wall

A wall that carries weight from above (other walls, floors, roof). In this design, all exterior walls and some interior walls are load-bearing. Walls aligned vertically floor-to-floor to simplify load paths. See **Section 4.1**.

### Load (electrical or plumbing)

The quantity of power or water consumed. Calculated to size equipment. See **Demand, Load Calculation**.

### Local-First

A design philosophy for smart home systems where all computation and data storage happens on devices in the home, rather than remote cloud servers. See **Section 10.1**.

### Loess

Wind-blown silt deposit. Common in eastern Oregon (Columbia Basin region). Stable when dry but **collapsible when wetted** — water destroys the fragile capillary-bonded structure. Mitigated by pre-wetting and compaction. See **Section 14.12, 14.15**.

### Low-E (glass)

"Low-emissivity" glass with a thin metallic coating that reflects infrared while allowing visible light through. Greatly improves window thermal performance.

### Low-Voltage

Electrical systems operating at 50V or less (NEC definition: Class 2 / Class 3). Includes ethernet, speaker wire, doorbell, thermostat control wire, security. Can often be run without conduit and with less stringent separation requirements than line-voltage. See **NEC 725**.

### LVT — Luxury Vinyl Tile / Plank

A synthetic flooring product. **Not** used in this design (shorter life, off-gassing concerns). See **Section 9.2**.

Orange or red spots in otherwise uniform-colored soil, indicating past saturation cycles. The orange/red is iron oxidation that occurred during periods when the soil was alternately wet and dry. A field indicator that the water table reaches that depth seasonally. See **Section 14.10, 14.14**.

A foundation using long structural elements (steel, concrete, wood) driven or pressed into the ground to reach firm bearing. Distinct from piers (which are usually shorter and concrete-filled drilled holes). For very heavy structures or very deep weak layers. See **Section 14.13.7**.

### PILR

Not a standard abbreviation; possibly encountered in specific products.

### PI — Penetration Index (DCP)

In the DCP context: the millimeters of penetration per blow. The fundamental DCP measurement. Lower PI = denser, stronger soil. Different from "Plasticity Index" (also abbreviated PI) which is from the Atterberg limits. Context determines which PI is meant. See **Section 14.4.2**.

### PI — Plasticity Index (Atterberg)

The numerical difference between Liquid Limit and Plastic Limit of a soil. Measures the range of moisture contents over which the soil is plastic. **High PI (>25) indicates expansive clay risk; PI >50 indicates highly expansive.** See **Atterberg Limits, Section 14.16**.

### Pitch (roof)

The slope of a roof, expressed as rise over run (e.g., 3:12 means 3 inches of rise per 12 inches of run). The Option B roof in this design has a 3:12 pitch.

### Plasterboard

UK term for drywall / gypsum board.

### Plaster Veneer

A thin (1/16 to 1/4 inch) hard plaster finish applied over gypsum lath. More durable than drywall; higher labor cost. Option for interior wall finish in Section 9.3.

### Plasticity (soil)

The property of clay-rich soils to deform without breaking when moist. Related to Atterberg limits. High plasticity = lots of clay = potentially expansive. See **Atterberg Limits, Plasticity Index**.

### Plasticizer

A concrete admixture that reduces the water needed for a given workability. Improves strength (lower water-cement ratio) without sacrificing placeability. Standard in this design's high-performance mix.

### Plate (electrical)

A cover plate for outlets and switches.

### Plug (electrical)

Used loosely for both the male connector on a cord and the female receptacle it plugs into. Technically, **plug** = male, **receptacle** = female.

### Plumb

Truly vertical. Verified with a **plumb bob** or spirit level.

### Plumbing

The water supply, drainage, and venting systems. Covered in **Section 6**.

### Ply

A layer of material. **Plywood** has multiple plies of wood veneer. **3-ply roof membrane** has three layers of membrane material.

### Plywood

An engineered wood panel of layered veneers with grain alternating 90 degrees between layers. More stable and stronger than solid wood of the same thickness.

### PoE — Power over Ethernet

A standard that delivers electrical power along with data over Cat5e/Cat6A cables. Used for IP cameras, Wi-Fi access points, and VoIP phones in this design. See **Section 7.16, 10.6**.

### Polyethylene

A plastic used for vapor barriers (6–15 mil sheet), water pipes (HDPE, PEX), and other applications.

### Polyisocyanurate / Polyiso

A rigid foam insulation. R-6 to R-6.5 per inch. Used in this design's wall cavities. See **Section 5**.

### Polyurethane

A plastic used for spray foam insulation, sealants, waterproofing membranes, and wood finishes.

### Portland Cement

The standard binder in concrete. Made by heating limestone and clay at high temperature. **Type I/II** is the general-purpose blend used in this design. Named for its resemblance to Portland stone (Isle of Portland, England), not related to Portland, Oregon.

### Post-Tension

A concrete reinforcement method where high-strength steel cables are stressed after the concrete cures. Used in some specialty structural applications; not used in this design.

### Potable

Water fit for drinking. See **Non-Potable**.

### Pozzolan

A silica- or alumina-containing material that reacts with calcium hydroxide (a byproduct of cement hydration) to form additional cementitious compounds. Fly ash, slag, and silica fume are pozzolans. This design's "pozzolanic mix" replaces part of the Portland cement with pozzolans for improved long-term durability. See **Fly Ash, Slag, Silica Fume, Section 4**.

### PPE — Personal Protective Equipment

Safety gear: hard hat, safety glasses, hearing protection, gloves, steel-toed boots, respirator. Essential on construction sites.

### PP — Polypropylene

A plastic used for fibers (in fiber-reinforced concrete), piping, and some storage applications.

### Pre-Cast Concrete

Concrete cast off-site at a plant and delivered to the site. Can include precast lintels, panels, or entire structural elements.

### Pre-Loading / Surcharging

Soil improvement method: place a temporary heavy load on a site to consolidate compressible clay BEFORE construction. Slow (months to years) but cheap and effective. See **Section 14.13.3**.

### Pressure Drop

The loss of pressure as fluid (water or air) flows through a pipe, duct, or fitting. Managed by proper sizing.

### Pressure Regulator / PRV — Pressure Reducing Valve

A valve that reduces incoming water pressure to a safe level for the house. Required by code if incoming pressure exceeds 80 psi. This design uses a whole-house PRV at the interconnect box and individual PRVs at specific fixtures. See **Section 6**.

### Pressure Test

Filling a plumbing or HVAC system with pressurized fluid or air to check for leaks. Standard test pressures: 100 psi for water supply, manufacturer spec for gas and refrigerant.

### Priming

Applying a preparatory coat. **Paint primer** before finish paint. **Solvent primer** before PVC cement.

### Primus (Schlage Primus)

A Schlage high-security lock line with restricted keyways. See **Section 9.13**.

### Proctor Test (Standard / Modified)

The standardized laboratory test for soil compaction characteristics. A sample is compacted at varying moisture contents, and density is measured. Output: Maximum Dry Density (lb/ft³) and Optimum Moisture Content (%). Field compaction is then specified as a percentage of the Proctor Maximum (typically 90–95%). See **Section 14.11**.

### PSI — Pounds per Square Inch

Pressure unit. Water pressure: 40–80 psi is normal residential. Concrete compressive strength: 3,000–10,000 psi typical. Air pressure: 14.7 psi = 1 atmosphere.

### P-Trap

A curved section of drain pipe that holds water, forming a seal that prevents sewer gas from escaping into the room. Every plumbing fixture needs a P-trap. See **Section 6, Trap**.

### Punch List

A list of final items to complete before project closeout. "Punching out" is completing these items.

### PVC (flexible)

Some flexible products use plasticized PVC — weather-strip, window flashing tape, etc.

A rigid white plastic used for drain pipes (Schedule 40 DWV), electrical conduit (Schedule 40/80), and some cold-water supply lines. This design uses PVC for drain/waste/vent and conduit. See **Section 6, 7, Conduit**.

### PVD — Prefabricated Vertical Drains

Plastic strip drains pushed vertically into compressible clay layers to accelerate consolidation. Speed up pre-loading from years to months. Specialty equipment. See **Section 14.13.3**.

### RAUPEX

Rehau's brand of PEX-A tubing.

### REA — Rural Electrical Association / Rural Electric Association / Rural Electric Cooperative

A consumer-owned electric utility serving rural areas. Common in rural Oregon.

### Rebar / Reinforcing Bar

Steel bars used to reinforce concrete, providing tension capacity (which concrete lacks). Sizes in 1/8-inch increments: #3 = 3/8 inch diameter, #4 = 1/2 inch, #5 = 5/8 inch, and so on. Standard mild steel (black rebar) or epoxy-coated or stainless for severe exposure. This design uses plain black rebar protected by superior concrete (low-permeability mix + corrosion inhibitor + ample cover). See **Cover, Section 4**.

### Receptacle

An electrical outlet where plugs connect. Standard: 120V 15A or 20A duplex. Special: 240V for large appliances, weatherproof for outdoor, tamper-resistant for homes.

### Refrigerant

The working fluid in a heat pump or AC. Historically R-22 (HCFC, phased out), now R-410A (HFC, being phased out by EPA), transitioning to R-454B, R-32, or CO2-based (R-744) depending on application. Refrigerant handling requires EPA 608 certification.

### Refusal (DCP)

The condition when the DCP rod cannot penetrate further despite continued blows. Defined as 50+ blows in a single 100mm interval. Indicates bedrock, hardpan, or buried obstacle. Stop testing; further blows damage equipment. See **Section 14.4.3**.

### Register / Diffuser / Grille

An outlet or inlet in ducted HVAC. **Supply register**: outlet for conditioned air. **Return grille**: inlet for air to be returned to the equipment. **Exhaust grille**: outlet for air being exhausted.

### Reinforcement

Steel rebar or mesh embedded in concrete to provide tensile strength. See **Rebar**.

### Residency Requirement

### Resilient (acoustic)

Springy, vibration-absorbing. Resilient channels separate drywall from joists to reduce sound transmission.

### Restricted Keyway

A lock cylinder design where key blanks are not commercially available — keys can only be duplicated with manufacturer authorization. Used in high-security applications. See **Section 9.13, 10.7**.

### Retaining Wall

A wall holding back soil, typically where grade changes significantly. Structural design depends on soil pressure and drainage.

### RG6 — Coaxial Cable Type

The standard residential coaxial cable. See **Coax**.

### RGC — Rigid Galvanized Conduit — see **RMC**

### RH — Relative Humidity

See **Humidity**.

### Ribbon Test

A field test for clay content. A moist soil sample is rolled into a thin thread; the length of ribbon achievable indicates clay content and plasticity. Long ribbons (3+ inches) indicate high-plasticity clay (potentially expansive). See **Section 14.3**.

### Rust

### Saddle (plumbing)

A fitting that straps over a pipe and allows a tap for a branch line. Alternative to cutting and fitting a T.

### Safety Factor

The ratio of material capacity to design load. A safety factor of 2 means the material can handle twice the expected load. Structural design typically uses safety factors of 1.5–3.

### Salice

Italian cabinet hardware manufacturer. Quality hinges and drawer slides. See **Section 9.7**.

### Sanitary Tee / Sanitary Fitting

DWV fittings designed for gravity drainage (not pressure). Configured to prevent backflow and maintain smooth water flow.

### Saprolite

Weathered rock that retains the original rock's structure but is soft enough to dig with hand tools. Intermediate between soil and bedrock. Common in Oregon coast and cascade regions. Variable bearing capacity depending on degree of weathering. See **Section 14.10, 14.15**.

### SA — Small Appliance (kitchen circuit)

NEC designation for the two (or more) 20A 120V circuits serving kitchen countertop outlets and the refrigerator. See **Section 7.7**.

### Schluter

A manufacturer of tile installation products — waterproofing membranes (Kerdi), transition strips, edge profiles. Industry standard.

### SCH — Schedule (pipe)

A numerical designation for pipe wall thickness. **Schedule 40** (thinner) and **Schedule 80** (thicker) are common in PVC. Schedule 40 is the residential default.

### Scope Creep

The gradual expansion of project scope beyond original plans. Managed by disciplined planning and decision-making. See **Section 11.18**.

### Screed

A flat edge used to level wet concrete or mortar. Also the action of leveling.

### SC (soil classification)

USCS code for **clayey sand**. Sand with significant clay content. See **Section 14.3**.

### Scupper

A drainage opening in a parapet wall. Alternative or supplement to internal roof drains. See **Section 4, Parapet**.

### SEER — Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio

A seasonally-weighted efficiency rating for cooling systems. Higher = more efficient. Modern heat pumps: SEER 14–22. Contrast with **EER** (single operating condition). See **COP, EER**.

### Seismic

Related to earthquakes. Structural design must account for seismic loads based on site seismicity. Oregon has moderate seismic risk from the Cascadia subduction zone. See **Section 4**.

### Sensible Heat

Heat associated with temperature change (not phase change). Contrast with **latent heat** (associated with moisture change). ERVs exchange both; HRVs exchange only sensible. See **ERV, HRV**.

### Series

Electrically or hydraulically, two components connected end-to-end so the same current or flow passes through both in succession. Pumps in series for higher pressure; in parallel for higher volume. See **Parallel**.

### SER — Service Entrance (cable)

Aluminum or copper cable rated for service entrance use. SER type is the most common for residential service. See **Section 7.2**.

### Service

The utility's supply to a building. See **Electrical Service**.

### Service Layer

A design feature of this home: a 4-to-5-foot-tall walkable space between floors, containing all plumbing, electrical, HVAC ducts, and other services. Accessible via perforated metal grating. Enables lifetime serviceability. See **Section 4.6, 7.12, 8.11**.

### Setback

The minimum distance from a property line that a building can occupy. Set by zoning regulations. Varies: 10 feet to 100+ feet for rural. See **Section 1**.

### Set (concrete)

The progressive stiffening of concrete after mixing. **Initial set** (~1–3 hours after mixing): concrete can no longer flow. **Final set** (~6–10 hours): concrete is rigid but not strong. Full strength develops over 28 days.

### Settlement

Downward movement of foundation due to compression of underlying soil. Three types:
- **Immediate (elastic) settlement** — happens during/just after construction
- **Consolidation settlement** — slow, over months to decades, in clay layers
- **Secondary compression** — long-term creep, especially in organic soils

See **Section 14.2, 14.7**.

### Settlement Plate

A surveying reference set on the ground surface to monitor settlement during pre-loading or construction. A simple steel plate with a vertical pipe rising up. Plate elevation is surveyed periodically. See **Section 14.13.3**.

### Sewer

The underground pipe carrying wastewater to municipal treatment or to the septic system.

### Sheathing

A continuous covering over structural framing — plywood or OSB on wood walls, cement board on a tile substrate, etc. Less relevant in masonry construction.

### Shim

A thin wedge used to level or plumb an element during installation.

### Shoring (excavation)

Temporary structural support for excavation walls. Required by OSHA for excavations over 4–5 feet deep that aren't sloped. Types: sheet piles, soldier piles with lagging, hydraulic shoring, trench boxes. See **Section 14.5.1, OSHA**.

### Short Circuit

An unintended direct connection between two conductors, causing very high current flow. Detected by the circuit breaker, which trips.

### Shrink-Swell — see **Expansive Clay**

### Sidelight

A narrow window alongside a door, letting light into the entry.

### Silica Fume

An extremely fine pozzolanic material, a byproduct of silicon metal production. Dramatically improves concrete durability and strength at small dosages (5–10% replacement of cement). Core component of this design's pozzolanic mix. See **Pozzolan, Section 4**.

### Silicone

A flexible sealant. Pure silicone is non-paintable but highly waterproof — used in bathrooms, kitchens, and around fixtures. Siliconized acrylic is paintable but less flexible.

### Sill (window / door)

The horizontal bottom frame of a window or door. Must shed water away from the building.

### Siphon

The unintended withdrawal of water from a trap by negative pressure in the drain. Prevented by venting the drain line. See **Section 6, Trap, Vent**.

### Site Classification (seismic)

Per ASCE 7, sites are classified A through F based on soil stiffness in the upper 100 feet. Site Class C and D cover most Oregon residential sites. Site Class E and F require special design considerations. See **ASCE 7, Section 14.2**.

### Site Plan

An architectural drawing showing the building's location on the lot, with setbacks, driveways, utilities, and landscape features. Required for permits. See **Section 11.5**.

### Skin Friction (piles)

The frictional resistance between a pile/pier surface and the surrounding soil. Together with end bearing, supports the pile load. See **Section 14.13.6, 14.13.7**.

### Slab

A flat, horizontal concrete surface. **Slab on grade** = floor slab directly on the ground. **Structural slab** = elevated slab supported by walls or beams. **Topping slab** = thin (1.5–2 inch) slab on top of another slab, often containing radiant tubing. See **Section 4**.

### Slag / GGBS — Ground Granulated Blast-Furnace Slag

A pozzolanic byproduct of iron manufacture. Replaces a portion of Portland cement, improving strength and reducing heat of hydration. See **Pozzolan, Section 4**.

### Slag — see main glossary

Note: in geotechnical context, GGBS (Ground Granulated Blast-furnace Slag) is sometimes also used as a soil stabilizing additive, separate from its use as a cement replacement.

### Slate

A natural metamorphic stone. Used for roofing (traditional), flooring, and accent surfaces. Durable, heavy.

### Slickensides

Smooth, polished surfaces visible in test pit walls, indicating past slip movement of the soil along that surface. A characteristic feature of expansive clays — the surfaces form during repeated swell-shrink cycles. See **Section 14.16**.

### SLIDO — Statewide Landslide Information Database for Oregon

DOGAMI's online database of mapped landslides, landslide-prone areas, and historical slide events. Free access at oregongeology.org. Essential check before purchasing rural Oregon property. See **Section 14.6, 14.15**.

### Slope Stability

The resistance of a slope to failure (sliding). Depends on slope angle, soil strength, water content, and external loads. Active landslide areas, oversteepened slopes, and saturated clays all reduce stability. See **DOGAMI SLIDO**, **Section 14.12**.

### Slump

A concrete workability measurement. A cone is filled with concrete and inverted; the concrete "slumps" and the drop distance is measured. Higher slump = more flowable. Target for this design: 4–6 inches. See **Section 12.3**.

### Smart Home

Integrated home automation and control systems. Covered in **Section 10**.

### Smoke Alarm / Smoke Detector

Required in bedrooms, outside sleeping areas, and on every floor per ORSC R314. Hardwired and interconnected with battery backup. Distinct from **CO detectors** (though combination units exist).

### SM (soil classification)

USCS code for **silty sand**. Sand with significant silt content. See **Section 14.3**.

### Snow Load

The weight of accumulated snow on a roof. Design requirement varies by geography — Oregon Cascade region: 50–100+ psf; Willamette Valley: 20–30 psf.

### Soaking Tub

A deep bathtub for soaking rather than showering. Freestanding or drop-in. See **Section 9.8**.

### Socket (electrical) — see **Receptacle**

### Softener — see **Water Softener**

### Soil Series (NRCS)

A specific named soil type in the NRCS classification system. Each series has documented physical and engineering properties. Examples: Woodburn silt loam (Willamette Valley), Astoria silty clay loam (Coast Range). Found via Web Soil Survey. See **Section 14.15**.

### Solarium / Sunroom

A room with extensive glazing on multiple sides. Not specifically in this design but an option for custom variants.

### Solar — Photovoltaic (PV)

### Spackle

A fine filler for nail holes and minor wall defects before painting.

### Span

The horizontal distance a beam or joist covers between supports.

### SPD — Surge Protective Device

### Specific Gravity

The density of a substance relative to water. Used in chemistry and materials science; less common in building.

### Spray Foam

Polyurethane insulation sprayed as a liquid that expands and hardens. **Open-cell** (lower R, less water-resistant) or **closed-cell** (higher R, water-resistant). Used in some portions of this design for air sealing. See **Open-Cell, Closed-Cell**.

USCS code for **poorly-graded sand**. Sand with relatively uniform particle size. Less stable than well-graded. See **Section 14.3**.

### SPT — Standard Penetration Test

The professional benchmark soil test. A 2-inch outside diameter split-spoon sampler is driven into the bottom of a borehole using a 140 lb hammer falling 30 inches. ### Square Footage

A house's living area, conventionally measured within the exterior walls. Conditioned vs. unconditioned, above-grade vs. below-grade — measurement conventions vary.

Power consumed by equipment that is on but not actively working (computer in sleep mode, unused transformer, etc.). Reduced by good design.

### Static Water Level

### Stem Wall

A foundation wall rising from a footing to grade. Supports the above-grade structure. This design uses a mat foundation with thickened edges rather than discrete stem walls.

### Stick-Built

Constructed piece-by-piece on site, versus prefabricated off-site. Most US residential construction is stick-built. This design, with on-site CMU laying, is effectively stick-built masonry.

### Stone Columns / Vibro-Replacement

Soil improvement method: vertical columns of compacted aggregate created by vibrating probe. Densifies surrounding soil, drains it, and reinforces it. Specialty contractor only. See **Section 14.13.5**.

### Stoop

A small platform at a door, typically concrete.

### Strand (rebar)

High-strength steel cables used in post-tensioned concrete. Not used in this design.

### Stucco

A cement-based exterior finish applied over a lath substrate. Traditional in the Southwest and Mediterranean architecture. Not used in this design.

### Stud

A vertical member in a wall framing. Wood studs are traditional; metal studs are used in this design's interior partitions. See **Section 9.3**.

### Styrofoam

A Dow brand of XPS insulation, often used generically. See **XPS**.

### Subfloor

### Subgrade

The soil surface immediately below structural elements. The "subgrade" of a slab is the prepared ground the slab rests on. Subgrade preparation includes compaction, leveling, and material adequacy. See **Section 14.6, 14.9**.

### Submersible Pump

A pump designed to operate underwater, used in wells. Durable, quiet, efficient. See **Section 6, Well**.

### Subpanel

### Subsoil

### Sump Pump

A pump in a pit (sump) at the low point of a basement or crawlspace, removing any water that collects. Protects against flooding.

### Supply (plumbing or HVAC)

The line delivering fresh water or conditioned air to a fixture or space.

### Surge — see **SPD**

### Sweat Fitting (plumbing)

A copper pipe joint made by soldering. See **Soldered Joint**.

### Switch

A wood-eating insect. Oregon has subterranean termites in some regions. This design's all-masonry envelope is termite-proof (no wood for termites to eat).

### Termite Shield

A metal flashing at the top of the foundation wall that stops termites from traveling into wood framing. Not needed in this design (no wood).

### Terrazzo

A composite floor material of marble or glass chips in a cementitious or epoxy matrix, polished smooth. Durable, beautiful, slippery when wet. Alternative to polished concrete.

### Test Pit

A hole dug for soil investigation, typically with an excavator. Allows visual inspection of soil layers, sampling at depth, and detection of obstructions. Complements DCP testing. See **Section 14.5.1**.

### Thermal Break

A non-conductive element separating two conductive elements to prevent heat transfer. Aluminum window frames have thermal breaks (plastic strips) between interior and exterior aluminum. See **Section 5, 9.13**.

### Thermal Bridge / Thermal Bridging

A high-conductivity path through insulation that reduces effective R-value. Wood studs in framed walls, steel fasteners in SIPs, metal ties in cavity walls — all thermal bridges. This design avoids them via FRP ties, continuous exterior insulation, and careful detailing. See **Section 5, 12.6**.

### Thermal Camera / Thermal Imager

A camera that detects infrared radiation, showing heat patterns as images. Used to find leaks, verify insulation, and inspect electrical panels for overloaded components. See **FLIR, IR, Section 12.6**.

### Thermal Mass

The capacity of a material to store heat. Concrete, masonry, and water are high thermal mass; wood, foam, and air are low. High thermal mass smooths temperature swings and enables radiant heating strategies. Fundamental to this design.

### Thermal Resistance — see **R-Value**

### Thermocouple

A temperature sensor using dissimilar metals. See **K-Type Thermocouple**.

### Thermostat

A temperature-sensing control for HVAC. Wired or wireless, dumb or smart. Per-room thermostats in this design enable zoning. See **Section 8.4**.

### Thermostatic Mixing Valve (TMV)

A valve that mixes hot and cold water to deliver a consistent temperature, despite varying inlet pressures or temperatures. Used at shower heads (anti-scald) and centrally for the whole house. See **Section 6.8, 9.8**.

### THHN / THWN-2

Insulation types for building wire. **THHN** = thermoplastic high-heat-resistance nylon-jacketed. **THWN-2** = same, wet-rated. Standard conductors for conduit in this design. See **Section 7.12**.

### Thread

A wireless mesh protocol for smart home devices. Low-power, IP-based, backbone of Matter. See **Matter, Section 10.3**.

### Threshold

The bottom of a door frame. Seals against weather and may have a drip edge.

### Tie (masonry)

A connector linking the two wythes of a cavity wall. Historically steel; in this design, FRP to eliminate thermal bridging. See **Cavity Wall, FRP, Section 4.5**.

### Tile

Ceramic, porcelain, stone, or glass pieces installed on floors, walls, or countertops. See **Section 9.2, 9.8**.

### Timber

Structural wood. **Heavy timber** construction uses large dimensions. Not used in this design.

### Title

Legal ownership of real property. **Title search** identifies any liens, easements, or encumbrances. **Title insurance** protects against undisclosed issues.

### Toilet

A fixture for waste disposal, using water to flush into the drain system. Modern models use 1.28–1.6 GPF. Comfort-height (17–19 inch seat) preferred. See **Section 9.8**.

### Tongue and Groove (T&G)

A joinery profile where one piece has a protruding tongue that fits into the groove of the adjacent piece. Used in wood flooring, some paneling.

### Topography

The shape and elevation of land. Steep topography complicates construction; flat topography is easier.

### Topping Slab

A thin (1.5–2 inch) concrete slab poured over a structural slab, often containing radiant tubing. See **Section 4, 8.3, 11.13**.

### Top Plate

In wood framing, the horizontal member at the top of a wall. Less relevant in masonry construction (the top course of block serves this function, often as a bond beam).

### Topsoil

The uppermost soil layer, rich in organic matter. NEVER bears a foundation; must be stripped before construction. Stockpile for landscape reuse. Typically 4–18 inches thick in undisturbed Oregon sites. See **Section 14.3**.

---

### Torque

Rotational force. Fasteners are torqued to specified values for proper tension (without overstressing).

### TPO — Thermoplastic Polyolefin

A roofing membrane type. White, reflective, single-ply, mechanically fastened or adhered. Long-lived (20–30 years). One option for the Option A flat roof. See **EPDM, Membrane**.

### T&P Valve — Temperature and Pressure Relief Valve

A safety device on water heaters that opens if temperature or pressure exceeds safe limits. Required by code.

### Track Lighting

Linear lighting with movable heads on a track. Decorative and functional.

### Transfer Grille

An opening between rooms allowing air to pass from one to the other (for return air). Typically low in the wall or over a door. See **Section 8.11**.

### Transformer (utility)

Equipment that steps high-voltage utility distribution (e.g., 7,200V) down to service voltage (240V split-phase for residential). Usually on a pole (overhead service) or pad-mounted (underground service).

### Trap

A curved section of drain pipe holding water to prevent sewer gas escape. Every fixture has a trap. Most are P-traps (looks like a P on its side). See **P-Trap**.

### Tread

The horizontal part of a stair step (what you walk on). Contrast with **riser** (the vertical face).

### Tree Removal

Oregon has specific rules about tree removal on certain types of land. Some trees may require permits.

### Trench

A narrow excavation for burying utilities or footings.

### Trim — Exterior

The finish members at the edges of a building — fascia, rake board, corner boards, frieze. Less prominent in masonry construction.

### Trim — Interior

The finish members framing doors and windows, covering wall/floor joints, etc. See **Section 9.6**.

### TR — Tamper-Resistant (receptacle)

Required by NEC 406.12 in dwellings for all 125V 15A and 20A receptacles. Internal shutters prevent objects from being inserted into individual slots, protecting small children.

### Truss

A structural assembly of smaller members triangulated for strength. Roof trusses and floor trusses are common. Rare in this design.

### TV — Television

Referenced for home entertainment wiring in Section 10.8.

### Type X Drywall

Fire-rated drywall (5/8 inch thick, with fiber reinforcement). Required for fire-separation walls, garage-to-house separation. Specified throughout this design for fire resistance.

## U

### U-Factor

A measure of heat transmission through a window or assembly. Lower U-factor = better insulation. Inverse of R-value: **U = 1/R**. Window performance reported as U-factor.

### Ufer Ground — see **CEE**

### Ufer Ground — see main glossary (CEE)

### UF — Underground Feeder (cable)

Electrical cable rated for direct burial. Used sparingly; conduit is preferred in this design. See **NEC 340**.

### UL — Underwriters Laboratories

A product safety certification organization. "UL Listed" means a product has been tested and certified for safety. Required or strongly recommended for most electrical products.

### Underlayment

A material beneath finish flooring (e.g., rosin paper, foam, cork) providing cushioning, moisture protection, or sound isolation.

### Underwriters Knot

A specific knot in an extension cord to relieve strain on the terminal connections. A trade detail.

### Uniformity Coefficient (Cu)

A measure of how varied the particle sizes are in a soil. Higher Cu = more varied = better packed. Used in classifying granular soils as well-graded (GW, SW) vs. poorly-graded (GP, SP). See **Section 14.3**.

### Unit (plumbing fixture unit)

A standard measure of fixture water demand, used in pipe sizing. A sink = 1 unit; a tub = 2 units; etc. Per ORSC.

### UPC — Uniform Plumbing Code

The base plumbing code that Oregon (and some other Western states) adopts with state-specific modifications. Contrast with **IPC** (International Plumbing Code) used in most other states. Oregon's adopted version is the **OPSC**. See **OPSC**.

### Uplift (foundation)

Upward force on a foundation, typically from buoyancy in a high water table or expansive clay swelling. Empty basements on high water tables can be lifted out of the ground if not adequately weighted or anchored. See **Section 14.14**.

---

### UPS — Uninterruptible Power Supply

A battery backup device for electronics. Provides temporary power during outages and cleans dirty power. Used for the networking rack in this design. See **Section 10.13**.

### Urban Growth Boundary (UGB)

Oregon's land-use planning tool that limits sprawl. Property outside the UGB is typically zoned for rural or resource use.

### USCS — Unified Soil Classification System

The standard system for classifying soils by particle size and plasticity. Uses 2-letter codes (GW, GP, SM, SC, ML, CL, MH, CH, OL, OH, Pt). The first letter indicates dominant fraction (G, S, M, C, O, P); the second indicates secondary characteristic (W, P, M, C, H, L). See **Section 14.3**.

### UV — Ultraviolet

Electromagnetic radiation shorter than visible light. Degrades plastics and rubber; kills bacteria. **UV water treatment** kills pathogens in well water. **UV-resistant** materials are formulated to resist sun damage.

## V

### Vacuum Breaker

A backflow prevention device on hose bibs and other potential cross-connections.

### Valve

A device controlling fluid flow. Many types: ball, gate, globe, check, zone, mixing, pressure-reducing, relief.

### Vanity

The cabinet and countertop at a bathroom sink. See **Section 9.8**.

### Vapor Barrier / Vapor Retarder

A material that resists moisture movement. Vapor drive direction depends on climate. In Oregon's mixed climate, vapor management focuses on avoiding vapor condensation within wall assemblies through proper placement of barriers and drying potential. See **Condensation, Envelope, Section 5**.

### Vapor Drive

The natural movement of moisture from areas of high vapor pressure to low. In Oregon: inward in winter (humid outdoor to drier indoor after ventilation), outward in summer. Design must manage both directions.

### Varnish

A protective wood finish.

### VDC — Volts DC

A voltage level in direct current systems.

### Veneer

A thin layer applied over a substrate. **Brick veneer** = single wythe of brick over a wall. **Stone veneer** = stone facing, often thin-cut. **Wood veneer** = thin sheet of wood on a substrate.

### Ventilation

The deliberate exchange of indoor and outdoor air. Required for air quality in tight houses. This design uses balanced mechanical ventilation with heat recovery. See **ERV, HRV, Section 8.6**.

### Vent (mechanical)

An opening for air movement. **Attic vent, soffit vent, ridge vent** for unconditioned attics (not applicable here).

### Vent (plumbing)

A pipe that admits air to the drain system, preventing siphonage and sewer gas entry. In this design, every fixture has an individual vent (no AAVs). See **AAV, Section 6**.

### Veratile

Adaptable, flexible in use. Used descriptively, not technically.

### Vermiculite

A lightweight mineral used as insulation (historical) or soil amendment. Some vintage vermiculite contains asbestos.

### VFD — Variable Frequency Drive

An electronic motor controller that varies speed. Used in modern HVAC equipment for efficiency.

### Vibroflotation

Specialty soil improvement method using a vibrating probe to densify loose granular soils, especially for liquefaction mitigation. The probe is inserted, vibrated to densify surrounding sand, then withdrawn. Stone may be added to backfill. See **Section 14.13.2**.

---

### Viega

A plumbing manufacturer (PEX, radiant systems, press fittings). Referenced in Section 8.

### Viewer — see **Peephole**

### Visqueen

A brand of polyethylene sheeting used for vapor barriers, often used generically. 6-mil minimum for under-slab; 10-15 mil preferred. See **Polyethylene**.

### VLAN — Virtual Local Area Network

A network segment created in software rather than physical wiring, enabling network segregation on shared infrastructure. Used in this design's smart home network security. See **Section 10.12**.

### VOC — Volatile Organic Compound

Chemical emissions from paints, sealants, adhesives, and finishes. Contributes to poor indoor air quality. Low-VOC or zero-VOC products specified throughout this design. See **Section 9.3**.

### Voltage

Electrical potential. Standard US residential: 120V (normal outlets), 240V split-phase (large appliances). See **AC, NEC**.

### Voltage Drop

The reduction of voltage along a wire run due to resistance. Targets: ≤3% on branch circuits, ≤5% combined feeder + branch. Addressed by adequate wire gauge. See **AWG, Section 7**.

### VPN — Virtual Private Network

An encrypted network connection between remote devices and the home network. Used in this design for remote access without relying on cloud services. See **Section 10.6**.

## W

### Wafer (pipe fitting)

A thin fitting fitting between flanges.

### Walk-In

A room large enough to walk into. **Walk-in closet, walk-in pantry, walk-in shower.**

### Wall Tie — see **Tie**

### WAN — Wide Area Network

The outside network, usually meaning the internet connection. Contrast with **LAN** (local area network inside the house). See **Section 10**.

### Washer (appliance) / Dryer

Laundry appliances. See **Section 9.9**.

### Water Closet (WC)

Archaic term for a toilet; still used in some contexts to refer to the entire small room housing a toilet.

### Water Hammer

A pressure spike caused by rapid valve closure in a plumbing system. Creates noise and can damage piping over time. Mitigated by water hammer arrestors — small air chambers at problem fixtures.

### Water Heater

A device that heats potable water for use. Types: storage tank (electric, gas, heat pump); tankless (electric or gas); indirect (through a boiler coil). Heat pump water heater preferred in this design. See **Section 6**.

### Water Main — see **Main**

### Waterproofing

A performance-grade moisture barrier, typically membrane-based. Distinct from **damp-proofing**. Used on the exterior of this design's foundation. See **Damp-Proofing, Membrane, Section 5**.

### Watershed

The geographic area draining to a single point. Relevant to rural site selection (do downstream neighbors depend on this site's runoff quality?).

### Water Softener

A device that removes calcium and magnesium (hardness) from water using ion exchange. Reduces scaling in pipes and fixtures. See **Section 6**.

### Water Table

The level below which soil is saturated with water. Affects foundation design; high water tables require robust waterproofing and drainage.

### Wattage

The rate of electrical power consumption. 1 watt = 1 joule per second. Light bulbs, heaters, and appliances are rated in watts.

### Weather Barrier

A layer that stops bulk water from entering the envelope. Distinct from vapor barrier (slows vapor diffusion) and air barrier (stops air movement). Often the same product serves multiple functions. See **Envelope, Section 5**.

### Weathered Rock — see **Saprolite**

### Weatherstripping

Flexible seals at door and window edges. Reduces air infiltration and sound transmission.

### Web Soil Survey (NRCS)

Free online soil mapping tool providing soil series identification and engineering limitations for any US address. URL: websoilsurvey.nrcs.usda.gov. See **NRCS, Section 14.15**.

### Weep Hole

A small opening in masonry that allows any water in the cavity to drain out. Placed at the base of veneer walls, above flashings. See **Cavity Wall, Section 4, 5**.

### Well

A water supply from groundwater. In Oregon, wells require permits from the Water Resources Department. Rural homes typically rely on wells. See **Section 6, 11.6**.

### Wet Wall

A wall containing plumbing supply and drain lines. Typically grouped to minimize the number of wet walls in a building.

### Wick Drains — see **PVD**

### Wi-Fi / WiFi

Wireless networking. Common abbreviations come from "Wireless Fidelity." Standards: 802.11 a/b/g/n/ac/ax/be. See **Section 7.16, 10.3**.

### Wilco

Informal mention; may refer to a specific product or brand.

### Willamette Silt

A wind-blown (loess-like) silt deposit common on Willamette Valley benches and uplands. Variable plasticity — can be expansive in places. Many Willamette Valley homes are built on it. See **Section 14.15**.

### Wind Load

The horizontal force wind exerts on a building. Design requirement depends on location. Oregon basic wind speeds: 90–115 mph depending on region.

### Window

A glazed opening in a wall. See **Glazing, Section 5, 9, 11.11**.

### Wind Washing

Air movement behind or through insulation, reducing effective R-value. Prevented by continuous air barriers.

### Wire

See **Conductor, AWG**.

### Wireway / Raceway — see **Raceway**

### Wood

Lumber. Used extensively in conventional construction; specifically avoided in this design's envelope for longevity (fire, rot, termites, moisture). See **Timber**.

### WR — Weather-Resistant (receptacle)

Required in outdoor and damp locations per NEC 406.9. Rated for exposure to moisture.

### Wythe

A single layer of masonry. This design's cavity wall has two wythes: an 8-inch structural inner wythe and a 4-inch decorative outer wythe. See **Cavity Wall, CMU**.
