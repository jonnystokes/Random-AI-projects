# Section 9 — Interior and Finishes

*What you see, what you touch, what makes a concrete bunker feel like home*

This section covers the visible, tactile, livable surfaces of the house: floors, walls, ceilings, doors, trim, cabinets, counters, bathrooms, storage, and the overall aesthetic logic that turns a high-performance concrete envelope into a warm, inviting home.

The guiding philosophy: **the structure does the structure's job** (1000-year lifespan, thermal mass, fire resistance); **the finishes do the living's job** (comfort, beauty, replaceability on a 20–50 year cycle). Finishes are expected to be updated over the building's life — they're designed to be easy to refresh without disturbing the envelope.

---

## 9.1 Finish Philosophy

**Plain language:** The concrete and steel are permanent. The finishes aren't. Over 1000 years, the kitchen will be renovated many times, the bathrooms will be redone, paint will get redone every 15 years, flooring will be replaced every 30–50 years. So the design treats finishes as a layer sitting on top of the permanent building — beautiful, comfortable, and deliberately easy to remove and replace without damaging the structure underneath.

**Technical detail:** Core finish principles:

- **Structure and finish are separate layers** — finishes bolt to, attach to, or float over the structural envelope without penetrating it
- **No finish material is allowed to hide structural problems** — every structural surface stays accessible for inspection via removable finish panels, service layer access, or both
- **Natural materials preferred** — real wood, stone, tile, plaster, natural fiber — over synthetic substitutes when lifecycle cost and aesthetic both favor the natural option
- **Regional and sourceable** — materials sourced from within a reasonable radius of the site when possible, for both environmental and resilience reasons
- **Timeless over trendy** — the design aims for aesthetic choices that read well in 2030, 2050, and 2100 (and hopefully well beyond)
- **Durable but replaceable** — each finish designed to last its reasonable lifetime, then get replaced without drama

**The three-tier durability model:**

| Tier | Lifespan | Example Materials | Replacement Approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 (structural) | 1000 years | Concrete, rebar, masonry, foundation | Never replaced |
| Tier 2 (semi-permanent) | 50–100 years | Cabinets, doors, tile, stone, hardwood, built-ins | Replaced once or twice per century |
| Tier 3 (consumable) | 10–30 years | Paint, carpet, appliances, light fixtures, faucets | Replaced multiple times per century |

**Why this matters:** decisions about any given finish should consider its tier. Tier 2 materials justify craft investment and cost premium. Tier 3 materials are practical — spend less, update more often.

## 9.2 Floor Finishes

**Plain language:** Every floor has a radiant heating slab underneath it, which means the floor finish needs to work well with heat coming up through it. Some materials (tile, stone, stained concrete, engineered wood) love radiant heat. Others (thick carpet, solid hardwood, some vinyl) don't. The design uses the radiant-friendly materials in a palette that fits each room's use.

**Technical detail:** Floor finish options by room type:

**Polished or stained concrete (primary floors — great room, kitchen, hallways):**
- **The topping slab from the radiant heating system** (see Section 8) serves as the finished floor
- **Ground and polished** to a matte or satin finish after the radiant loops have been pressure-tested
- **Stained with acid-etch or water-based dyes** for color variation — earth tones, gray-tones, sepia
- **Sealed with a penetrating sealer** (lithium silicate or similar) that doesn't form a film — the concrete breathes, ages, and develops patina
- **Maintenance:** occasional damp mop, reseal every 3–5 years
- **Thermal performance:** ideal — concrete is the radiant system's primary surface
- **Durability:** effectively permanent — 50+ year refinishing cycle, never replaced
- **Cost:** low material cost, higher labor for polishing and staining — net moderate

**Porcelain or ceramic tile (bathrooms, mudroom, laundry, entry):**
- **Large-format tile** (18 x 18 inch, 24 x 24 inch, or larger) — minimizes grout lines, simpler cleaning
- **Through-body porcelain** — chips and scratches don't show the substrate
- **Matte finish** in wet areas — slip resistance above 0.42 (DCOF — dynamic coefficient of friction) per ANSI A137.1
- **Set over a thin-set mortar bed** directly on the topping slab, with a waterproof membrane (Schluter Ditra, USG Durock, or equivalent) as the isolation and waterproofing layer
- **Grout:** epoxy grout in wet areas (no staining, no re-sealing), cementitious grout elsewhere
- **Thermal performance:** excellent with radiant — tile is a natural radiator
- **Durability:** 50–100+ years
- **Cost:** moderate material, moderate labor

**Engineered wood (primary bedroom, guest bedrooms, home office):**
- **Engineered wood (thin hardwood veneer on a plywood core)** specifically rated for radiant heat — solid hardwood expands and contracts too much over radiant
- **Brands:** any reputable engineered wood with a radiant-rating from the manufacturer
- **Species choices:** white oak (durable, classic), walnut (warmer, darker), hickory (hardest, dramatic grain), ash (light, neutral)
- **Finish:** pre-finished with aluminum-oxide or similar hard finish, or unfinished and site-finished with oil (Rubio Monocoat, Osmo) for warmth and repairability
- **Installation:** floating (click-lock) over an acoustic underlayment designed for radiant — no nailing into the topping slab
- **Thermal performance:** good with radiant if correctly specified — watch for thermal resistance rating below R-1.0 for best heat transfer
- **Durability:** 40–80 years (refinish the wear layer every 15–25 years if solid-feel engineered wood is chosen with a re-sandable veneer)
- **Cost:** moderate to high

**Natural stone (formal entry, possibly primary bathroom — optional luxury accent):**
- **Slate, limestone, travertine, granite, or marble** in selected areas
- **Honed finish** for slip resistance; polished only where slipping is not a concern
- **Set over thin-set on waterproof membrane** — same detail as tile
- **Thermal performance:** excellent
- **Durability:** 100+ years
- **Cost:** high material, high labor

**Natural cork (home office, library, possibly children's bedrooms):**
- **Natural cork tile or plank** glued or floated over the topping slab
- **Warm, quiet, slightly yielding** underfoot — reduces noise and foot fatigue
- **Radiant-compatible** at reasonable thicknesses
- **Refinishable** with renewable finishes
- **Lifespan:** 25–50 years
- **Cost:** moderate

**What's intentionally NOT used:**
- **Wall-to-wall carpet** — insulates the radiant floor, traps dust and allergens, short lifespan
- **Solid hardwood over radiant** — too much movement, gaps develop
- **Vinyl plank / LVT** — shorter life than the other options, off-gassing concerns in some products, poor radiant performance
- **Area rugs are welcome, permanent carpet is not** — the design embraces rugs for warmth and character but doesn't commit the floor to them

**Floor finish map for a typical 3,000 sqft home:**

| Room | Primary Finish | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Great room | Polished concrete | Primary living, radiant master, easy clean |
| Kitchen | Polished concrete | Durable, cleanable, continuous with great room |
| Dining | Polished concrete or engineered wood | Continuous or accent |
| Entry / mudroom | Tile (porcelain) | Weather and dirt |
| Hallways | Polished concrete | Continuous with adjacent rooms |
| Primary bedroom | Engineered wood | Warmth underfoot |
| Secondary bedrooms | Engineered wood or cork | Warmth, comfort |
| Bathrooms | Large-format tile | Water, slip-resistance |
| Laundry | Tile | Water tolerance |
| Office | Cork or engineered wood | Comfort for long sitting |
| Basement | Polished concrete | Durable, moisture-tolerant |

**Key code references:**
- ORSC R302 (fire-resistance rated construction — applies to finish materials)
- ANSI A137.1 (tile slip resistance)
- OMSC 1301 (radiant floor compatibility — coordinate with flooring manufacturer)

## 9.3 Wall Finishes

**Plain language:** The exterior walls are thick concrete block on the inside. Either they stay visible (painted, stained, or left raw) as a design statement, or they get covered with drywall attached to wood furring strips. Interior partition walls are simpler — typically metal stud with drywall, or another course of CMU for sound and fire separation between rooms.

**Technical detail:** Wall finish options by wall type:

**Exterior masonry wall interior face — Option A: exposed CMU:**
- **Raw or painted concrete block** as the finish surface
- **Requires higher-quality block laying** — joints struck flush, mortar colors coordinated, block face selected for appearance
- **Finished with:** 
  - Clear penetrating sealer for natural look
  - Paint (matte latex) for solid color
  - Lime wash or mineral paint for breathable, textured finish
  - Integral color block (pigmented during manufacture) for permanent color
- **Aesthetic:** industrial / loft / warm minimalist
- **Maintenance:** paint every 15–25 years; sealer every 10 years
- **Cost:** lowest material cost, zero finish trim work
- **Acoustic:** hard, reflective — may need acoustic treatment in large rooms (rugs, upholstery, acoustic panels)
- **Thermal:** maximum thermal mass contact with the room — the block itself participates in the thermal flywheel

**Exterior masonry wall interior face — Option B: drywall on furring:**
- **Wood or metal furring strips** (1.5 to 3.5 inch deep channels) attached to the CMU face with masonry anchors
- **Cavity between furring strips** used for electrical conduit, low-voltage wiring, and optional additional insulation
- **Drywall (5/8 inch Type X, fire-rated)** screwed to the furring
- **Finished with:** paint, wallpaper, plaster veneer, paneling — any conventional finish
- **Aesthetic:** conventional interior — warm, familiar, infinitely customizable
- **Maintenance:** paint every 10–15 years; drywall repairs easy
- **Cost:** moderate material, moderate labor
- **Acoustic:** softer, less reflective than bare CMU
- **Thermal:** slightly reduces thermal mass contact (the drywall adds insulation between the CMU and the room) but negligible impact on overall performance

**Option B.1 — metal studs vs. wood furring:**
- **Metal studs (25 gauge, 1-5/8 or 2-1/2 inch)** align with the no-wood-in-the-envelope philosophy
- **Wood furring (1x3 or 2x3)** is simpler, cheaper, and easier for the owner-builder to work with
- **Both are acceptable** — metal is preferred for consistency with the all-masonry ethos; wood is pragmatic
- **Decision:** per-project choice based on builder preference

**Exterior masonry wall interior face — Option C: plaster veneer:**
- **Thin-coat plaster** (Structolite, USG plaster, lime plaster) applied directly to the CMU after prep
- **Smooth or textured finish**
- **Finished with:** breathable mineral paint, left natural, or polished for a Venetian-plaster appearance
- **Aesthetic:** Old-world European, warm, textured, sophisticated
- **Maintenance:** repair minor cracks every 10–20 years; reapply surface color every 20–30 years
- **Cost:** moderate material, high skilled labor (good plasterers are rare)
- **Acoustic:** similar to drywall
- **Thermal:** very high — direct contact with the CMU mass with minimal added insulation

**Interior partition walls — metal stud with drywall (primary method):**
- **25 gauge metal studs**, 2-1/2 or 3-5/8 inch, 16 inches on center
- **Double drywall on both sides** for sound isolation where needed (bedroom to hallway, bathroom to bedroom, office to living area)
- **Single drywall on both sides** for standard partitions
- **Insulated cavity** with mineral wool batts for sound control
- **Fire rating:** Type X (5/8 inch fire-rated) drywall provides 1 hour rated wall assembly — overkill for residential but cheap insurance
- **Why metal:** aligns with the no-wood-in-the-envelope philosophy, better straightness, doesn't warp, fire-resistant, doesn't host termites

**Interior partition walls — CMU option:**
- **8-inch CMU partition** between rooms where maximum sound isolation and fire separation are desired
- **Useful for:** separating the primary bedroom suite from the rest of the house, separating a home theater, separating a music practice room
- **Finished:** same options as exterior CMU walls (paint, plaster, drywall on furring)
- **Limitations:** can't easily relocate later, heavy load on the floor slab (requires coordination with structural design)

**Wet-area walls (bathrooms, laundry, kitchen):**
- **Cement board (Durock, HardieBacker)** replaces drywall in wet zones — water resistant, mold resistant, tile-ready
- **Behind tile surfaces only** — the rest of the bathroom uses moisture-resistant drywall ("green board" or "purple board") painted with moisture-resistant paint
- **Full-height tile in shower surrounds** with proper waterproofing membrane behind the cement board (Schluter Kerdi or equivalent)

**Paint strategy (for all painted wall surfaces):**
- **Low-VOC or zero-VOC** paint throughout (Sherwin-Williams Harmony, Benjamin Moore Natura, Farrow & Ball, and similar)
- **Matte or eggshell finish** in living areas (hides imperfections, forgiving)
- **Satin or semi-gloss** in wet areas and trim (washable)
- **Semi-gloss** for doors and cabinets (durable, wipeable)
- **Flat** in ceilings (no glare, hides any imperfections)
- **Two coats over a quality primer** — tinted primer on deeply colored walls for better coverage

**Key code references:**
- ORSC R302 (fire-resistance — partition wall ratings)
- ASTM C1396 (drywall standard)
- ASTM C1178 (cement board standard)
- OSC R702 (interior covering — drywall, plaster, wood, etc.)

## 9.4 Ceiling Finishes

**Plain language:** The structural ceiling is a concrete slab. That slab is either left exposed, painted white or another color for a loft / industrial feel, or covered with drywall hung from the underside. The service layer above the ceiling provides the route for any ventilation ducts and wiring — no above-ceiling space is needed for those, so the ceiling can be flat and uninterrupted.

**Technical detail:** Ceiling finish options:

**Option A — Exposed concrete ceiling:**
- **The underside of the floor slab above** serves as the finished ceiling
- **Requires high-quality concrete forming** to achieve a clean visible finish — smooth wood forms, or matched panel forms, or board-form texture for character
- **Finish options:**
  - Left raw (natural concrete color, variegated)
  - Painted white (brightens the room, classic modern)
  - Painted in a deep color (dramatic, moody, works in specific rooms)
  - Stained (subtle color variation)
  - Lime-washed (soft, breathable, slightly textured)
- **Lighting:** surface-mounted or pendant fixtures (no recessed lights — no cavity to recess into) **except** where the slab has pre-cast cavities or where the topping slab on the upper floor has a channel that a recessed fixture can fit into
- **Aesthetic:** industrial, minimalist, honest
- **Maintenance:** paint every 15–25 years
- **Acoustic:** hard, reflective — plan for soft furnishings or acoustic panels as needed

**Option B — Drywall ceiling hung from the slab:**
- **Metal furring channel** (or wood strapping) attached to the underside of the slab with concrete anchors
- **Drywall screwed to the furring** — 1/2 inch or 5/8 inch
- **Small cavity** (3/4 to 1-1/2 inch) between drywall and slab — enough for recessed lighting cans, ceiling speakers, smoke detector wiring
- **Finished with paint** — typically flat white or subtle off-white
- **Aesthetic:** conventional, familiar, warm
- **Allows:** recessed lighting, ceiling speakers, flush-mount HVAC supply registers — features that are difficult with exposed concrete
- **Maintenance:** paint every 10–15 years

**Option C — Plaster ceiling:**
- **Thin-coat plaster** applied directly to the slab after prep
- **Smooth finish typically** — no lath or framing needed (unlike wood-stud ceilings)
- **Finished with:** mineral paint, lime wash, or left natural
- **Aesthetic:** Old-world European, handcrafted, warm
- **Cost:** moderate to high due to skilled labor

**Option D — Exposed coffers or beams (advanced):**
- **Decorative concrete coffers** or integrally-cast beams visible on the ceiling
- **Requires forming work during the pour** — more complex, more expensive, but architecturally striking
- **Suitable for:** great room, primary bedroom, formal dining — statement rooms

**Design recommendation by room:**

| Room | Ceiling Finish | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Great room | Exposed concrete, painted white | Maximum drama, high ceiling, showcase |
| Kitchen | Drywall with recessed lighting | Task lighting needs, clean look |
| Bedrooms | Drywall with ceiling fan/light | Standard, warm, functional |
| Bathrooms | Drywall painted (moisture-resistant paint) | Moisture tolerance, lighting |
| Hallways | Exposed concrete painted or drywall | Per adjacent rooms |
| Office | Exposed concrete painted | Character, minimal fixtures |
| Basement | Exposed concrete painted white | Industrial ok, lots of service access |

**Recessed lighting limitations with exposed concrete ceilings:**
- **Can't cut into the slab** — it's structural
- **Can use pendants, surface-mount, track lighting** instead
- **Can route conduit along the ceiling surface** and into wall sconces for indirect lighting
- **Can embed lighting channels during the concrete pour** if planned at design time

**Suspended ceiling grid (for basement workshop or specific utility areas only):**
- **Drop ceiling with 2x2 or 2x4 panels** in spaces where easy access to the service layer above is priority (workshop, mechanical room)
- **Not used in habitable living areas** — aesthetically commercial, and the service layer access is already provided via grating

**Acoustic treatment:**
- **Acoustic wall panels** (fabric-wrapped fiberglass or mineral wool) in rooms with hard ceilings where echo would be a problem — music room, home theater, open great room
- **Rugs, upholstery, bookshelves** as natural acoustic absorbers
- **Ceiling-hung acoustic baffles** (a design choice, not required) for maximum acoustic control in specialized rooms

**Key code references:**
- ORSC R302 (fire-resistance — ceiling assemblies)
- ASTM C1396 (drywall)

## 9.5 Interior Doors

**Plain language:** Interior doors are solid-core, real wood or engineered, with quality hinges and hardware. No hollow-core doors, no plastic-faced doors. Every door is a piece of long-lived hardware that will outlast the occupants. Pocket doors and barn-door-style sliders are used where space-saving matters. Every door seals well enough that sound and light don't leak around the edges.

**Technical detail:** Interior door specifications:

**Standard door specifications:**
- **Solid-core doors** — either solid wood (most expensive, most beautiful) or engineered solid-core (MDF core with hardwood veneer, more stable, less expensive)
- **Thickness:** 1-3/8 inch standard interior; 1-3/4 inch for primary bedroom, home office, and wherever acoustic isolation matters
- **Height:** 8 feet (taller than standard 6'8") — matches the 9 foot ceiling height for better proportion; standard 6'8" doors are an option if budget constrained
- **Width:** 30 inch minimum for bathrooms and closets; 32 inch for most rooms; 36 inch for primary bedroom, primary bath, home office (wheelchair accessibility without calling it that)
- **Styles:** 
  - Flush (modern, clean, no panels)
  - Shaker (2- or 3-panel, transitional, warm)
  - Craftsman (thicker stiles, substantial, classic)
  - Flat-panel modern (clean, architectural)
- **Species (for real-wood doors):** white oak, walnut, cherry, alder — all durable, all beautiful, all refinishable

**Hinges:**
- **Three ball-bearing hinges per door** — extra hinge (vs. standard two) for heavy solid doors, prevents sag
- **Solid brass or stainless steel** — doesn't rust, doesn't fail, lasts indefinitely
- **Finish coordinated with door hardware** — polished nickel, oil-rubbed bronze, matte black, satin brass

**Door hardware:**
- **Lever handles rather than knobs** — easier for wet hands, children, arthritis — more accessible
- **Solid brass or stainless steel construction**
- **Privacy locks on bedrooms and bathrooms** — with emergency release accessible from outside
- **Passage sets (no lock) on closets, pantry, utility rooms**
- **Entry sets (keyed) on exterior doors only** (see 9.10 for exterior doors)

**Weatherstripping and seals:**
- **Full-perimeter weatherstripping on bedroom and bathroom doors** — reduces sound transmission, maintains privacy
- **Automatic door bottoms** (Pemko, Zero) drop when the door closes, seal the gap at the floor — effective sound isolation
- **Smoke seals on doors between attached garage and house** if a garage is present (see Section 11 — build process — if garage added later)

**Door frames:**
- **MDF or solid wood casings** matched to door style
- **Simple rectangular or modestly-detailed profiles** — avoids ornate trim that dates quickly
- **Consistent casing profile throughout the house** for visual coherence

**Pocket doors (for specific locations):**
- **Pocket doors in interior partition walls** where swing clearance is constrained — common at bathroom-to-bedroom, office-to-hall
- **Soft-close pocket door hardware** — Johnson 1500 SC, Hafner, or similar
- **Pocket must be framed with metal stud** (not wood) to align with partition framing
- **Full acoustic gasket** on the latch edge for sound isolation

**Barn doors (for specific locations):**
- **Ceiling-mounted barn door track** for primary closet, home office, laundry — rooms where a full swinging door is awkward
- **Real wood or architectural-grade panel** on the door itself
- **Soft-close barn door hardware** — Krownlab, Goldberg Brothers, or similar
- **Top-mounted track, not floor-mounted** — no floor trip hazard
- **Caution:** barn doors don't seal well (for sound or light) — use only where that's acceptable

**Door fire ratings:**
- **Door between garage (if present) and house:** 20-minute fire-rated, self-closing, solid-core — required by ORSC R302.5
- **Other interior doors:** no rating required in a single-family home
- **Stairwell doors (if any):** no rating required

**Key code references:**
- ORSC R302.5 (dwelling-garage separation — door requirements)
- ADA / ANSI A117.1 (accessible design — door width and hardware — voluntary but informs good design)

## 9.6 Trim, Millwork, and Casings

**Plain language:** The trim is the thin wood (or synthetic substitute) around doors, windows, at the top of walls (crown molding), at the bottom of walls (baseboard), and anywhere two surfaces meet. It covers gaps, adds a finished edge, and quietly sets the tone for the whole interior. This design uses simple, clean trim — substantial enough to feel permanent, restrained enough to stay out of the way.

**Technical detail:** Trim design:

**Trim material:**
- **Solid hardwood** (poplar, maple, oak, alder) — long-lived, refinishable, premium choice
- **MDF with a primed factory finish** — stable, paint-ready, more economical — strong default choice
- **Finger-jointed softwood** — OK for paint-grade installations, not for stained
- **PVC or composite trim** for exterior or wet locations — not visible inside in normal spaces

**Profile selection:**
- **Simple Shaker / Craftsman profiles** — flat stock with minimal edge detail
- **Consistent throughout the house** — same profile for door casings, window casings, baseboards (scaled appropriately)
- **No ornate Victorian or Federal profiles** — they date quickly and don't match the overall aesthetic

**Standard profiles:**

| Trim Element | Typical Size | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Baseboard | 5-1/4 to 7-1/4 inch tall, 5/8 inch thick | Substantial, grounded feel; taller in rooms with higher ceilings |
| Door casing | 3-1/2 inch wide, 5/8 inch thick | Clean, proportional |
| Window casing | 3-1/2 inch wide, matches door casing | Consistent language |
| Window stool & apron | 3/4 x 4 inch stool, 5-1/2 inch apron | Rested sill, not a flush picture-frame |
| Crown molding | 3 to 4-1/2 inch | Optional — used in formal rooms or skipped for clean-modern look |
| Chair rail | 2-1/2 inch at 36 inch AFF | Optional, dining room if using two-tone paint or wainscoting |
| Ceiling transition | None (square drywall edge) or 3/4 inch cove | Simple |

**Primary paint-grade default:**
- **MDF trim, factory-primed, painted on-site** with semi-gloss white or off-white
- **Same color throughout** for all trim — simpler, cleaner, timeless
- **Caulk every trim-to-wall joint** before painting to eliminate gaps

**Stain-grade option for feature rooms:**
- **Solid hardwood trim** (white oak, walnut) stained and sealed
- **Used in:** library, home office, primary bedroom, dining — rooms that benefit from warm wood
- **Coordinates with** cabinetry and built-ins in those rooms

**Built-in millwork:**
- **Bookcases, window seats, entry benches, mudroom lockers, office built-ins** — site-built or semi-custom with cabinet-grade plywood and hardwood face frames and doors
- **Finish:** painted or stained to match room palette
- **Hardware:** concealed hinges, soft-close drawers, quality pulls
- **Lifespan expectation:** 50+ years — built-ins are Tier 2 durability, worth investing in

**Window treatments (attached to the trim or walls):**
- **Inside-mount roller shades** (light-filtering or blackout) in bedrooms
- **Wood or composite blinds** as an alternative
- **Cellular shades** for better insulation on north-facing or large windows
- **Curtains/drapes** as secondary layer — decorative, acoustic, light-blocking
- **Motorized shades** in hard-to-reach locations (tall windows, above sinks) — wired or battery-powered

**Key code references:**
- No specific code — trim is a finish choice
- ORSC R316 (foam plastic — if using foam-core trim near heat sources)

## 9.7 Kitchen Cabinets and Counters

**Plain language:** The kitchen is the most-used room in the house. The cabinets are built to last — real wood boxes, dovetailed drawers, solid-wood doors or high-quality engineered doors, quality hardware. The counters are a material that lasts and cooks well on — quartz, granite, or a thick wood butcher-block island. The sink is under-mount and real metal. Nothing here is throwaway.

**Technical detail:** Kitchen cabinet specifications:

**Cabinet construction:**
- **Plywood boxes** (3/4 inch cabinet-grade plywood, not particleboard) — stronger, holds screws better, resists moisture
- **Dovetailed solid-wood drawer boxes** — classic joinery, lasts indefinitely
- **Full-extension soft-close drawer slides** (Blum, Salice, or similar) — quality hardware that doesn't fail
- **Concealed soft-close hinges** on doors (Blum BLUMOTION or equivalent)
- **Adjustable shelves** with metal shelf pins (not plastic)

**Cabinet face options:**
- **Solid wood doors and drawer fronts** — white oak, walnut, cherry, or painted maple
- **Inset construction** (door is flush with the frame) — traditional, highest quality
- **Overlay construction** (door sits over the frame) — more common, less expensive, also fine

**Style:**
- **Shaker style** — 5-piece recessed panel doors, flat and clean — classic, timeless, works in traditional and modern contexts
- **Slab / flat-panel modern** — no frame, single piece — strongly modern look
- **Decision:** depends on overall home aesthetic; Shaker is the safer timeless choice

**Cabinet layout principles:**
- **Work triangle** (sink, range, refrigerator) with each leg 4 to 9 feet — ergonomic baseline
- **Generous counter space** — at least 24 inch counter on each side of the range, 18 inch on the handle side of the refrigerator, 24 inch each side of the sink
- **Deep drawers (not lower cabinets with shelves)** in most base cabinets — far better storage access
- **Pull-out pantry cabinets** — tall, narrow cabinets with roll-out shelves inside — unbeatable for canned goods and dry storage
- **Lazy Susan or swing-out corner cabinets** — use the corner effectively
- **Trash pull-out** integrated into a base cabinet adjacent to the sink
- **Under-cabinet lighting** on every upper cabinet (LED strips — see Section 7)

**Countertop options:**

**Quartz (engineered stone):**
- **Premium brands:** Caesarstone, Silestone, Cambria
- **Pros:** non-porous (no sealing), consistent color, highly stain-resistant, durable
- **Cons:** less heat-resistant than natural stone (no hot pans directly on it), looks engineered (can be a pro or a con)
- **Lifespan:** 50+ years with care
- **Cost:** $80–$150 per square foot installed

**Granite:**
- **Natural stone** — each slab unique
- **Pros:** heat-resistant, hard, timeless, variety of colors
- **Cons:** requires sealing (every 1–3 years), porous, can stain
- **Lifespan:** 50–100+ years
- **Cost:** $60–$150 per square foot installed

**Marble:**
- **Natural stone** — classic luxury
- **Pros:** beautiful, timeless, cool for pastry/baking
- **Cons:** soft, stains easily, etches from acids (lemon juice, wine) — develops patina that some love and some hate
- **Lifespan:** 100+ years (with patina tolerance)
- **Cost:** $75–$200+ per square foot
- **Recommended for:** a baking area or specific accent, not necessarily the whole kitchen

**Butcher block (island or specific counter zones):**
- **Solid hardwood** (maple, walnut, cherry) in thick slabs (1.5 to 2.5 inch)
- **Oil finish** — food-safe, renewable
- **Pros:** warm, beautiful, knife-friendly, ages gracefully
- **Cons:** requires regular oiling, not heat-resistant, not water-resistant at the edges
- **Best as:** island top or dedicated prep zone, paired with stone elsewhere
- **Lifespan:** 50+ years with maintenance

**Recommended kitchen plan for this design:**
- **Quartz or granite on the perimeter counters** (durable, low-maintenance, handles water and spills)
- **Butcher block on the island** (warm, functional, visual break)
- **Full-height backsplash in tile or stone** from counter to upper cabinets

**Sink:**
- **Under-mount stainless steel** — large single bowl (30–36 inch wide)
- **Minimum 16-gauge steel** (lower gauge number = thicker = better)
- **Heavy-duty strainer basket, insulated sides** (reduces noise)
- **Pull-down faucet** with spray function — brass body, ceramic cartridge
- **Optional: prep sink** (smaller, secondary) in the island

**Appliances (coordinate with Section 7 for circuits):**
- **Refrigerator:** counter-depth, 36-42 inch wide, French door or side-by-side
- **Range or cooktop + wall oven:** induction cooktop preferred (see Section 8), 30–36 inch wall oven (or double-oven)
- **Dishwasher:** quiet (≤44 dBA), Bosch, Miele, Asko — one or two units
- **Microwave:** drawer-style under the counter, or built-in over the range
- **Optional:** wine refrigerator, beverage drawer, pot filler over the range

**Key code references:**
- ORSC R303 (ventilation)

## 9.8 Bathrooms

**Plain language:** The bathrooms are spa-grade — thoughtful lighting, quality fixtures, good ventilation, and finishes that hold up to water for decades. Every bathroom has a proper tiled shower with glass enclosure, good counter space, and enough storage. The primary bathroom adds a separate tub, double vanity, and room to move.

**Technical detail:** Bathroom specifications:

**Primary bathroom (Floor 2):**

**Layout elements:**
- **Double vanity** — two sinks, 60 inch wide minimum, quartz or granite top
- **Separate water closet** (toilet room) with its own door — privacy
- **Walk-in shower** (60+ inch wide, 36+ inch deep) with glass enclosure
- **Freestanding or built-in soaking tub** — 60–72 inch
- **Linen storage** integrated into the vanity or in a separate tall cabinet
- **Heated towel warmer** (hydronic, fed from the radiant system — see Section 8) — luxury touch, low cost

**Secondary full bathroom (Floor 1 or Floor 2):**

**Layout elements:**
- **Single vanity** — 36–48 inch
- **Toilet in the main space** (not separate)
- **Shower-tub combination** OR **walk-in shower** with glass enclosure
- **Medicine cabinet** over the vanity

**Powder room (half-bath, Floor 1):**

**Layout elements:**
- **Pedestal sink or small vanity**
- **Toilet**
- **Small footprint** — 4 x 5 feet or 5 x 6 feet typical
- **Strong statement tile or wallpaper** — small room, budget for drama is small

**Shower specifications:**
- **Curbless (zero-entry) showers** — no step to trip over, fully accessible, modern
- **Linear drain at the back wall or along one side** for clean visuals and good drainage
- **Tile walls to the ceiling** in the shower — fully tiled, no drywall in the wet zone
- **Waterproofing:** Schluter Kerdi or equivalent membrane over the entire shower floor and walls, behind the tile
- **Shower pan:** sloped toward the drain at 1/4 inch per foot minimum
- **Glass enclosure:** 3/8 inch tempered safety glass, frameless or minimum-frame
- **Fixtures:** thermostatic mixing valve (prevents scalding when another fixture is used), rain shower head, handheld shower head on a slide bar, optional body sprays
- **Bench seat** built into the shower — tile-clad concrete or stone — luxury and accessibility feature
- **Niche for shampoo/soap storage** — tiled recess in the wall

**Toilet:**
- **Comfort-height (17–19 inch seat height)** — more accessible than standard 14–15 inch
- **Dual-flush (0.8 / 1.6 GPF)** — water efficient
- **Quiet fill valve**
- **Skirted trapway** — easier to clean
- **Separate tank-and-bowl or one-piece** — one-piece is cleaner looking, two-piece is less expensive
- **Brands:** Toto, Kohler, American Standard all make excellent options

**Vanity:**
- **Countertop:** quartz, granite, or marble (marble is fine here — bathroom use is gentler than kitchen)
- **Cabinet:** same construction quality as kitchen cabinets — plywood box, solid wood face
- **Under-mount sinks** — easier to clean (no rim trapping water)
- **Quality faucets** — solid brass body, ceramic cartridge, quality finish — 25+ year lifespan

**Lighting:**
- **Vanity lighting:** sconces on either side of the mirror (best for face lighting — no shadows) OR a bar light above the mirror
- **Recessed lighting:** general illumination throughout
- **Shower recessed light** — IC-rated, wet-location rated, in the shower ceiling
- **Dimmer on all circuits** — brightness matches time of day and task
- **Color temperature: 3000K–3500K** — flattering for grooming, accurate for makeup

**Ventilation (coordinated with Section 8):**
- **Humidity-sensing exhaust fan** tied into the ERV system, or separate humidity-controlled fan for bathroom
- **Timer switch** as an alternative — run fan for 20 minutes after shower
- **Minimum 50 CFM**; 80–100 CFM for bathrooms with separate tub (more steam volume)

**Wall and floor finishes:**
- **Tile floor** (large format porcelain, over radiant heat)
- **Wet wall tile** — full-height in the shower, wainscot height (48 inch) in other wet zones, optionally full-height throughout for luxury
- **Paint on drywall above tile** — moisture-resistant paint on moisture-resistant drywall
- **Stone or tile baseboard** (not wood) — better moisture resistance

**Key code references:**
- ORSC R303 (ventilation — bathroom minimums)
- OPSC 411 (plumbing fixture clearances)
- OPSC 407 (water-conserving fixtures)
- Oregon Energy Efficiency Specialty Code (shower flow rate limits, etc.)

## 9.10 Interior Lighting Design (Coordinated with Section 7)

**Plain language:** The lighting design has three layers: ambient (general room lighting), task (focused light where you work), and accent (decorative or highlight). Every room has all three. The controls are all dimmable so that bright morning light and soft evening light are both available.

**Technical detail:** Three-layer lighting in each space (see Section 7.11 for wiring and control detail):

**Ambient layer — general illumination:**
- **Recessed cans** in drywall ceilings
- **Surface-mounted fixtures or pendants** in exposed-concrete ceilings
- **Flush-mount ceiling fixtures** in bedrooms, hallways, closets
- **Linear LED** integrated into coves or architectural features (optional, accent)

**Task layer — focused work light:**
- **Under-cabinet lighting** in kitchens (LED strips)
- **Desk lamps** in offices (plug-in, on dedicated circuits)
- **Bedside lamps** in bedrooms (plug-in)
- **Reading lights** at seating areas (plug-in or wall sconces)
- **Vanity lights** in bathrooms (see 9.8)
- **Workshop task lights** in the basement

**Accent layer — decorative and highlight:**
- **Wall sconces** in hallways, stairways, powder rooms
- **Picture lights** over art (low-voltage, hardwired)
- **Cabinet interior lighting** — touch-on when doors open, for display cabinets
- **Toe-kick LEDs** under cabinets in kitchens and primary bathrooms (night-walking lighting)
- **Cove lighting** in formal rooms or primary bedroom (indirect, ambiance)
- **Step lights** on stair stringers (low-voltage, wall-mounted)

**Control strategies:**
- **Dimmer on every lighting circuit** (see Section 7)
- **Three-way switches at every room entry**
- **Occupancy sensors** in closets, pantry, laundry (auto-off when empty)
- **Scene controls** in primary rooms — "bright morning," "evening," "movie," "night" — via smart switches or keypad controllers
- **Path lighting** triggered by occupancy sensors for night-time hallway and bathroom routes

**Color temperature by room (see Section 7.11):**
- **2700–3000K** in living, bedroom, dining — warm, relaxing
- **3500–4000K** in kitchen work zones, bathrooms — cleaner, more accurate
- **5000K** in shop, garage, service layer — maximum visibility

**Outdoor / landscape lighting:**
- **Wall-mounted downlights** at every exterior door — motion-activated, photocell-off in daylight
- **Path lighting** to driveway and around the house — low-voltage landscape system
- **Accent uplights** on architectural features (the concrete block walls themselves can be beautifully lit from below)
- **Holiday outlet circuits** (see Section 7) — switched from inside, at the eave line at every corner

**Key code references:**
- NEC 210.70 (required lighting outlets)
- ORSC R303 (ventilation and lighting minimums)
- Oregon Energy Efficiency Code (≥90% high-efficacy lamps required)

**Plain language:** The whole house has a coordinated color and material palette — not matchy-matchy, but coherent. ## 9.12 Accessibility and Aging-in-Place

**Plain language:** Even if the owner is young and healthy now, designing for accessibility means the house stays comfortable as the owner ages, works for visitors of all abilities, and raises the resale value. Many features are free if planned from the start (wider doors, lever handles, zero-entry showers) and hugely expensive to retrofit later.

**Technical detail:** Aging-in-place features:

**Free or nearly-free accessibility features built in from day one:**

- **32–36 inch interior doors** — wheelchair-accessible (standard 30 inch is not)
- **Lever handles on all doors** (see 9.5) — easier than knobs
- **Curbless (zero-entry) showers** (see 9.8) — no trip hazard, wheelchair-accessible
- **Comfort-height toilets** (17–19 inch seat) — easier for anyone with knee issues
- **Good lighting everywhere**, especially hallways, stairs, entries — critical for aging eyes
- **Blocking in bathroom walls for future grab bars** — 2x8 or 2x10 solid blocking installed behind the tile at the toilet, shower, and tub locations; invisible until needed, then grab bars bolt into solid material
- **Walk-in primary bedroom closet at ground floor level (Floor 1)** — if space allows, locate the primary suite on Floor 1 to enable single-floor living as mobility changes
- **Bench seats in showers** (see 9.8)
- **Hand shower on slide bar** (see 9.8) — useful for anyone seated
- **Thermostatic mixing valves on all showers** — prevent scalding
- **Rocker light switches instead of toggles** — easier for arthritic hands
- **Electric or automatic window shades** in hard-to-reach windows
- **Outlets at 18 inches AFF instead of 12** — easier to reach without bending (still compliant with NEC)
- **Outdoor motion-activated lighting** — safe arrival home after dark

**More involved features to consider:**

- **Curbless main entry** — one threshold, flush transition, slight slope — avoids a step at the front door
- **Elevator shaft provisioned** (stacked closets that align floor-to-floor, 5 x 5 feet minimum) even if elevator not installed — can be retrofitted later for a residential elevator or stair lift
- **Roll-in shower with integrated bench and dual shower heads** — fully accessible for any mobility
- **Wider hallways (42 inch minimum instead of 36)** — wheelchair turn radius
- **Single-floor living option** — primary bedroom, primary bath, kitchen, and at least one living area all on Floor 1

**Decision framework:**
- **Always do:** the free items (door width, lever handles, blocking, comfort toilets)
- **Strongly consider:** single-floor living layout, elevator provisions, curbless entry
- **On request:** roll-in showers, wider halls, specific mobility accommodations

**Key code references:**
- ADA and ANSI A117.1 are not required for private single-family residences but provide the best guidance
- Fair Housing Act (applies to multi-family; informs best practices for single-family)
- Oregon Residential Specialty Code has limited accessibility requirements for single-family

## 9.13 Exterior Doors

**Plain language:** The exterior doors are the hardest-working parts of the building envelope. They have to seal tight against weather, resist break-ins, look good, and last decades. This design uses solid wood or steel-and-glass doors with multi-point locks, quality weather seals, and tall sidelights or transoms to let light into the entry.

**Technical detail:** Exterior door specifications:

**Front entry door:**
- **Solid wood** (3 inch thick, premium hardwood — mahogany, oak, cherry, walnut) OR **steel-clad insulated** OR **fiberglass-clad** — all three are legitimate; wood is warmest, steel is strongest, fiberglass is most dimensionally stable
- **Multi-point lock** (3-point with keyed deadbolt, handle latch, and rod locks into top and bottom of the frame) — far more secure than standard single-bolt
- **Weather seals** — continuous gasket around three sides, threshold seal at the bottom
- **Thermally-broken threshold** — prevents cold transfer through the metal threshold
- **Sidelights or transom** — for natural light in the entry
- **Storm door (optional)** — secondary weather barrier, summer screen — adds complexity and another hinge point, optional

**Back/side exterior doors:**
- **Insulated steel or fiberglass** — same sealing and security standards
- **Standard-bolt deadbolt + handle latch** — same strike plate reinforcement as the front door

**Garage-to-house door (if garage added later):**
- **20-minute fire-rated, self-closing, solid-core** per ORSC R302.5.1
- **Standard weather stripping** (this door is between conditioned spaces in a present-garage scenario — sealing matters less, fire rating matters more)

**Door frames:**
- **Steel or aluminum frames set into the masonry wall** — better than wood frames for durability and security
- **Thermal break** between interior and exterior frame portions
- **Masonry opening sized precisely** — no shimming with wood, no gaps filled with non-structural foam
- **Frame anchored to the CMU with masonry screws or direct bolts**

**Threshold and sill details:**
- **Sloped sill** — water runs outward
- **Drip edge** under the threshold — water doesn't wick back toward the door
- **Sill extends beyond the wall plane** — shadow line, water shedding
- **Concrete stoop or porch** on the exterior side — stable, maintenance-free

**Weather sealing:**
- **Kerf-in weatherstripping** (continuous gasket inset into the frame) on three sides
- **Adjustable threshold sweep** at the bottom
- **Compression-style seals** at the hinge side (slightly compressed when door is closed)
- **Annual inspection and adjustment** — part of the maintenance schedule

**Hardware:**
- **Heavy-duty ball-bearing hinges** (minimum 4 per door, 3.5 x 3.5 inch)
- **Solid brass or stainless steel** — long-life
- **Security strike plates** — 3-inch screws into the masonry, reinforced
- **Door viewer (peephole)** — at appropriate height for primary residents
- **Smart lock (optional)** — Level, August, or similar — battery-powered, unlock by phone or code, logs entry, interfaces with home automation

**Key code references:**
- ORSC R311 (means of egress — exterior doors)
- ORSC R302.5.1 (garage-to-house door)
- ORSC N1102.4 (air leakage — exterior door weatherstripping required)

## 9.14 Furniture and Move-In Readiness

**Plain language:** The house is designed to work with a normal household's furniture, not to require special custom pieces. Standard bed sizes fit in the bedrooms. Standard couches fit in the living areas. But the house also has enough built-in storage and considered electrical placement that the free-standing furniture can be simple and not have to compensate for a badly-laid-out house.

**Technical detail:** Design accommodations for furniture:

**Bedroom planning:**
- **Primary bedroom:** accommodates a king bed (76 x 80 inch) with nightstands, a dresser, and optional seating area (two chairs + small table)
- **Secondary bedrooms:** accommodate a queen bed (60 x 80 inch) with nightstands, a dresser, and a desk OR a twin/full bed with more furniture options
- **Bed headboard wall:** outlets on the headboard wall at 36 inch AFF (for nightstand lamps, phone charging) — slightly higher than standard so they're accessible above the mattress
- **Ceiling fan and light in the center of the room** — hardwired, switched from the door
- **Window placement** — avoid putting windows directly over the expected bed headboard location (outdoor noise, drafts)

**Living area planning:**
- **Great room / living room:** accommodates a large sofa (84–96 inch), a sectional, or two smaller sofas facing each other; coffee table; two accent chairs; entertainment wall
- **Outlets on every wall** — avoids extension cords
- **Cable/ethernet/coax** at the planned entertainment wall (see Section 7.16)
- **Ceiling speaker wiring** provisioned for optional surround sound
- **Dimmable lighting** for movie-watching mode

**Dining area:**
- **Accommodates a table seating 6–8**, plus hutch or buffet against one wall
- **Pendant light or chandelier** centered over the table — height adjustable during installation (31–36 inches above table surface)
- **Outlets at buffet height** (32 inch AFF) on the buffet wall — for warming plates, coffee, etc.

**Office:**
- **Desk plus secondary surface** (credenza or bookshelf)
- **Outlets and data at desk locations** — at minimum two outlets and two data jacks, placed where they land behind a typical desk
- **Bookshelf walls** (optional built-ins, see 9.6)

**Move-in essentials:**
- **Every room has working lighting** — at least one ceiling fixture
- **Every room has enough outlets** — no extension cords or power strips needed on day one
- **Appliances installed and working** before move-in
- **Window coverings installed** before move-in (privacy, light control from night one)

**The "house should work" test:**
- **Every lamp plugs into an outlet that's accessible without moving furniture**
- **Every TV location has cable/data/power nearby**
- **Every bed location has outlets for nightstand lamps and phone charging**
- **Every frequently-used light is controlled from every entry to the room**
- **The trash goes from every room to the garbage area without crossing living space unnecessarily**

**Key code references:**
- NEC 210.52 (receptacle placement — see Section 7)
- OMSC ventilation requirements per room
- Furniture placement is not code-regulated — it's design quality
