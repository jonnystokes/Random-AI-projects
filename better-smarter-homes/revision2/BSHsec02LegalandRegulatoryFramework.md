# Section 2 — Legal and Regulatory Framework

*What you can and can't legally do as an owner-builder*

Oregon, like most states, regulates who can build and sell homes. The rules exist to protect buyers from shoddy work and to ensure public safety. The good news is that Oregon has a specific exemption for people building their own home on their own land — the "owner-builder exemption" — which allows a person to build without a contractor license as long as a few clear rules are followed.

This section explains those rules, compares the owner-builder path to the licensed contractor path, covers what it would take to do the plumbing and electrical work legally, and clarifies how the Better Smarter Homes LLC brand can appear in marketing without creating a legal problem.

This section uses Oregon-specific rules as the example. Other states have different rules. Anyone reading this in a different state should verify equivalent rules with their state's construction licensing board.

---

## 2.1 The Owner-Builder Exemption

**Plain language:** In Oregon, a person can legally build their own home on their own land without being a licensed contractor. This is the cheapest, most flexible path for this project, and it matches the "I want to build it myself" instinct that drives this whole design.

**Technical detail:** Oregon's Construction Contractors Board (CCB) requires anyone building homes for compensation to hold a contractor license. The owner-builder exemption (under ORS 701.010 and ORS 455.320) creates a specific carve-out: a person who owns the property and intends to occupy the home they build does not need a contractor license.

**Key requirements for the exemption to apply:**
- The builder must own the property (in their own name, not through an LLC or corporation)
- The builder must intend to occupy the home as their primary residence
- The builder must pull all permits personally, as a homeowner
- The home must genuinely be used as the builder's primary residence after completion
- Only one home may be built per year under this exemption

**Why the LLC can't own the property:** Oregon case law (Tandem Properties LLC v. CCB, 2002) specifically found that an LLC building to sell is acting as a contractor, not as a homeowner, and therefore needs a CCB license. To stay in the exemption, the land and home must be in the individual person's name. The LLC can own everything else (brand, designs, content) but not the real estate.

**Key code references:**
- ORS 701.010 (contractor licensing — exemptions from licensure)
- ORS 455.320 (owner-built dwellings — exempt from certain structural code provisions)
- Tandem Properties LLC v. CCB, 184 Or App 28 (2002) (case law — LLC-owned build-to-sell treated as contracting)

## 2.2 The 3-Year Residency Rule

**Plain language:** After building the home, the builder must live in it for at least 3 years before selling. Selling sooner breaks the exemption and retroactively treats the build as unlicensed contracting.

**Technical detail:** The exemption requires genuine residential occupancy, not a sham. Selling within 3 years is presumptive evidence (legal term meaning the state assumes it to be true unless proven otherwise) of intent to sell, which converts the build into unlicensed contracting.

**What counts as proof of occupancy during the 3 years:**
- Driver's license listing the address
- Voter registration at the address
- Utility accounts (electric, internet, water) in the builder's name at the address
- Tax returns listing the address as primary residence
- Homestead property tax exemption filing
- Mail delivery and bank statements at the address

**Building and selling pace:**
- One home every 3 years is the realistic maximum under this exemption
- The 3-year clock starts at Certificate of Occupancy or when the owner moves in, whichever is later (see Section 2.3)
- Most of the 3 years can be used productively — finishing interior work, refining the design, saving for the next build, growing the brand and YouTube audience

**Why this rule exists:** The rule prevents people from abusing the owner-builder exemption to operate as unlicensed developers. The state wants genuine homeowner projects, not commercial builders hiding behind the exemption.

**Key code references:**
- ORS 701.010 (contractor licensing — prima facie evidence of intent to sell if not occupied)

## 2.3 What Counts as "Lived In" (Certificate of Occupancy)

**Plain language:** The home is legally habitable once the building official issues a Certificate of Occupancy (CO). That's when the 3-year residency clock can start. The CO doesn't require the home to be finished — only the life-safety parts need to be done.

**Technical detail:** Under Oregon Administrative Rule (OAR) 918-480-0140, no residential building can be occupied until the building official issues either a Certificate of Occupancy or a Temporary Certificate of Occupancy.

**Minimum requirements for a CO or Temporary CO:**
- Building, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical final inspections all passed
- At least one fully functional bathroom with shower or tub
- Working kitchen sink
- Heating system installed and working
- Water heater installed and working
- Final electrical sign-off
- Smoke alarms and carbon monoxide detectors installed and working
- Site development (driveway, grading) complete per permit conditions

**What does NOT have to be done for the CO:**
- Interior finish work (paint, trim, interior doors except fire-rated)
- Flooring (carpet, tile, other finish floor)
- Cabinetry beyond the minimum kitchen sink
- Landscape or exterior aesthetics
- Most of the fancy finishes and details

**Strategic implication:** The builder can reach CO and begin occupancy long before the home is "finished." Painting, trim work, flooring, detailed kitchen buildout, stair railings, exterior aesthetics, landscaping, and all the polish work can happen after move-in. This means 6-12 months or more of the 3-year residency can be used for ongoing finish work and improvements, while the residency clock is already ticking.

**Temporary CO (TCO):** If the building is substantially complete but a few items remain (often landscape, minor mechanical items), a TCO can be issued. TCOs are typically valid for 30 days and can be extended in 30-day increments with additional fees. TCOs count as occupancy for residency purposes.

**Key code references:**
- OAR 918-480-0140 (certificates of occupancy — residential)
- ORSC R110 (certificate of occupancy — issuance conditions)
- ORS 455.055 (uniform permit, inspection and certificate of occupancy requirements)

## 2.4 Owner-Builder vs. Licensed Contractor Paths

**Plain language:** Two paths exist for legally building and selling homes. The owner-builder path is cheaper and allows the builder to do all the work themselves, but it's slow (3-year cycles). The licensed contractor path is faster and has no residency requirement, but requires buying licenses, bonds, and insurance, and requires hiring licensed plumbers and electricians for those trades. This project is built around the owner-builder path.

**Technical detail:** Side-by-side comparison:

**Owner-Builder Path:**
- No contractor license required
- Land and home owned personally
- All trades (including plumbing and electrical) can be done by the owner under the homeowner exemption
- Must live in the home for 3+ years before selling
- One home every 3+ years maximum
- Upfront cost: permit fees only (typically $3,000-$8,000 for a new build)
- Annual cost during residency: homeowner's insurance, property tax, utilities (normal homeowner expenses)
- Legal scope: build, live in, sell as a private homeowner

**Licensed Contractor Path (CCB — Construction Contractors Board):**
- Residential General Contractor (RGC) endorsement required
- 16-hour pre-licensing course ($200-$400)
- Oregon CCB exam ($60)
- $20,000 surety bond (annual premium roughly $200-$400)
- General liability insurance, minimum $500,000 ($600-$1,500/year)
- License application fee: $400 every 2 years
- LLC or business entity registration with Oregon Secretary of State
- Plumbing and electrical work must be done by licensed plumbers and electricians (cannot be done by the contractor themselves unless they also hold those trade licenses)
- No residency requirement — can build, sell, build, sell at any pace
- Upfront cost: ~$700-$1,500 for licensing and initial bond
- Annual cost: $1,500-$2,500 for bond, insurance, renewals
- Legal scope: unlimited home construction for sale, subject to implied warranty of habitability and other buyer-protection rules

**Why this project uses the owner-builder path:**
- Zero licensing cost
- Maximum DIY scope (all trades, including plumbing and electrical)
- Fits the "test everything, build slowly, learn each home" design philosophy
- No pressure to meet an external build schedule
- The 3-year residency is productive time for finish work and design refinement
- Matches the lead author's situation of building on weekends while working full-time

**Key code references:**
- ORS 701.010 (contractor licensing — registration required for construction activity)
- ORS 701.068 (surety bond — residential contractor bond requirements)
- ORS 701.069 (insurance — residential contractor insurance requirements)

## 2.5 Cost and Time to Become a Licensed Plumber or Electrician

**Plain language:** Some people imagine becoming their own licensed plumber or electrician to skip the cost of hiring one. Oregon's rules make this essentially impossible in a reasonable timeframe. Each trade requires a 4-year paid apprenticeship. Doing both would take 4-8 years of full-time work in the trades before ever building the home. It is not a practical shortcut.

**Technical detail:** Oregon's Building Codes Division (BCD) licenses individual plumbers and electricians. Each license requires a combination of classroom education and on-the-job training under a licensed contractor.

**Journeyman Plumber requirements:**
- 576 hours of classroom instruction (typically evening classes over 4 years)
- 7,700 hours of on-the-job experience (roughly 4 years of full-time work)
- Must work under a licensed plumbing contractor throughout the apprenticeship
- Pass the Oregon Journeyman Plumber exam
- $100 license fee
- Total realistic cost: $0 to $5,000 in class fees (the apprentice typically gets paid $18-$32/hour during training, offsetting costs)
- Total realistic time: 4 years, full-time

**Journeyman Electrician requirements:**
- 576 hours of classroom instruction
- 8,000 hours of on-the-job experience (roughly 4 years of full-time work)
- Must work under a licensed electrical contractor throughout the apprenticeship
- Pass the Oregon Journeyman Electrician exam
- License fees
- Total realistic cost: $0 to $5,000 in class fees (paid during training)
- Total realistic time: 4 years, full-time

**Why this isn't a shortcut:**
- Cannot realistically do both apprenticeships simultaneously — each is essentially a full-time job with required classroom hours
- Sequential training would take 8 years before the first shovel of dirt
- The apprenticeship must be done working for an existing licensed contractor, not on one's own projects
- The lead author's situation (working a full-time job in a different field while saving to build) is incompatible with a 4-year trade apprenticeship

**Implication:** The owner-builder exemption is the only practical path that allows the builder to legally do their own plumbing and electrical work without a multi-year career detour into the trades.

**Key code references:**
- OAR Chapter 918, Division 695 (plumbing licensing — journeyman plumber requirements)
- OAR Chapter 918, Division 282 (electrical licensing — journeyman electrician requirements)
- ORS 670.380 (trade licensure — classroom and experience requirements)

## 2.6 Homeowner Doing Own Plumbing and Electrical (Legal Under Exemption)

**Plain language:** Under the owner-builder exemption, the homeowner can legally do their own plumbing and electrical work on their own home. This is the key advantage of the owner-builder path. All the work still has to be inspected by the building department, but the work itself can be done by the owner without any trade license.

**Technical detail:** Oregon law (OAR 918-695-0100 for plumbing, and parallel rules for electrical) specifically allows a person to do their own work on their own residential building on their own premises, without any plumbing or electrical license, provided:
- The building is a single-family residence
- The person owns the property and the building
- The person will occupy the building
- The building is not intended for sale, lease, or rent at the time the work is performed
- All work complies with the applicable specialty codes (Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code, Oregon Electrical Specialty Code)
- All required permits are pulled by the homeowner
- All required inspections are scheduled and passed

**What the homeowner can legally do:**
- Full rough-in plumbing (drain, waste, vent, water supply)
- Full finish plumbing (fixture installation, connections)
- Full electrical service entrance from the meter base onward
- Main panel installation
- All branch circuit wiring
- All device installations (outlets, switches, fixtures)
- Grounding and bonding
- Low-voltage wiring (Ethernet, audio/video, security)

**What the homeowner cannot legally do under this exemption:**
- Work on anyone else's property
- Work on rental property (even if they own it)
- Work on property they intend to sell immediately
- Hire themselves out to others for plumbing or electrical work

**Required inspections are the same:** Whether work is done by the homeowner or by a licensed plumber/electrician, the same inspections apply. The building department's inspector verifies the work meets code regardless of who did it.

**Practical tip:** The homeowner should over-study the applicable code sections before doing the work. Code books are available through the Oregon Building Codes Division. Free code reading and online tutorials are plentiful. A first-time owner-builder doing their own plumbing and electrical will likely spend 50-100 hours studying codes before starting the work. Time well invested.

**Key code references:**
- OAR 918-695-0100 (plumbing licensing — homeowner exemption)
- OAR 918-282-0230 (electrical licensing — homeowner exemption)
- Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code (OPSC) — entire code applies to work performed
- Oregon Electrical Specialty Code (OESC) — entire code applies to work performed

## 2.7 When to Hire a Licensed Plumber or Electrician (Optional Under Exemption)

**Plain language:** Under the owner-builder path, hiring licensed trades is optional. The owner can do all of it themselves. Some people choose to hire help for the most technical parts, or for the parts they are least comfortable with. This section describes what a hybrid arrangement looks like if the owner wants to do that.

**Technical detail:** Even on a homeowner-exempt project, a licensed plumber or electrician can be hired for specific tasks. They pull their own permits for the work they do, or the homeowner can pull a homeowner permit and hire the licensed tradesperson as labor.

**Common hybrid arrangements:**

**Design consultation only:** A licensed tradesperson visits the site before work begins, reviews the layout, flags any code issues, and leaves. Homeowner does all the work. Cost: $150-$400 for a 1-2 hour visit.

**Verification walk before inspection:** The tradesperson visits after the homeowner's work is complete but before the building inspector arrives, to catch any issues the homeowner missed. Cost: $100-$200 for a 1-hour visit.

**Partnered execution:** The homeowner does all the layout, drilling, wire pulling, pipe staging, and mounting. The tradesperson arrives for a single work session to make the regulated joints (soldered or glued pipe connections, crimped electrical terminations, panel wiring). Cost: $600-$1,400 for a full day of skilled labor.

**Full hire:** The homeowner decides they don't want to do that trade at all. Hires a licensed tradesperson to do the whole job. Cost: $8,000-$15,000 for plumbing on a typical home, $8,000-$15,000 for electrical.

**Finding tradespeople willing to work on owner-builder projects:**
- Small family-owned shops and solo operators are more flexible than franchises
- Semi-retired licensed tradespeople often welcome occasional paid work
- Local Facebook groups and Craigslist searches for "owner-builder seeking licensed plumber/electrician for partnership"
- Expect to call 5-10 before finding a good fit

**This is optional.** The owner-builder exemption means none of this is required. A homeowner who wants to do 100% of the work themselves can do so legally.

## 2.8 Permit Sequence Overview

**Plain language:** Building a home requires a series of permits, each covering a different part of the work. The permits set the rules the work must follow, and the inspections that go with each permit verify the work was done correctly. Section 3 covers the inspections in detail.

**Technical detail:** Main permits required for a new residential build:

**Land use and zoning approvals** (pre-construction)
- Zoning verification or conditional use permit if required
- Land use compatibility statement
- Septic construction permit (if on-site septic system)
- Well permit (if new well)
- Driveway access permit (if new access to a public road)

**Building permit (structural)**
- Main permit covering the structure: foundation, walls, floors, roof
- Includes framing, masonry, steel, and concrete work
- Pulled by the owner under the owner-builder exemption
- Typical cost for a new home: $3,000-$8,000 depending on size and jurisdiction

**Trade permits (under the building permit)**
- Plumbing permit — covers drain, waste, vent, water supply
- Electrical permit — covers service, panel, branch circuits, devices
- Mechanical permit — covers heating, cooling, ventilation
- Each trade permit has its own inspections, scheduled separately from the building permit inspections

**Other potential permits**
- Grading permit (if significant earthwork)
- Erosion control permit (required in some jurisdictions during construction)
- Fire department approval (for sprinklers if required)
- Radon mitigation installation (in some counties)

**How to pull permits as a homeowner:**
- Most counties have an online permit portal (Oregon ePermitting, county-specific systems)
- In-person at the county building department also works
- The permit application includes plans, specifications, and a declaration that the applicant is the owner building for their own occupancy
- The homeowner signature on the permit triggers the owner-builder exemption language

**Permit timing:**
- Apply for permits after plans are complete and before breaking ground
- Approval typically takes 2-8 weeks depending on jurisdiction and plan complexity
- Permits are valid for a fixed period (often 180 days from approval) and must show progress (inspections passed) to remain valid
- Permits can be extended if needed

**Cost planning:** Total permit fees for the full build (building + plumbing + electrical + mechanical + septic + other) typically run $4,000-$12,000 depending on square footage, jurisdiction, and specific fees. This is a significant line item but small compared to materials.

**Key code references:**
- ORSC R105 (permits — when required)
- ORSC R106 (construction documents — what plans must include)
- ORS 455.055 (uniform permit requirements)

## 2.9 Better Smarter Homes LLC — Brand vs. Ownership Separation

**Plain language:** The builder is building a home for themselves on their own land. Better Smarter Homes LLC is a separate company owned by the builder that handles the brand, designs, and content. The LLC does not own the land, does not own the home, does not sell the home, and does not receive any money from the sale. The LLC exists to make the whole operation look professional while staying completely separate from the legal ownership of the real estate.

**Technical detail:** The LLC and the owner-builder are kept cleanly separate to preserve the owner-builder exemption.

**What the person (not the LLC) owns and does:**
- Owns the land, in their own name jointly with their spouse or individually
- Owns the home, in their own name
- Pulls all permits as the homeowner
- Performs all construction work personally or with family/friends
- Holds the homeowner's insurance policy
- Lives in the home for the 3-year residency
- Signs the eventual sale contract as the seller
- Receives all proceeds from the sale

**What Better Smarter Homes LLC owns and does:**
- Owns the brand, logo, trademarks
- Owns all designs, blueprints, technical specifications, test data, and this document
- Owns the YouTube channel, website, and all content
- Publishes videos and blog posts about the design methodology
- Licenses designs to other owner-builders (as a potential future revenue stream)
- Provides consulting and design services (if pursued as a future business line)
- Never owns real estate
- Never signs construction contracts with buyers
- Never receives payment for construction services
- Never markets itself as "building homes for sale"

**How the brand appears in marketing:**
- The home is marketed as "Designed by Better Smarter Homes LLC — Built by [owner's name], owner-builder"
- The listing (when the home is eventually sold) is a personal sale by the homeowner, with BSH-branded marketing materials (photos, videos, brochure) produced by the LLC
- A bronze plaque or similar permanent marker on the home can identify it as a Better Smarter Homes design without triggering any legal issue
- The LLC's website, YouTube channel, and social media can feature the home as a case study of the BSH methodology

**Why this separation matters:**
- Preserves the owner-builder exemption (the LLC doesn't own or sell the home)
- Avoids triggering Tandem Properties case law (LLC doesn't act as a developer)
- Protects the builder from having the CCB treat them as an unlicensed contractor
- Still lets the brand build reputation and future value

**What the LLC must never do:**
- Own the real estate
- Sign the sale contract
- Receive construction payments from the buyer
- Hire out construction services
- Market itself as a homebuilder for hire
- Accept deposits from future buyers in exchange for a home build

**Setting up the LLC:**
- File with Oregon Secretary of State ($100 filing fee)
- Annual LLC renewal: $100
- Business bank account separate from personal accounts
- Separate records for LLC income and expenses
- Optional: federal and state trademark registration for the brand name and logo ($350-$500 per class, 6-12 months)

**Key code references:**
- ORS 701.010 (contractor licensing — LLC-owned builds to sell are contracting)
- Tandem Properties LLC v. CCB, 184 Or App 28 (2002) (case law — LLC/business entity triggers licensing)
