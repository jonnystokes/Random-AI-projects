# Section 6 — Plumbing

*Water in, water out, hot water everywhere fast*

This section covers every pipe in the building: where water comes in, how it gets pressurized and distributed, how waste leaves, how vents work, and the specific design choices that make this plumbing system luxury-grade rather than standard residential.

The guiding philosophy: oversized trunk lines, per-fixture pressure control, group the wet areas together, and treat every component as accessible and replaceable via the service layer.

---

## 6.1 Wet-Zone Philosophy

**Plain language:** All the plumbing in the house is grouped together. Kitchen, laundry, and bathrooms all cluster in one corner or one side of the building. This keeps the pipes short and simple, reduces the chance of leaks and clogs, makes the hot water arrive fast, and makes every pipe easy to reach for repair.

**Technical detail:** Wet-zone design principles:

- Kitchen, laundry, and bathrooms grouped into a single vertical stack area
- All drain, waste, and vent (DWV) piping concentrated in one corner of the 3x3 grid
- Hot and cold water supply trunk rises through the same area
- Floors 1 and 2 each have at least one bathroom stacked directly above the one below
- Long pipe runs (kitchen sink far from the main stack, for example) are considered exceptions that add cost and failure points — advised against but possible if the floor plan demands it

**Why grouping matters:**
- Less pipe = less cost, less installation time, less to go wrong
- Shorter runs = less water waiting in the pipe, hot water arrives at the tap faster
- Concentrated stack = fewer roof or wall penetrations
- Single-area shutoff for maintenance
- All wet-related work clustered in one corner simplifies the service layer routing

**Breaking the grouping:** If a specific home design requires a bathroom on the opposite side of the house from the main stack, it can be done, but:
- Adds a long run of supply and drain lines
- Requires an additional vent
- Adds hot water delivery time to that fixture
- Increases pipe exposure to potential issues
- This is flagged as a conscious trade-off, not a default choice

## 6.2 Bathroom Count and Placement

**Plain language:** Two full bathrooms minimum — one per main floor, stacked directly above each other in the wet corner. A third bathroom in the basement is optional and probably won't be a standard wet bathroom (because no sewage exits the basement by design).

**Technical detail:** Bathroom count and features:

**Full bathrooms (code-qualified):**
- **Floor 1 bathroom:** Sink, toilet, shower AND tub (separate, not combined — premium feature)
- **Floor 2 bathroom:** Sink, toilet, shower AND tub (separate) — or one full bath plus a second smaller bath
- **Both stacked in the same corner** (wet zone), sharing the vertical drain stack and individual vents

**Total expected count:** 2 to 3 full bathrooms above the basement level

**Basement bathroom options:**
- **Dry toilet room** (composting toilet or incinerating toilet) with portable handwash station — see 6.3
- **Full wet bathroom** if and only if the site's topography allows gravity drainage out the side of the basement (sloped lot with a daylight exit for sewage)
- **No bathroom** if neither option fits the specific home and site

**Why separate shower and tub:**
- Premium feature that increases home value at resale
- Tub for relaxation, shower for daily use
- Neither has to compromise on size
- Requires more space and more plumbing but worth it for this design's philosophy

**Key code references:**
- ORSC R303 (light, ventilation, and heating — bathroom requirements)
- OPSC 412 (plumbing fixtures — minimum count per dwelling)

## 6.3 Basement Toilet Options

**Plain language:** Because the design avoids pipes under the concrete slab, getting sewage out of the basement requires either pumping it up or using a waterless toilet. Three options exist. None of them is a standard flush toilet connected to the septic system.

**Technical detail:** Options for basement toilet:

**Option A — Composting toilet:**
- Waste collects in a chamber beneath or behind the toilet
- Aerobic decomposition over weeks to months reduces volume and produces safe compost
- Requires a small exhaust fan for odor control
- Requires periodic servicing (emptying the compost chamber)
- Graywater from handwashing goes separately (see handwash options below)
- Code-approved in Oregon under DEQ rules with proper setup

**Option B — Incinerating toilet:**
- Waste is burned after each use by an electric heating element
- Produces a small amount of sterile ash (remove every 40-100 uses)
- Requires 1.5-2 kW of electrical power during burn cycles
- Requires a flue pipe exhausting outside
- No waste holding, no servicing beyond ash removal
- Slightly higher equipment cost than composting

**Option C — Full flush toilet with Venturi pump to upper-floor plumbing:**
- Standard toilet with a sealed holding tank and a Venturi pump
- Venturi pump uses a high-pressure fresh water stream from the upper floor plumbing to create suction, lifting the wastewater up to the main drain stack on floor 1
- No mechanical parts to jam, no impeller to clog
- Requires: fresh water supply line from above, suction line up to the main stack, basic plumbing knowledge
- This is the option that matches the "no mechanical pumps in the basement" design philosophy best, while still providing a standard toilet experience

**Handwash station options (needed for all three toilet options):**
- Portable handwash station with fresh water supply from upper floor
- Graywater drain pumped up to the main stack via Venturi
- UV light sterilization in the fresh water tank (optional, matches the design philosophy of on-site UV treatment at all tank points)

**Legality note:** Under Oregon DEQ rules, composting and incinerating toilets are permitted in residential buildings. The graywater from any handwash station must be disposed of through an approved system (septic, dry well with permit, or graywater irrigation). Dropping graywater into the ground in the basement is NOT permitted.

**Key code references:**
- OAR Chapter 340, Division 71 (onsite wastewater — composting toilet standards)
- OPSC Appendix K (alternate wastewater systems)
- OPSC 412 (plumbing fixtures — alternate fixture approval)

## 6.4 Water Source Options

**Plain language:** Three ways to get water into the house: city water (municipal connection), a well with a pump, or a combination. Whichever the property has, the water goes into a storage tank inside the house before being distributed. This gives control over pressure, volume, and quality.

**Technical detail:** Water source options:

**Municipal (city) water:**
- Main water line from the street or utility connection
- Typically provides 20-110 psi at the property
- Pressure regulator required at entry if above 80 psi (code requirement)
- Water quality typically meets drinking water standards
- Metered usage billed monthly by the utility

**Well water:**
- Well drilled on the property (depth varies 50-800+ ft depending on geology)
- Submersible pump at depth, or jet pump at surface
- Pressure tank at the house for buffering demand
- Water treatment typically required (iron, sulfur, hardness varies by location)
- Annual cost: power to run the pump, occasional filter and pump maintenance
- Requires well permit from Oregon Water Resources Department

**Combined (both):**
- Municipal for drinking water and bathrooms
- Well for irrigation, outdoor washing, and non-potable use
- Separate piping and backflow prevention between the two systems
- Most flexibility but most complex

**Storage tank (regardless of source):**
- Large tank in the utility area (500-2,500 gallons)
- Feeds a booster pump system that delivers pressure and volume beyond what the source can provide alone
- UV sterilization inside the tank maintains water quality
- Provides a reservoir during power outages or utility interruptions
- Allows the incoming service line to be smaller (filling slowly) while the distribution system uses a larger pipe

**Why the tank approach matches this design:**
- City water often delivers only 5-15 gallons per minute at code pressure; this design wants 200+ gpm peak flow for the hot tub fill and other luxury fixtures
- Tank buffers low-flow source into high-flow delivery
- Power outage resilience: tank holds many days of drinking water
- UV treatment at the tank protects against any source contamination
- Redundancy: if source fails, tank buffer covers the gap

**Key code references:**
- ORSC P2903 (water supply — source requirements)
- OAR 333-061 (drinking water quality standards)
- OPSC 608 (water service — materials and sizing)

## 6.5 Pump and Tank System for Boosted Pressure

**Plain language:** Inside the house, a tank holds water and a set of pumps boost the pressure and flow to whatever the house needs. Add more pumps in parallel for more gallons per minute. Add pumps in series (output of one feeds input of the next) for higher pressure. Scale it to whatever the design demands.

**Technical detail:** Pump system configuration:

**Primary storage tank:**
- 500-2,500 gallons, sized to provide 1-3 days of household use
- Non-pressurized (atmospheric pressure inside)
- Fitted with UV sterilizer for ongoing water quality
- Overflow and vent to outside
- Fill from city or well source with float switch control

**Booster pump system:**
- Variable-frequency drive (VFD) pump delivering controlled pressure to the distribution system
- Multiple smaller pumps in parallel — as demand rises, more pumps turn on automatically
- Pressure tank on the output side (20-120 gallon pressure tank) buffers short demand spikes without cycling pumps
- Target output pressure: 100 psi (dropped to 60-80 psi at fixtures via per-fixture pressure regulators)

**Why this matters:**
- Code-minimum residential water systems deliver 40-60 psi at 5-15 gpm
- This design targets 200+ gpm peak flow (hot tub fill in 2 minutes)
- Standard municipal connections can't deliver that peak
- The tank-and-booster approach decouples incoming supply rate from delivery rate
- Tank fills slowly over time; pumps deliver fast when needed

**Redundancy:**
- Minimum 2 pumps in parallel so one can fail without losing service
- Manual bypass valve allowing direct connection from source to distribution in emergencies
- Low-water cutoff on tank prevents pump damage if tank empties

**Key code references:**
- OPSC 612 (pumps — installation and protection)
- ORSC P2903.6 (water pressure — booster systems)

## 6.6 Main Trunk Design — 2 inch Throughout

**Plain language:** The main water pipes are 2 inches thick, running all the way from the tank to every room. At each fixture, a smaller pipe branches off and adjusts the pressure for that fixture. Thick main pipes mean fast water flow, no pressure drop, no hissing or water hammer noise, and lots of margin for the luxury fixtures.

**Technical detail:** Distribution pipe sizing:

**Main trunk:** 2 inch nominal (copper Type L or Pex-A)
- Runs from the booster pump output through the service layers of every floor
- Delivers to within a few feet of every fixture
- Splits into hot and cold trunks after the water heater

**Branch lines:** Step down as needed
- 1 inch for a cluster of fixtures in a single room
- 3/4 inch for a single medium-flow fixture (shower, sink)
- 1/2 inch for low-flow fixtures (lavatory sink)
- Per-fixture pressure regulators mounted just before each fixture

**Material options:**

**Copper Type L (recommended for main trunk):**
- Rated for high pressure (500+ psi) and high temperature (any residential hot water)
- 50+ year life expectancy
- Expensive ($15-$25 per foot for 2 inch)
- Heavy, requires careful support
- Solid choice for the permanent backbone

**PEX-A (recommended for branch lines):**
- Flexible tubing with expansion fittings
- Rated for 100 psi at 180°F
- Freeze-resistant (can expand without bursting in rare cases)
- Cheaper than copper, faster to install
- 25-50 year life expectancy
- Perfect for the service-layer branch runs

**Hybrid recommended approach:**
- 2 inch copper Type L main trunk through service layers
- PEX-A branches off the trunk to each fixture
- Transitions at a manifold (a central point where multiple branches start)

**Installation notes:**
- Hangers and supports every 4-10 feet depending on pipe material and size
- Insulate hot water lines to reduce heat loss and prevent condensation on cold water lines
- Color-code with adhesive labels: red for hot, blue for cold
- Label every shutoff valve with what it isolates

**Key code references:**
- OPSC 604 (water distribution — materials and sizing)
- OPSC Table 610.3 (water supply fixture units — sizing tables)

## 6.7 Per-Fixture Pressure Regulation

**Plain language:** Every fixture gets its own pressure adjustment valve, tuned to make that fixture feel perfect. Shower, bathtub, kitchen sink, bathroom sink, toilet, outdoor hose — each one dialed in to the right pressure. No more harsh spraying at the kitchen sink, no more weak showers, no more water hammer.

**Technical detail:** Per-fixture pressure regulator (PRV) strategy:

**Concept:** The main trunk runs at 100 psi. Each fixture branch has a small PRV just before the fixture, adjusting the pressure down to the ideal for that fixture.

**Target pressures by fixture type:**
- **Toilet fill:** 30-40 psi (slower, quieter fill)
- **Bathroom lavatory sink:** 35-45 psi (gentle flow)
- **Kitchen sink:** 40-50 psi (good working flow, not splashy)
- **Shower:** 45-60 psi (firm spray without harshness)
- **Bathtub fill:** 60-80 psi (fast fill)
- **Hot tub fill:** 80 psi open (maximum delivery rate)
- **Washing machine:** per appliance spec, typically 20-80 psi
- **Dishwasher:** per appliance spec, typically 20-120 psi
- **Outdoor hose bib (garden):** 60-80 psi (good watering pressure)
- **Outdoor hose bib (pressure washer feed):** wide open for pressure washer appliance to boost further

**PRV components:**
- Brass pressure-reducing valve (Watts N45B or similar): $25-$40 each
- Built-in adjustment screw with lock
- Install just upstream of the fixture's shutoff valve
- Accessible from the service layer for adjustment or replacement

**Upper bound:**
- Code requires fixture delivery pressure no higher than 80 psi
- Per-fixture PRVs satisfy this everywhere, even with the 100 psi main trunk

**Benefits:**
- Every fixture feels like it was custom-designed
- No splashing, no harshness, no wasted water
- Quiet operation (no water hammer because the main trunk pressure doesn't shock the fixtures)
- Lower wear on fixture internals (gentler pressure extends fixture life)
- Easy tuning after installation (turn the screw until it feels right)

**Key code references:**
- OPSC 608.2 (water pressure — maximum pressure at fixtures)
- ASSE 1003 (performance requirements for water pressure reducing valves)

## 6.8 Hot Water System

**Plain language:** One or more water heaters provide hot water to the house. Exact count and configuration is left open — could be one large tank, could be multiple tanks in parallel to increase peak flow capacity. What matters is the temperature rules and the delivery loop.

**Technical detail:** Hot water system considerations:

**Count and sizing:**
- A single large tank (80-120 gallon) serves a typical family
- Multiple tanks in parallel increase peak flow capacity for luxury demands (simultaneous showers plus dishwasher plus laundry, or hot tub fills)
- Specific configuration determined per home based on fixture count and peak usage

**Storage temperature:**
- Storage tank(s) held at 140°F minimum (kills Legionella bacteria — Legionella grows between 77°F and 113°F)
- Thermostatic mixing valve after the tank(s) blends cold water to produce 120°F output to the distribution system

**Distribution temperature:**
- Water delivered to fixtures is 120°F maximum (anti-scald code requirement)
- Dedicated outdoor equipment loops (non-potable, no human touch) can be higher if labeled and protected per code

**Hot water recirculation loop:**
- Hot water return line runs from the farthest fixture back to the water heater
- Small circulator pump (5-25 watts, Grundfos UP15 or Taco 003) continuously moves water through the loop at 1-2 gpm
- All loop piping insulated to minimize heat loss
- Check valve prevents reverse flow
- Result: hot water arrives at every fixture within 2-5 seconds of opening the tap
- Energy cost: roughly $30-$60 per year for the pump and slightly higher heating load from standby losses

**Anti-scald:**
- Thermostatic mixing valve at the water heater
- Point-of-use anti-scald valves at showers and bathtub fills (ASSE 1016 rated)

**Key code references:**
- OPSC 413 (water temperature — anti-scald requirements)
- OPSC 501 (water heaters — installation)
- ASSE 1017 (master mixing valves for temperature control)
- ASSE 1070 (point-of-use temperature-limiting valves)

---

## 6.9 Temperature Limits Summary

**Plain language:** Hot water is dangerous if it's too hot at the tap. Code says 120°F is the maximum at anything you touch. Inside the hot water system, it can be hotter for bacteria control. Outdoor equipment can be as hot as the equipment is rated for.

**Technical detail:** Temperature limits by location:

| Location | Maximum Temperature | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Interior of storage tank | 140°F | Legionella kill zone |
| Hot water distribution to fixtures | 120°F | Code maximum (anti-scald) |
| Recirculation loop | 120°F | Already tempered |
| Outdoor hot water hose bib (human-touch) | 120°F | Tempered at the hose bib |
| Outdoor equipment supply (no human touch) | 140°F+ | Labeled non-potable, to equipment only |

**Thermal management:**
- Mixing valve at the storage tank blends cold to produce 120°F outlet
- Point-of-use anti-scald valves at showers and tubs for double safety
- Insulation on all hot water pipes to minimize heat loss between tank and fixture

**Key code references:**
- OPSC 413 (anti-scald requirements at fixtures)
- ASSE 1016 (performance requirements for automatic compensating valves for showers)
- ORS 480 (Oregon Boiler and Pressure Vessel Law — if any pressurized water system qualifies as a boiler)

## 6.10 Drain, Waste, and Vent (DWV) Design

**Plain language:** The pipes that carry dirty water out of the house are separate from the pipes that carry clean water in. Drains rely on gravity — everything slopes down to the sewer or septic. Vents let air into the drain system so water flows smoothly without gurgling or siphoning the traps dry.

**Technical detail:** DWV system design:

**Drain sizing:**
- **Lavatory (bathroom sink):** 1-1/4 inch or 1-1/2 inch drain
- **Kitchen sink:** 1-1/2 inch drain
- **Shower:** 2 inch drain
- **Bathtub:** 1-1/2 inch drain (tub) or 2 inch (premium tubs with high fill rate)
- **Toilet:** 3 inch drain
- **Laundry/washing machine:** 2 inch drain
- **Main building drain:** 4 inch

**Slope:**
- 1/4 inch per foot minimum for pipes 3 inch and smaller
- 1/8 inch per foot minimum for pipes 4 inch and larger
- Maximum slope: 1 inch per foot (anything steeper causes water to race ahead and leave solids behind)

**Materials:**
- ABS (acrylonitrile butadiene styrene) or PVC (polyvinyl chloride) for all drain lines
- Solvent-welded joints (chemical bond, not glued in the mechanical sense)
- Cast iron acceptable but unnecessary for residential

**Cleanouts:**
- At every change of direction greater than 45 degrees
- At the base of every vertical stack
- Every 100 feet of horizontal run
- Accessible from the service layer (matches the design's repairability philosophy)

**Traps:**
- Every fixture has a trap (a U-shaped pipe section that holds water as a barrier against sewer gas)
- Trap must be within the maximum trap arm distance for the vent (see 6.11)
- Properly sized to hold water without siphoning

**Key code references:**
- OPSC Chapter 7 (sanitary drainage — sizing, slope, materials)
- OPSC Chapter 10 (traps and interceptors)

## 6.11 Venting Strategy — Individual Vent Per Fixture

**Plain language:** Drains need air behind them to flow properly. Without air, the water glugs down like pouring a soda bottle upside down. This design gives every fixture its own dedicated vent pipe for maximum drain performance. All the vent pipes join together and exit through a single termination above the roof or out a sidewall.

**Technical detail:** Vent system design:

**Per-fixture individual venting:**
- Each fixture gets its own vent pipe connecting directly from near the fixture trap to the main vent stack
- No Air Admittance Valves (AAVs) used — eliminates the 3-per-home limit and the 15-30 year replacement cycle
- All vents join into the main stack at proper height (above the flood rim of any fixture served)
- Fewer moving parts, nothing to fail, quieter operation

**Main vent stack:**
- 3-inch PVC or ABS vent pipe running up through the wet corner of the building
- Collects all individual fixture vents as it rises
- Terminates through the exterior wall (sidewall termination) or through the roof, depending on building design choice

**Termination location (sidewall option):**
- At least 10 feet horizontally from any door, openable window, or air intake
- OR 3 feet above if within 10 feet horizontally
- At least 10 feet from the property line
- At least 12 inches above the finish grade
- Hooded termination with insect screen
- Matches the design philosophy of minimizing roof penetrations

**Termination location (through-roof option):**
- Standard roof penetration with flashing
- Minimum 6 inches above the roof surface
- At least 10 feet from any operable roof window or skylight
- Available if the specific home's design favors it over sidewall termination

**Individual vent sizing:**
- Minimum vent size is 1/2 the drain size it serves
- Minimum 1-1/4 inch for any vent
- Common sizes: 1-1/2 inch for lavatory, 2 inch for shower or kitchen sink, 3 inch for toilet
- Trap arm length (distance from trap to vent) within code limits for each drain size — typically 3-8 feet depending on pipe diameter

**Why individual vents instead of AAVs:**
- Better flow performance (atmospheric air supply is effectively unlimited; an AAV is a valve that only opens a small amount)
- No replacement cycle (AAVs fail every 15-30 years)
- Quieter (AAVs can chatter or seal imperfectly over time)
- Fewer failure points
- More permanent installation matching the 1000-year design philosophy
- Slightly more piping material but trivial cost difference

**Key code references:**
- OPSC Chapter 9 (vents — sizing, termination, materials)
- OPSC 906 (vent termination — roof and sidewall requirements)
- OPSC 908 (individual vents — one vent per fixture)
- OPSC Table 10-1.1 (trap arm length — maximum distance from trap to vent)

## 6.12 Backflow Prevention

**Plain language:** Backflow means dirty water accidentally getting pulled back into the clean water supply. Happens during pressure drops, broken water mains, or suction events. Every fixture that could possibly contaminate the water supply needs a device that prevents backward flow. This is a code requirement everywhere, for obvious health reasons.

**Technical detail:** Backflow prevention by fixture type:

**Vacuum breakers (cheapest, for lower-risk fixtures):**
- Hose bibs (outdoor faucets): built-in vacuum breaker or add-on
- Toilet fill valves: built-in (modern fill valves include this)
- Cost: included with fixture or $10-$20 for add-on

**Double Check Valves (DCV):**
- Irrigation systems (underground sprinklers)
- Hot tub / pool makeup lines
- Cost: $100-$300

**Reduced Pressure Zone (RPZ) valves:**
- Highest-risk connections (fire sprinkler systems, commercial chemical dispensers)
- Two check valves plus a relief port that dumps if the check valves fail
- Cost: $300-$800

**Air gap:**
- Physical gap between a water outlet and the receiving fixture (e.g., kitchen sink faucet above the rim of the sink)
- Simplest and most reliable form of backflow prevention
- Built into all plumbing fixtures by design

**For this design specifically:**
- Every outdoor hose bib has built-in vacuum breaker (frost-free sillcock)
- Hot outdoor hose bib has additional tempering valve for anti-scald (also provides backflow prevention role)
- Pressure washer feed line has a dedicated DCV
- Irrigation system (if connected to potable) has a DCV or RPZ
- Any non-potable rainwater system has complete air gap separation from potable system

**Key code references:**
- OPSC 603 (backflow prevention — types and applications)
- OPSC Table 603.1 (minimum backflow prevention by fixture type)

## 6.13 Outdoor Hot Water Hose Bib and Utility Service Bay

**Plain language:** A dedicated outdoor spot with hot and cold water, electrical outlets, and equipment connections for washing cars, tractors, sidewalks, and whatever else. Kind of like a full service station on the side of the house.

**Technical detail:** Outdoor utility bay components:

**Hot and cold water (human-touch):**
- Frost-free combination sillcock (Woodford Model 22 or Prier C-244)
- Separate hot and cold handles at the exterior wall
- Built-in vacuum breaker
- Hot side: 120°F max, tempered at the hose bib
- Labeled "potable"

**High-pressure hot water for equipment:**
- Separate line from a dedicated hot loop at higher temperature
- 140°F+ supply, labeled "non-potable, equipment use only"
- Dedicated backflow prevention (DCV minimum)
- Connection to a pressure washer appliance that boosts pressure beyond code limits for the connection
- The pressure washer appliance is UL-listed and regulated separately from the building plumbing

**Compressed air:**
- Dedicated line from an air compressor in the utility area (if included)
- Quick-disconnect fitting at the service bay
- Moisture separator in the line

**Electrical outlets:**
- GFCI-protected receptacles at the service bay
- Weather-resistant covers
- 120V and 240V outlets for equipment

**Utility sink (optional):**
- Large deep sink for washing tools, fish, garden harvest
- Drain to the main stack (if wet corner is adjacent) or to a dry well with permit

**Layout:**
- All components grouped on one exterior wall near the driveway or parking area
- Protected from sun and weather by a small roof overhang
- Labeled and organized
- Matches the design philosophy of useful function, clean appearance

**Key code references:**
- OPSC 603 (backflow prevention — hose bib vacuum breakers)
- OPSC 608 (water distribution — outdoor hose bibs)
- OESC 210.8 (GFCI protection — outdoor receptacles)

## 6.14 Rainwater Cistern for Non-Potable Outdoor Use

**Plain language:** Rain falling on the roof is collected into a large tank, then used for non-drinking purposes — watering the garden, washing cars, feeding the pressure washer. Never connected to the drinking water system.

**Technical detail:** Rainwater collection system:

**Collection:**
- Gutters on every eave
- Downspouts routed to an underground or above-ground cistern
- First-flush diverter removes the initial runoff (which contains dust and bird droppings)
- Screen mesh at the cistern inlet keeps out leaves and debris

**Cistern:**
- 500-5,000 gallons depending on roof area and intended use
- Options: underground tank, above-ground poly tank, or concrete cistern
- Overflow routed to a safe discharge (not toward the building, not toward the septic drainfield)

**Distribution:**
- Separate pump and distribution piping from the potable system
- All piping labeled "non-potable" per code
- Dedicated hose bibs marked with purple handles or signage

**No cross-connection:**
- Absolutely no direct connection to the potable water system
- If a makeup line is needed (to add potable water to the cistern during dry periods), it must use a full air gap per code

**Uses:**
- Outdoor cleaning (vehicles, driveways, concrete)
- Garden and lawn irrigation
- Pressure washer feed for equipment cleaning
- Toilet flushing in some designs (requires separate permitting and is NOT part of this design's default scope)

**Benefits:**
- Free water for outdoor use (cisterns pay for themselves in 5-15 years depending on water cost)
- Reduces stormwater runoff from the site
- Resilience during drought or water service interruptions

**Key code references:**
- OPSC Appendix K (rainwater catchment systems)
- OAR Chapter 340, Division 53 (graywater reuse)
- Oregon Department of Consumer and Business Services (rainwater harvesting standards)
