# 05. Liquid Level, Acid and Alkali, and Getting Flavor Deep

Owner's questions: how much water for each method and does submerging hurt; will a
days-long cook dry out; what acid (vinegar, lemon, tomato) or alkali (baking soda,
soda ash) does; and how to get flavor deep into the meat, when and how, per method.
Sources are added to [sources.md](sources.md) under "Liquid, acid, flavor".

## Part 1. How much liquid, and does submerging hurt

### The two jobs liquid does

1. **Carry heat.** Water and steam at the same temperature cook the meat the same
   way. A bird sitting above 1 cup of water in a sealed pot is cooked by 212°F steam
   exactly as a submerged one is cooked by 212°F water. Tenderness does not depend
   on being submerged.
2. **Trade flavor.** This is one-way in practice. Meat leaks its own juices and
   flavor into the liquid; the liquid's flavor does not travel into the meat beyond
   a few millimeters (see Part 3). More liquid means more of the hen's flavor ends
   up in the pot and less in the meat. That is the real cost of submerging: not
   toughness, but "boiled" meat and good broth.

So the rule: **submerge when you want the liquid** (soup, broth, stew); **keep
liquid low when you want the meat**. Halfway up (a braise) splits the difference and
is the classic method for a reason.

### Why slow cooker recipes say "a little liquid"

A slow cooker is a sealed system. Steam condenses on the lid and drips back, so
almost nothing evaporates. A whole chicken releases roughly a cup of its own juice
over a long cook. Add a cup of stock and the bird ends up sitting in two cups it did
not need, the flavor is diluted, and the sauce is thin. Recipes written for the
stovetop, where a third of the liquid boils off, come out watery when moved to a
slow cooker. The 1/2 cup guidance is about flavor concentration, not about protecting
the meat.

### Will it dry out over days?

Depends entirely on the lid:

| Setup | Loss rate | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Sealed pressure lid, valve closed (pressure cooking) | zero | cannot dry out, but limited to 2 to 4 hours per run |
| Pressure lid closed, valve set to venting (slow cook or sous vide mode) | small; a slow drip of steam through the valve | fine for 24 hours; check once a day on multi-day holds |
| Slow cooker glass lid | very small (condenses and returns) | fine for 12 to 24 hours |
| Open pot or lid ajar at 165°F | about 1 cup per hour measured in an 8 liter bath (roughly 6 liters a day) | will run dry in a day; do not do this |
| Open simmer at 212°F | 20 to 35% of the liquid in a few hours | top up hourly |

Instant Pot's manual for the sous vide program says the pressure lid must be fully
closed or completely off; a glass lid or a lid resting loosely can let the
temperature overshoot. For a multi-day hold, the closed pressure lid with the valve
on venting is the right setting, and a water-level check every 12 to 24 hours costs
nothing.

### Liquid guide by method (for a 4 to 6 lb hen in a 6 quart pot)

| Method | Liquid | Height on the bird | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slow cooker, meat is the goal | 0 to 1/2 cup | bottom 1/2 inch or none | bird self-bastes; no evaporation; keeps flavor in the meat |
| Slow cooker, soup or broth is the goal | cover the bird | submerged | you want the flavor in the liquid; no drying risk |
| Pressure cooker, whole bird, meat is the goal | 1 cup (6 qt) or 1.5 cups (8 qt) on a trivet | bird above the water | steam cooks it; firmer, sliceable, skin less soggy; minimum liquid the cooker needs to build pressure |
| Pressure cooker, pieces, tender and shreddable | 1.5 to 2 cups | roughly halfway up the pieces | braise; pieces sit partly in liquid, which also makes the natural release gentler on the meat |
| Pressure cooker, broth stage (bones back in) | cover the bones, stay under the 2/3 line | submerged | extraction wants water contact |
| Simmer on the stove | cover the bird | submerged, lid partly on | evaporation is the enemy; top up |
| Low-temperature hold, in a bag | 1 to 2 tbsp in the bag; bath full | bag submerged, meat barely wet | flavor stays with the meat; the bath is just a heater |
| Low-temperature hold, no bag (direct poach) | cover the pieces completely in salted, seasoned liquid | fully submerged | at 165°F the air above the liquid is a poor heater and the exposed surface dries; submersion is required here |

Two limits on the pressure cooker: never above the 2/3 line, and never below the
manual's minimum (about 1 to 1.5 cups at 6 quart, 2 cups at 8 quart) or the unit
will not pressurize and may throw a burn warning.

### Direct answer to "would it hurt if it was submerged?"

- **Tenderness:** no. Same temperature, same collagen, same result.
- **Juiciness:** slightly worse in a pressure cooker (fully submerged pieces come out
  softer and more shreddable, which for an old hen is often what you want).
- **Flavor:** yes, unless you use the liquid. Submerged meat gives its flavor to the
  pot. Salting the liquid to about 1% (2 teaspoons per quart) stops the salt from
  leaching out of the meat and makes the trade much less one-sided.
- **Skin:** always soggy when submerged. Irrelevant for shredded meat.

## Part 2. Acid, alkali, enzymes, and salt

### Acid (vinegar, lemon, wine, tomato)

What acid does, from the research:

- Lowers meat pH from about 5.7 to 3 to 4.5, which makes the proteins swell and hold
  water, and makes collagen more soluble. A citrus marinade on beef shin raised
  collagen solubility from 9% to 29% and cut shear force by about 75%. Meat also
  soaked up about 65% of its weight in marinade.
- **Tested on spent laying hens specifically** (83-week-old birds, 2025): a 24 hour
  fridge marinade in 30% fruit vinegar (about 1.2 to 1.5% acid in the marinade),
  equal weight meat to marinade, cut shear force from about 31 N to 11 N, reduced
  cooking loss from 50% to 39%, and improved water-holding and texture scores.
  Pineapple and strawberry vinegars did best, but plain vinegar at the same acid
  level is the same chemistry.
- Too strong or too long turns the outer few millimeters chalky and mushy. Cook's
  Illustrated found the surface degrades with long acid soaks, and a review found
  1.5% citric acid made meat harder while 1% was fine.
- Acid does not penetrate much deeper than other marinade ingredients (a few mm),
  so its tenderizing is surface-weighted. It matters most on pieces, least on a
  whole bird.

How to use it for old hens:

- **Pre-marinade, the strongest lever:** 1 part 5% household vinegar to 3 parts
  water (about 1.2% acid), plus 2% salt by weight of the meat, 12 to 24 hours in the
  fridge, meat cut into pieces. This copies the spent-hen study. Lemon juice at
  about 1 part to 5 parts water does the same.
- **In the pot:** wine, tomato, or a splash of vinegar in a braise or pressure cook
  lands at a mild pH 4 to 5. Modest tenderizing, real flavor, and it keeps the
  broth clear. Coq au vin's wine braise is this.
- **In the broth stage:** 1 to 2 tablespoons of vinegar per gallon. The "pulls
  minerals from bones" claim measures out to about 2 mg of calcium per serving,
  which is nothing; the vinegar is there for flavor balance.
- **In a 12 to 24 hour low-temperature hold:** keep acid mild (wine-level, not
  vinegar-level). Acid keeps working for the whole hold and a strong acid will
  chalk the surface.

### Alkali (baking soda; soda ash)

- **Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate)** raises surface pH from about 5.5 to 6.5.
  Proteins then bind less tightly and hold more water, which is the "velveting"
  trick. It does nothing to collagen, so it does not fix an old leg; it does help
  the **breast**, which is the part that dries out. Use 1/2 teaspoon per pound of
  chicken, 15 to 20 minutes, then rinse. Or a 0.3% solution, which a food-science
  review names as the maximum before color darkens and flavor turns soapy.
- **Soda ash (sodium carbonate)** is about ten times more alkaline, is used in
  food only as ramen "kansui," and industrial grades are not food safe. It has no
  place on meat. Baking soda does the same job with a wide safety margin.

### Enzymes (papain, bromelain, ginger)

- Papain (papaya) and bromelain (pineapple) cut muscle proteins; papain also cuts
  collagen. Fresh ginger contains a similar enzyme. Commercial "meat tenderizer"
  powder is papain plus salt.
- They work at fridge temperature and keep working up to about 140 to 160°F, so
  they are active during the slow ramp of a pressure or slow cook and during the
  first hours of a low-temperature hold.
- Dose and time are everything: 30 minutes to 2 hours, up to 4 for thick pieces.
  Overnight or overdosed and the meat goes to paste. A study on tough meat found
  0.1% bromelain and 25% ginger extract caused "excessive softening and structural
  disintegration."
- Practical: a tablespoon of grated fresh ginger or 1/4 cup pineapple juice in the
  acid marinade above, for the last 1 to 2 hours only. Good for the breast; useful
  but not necessary for legs, which the heat will handle.

### Salt: the tenderizer that actually reaches the middle

- Salt penetrates. 1 to 2 cm in 12 hours, and all the way to the center of a piece
  in 24 to 48 hours, or during any long low-temperature hold.
- Salted proteins hold more water. In a 24-hour sous vide test, meat cured in 2%
  salt lost 4 to 7% of its weight; unsalted meat lost 12%. In a 10-hour 158°F sous
  vide of duck, brining cut weight loss from 39% to 30%.
- Too much salt goes the other way (above about 6% brine strength the meat firms
  up and starts to cure). Stay at 1.5 to 2% by meat weight for a dry brine, or a 5%
  brine (50 g per liter) for 4 to 8 hours, or an equilibrium brine at 1.5 to 2% of
  the combined weight of meat plus water for 24 to 48 hours (cannot over-salt).

## Part 3. Getting flavor deep into the meat

### What actually moves into meat

| Ingredient | How deep | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Salt | to the center, given 24 to 48 hours | the only thing that goes all the way; also improves juiciness |
| Sugar, small water-soluble molecules (garlic and onion powder, MSG, soy) | a little, with salt, over a day | ride along with brine |
| Acid | a few mm | surface tenderizing and flavor |
| Herbs, spices, aromatics, oil-soluble flavors | 1 to 3 mm even after 18 to 24 hours | wine penetrated less than 1 mm into short ribs in 18 hours (Cook's Illustrated) |

Marinating "longer" does not fix this. It is diffusion through dense muscle; the
outside gets everything, the inside gets salt.

### The four ways that work, in order of effect

1. **Equilibrium brine or dry brine, 24 to 48 hours.** 1.5 to 2% salt by weight,
   with sugar and powdered aromatics. Seasons the core, adds juiciness. Works with
   every cooking method. This is the baseline for every old hen.
2. **Injection.** The only way to put anything other than salt deep inside. About 1
   ounce per pound of a 2 to 4% salt solution with fully dissolved flavors (stock,
   soy, Worcestershire, garlic powder, melted butter). Strain out solids so the
   needle does not clog. Inject a grid every 1 to 1.5 inches into thighs, legs and
   breast, massage, and rest 2 to 24 hours. Works with every method; especially
   valuable for the breast.
3. **More surface area.** Cutting the bird into 8 pieces roughly doubles the surface
   that marinades and braising liquid can reach, and lets you pull the breast early.
   For shredded meat it is the single biggest flavor gain after salt.
4. **Cook in the flavor, then rest in it.** Meat takes up liquid as it cools. A
   braise or pressure cook rested 20 to 30 minutes in its own liquid, or shredded
   meat tossed with the reduced liquid, tastes far more seasoned than meat pulled
   hot. The classic advice that stews taste better the next day is this effect.

### When and how, per method

| Method | Before | During | After |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pressure cook | brine or inject 12 to 24 h ahead; optional 1.2% vinegar marinade | aromatics and wine in the liquid; salt the liquid to about 1% if the meat is submerged | natural release; rest meat in the liquid 20 to 30 min; shred into reduced liquid |
| Slow cooker | same brine or injection | little liquid; onions and aromatics under the bird; skin side up | rest in juices; reduce juices on the stove for a sauce |
| Low-temperature hold (bag) | salt in the bag is enough, it equilibrates during the hold; add aromatics and a spoon of wine or stock | nothing to do | chill in the bag for later or shred into the bag juices, which are gelatin-rich |
| Low-temperature hold (no bag) | brine not required; salt the poaching liquid to 1 to 1.5% | keep pieces fully submerged; lid closed, valve venting | cool the meat in the liquid; use the liquid as broth |
| Simmer | brine or inject | salt the water to 1%; aromatics | cool in the broth |

### Old hen specifics

- The hen already has more flavor than a supermarket bird; the job is seasoning and
  moisture, not adding flavor.
- Dark meat: brine (or equilibrium brine), optional vinegar marinade, cook long.
- Breast: brine or inject, optional baking soda or ginger for 30 minutes to 2
  hours, pull early, or accept it as shred-and-sauce meat.
- Do not stack every tenderizer at once. Salt plus one acid pre-marinade covers
  most of the gain. Adding enzymes on top of acid on top of a long cook is how a
  hen turns to paste.
