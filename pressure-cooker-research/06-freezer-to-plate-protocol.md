# 06. Freezer-to-Plate Protocol for an Old Laying Hen

A single recommended sequence, built from documents 02, 04 and 05. Every step
lists what it does, every addition lists every trait it brings. Times are for a
4 to 6 lb bird.

## A. Timeline

| When | Step | What to do | What it does (purpose) | If skipped |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Day 0 (4 days out) | Freezer to fridge | Move the bird to the fridge, still wrapped, on a tray | Slow thaw below 40°F. If the bird was frozen before rigor resolved, slow thawing largely prevents "thaw rigor," the violent contraction that makes pre-rigor frozen meat extremely tough. Also keeps the surface out of the bacterial danger zone the whole time | Counter or warm-water thaw triggers the worst-case contraction and puts the surface in the danger zone for hours |
| Days 0 to 2 | Wait | 2 to 3 days for a 4 to 6 lb bird | Same as above; a whole bird thaws about 1 day per 4 to 5 lb | Cooking from frozen is safe under pressure but fast-thaws the meat inside the pot, which is the wrong choice for a possibly pre-rigor bird |
| Day 2 to 3 | Post-thaw rest | Leave the thawed bird in the fridge 24 hours more | Lets any post-thaw rigor resolve and gives the meat's own enzymes a day of aging, which loosens muscle proteins. Homesteaders who rest birds before freezing report noticeably more tender meat | Meat may be stiffer and chewier than it needed to be |
| Day 3 | Cut into 8 pieces | Two breasts, two thighs, two legs, two wings; back and neck to the broth pile | Doubles the surface the brine and acid can reach; lets the breast be treated and pulled separately; pieces cook evenly; everything fits under the fill line | Whole bird: brine reaches the center slower, breast overcooks before legs are done, flavor and acid reach less meat |
| Day 3, then 12 to 24 h | Brine and acid marinade | Per pound of meat: 1 teaspoon (2%) salt, 1/2 teaspoon sugar, enough 1:3 vinegar-and-water to cover, onion, garlic, bay, peppercorn. Zip bag or covered bowl, fridge | See Section B for every trait. In short: seasons to the center, holds juice in, partly dissolves collagen at the surface, adds brightness | Meat is bland inside, loses about twice the juice in cooking, and is measurably tougher (spent-hen study: shear force roughly three times higher without the acid marinade) |
| Day 3 alternative for a whole bird | Inject | 1 oz per pound of a 3% salt solution with dissolved flavors, grid every 1 to 1.5 in; rest 2 to 24 h | Only way to get salt and dissolved flavor deep into a whole bird fast; adds fat if butter is included | Whole bird brined only from the outside needs 48 h for salt to reach the center |
| Day 4, last 1 to 2 h before cooking (optional, breast only) | Enzyme or baking soda on the breast | Either grated ginger or pineapple juice in the marinade for 1 to 2 h, or 1/2 tsp baking soda per lb rubbed on for 15 to 20 min and rinsed | Breast has no collagen to soften into, so heat cannot rescue it. Enzymes cut fibers; baking soda makes fibers hold water | Breast is dry. Fine if it is going to be shredded into sauce anyway |
| Day 4 | Drain, pat dry, optional sear | Discard the marinade. Sear skin side in the pot 3 to 4 min if you want browned flavor | Browning adds roasted flavor to the surface and the broth; it does nothing for tenderness | No loss in tenderness; less depth in the broth |
| Day 4 | Cook | Fast track or best track, Section C | Converts collagen to gelatin | The only irreversible step: too short is rubbery, quick release is stringy |
| Day 4 | Rest in the liquid | 20 to 30 min in the cooking liquid before pulling the meat | Meat reabsorbs liquid and seasoning as it cools; juices redistribute | Meat pulled hot tastes less seasoned and bleeds juice on the board |
| Day 4 | Broth stage | Bones, skin, cartilage, back, neck, feet if you have them, vegetables, 1 tbsp vinegar, water to cover; 90 to 180 min high pressure, natural release | Recovers the gelatin, flavor and minerals from the 60% of the bird that is not meat; pressure gives a darker, meatier stock than a simmer | Most of an old hen's value is thrown away |

Total: about four days, of which about 30 minutes is hands-on. If you are in a
hurry, the minimum that still matters is the fridge thaw plus a 12 hour brine.

## B. Every addition and every trait it brings

| Addition | Amount | Traits it brings | Traits to watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Salt** | 1.5 to 2% of meat weight (about 1 tsp table salt per lb), or an equilibrium brine at 1.5 to 2% of meat plus water | 1. Seasons to the center; it is the only ingredient that reaches the core (1 to 2 cm in 12 h, all the way in 24 to 48 h). 2. Osmosis runs in two stages: it first draws water out to the surface, then that salty liquid is drawn back in and carries dissolved flavors (sugar, garlic, soy) with it. 3. Dissolves the protein myosin and changes protein charge so fibers hold water: cooking loss drops from about 12% to 4 to 7% in sous vide tests and from 39% to 30% in a duck study. 4. Mild tenderizing by swelling fibers. 5. Slows spoilage slightly during the rest | Over about 3% by meat weight or a brine above 6 to 8% the meat firms up and starts to cure. Rinse is not needed at 2% |
| **Acid (vinegar, lemon)** in the marinade | 1 part 5% vinegar to 3 parts water (about 1.2% acid), or 1 part lemon juice to 5 parts water | 1. Drops pH from about 5.7 to 4 to 4.5 so proteins swell and take up liquid (beef shin gained 65% of its weight in a citrus marinade). 2. Makes collagen more soluble (9% to 29% in one study). 3. On spent hens: shear force cut by two thirds, cooking loss from 50% to 39%, higher water-holding. 4. Bright flavor. 5. Mild antimicrobial on the surface | Stronger than about 1.5% acid or longer than 24 h chalks and mushes the outer few mm. Penetrates only a few mm, so its tenderizing is surface-weighted; pieces benefit, a whole bird barely does |
| **Sugar** | 1/2 tsp per lb in the marinade | 1. Balances salt and acid. 2. Rides in with the salt for a rounder flavor at the core. 3. Slight extra water retention. 4. Helps browning if you sear | Too much tastes like ham brine |
| **Aromatics (onion, garlic, bay, pepper, herbs)** | a handful in the marinade and again in the pot | 1. Surface flavor. 2. Flavor in the cooking liquid and broth. 3. Garlic and onion powder, being water-soluble, travel a little way in with the brine | None of it goes deeper than 1 to 3 mm; that is why the broth and sauce carry most of the flavor |
| **Enzyme (fresh ginger, pineapple juice, papain powder)** | 1 tbsp grated ginger or 1/4 cup pineapple juice per lb, 30 min to 2 h; papain per the package | 1. Cuts muscle proteins (papain also cuts collagen) at fridge temperature and keeps working up to about 140 to 160°F, so it is active during the cooker's ramp. 2. Fast: the only tenderizer that works in an hour. 3. Ginger adds flavor | Overnight or overdosed turns meat to paste. Breast only; legs do not need it |
| **Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate)** | 1/2 tsp per lb, 15 to 20 min, rinse; or a 0.3% solution | 1. Raises surface pH to about 6.5 so proteins bind less tightly and hold water: the "velveted" breast effect. 2. Cheap and reliable | Does nothing for collagen, so it does not help old legs. Soapy taste and darkening if overdone or unrinsed. Do not use soda ash (sodium carbonate); ten times stronger and not food-grade unless sold as such |
| **Wine or tomato in the pot** | 1/2 to 1 cup wine, or a cup of crushed tomato, in the braising liquid | 1. Mild acid (pH 4 to 5 after dilution) for modest tenderizing across the whole cook. 2. Flavor and color in the sauce and broth. 3. Keeps broth bright. This is what coq au vin is | Long low-temperature holds: keep to wine strength, not vinegar strength, because the acid keeps working for 24 h |
| **Fat (butter or rendered chicken fat) in an injection** | 1 to 2 tbsp melted per cup of injection | 1. Mouthfeel and perceived juiciness. 2. Carries fat-soluble flavors (garlic, herbs) into the meat, which brine cannot | Must be warm and fully melted or it clogs the needle |
| **Cooking liquid (water or stock)** | 1 to 2 cups under pressure; halfway up the pieces for a braise; cover for broth; 1 to 2 tbsp in a sous vide bag | 1. Carries heat: steam and water at the same temperature cook identically. 2. Collects the hen's juices and gelatin, which become the broth and sauce. 3. Submerged meat tolerates pressure release better | More liquid means more of the meat's flavor ends up in the pot: fine if you keep the liquid, a loss if you pour it out. Salt the liquid to about 1% when meat is submerged so the meat does not lose salt to it |
| **Vinegar in the broth stage** | 1 to 2 tbsp per gallon | Flavor balance | The "pulls minerals from bones" claim measures out to about 2 mg of calcium per serving; negligible |
| **Chicken feet or extra bones** | a handful | Gelatin, which is what makes broth set and gives shredded meat its silky mouthfeel | None |

## C. The cook, two tracks

| | Fast track (about 2 hours) | Best track (overnight, hands-off) |
|---|---|---|
| Equipment mode | High pressure | Sous vide or custom keep-warm mode at 165 to 175°F (no pressure); or a slow cooker at Low |
| Setup | Pieces in the pot, 1.5 to 2 cups liquid with wine and aromatics, halfway up the pieces | Legs, thighs and wings in a bag with a spoon of wine and the aromatics, or fully submerged in 1% salted stock with the pressure lid closed and the valve on venting. Breast in its own bag |
| Time | 40 to 45 min for tender pieces that hold together; 75 to 90 min for fall-apart shredding meat | Legs and thighs 12 to 24 h at 165 to 175°F (older bird, longer; slow cooker Low about 8 to 12 h). Breast 2 to 4 h at 150 to 155°F, done separately |
| Release | Full natural release, 20 to 25 min. Never quick | Not applicable |
| Rest | 20 to 30 min in the liquid | Chill in the bag, or open and shred into the bag juices |
| Breast handling | Pull at 25 to 30 min if you want a sliceable breast, otherwise shred it into sauce | Separate bag, separate temperature |
| Broth | Bones back in, 90 to 180 min high pressure, natural release | Same, in the pressure mode afterwards |
| Result | Tender, braised texture, driest of the three, excellent broth | Juiciest and most tender an old hen gets; gelatin-rich juices in the bag |
| Check for done | Leg meat pulls from the bone with light pressure. If it resists, add 15 min under pressure; if it shreds into strings, it went too long | Same; at these temperatures overshooting by hours does little harm |

## D. What to skip if time is short

Priority order, most important first: fridge thaw (never counter thaw), salt for
12 hours, natural release, rest in liquid, acid marinade, cut into pieces, sear,
enzyme or baking soda on the breast.
