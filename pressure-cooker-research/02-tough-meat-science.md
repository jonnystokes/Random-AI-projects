## 1. Why old birds are tough

A supermarket broiler is 6 to 8 weeks old. A retired laying hen is 1.5 to 4 years
old. Two things change with age:

- **More connective tissue.** Muscles that have worked for years carry more collagen
  around and between the fibers.
- **More cross-linked collagen.** Young collagen dissolves easily. Mature collagen
  forms heat-stable cross-links that need higher temperature or longer time to
  break. This is the same reason an old cow's chuck needs more than a calf's.

Old-hen meat is also more flavorful (more myoglobin, more fat in the dark meat, more
flavor compounds), which is why classic dishes like coq au vin were built for an old
rooster.

Breast meat on an old hen is a special case. It has little collagen and little fat,
so it has nothing to become tender "through." Any cook long enough to soften the
legs will leave the breast dry. Plan to shred or chop it into dishes with sauce.

## 2. Collagen to gelatin

Collagen is a triple-helix protein. Heat unwinds it and water hydrolyzes it into
gelatin, which is soft, holds water, and gives braised meat its silky mouthfeel.
The reaction needs both heat and water, which is why a dry roast of a tough cut just
gets tougher while a braise gets tender.

Temperature thresholds (from Modernist Cuisine, Baldwin, McGee, and the sources
cited):

- Below about 122°F: nothing happens to collagen.
- 122 to 140°F: collagen starts to shrink and, very slowly, dissolve. Enzymes
  (collagenase) still help at these temperatures. Sous vide "tough cut" recipes live
  here: 131°F for 24 to 72 hours.
- 140 to 160°F: collagen shrinks hard, squeezing juice out. Dissolution is slow.
- 160 to 180°F: dissolution becomes practical. Hours, not days.
- 180 to 205°F: "rapid hydrolytic cleavage." This is the braise and simmer band.
- 212°F and above: fast. Under pressure (229 to 250°F) it is minutes to an hour.

**Rate vs temperature.** One time-to-fork-tender model used by a meat supplier
(consistent with first-order Arrhenius behavior; not peer reviewed but matches
kitchen experience) gives:

| Temperature | Hydrolysis rate | Time to 90% converted (fork tender) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 160°F | 3.5% / hr | ~26 hours |
| 175°F | 8.5% / hr | ~11 hours |
| 185°F | 15% / hr | ~6 hours |
| 195°F | 22.5% / hr | ~4 hours |
| 205°F | 34% / hr | ~2.6 hours |
| 240 to 250°F (pressure) | very fast | ~0.5 to 1.5 hours in practice |

Roughly, the rate doubles every 12 to 15°F. This is why 30 to 45 minutes at 244°F
does what 6 hours at 185°F does.

## 3. The trade-off: temperature vs moisture

The second protein story is the muscle fibers themselves. Above about 140°F the
fibers contract and expel water; the hotter they get, the more they expel. Cooking
loss (weight lost as juice) rises steadily from 130°F to 200°F and beyond.

So there are two clocks running in opposite directions:

- Collagen conversion: faster and more complete at higher temperature. Good.
- Fiber moisture loss: worse at higher temperature. Bad.

Every method for tough meat is a bet on how to trade these off:

- **Pressure (229 to 250°F):** wins the collagen clock outright and loses the
  moisture clock. Meat is tender fast but has given up a lot of juice into the pot.
  Overshoot the time and the fibers, already dry, separate into strings.
- **Simmer or braise (185 to 205°F):** the traditional compromise. Hours. Meat is
  tender and moderately juicy; the sauce collects the lost juice and gelatin.
- **Low-temperature hold (131 to 175°F):** wins the moisture clock and pays with
  time: 12 to 72 hours. Meat is tender and noticeably juicier because the fibers
  never got hot enough to wring themselves out.

A pressure cooker's pressure level moves you only within the top band (229 to
250°F). Dropping from 12 psi to 1.5 psi (244 to 217°F) buys a modest moisture gain
at roughly double the time. That is the honest scope of "slow cooking under
pressure": a small effect. The big moisture win is at 160 to 175°F, which is a
non-pressure mode.

## 4. Three regimes compared

| Regime | Temperature | Time for old hen legs | Tenderness | Juiciness | Texture risk | Broth quality |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| High pressure electric | 240 to 244°F | 30 to 45 min (holds together); 60 to 100 min (falls apart) | High | Lowest | Stringy if over time or quick released | Excellent, dark, meaty |
| Stovetop pressure | 250°F | ~20% less than above | High | Lowest | Same | Excellent |
| Low pressure electric | 229 to 233°F | ~40 to 60 min / 80 to 120 min | High | Slightly better | Same | Very good |
| Breville minimum | 217°F | roughly 1.3 to 1.5x the high pressure times (extrapolated) | High | Slightly better again | Same | Good |
| Slow cooker or simmer | 185 to 205°F | 4 to 6 h pieces; 10 to 12 h whole | High | Moderate | Mushy if far over | Clean, lighter flavor |
| Low-temperature hold ("sous vide" or custom keep warm) | 165 to 175°F | 12 to 24 h | High | Best | Very forgiving; gel collects in bag or pot | Not made in the same step |
| Very low hold | 143 to 150°F | 48 to 72 h | High (still slightly chewy per some cooks) | Best | Forgiving; needs bag | none |

Reading the table for the owner's question "fast, high pressure, or slow, which is
most tender?": all three reach "tender" if run long enough. They differ in juiciness
and texture. Ranked by final eating quality for dark meat: low-temperature hold,
then simmer, then pressure. Ranked by speed: exactly the reverse.

## 5. What the research says about meat from older animals

Peer-reviewed work on beef, directly relevant because the mechanism is the same:

- Naqvi et al., Meat Science 2021: low-value muscles (biceps femoris, semitendinosus)
  from young and older cattle cooked sous vide at 55, 65 and 75°C (131, 149, 167°F)
  for 1, 8 and 18 hours. Shear force fell with time and temperature in all cases, but
  **meat from older animals needed the highest temperature (167°F) and the longest
  time (18 hours) to match the tenderness young meat reached at gentler settings.**
- Chicken breast and thigh sous vide study (2023): thigh has about twice the collagen
  of breast (30 vs 16 g per 100 g dry basis). Cooking at 65°C (149°F) reduced shear
  force and collagen more than 55°C; longer time increased cooking loss.
- Baldwin's sous vide guide: tough cuts at 131°F for 24 to 48 hours; poultry legs
  confit-style at 176°F for 8 to 12 hours; higher temperature trades juiciness for
  speed.
- Hobby cooks with actual stewing hens report legs needing 3 days at 143°F to be
  tender, or 12 hours at 143°F for young chicken legs.

Practical translation: for an old hen, do not expect the 143 to 150°F "pink and
juicy" sous vide results that work on young thighs. Use 165 to 175°F for 12 to 24
hours, or go hot and short.

## 6. Old hens specifically: what real cooks report

Collected from homestead blogs and forums (full table in
`data/old-hen-cook-reports.csv`):

| Source | Cooker | Setting and time | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole-Fed Homestead | Instant Pot, bird cut in 8 pieces, 2 cups water | High, 30 min, natural release | "Tender enough to eat," slightly rubbery, best chopped into soups and casseroles. Bones then go back for 90 to 180 min of broth |
| Culinary Q (2.5 lb hen) | stovetop pressure cooker | 15 min | Tender for stew; yield only 9 oz meat |
| Backyard Chickens thread | pressure cooker | 30 min then 15 more | Tough at 30, stringy at 45; dark meat hard to separate from bone |
| Backyard Chickens (roosters) | pressure cooker | ~40 min | Then cut up for gumbo |
| Ever Growing Farm | stovetop, 8 cups water, whole | 45 min, natural release | "Delicious, tender meat"; rest the bird 4 to 5 days in the fridge first |
| Running to the Kitchen | Instant Pot, whole, water to max line | High, 100 min, 10 min natural then quick | "Literally falling off the bones" |
| The Top Meal | Instant Pot or Ninja, whole, 4 quarts water | High, 90 min, 10 min natural then quick | "Melt in your mouth tender," rich broth |
| Running to the Kitchen | slow cooker | Low, 10 to 12 h | Same as pressure result |
| Various | oven or stovetop braise, coq au vin | 200°F, 4 h or more | Edible and flavorful; never as tender as a young bird |

Two patterns:

1. There are **two useful endpoints**, not one. About 30 to 45 minutes at high
   pressure gives meat that is tender but intact (for pieces and chopping). About 90
   to 100 minutes gives meat that falls off the bone (for shredding into soup, tacos,
   pot pie) with broth made in the same step. The "stringy at 45 minutes" report sits
   in the valley between the two: fibers dried but collagen not yet fully gone, so
   the meat is neither intact nor falling apart. The way through the valley is more
   time, not less.
2. **Nobody gets young-chicken texture.** The best reports describe the meat as
   flavorful and tender in a braised way. The breast is universally the weak part.

## 7. Frozen birds: thaw rigor and how to handle it

This is the most important thing in this document for the owner's actual freezer.

After slaughter, muscle goes into rigor mortis over the first hours, then relaxes
over 1 to 3 days as enzymes work. Homesteaders rest birds 24 to 48 hours in the
fridge (some say 4 to 5 days for old hens) before freezing so that rigor has
resolved.

If a bird is frozen **before** rigor has resolved (within hours of slaughter), the
muscle still holds the fuel for contraction. On thawing it contracts violently, a
phenomenon meat scientists call **thaw rigor**. Thaw-rigor meat is described in the
literature as "extremely tough" with poor juiciness. Fast thawing at warm
temperatures makes it worst. Studies on pre-rigor frozen chicken breast and leg
found that slow thawing at about 28°F (just below freezing) largely eliminates the
shortening.

What to do with the birds already in the freezer:

- Ask whether they were rested before freezing. If they were rested 24 hours or more,
  thaw rigor is not a concern.
- If they were frozen the same day: thaw them **slowly in the refrigerator** over 2
  to 3 days, never in warm water or on the counter. Then leave the thawed bird in the
  fridge a further 1 to 2 days before cooking so the post-thaw rigor can resolve.
  This is the closest a home kitchen gets to the -2°C thawing the research
  recommends.
- Cooking straight from frozen is safe in a pressure cooker (a 4 to 5 lb bird needs
  60 to 70 minutes at high pressure plus a full natural release, and cannot be cut
  up first). It is the wrong choice for a pre-rigor frozen bird because the fast
  thaw inside the pot triggers the worst-case contraction.
- Cutting the bird into pieces before freezing (as one blog does) is a separate,
  purely practical convenience; it does not affect rigor.

## 8. The fast strategy

Goal: dinner in about 1.5 hours total from a thawed bird.

1. Cut the bird into 8 pieces (or at least halves). Pieces cook faster and more
   evenly than a whole bird, and let you pull the breast early.
2. Optional 5 minute sear of the skin side in the pot. Flavor only.
3. Add 1.5 to 2 cups liquid (water, or stock, plus a splash of vinegar or wine;
   acid helps a little with collagen and a lot with flavor). Meat submerged or nearly
   so; submerged meat tolerates release better.
4. High pressure (10 to 12 psi on an electric). Low pressure gains nothing here.
5. Time: 35 to 45 minutes for tender-intact pieces; 60 to 90 minutes if the goal is
   shredded meat. Add 20% at 6,000 ft.
6. **Full natural release, 15 to 25 minutes.** Never quick release meat.
7. Pull the breast pieces first if you want any chance of a moist breast; return
   dark meat and bones for more time if needed.
8. Two-stage variant: after step 7, pull all meat, return bones, skin, and
   cartilage with aromatics and water, and run 90 to 180 minutes for broth. Instant
   Pot allows 4 hours per run; Breville 2 hours (run twice).

Expected result: dark meat tender and flavorful, slightly more "braised" than
"roasted"; breast dry unless pulled early; excellent broth.

Faster still: a 15 psi stovetop cuts the pressure time by about 20% and ramps in a
third of the time, at the cost of the walk-away safety the owner wants.

## 9. The patient strategy

Goal: the softest, juiciest possible old-hen meat; time is not a constraint.

Option A, low-temperature hold in the multi-cooker (needs a sous vide or custom
keep-warm mode, or a separate immersion circulator):

1. Cut into pieces. Separate breast from legs and thighs.
2. Season. Vacuum bag, or zip bag with the air pressed out, or simply submerge the
   pieces in seasoned broth or rendered fat in the pot (a low-temperature poach or
   confit; same physics, no bag needed).
3. Legs and thighs: hold at 165 to 175°F for 12 to 24 hours. Older bird, longer.
   Breast: 150 to 155°F for 2 to 4 hours only, done separately.
4. Food safety: everything above 140°F is outside the danger zone; poultry held at
   150°F for a few minutes is already pasteurized, so a 12 hour hold at 165°F is safe.
5. Finish: chill in the bag for later, or crisp skin in a hot pan, or shred into
   the gelatin-rich juices from the bag.

Expected result: the most tender and juicy old-hen dark meat any method gives. The
breast, treated separately, is as good as an old breast gets.

Option B, slow simmer (any cooker with a slow cook mode that actually holds 185 to
200°F, or a stovetop pot):

1. Whole or pieces in liquid to cover, with acid and aromatics.
2. 185 to 200°F, 4 to 6 hours for pieces, 10 to 12 hours whole.
3. Cool in the liquid; shred. This is the classic stewing-hen method and gives a
   cleaner-tasting broth than pressure.

Caveat for Option B on an Instant Pot: its slow cook mode runs cool and does not
evaporate; use "More"/High and reduce the liquid, or use an actual slow cooker.

Option C, hybrid (best texture with useful speed):

1. Low-temperature hold at 165 to 175°F for 8 to 12 hours (overnight).
2. Then 10 to 15 minutes at high pressure with natural release to finish the last of
   the collagen and make the broth.

This has not been benchmarked in any source found; it is a logical combination of
the two regimes and is listed as something worth testing.

## 10. Answer to "optimal pressure and temperature"

There is no single optimum because pressure and temperature select between speed and
juiciness. Stated as choices:

- **Optimal for speed:** the highest pressure the cooker holds (12 psi electric, 244°F;
  15 psi stovetop, 250°F). Tenderness arrives in 30 to 90 minutes. Quality is
  "good braise," not "best possible."
- **Optimal for eating quality:** no pressure at all. 165 to 175°F held for 12 to 24
  hours for old dark meat. This is the temperature band where collagen converts at
  a useful rate while the fibers stay below the temperature that wrings them dry.
- **Under pressure but gentler:** the lowest pressure available (1.5 psi, 217°F on
  the one cooker that offers it; 6 to 7 psi, about 230°F on the rest) at 1.3 to 2x the
  high-pressure time. A small moisture gain. Not worth choosing a cooker for on its
  own, but a fine feature to have.

The single most important variable after temperature is **release method**: natural,
always, for meat.

## 11. Broth as a by-product

Pressure makes better broth than a simmer for the same time. America's Test Kitchen
compared wing stock at 1.5 hours under pressure vs 2 hours simmered: the pressure
stock was darker, richer, and "meatier" because the higher temperature extracts more
and breaks proteins into savory peptides. The simmered stock was cleaner and lighter.
Claims circulating online that a simmer extracts "only 65%" of the collagen a
pressure cooker does, or that simmering past 2 hours "pyrolyzes" collagen, are not
supported by a citable source and should be ignored.

For old hens: after pulling the meat, run bones, skin, cartilage, feet (if
available), an onion, a carrot, celery, and a spoon of vinegar for 90 to 180 minutes
at high pressure with natural release. Two 90 minute runs work when the cooker's
single-run limit is 2 hours. This is what makes the low meat yield of an old hen
(one cook reports 9 oz of meat from a 2.5 lb bird) worth the effort.

## 12. Open questions worth testing at home

1. Same bird, split: legs at 12 psi for 40 min vs 6 psi for 60 min vs 1.5 psi for
   80 min. Does the lower pressure give measurably juicier meat? No source has run
   this.
2. Low-temperature hold 165°F for 12 h vs 175°F for 12 h on old-hen legs: which is
   tender enough, and how much juicier is 165?
3. Hybrid (overnight hold then 10 min pressure) vs straight 90 min pressure: texture
   and time.
4. A freezer sample: thaw one bird slowly with a 2 day fridge rest vs one cooked from
   frozen. If the from-frozen bird is much tougher, the flock was frozen pre-rigor
   and every remaining bird should get the slow thaw.
